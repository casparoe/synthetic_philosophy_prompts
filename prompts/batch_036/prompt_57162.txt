I'm writing the methods chapter of a dissertation on mind-wandering and attention, in a lab that collects both beeper-prompted reports about the moment of the beep and longer retrospective interviews about single episodes. Everything we have is first-person, and we've had two internal arguments about how to say that in print without either overselling it or apologising for it. The first was settled by an analogy my supervisor now deploys constantly, usually in response to the reviewer-style objection that introspective reports are worthless. Her version runs roughly: "Introspection is a measuring instrument like any other. Nobody throws away a thermometer because it's noisy — you characterise the noise, calibrate it against a reference, average repeated readings, and train the operator. Our participants have had dozens of hours of interview training, and their reports get checked against task performance and against EEG, so that's calibration, and the sceptics are doing ordinary measurement-error talk with extra drama." She's right that this isn't her private metaphor: there is a published literature in consciousness studies that uses exactly this vocabulary, talking about validating and calibrating first-person and second-person methods, and a parallel line of work defending introspection from within against the classic confabulation results. The second argument was with a co-author who trained in metrology before switching fields, and who thinks the analogy is fraudulent in one specific place: for a thermometer there is something the instrument answers to that is not itself another thermometer, and he could not work out what plays that role for an experience.

Could you audit the analogy rather than take either side? I'd like an explicit correspondence table first — what plays the measurand, the instrument, the reading, the noise, the reference standard, the calibration procedure, the averaging over trials, the operator training, and the drift or repeatability — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually threaten my supervisor's intended conclusion and the ones that are merely inelegant. I'm especially interested in four pressure points and how much each costs her: (i) whether there is any candidate for the reference standard that isn't just another first-person report or a construct already inferred from such reports; (ii) reactivity, since attending to an experience may alter or even partly constitute it, which has no obvious thermometer counterpart; (iii) the possibility that the error is systematic and internally coherent rather than random — confabulated explanations tend to be plausible and consistent, so averaging many readings would not shrink the bias the way it shrinks thermal noise; and (iv) the distinction between reports about mental contents and reports about the processes that produced them, which might make the instrument accurate over one range and useless over another. Please also tell me what the case of the beeper study in which a philosopher and a psychologist collected the same participant's sampled reports, argued about them at length, and still ended up disagreeing about whether her introspection was accurate looks like from inside the analogy — two calibrators getting different results from the same reference, or something the analogy simply cannot represent? Then give me the verdict on the conclusion the analogy is being used to license: can trained first-person reports enter a dataset with characterised error bars alongside behavioural and neural measures, is that claim survivable in some restricted form (and if so, restricted how — by grain, by content type, by time of report, by what counts as validation), or was the analogy carrying the conclusion from the start? If it fails or only half-works, say what analogy would serve better and what that replacement costs: witness testimony under cross-examination, proprioception as a non-inferential but illusable channel, a gauge that is wired into the circuit it measures, a translator rather than a sensor, a user interface that trades fidelity for usability — or something else. No bibliography, please; I know the literature roughly and want the analysis.

Two requests about how you handle the epistemics. First, keep separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this — as opposed to what merely sounds settled in textbook summaries — from what is live and contested; I don't want either the strong reliability claim or the strong unreliability claim presented as the consensus view, and where the parties disagree I'd like to know whether the disagreement is substantive or largely about what counts as a successful report. Second, be self-critical about my framing: I came in expecting the metrology objection to win, so tell me where my setup quietly begs that question, and where the analogy has resources I've underplayed.
