I'm writing up a section of a talk on forgery and I need a case built to a fairly odd spec — not the usual "perfectly undetectable copy" setup, which I've never found any use for because it just begs the question against the people I'm arguing with.

Three positions on the table. (i) The view that nothing but what you can perceive in the object itself bears on its aesthetic value — provenance is beside the point. (ii) The historicist/contextualist view, on which how a thing was actually made fixes which categories it belongs to, and the categories fix what aesthetic properties it really has. (iii) The achievement view, on which a large chunk of a work's value is what the artist managed to bring off and how hard that was to bring off.

Now: design me a pair of objects — an old master picture and a modern forgery of it — such that (i) is completely indifferent, while (ii) and (iii) both have to rank the forgery *above* the original on precisely the quality the original is famous for. Not overall, and not as works of art: on the celebrated dimension. So the two objects must be perceptually matched — same support, same size, same marks, same surface — which means the whole difference has to come out of the history of making, and it has to come out the other way round from the standard story. I also want the flip to be triggered by a fact about how the *original* got made, one that only comes to light because of the investigation into the forgery, so both facts are public at once and nobody can shelter behind ignorance.

Constraints: no sci-fi, no robot arms or printers or projectors, no tracing — the copy is laid down by eye and hand by an exceptional human, and you owe me an account of why that's physically credible for this particular picture rather than just an assertion. Invent the artist and the work, no real ones, but keep the technical detail concrete and correct: name the actual methods and the actual equipment, and make sure they'd really deliver what you need them to deliver. Scenario in two paragraphs maximum, then the analysis.

In the analysis I want each of the three verdicts stated and then shown to fall out of that position's own commitments — that's the bit that matters to me, because a case where (ii) and (iii) side with the copy by stipulation rather than by their own lights is worthless. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if I pull it out: does it collapse back into the ordinary "the forgery is worse because its derivative and parasitic" result, or does something survive? And finish with the strongest thing a defender of the original can say — I'm assuming there's an obvious one about who came up with the thing in the first place — and give me your honest view on whether it rescues (ii) or just quietly changes the subject.

British English, please, and no bibliography or reading list, I'm not after references.
