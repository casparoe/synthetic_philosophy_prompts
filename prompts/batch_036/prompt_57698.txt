I teach a graduate seminar on the Essays and I'm in the middle of a running argument with two of my TAs, who are convinced Montaigne is just a cultural relativist ahead of his time — they read the cannibals essay, hear him say that every one of us calls barbarous whatever isn't his own custom, and stop right there. I don't think that's the right label, and I don't think it's even the interesting question. So for next week I want to treat him as a judge with a track record instead of as a slogan, and spring on the seminar a case he never had to rule on, and see which account of his method actually predicts his verdict. I've written the rulings out as carefully as I can below — everything you need is in here, so you don't have to go hunting, and I'm not after quotations or chapter references, just the reasoning.

Here's the record. On the peoples of the Brazilian coast: he reports that they take prisoners in war, keep them well for months, then kill, roast and eat them, and that they think it far worse to lacerate and torture a body that still feels than to eat one that is dead — and his verdict is that by the standard of reason we may call them barbarous, but that we outdo them in every kind of barbarity, and he lists the rack, the burnings, and the religious wars to prove it. On evidence: he says he would rather trust one plain honest man who reports only what he saw with his own eyes than the cosmographers who fill the gaps with invention, and his whole account leans on a servant of his who lived ten or twelve years in those parts. On their poetry: he turns a war song and a love song into French and rates them against the Greek lyric poets with no discount for who sang them. On their marriages, their long communal houses, their lack of trade, metal, agriculture and magistrates: he describes all of it without scandal and not without some envy. On the three visitors he met at Rouen as boys, through an interpreter: he records their two pieces of bewilderment — that grown armed men would take orders from a beardless child king, and that there were men starving at the gates while others went armed and well fed — and he's cross with himself for never having had the rest of their comments written down. On the conquest: his verdict is that what undid those peoples was not superior courage but bad faith, that they were bought and undone by men who came under the flag of trade and then betrayed the hospitality they'd been given, and that our vices and our diseases finished the job. Then two things elsewhere in the book that pull the other way. In the essay on custom he insists custom gets its grip on us by degrees — the woman in his story who carried a calf every day until she could carry the full-grown bull — and he counsels against lightly overturning a law once it is established, whatever its defects. And in the essay on cruelty he says his horror of bloodshed is not a reasoned virtue at all but a matter of temperament, that he cannot watch a chicken killed or hear a hare crying in the teeth of the hounds, and he openly doubts whether a man is any the better for a distaste he never chose.

The new case is this. A design student — it's modelled on a real bioart piece I saw a few years ago — has grown a steak in a lab from cells donated by a living volunteer, who is healthy, who consents, and who intends to eat it herself at a ticketed gallery dinner with the whole process documented and disclosed. Nothing is killed, nobody is hurt, nobody is deceived, and biologically the meat is her own. Half my seminar already insists this is the one form of cannibalism that can't be objected to, since every objection has to bottom out either in a victim or in a lie. So: predict Montaigne's verdict, and lay out the candidate rules his record above is jointly consistent with — I can see three or four and I suspect you'll turn up ones I've missed. For each rule, say what it delivers on this case and how confident you'd be in that delivery. Then commit to a single best guess and defend it, which means dealing with the fact that the cruelty-first rule and the custom rule point in opposite directions here while the appeal-to-nature strand seems to cut both ways depending on whether you take his temperament remarks as evidence or as confession. Finally, name the follow-up case I should bring in the week after: a second, minimally different case that would seperate the top two rules, so that whichever way he goes on it one of them is refuted. And be honest with me at the end — if he does condemn the steak, is that a genuine problem for the TAs' relativist reading, or just proof that they grabbed the wrong name for something he's actually doing?
