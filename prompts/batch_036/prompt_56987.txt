I'm writing a seminar paper for a graduate course on models of analogy and categorization, and I've spent the last three weeks working through the Copycat architecture: the slipnet of permanent concepts whose link lengths contract as the linked nodes gain activation, so that neighbours become reachable; the coderack and its population of codelets, both the bottom-up ones that continually notice small structures and the top-down ones that can only be posted by an activated concept; the workspace in which a single viewpoint holds sway until scouts try to topple it; and temperature, which functions as an inverse measure of how confident the system is in its current conceptual organization and which licenses more or less randomness in the choices codelets make. The microdomain is letter-string analogies of the form "abc is to abd as ijk is to what?", and the interesting cases are the ones where the direct application of the rule fails or competes with something else: in the harder variant where the target ends in a doubled letter, a good answer treats that doubled letter as a group and replaces the whole group by its doubled successor, which requires the description attached to the final object to slip from "rightmost letter" to "rightmost group." The claim being made on the basis of behaviour like this is not merely that the program solves puzzles, but that it exhibits conceptual fluidity — that it shows concepts bending under contextual pressure the way human concepts do.

Here is the worry I want the paper to adjudicate, and I should say that my reading group is split down the middle about it. One of us, who came from machine learning, insists that the whole thing is a hand-authored prior wearing a dynamics costume: a person who already knew which slippages look clever wrote down which concepts sit close to which in the slipnet, assigned the link lengths, and chose which codelets exist, so that when the system "discovers" that the rightmost letter may slip to the rightmost group, it is redelivering an intuition its designers planted. The other of us replies that this is unfair to the point of being trivially true, since no model of anything escapes designer-supplied structure — a trained network inherits its architecture and objective, an expert system inherits its rules, and a chess program inherits its evaluation function — and that Copycat's defenders explicitly maintain that the architecture's features were chosen for generality rather than tuned to produce particular answers, the aim being the fluidity of concepts rather than analogy-solving as such. To keep the argument from floating free, I want the paper to bear down on three controls I have invented and to say for each whether it would actually settle anything. First, a shuffled-slipnet control: keep the codelets, the workspace, the temperature dynamics and the parallel terraced scan exactly as they are, but re-randomize which concepts are adjacent and rescale link lengths accordingly, then ask whether the intuitively good answers survive. Second, a cold control: clamp temperature at its floor so that no thermally licensed slippage can occur, and see what is left of the answer distribution. Third, a barren-domain control: port the architecture unmodified into a microdomain chosen precisely so that no slip is attractive or intelligible — strings drawn from a heterogeneous inventory with no successor relation and no grouping pressure, where the right answer has to be stipulated rather than discovered. I also want the paper to consider whether these controls are well-posed at all, since one might argue that any slipnet rich enough to yield interesting answers must encode interesting structure, in which case the test is rigged against the project whatever its outcome.

What I want back is an essay on this controversy, not a survey of it. It should distinguish carefully between two versions of the objection that are usually run together: an epistemic version, on which we simply cannot tell whose intuitions are being modeled, and a constitutive version, on which the explanatory work is demonstrably being done by the hand-authored distance structure rather than by the dynamics that are supposed to be the interesting part. It should state the strongest available defenses — the design-for-generality claim, the regress that threatens every cognitive model, the suggestion that the real empirical content lies in the distribution of answers and in the shape of the temperature curve rather than in any single clever output, and the claim that hand-built structure is exactly what a theory of concepts is supposed to make explicit — and it should say which of these defenses, if any, actually answers the constitutive version rather than merely deflecting the epistemic one. Then I want a verdict: whether the hand-authored slipnet undermines the claim that Copycat models conceptual fluidity, whether it undermines only a stronger claim about the architecture being domain-general, or whether the objection fails altogether and, if so, where it fails. The essay should also say what would count as evidence on either side, given that I have access only to a public reimplementation running with default parameters and cannot plausibly run the controls myself. A couple of practical constraints: please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, because I cannot verify them and they will not appear in the paper; refer to the architecture by its components rather than by publication dates; and do not end on a both-sides shrug — if the honest answer is that the objection lands against one claim and not the other, say that plainly and defend it.
