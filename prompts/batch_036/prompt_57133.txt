I write about science and ideas for a weekly magazine and I've got a reported piece coming up on consent — specifically the gap between the paperwork and what people actually agree to. Before I sit down with the philosopher I'm profiling I want to get properly bloodied by their view, so: play that philosopher. Not a real named person, just the committed proponent of the position I'll spell out below, and hold it across the whole conversation until I type stop.

The view. Consent isn't valid or invalid because of what's going on inside the consenter's head — not their beliefs, not their understanding, not their desires or motives. It's about the shape of the choice situation. An act of consent is valid when the cost of saying no, measured against the alternatives that person actually has, is low enough that saying yes isn't the only live option on the table. On this picture deception, coercion and incompetence aren't three separate invalidators, they're one thing wearing three hats: each of them raises the price of refusal. Misinforming somebody hides the exits; threatening them makes the exit expensive; being non-competent means you can't locate the exit even when it's clearly marked. And the practical upshot — the part they actually care about — is that you fix bad consent by lowering exit costs, not by stacking more disclosure forms on people.

They're sharp and they are not a strawman. They'll happily concede that background injustice is grotesque, that plenty of high-friction agreements shouldn't be legally enforceable, and that disclosure has real instrumental value. What they will never concede is that validity turns on inner states rather than on the cost of refusal, and they will not let deception be its own separate category. If I hand them a case they genuinely can't handle, they say so and show exactly where it breaks — but they don't strawman my cases and they don't send me to the literature. No citations, no reading list, no name-dropping: my editor cuts all that and I couldn't check it anyways. Keep replies to two paragraphs, conversational, quotable. If you need a fact about my case, ask me for it the way an interviewee pins down a reporter's premise.

My opening, and I want you to answer it rather than restate it: I spent a week at a Phase I trial site that pays $4,500 for fourteen inpatient days. Nobody is threatened, nobody is lied to, the disclosure is unusually good, and every participant is a competent adult who understands precisely what they're signing up for. Most of them are between jobs and the $4,500 is thier rent. Your sponsor didn't create any of the friction — the price of saying no was set by their landlords and the labor market — so on your account the consent comes out valid, and if you don't like that verdict your only available move is to pay people less so refusing gets cheaper. Tell me that's the right call, or tell me where I've got your baseline wrong.
