I have no academic training in economics or philosophy. I have spent the last few years reading about climate policy on my own — carbon taxes, cap-and-trade, offsets, and the degrowth position — after a bitter argument broke out in my town over a proposal to price downtown parking and spend the revenue on transit. Around the same time I was reading Dr. Seuss's The Lorax aloud to my daughter, and it struck me that both camps in that argument would happily claim the book without anyone examining it closely. I would like a serious reading of it, written in American English.

Here is the tension I cannot resolve on my own. On one side, the book looks like a story about a missing price. The Once-ler pays nothing for the smoke, the fouled ponds, or the habitat he liquidates. The Lorax is, in effect, a regulator who has a voice but no instrument: he can warn, shame, and plead, but he cannot tax, cap, or license. And the creatures actually harmed — the ones who lose their food and their water and are sent away to find somewhere else — have no standing and no money, so they cannot bargain either. On that reading the tragedy is simply that the damage never appeared in the Once-ler's ledger, and a charge on each Thneed equal to that damage would have changed the arithmetic. On the other side, the book looks like a story about the growth imperative itself, which is what the degrowth people say the price-setters miss. The Once-ler is not greedier than any other small manufacturer; he keeps making the operation bigger because that is what the operation requires, and his relatives arrive precisely to accelerate it. If the machinery has to keep expanding, a tax that leaves profit positive at a somewhat reduced scale still ends at the last tree, only later. And demand for the Thneed is manufactured by advertising for a garment nobody needed, so the price signal is not tracking any real want to begin with.

What I would like from you:

- Tell me which of those readings the book actually supports. Take both seriously. I do not want the standard summary of its environmental message, and I do not want the book reduced to an illustration of either doctrine; where the reading strains against the story, say so, including what the Lorax's relation to the trees seems to involve that no price could express.
- Take up the ending. The Lorax departs leaving a pile of stones bearing the single word unless, and the Once-ler, after decades alone, hands the visiting boy the last Truffula seed. Is that seed functioning as an offset — a small future credit against a completed liquidation? Is the book's moral, that everything turns on someone caring, a substitute for institutions or a precondition of them?
- If you know the 2012 film well: Thneedville is a walled town of artificial vegetation whose mayor owns the company selling bottled air, and the climax is a crowd turning on him and the seed being planted in public. Does the adaptation convert a story about an irrecoverable loss into a story about a winnable political contest? Is the air-selling mayor the better villain for the degrowth reading, since he is defending a commodified substitute for the thing that was destroyed?
- Say what the book shows that a policy paper cannot, and what an economist would say it gets badly wrong — for instance, whether the Once-ler's ruin once the forest is gone assumes there are no substitutes and no replanting, and whether what is really missing in that valley is a tax or an ownership right.

Constraints: I am not asking for quotation and you should not attempt any — paraphrase, and state which version you are working from, the book or the film. If you are unsure of a scene, flag that rather than filling it in. No citations, no bibliography, no suggested reading; I want your own reasoning, about 1,200 to 1,500 words, ending with a clear statement of which side you come down on and why.
