I write literary fiction, and I am trying to break a structural problem in a novel set in a county clerk's office. Before I attempt the book's middle section, I want to see the conceit handled at short-story length.

Write me a story about a woman who has spent nineteen years solemnizing marriages, administering oaths, and witnessing affidavits in a small jurisdiction, and who learns in the opening pages that the authority underwriting all of it — her commission, or the office itself, quietly abolished in a consolidation she never read — lapsed years ago. Everything she has done since is, in Austin's terms, a misfire: not a hollow act but a void one, words that never became deeds. Set against this a single act she performed while fully authorized and wholly insincere — an oath she administered to a man she despised, or a vow she accepted knowing it would not be kept — which is therefore an abuse: hollow, but done, and binding.

I want the story to turn on that asymmetry rather than merely illustrate it. The void acts cannot be undone because they never happened, and no one can be told about them; the hollow one can only be undone, and undoing it will injure someone who has built a life on it. Let the distinction decide what your protagonist is able to repair.

No jargon, no essayistic asides, no lectures to the reader: nobody in the story may name the concepts. Keep it near three thousand words and end on a decision rather than a revelation. If you bring in the law's own remedy for such cases — the rule that treats the acts of an officer who served under color of title as good against the world — let it arrive as a sentence recited at her, offering no comfort.
