I am drafting the central section of a paper in which I want to argue that "solving the frame problem" admits of degrees, and that a good deal of the old dispute between logic-based AI and its philosophical critics turns on treating it as all-or-nothing. Before I commit to that thesis, I want to know whether my own verdicts hang together. What follows is the raw material: a series of cases, each with the verdict I actually have. Your task is to work out what principle, if any, generates them.

The cases. Let S be any system that must determine what holds after an action, where the target claim concerns something the action intuitively leaves alone.

1. A program in the original situation-calculus setting, which must derive from axioms written out one per action–property pair that pushing a wagon out of a room leaves the wagon's colour, the number of walls in the room, and a colleague's telephone number as they were. Verdict: defective, and not merely slow. The number of axioms it needs scales with the size of what it knows about the world rather than with what it is doing, so the system is worse off precisely in proportion to its knowledge.

2. The same program, except that each persistence assumption is defeasible and may be retracted when evidence of a change arrives. Verdict: a solution, and a full one rather than a stopgap — even though such a system can in principle conclude falsely that something stayed the same.

3. A system that does not derive persistence at all, but instead selects from among the outcomes consistent with its laws one that changes as little as possible, where what counts as little change is fixed by an ordering or metric I supply from outside. Verdict: a solution, and a better one than 2. But the improvement comes from relocating the work into my choice of ordering rather than from the system's own resources, and I want simultaneously to say that the system has solved the problem and that it has inherited a relevance problem from me. I cannot see how to make those two attitudes consistent.

4. A system handed a small set of causal laws from which, for each property, it can derive a complete statement of the conditions under which that property changes. Verdict: a solution, but only relative to the assumption that the laws it was handed are complete. If they are not, I do not say the system failed to solve the frame problem; I say it was misinformed about the world. I want to preserve that distinction between an incomplete theory and a defective inference.

5. A strong human chess player who moves a knight without representing, even dispositionally, that the colour of the squares, the rules for castling, and the weather outside the tournament hall are unaffected. Verdict: not a solution, because there is no stage in the player's processing at which the irrelevant facts are candidates for revision. That is a better state of affairs than solving the problem, but it makes "solved" the wrong word — nothing needed solving.

6. A household robot that uses a learned filter to decide which properties are candidates for change after each action. The filter is right about ninety-nine per cent of the time; when it is wrong, the robot notices through perception and corrects itself. Verdict: a solution, full stop, and not a second-class one.

7. A theorem prover reasoning about a physical theory, using the same learned filter with the same error rate. Verdict: not a solution. It is required to be right about what does not change, not merely usually right.

8. A system that, for every action type, enumerates as far as it can the circumstances in which the action fails to produce its usual effect — the damp match, the locked door, the unnoticed exception. Verdict: this addresses a different problem from the one in cases 1–4, and a system could fail at this one while acing those or vice versa. I want the two kept strictly apart. I also think a system that solves the case-1 problem and not this one has done the more important half, and I cannot explain why the more important half should be the one that admits of the cleaner solution.

9. A system that gets every direct effect right and every persistence claim right, but cannot handle effects that arise because other effects arise: it knows the wagon will be outside and the lamp unchanged, yet cannot work out that the moving wagon knocks the lamp over. Verdict: it has solved the problem of cases 1–4 and not that of case 8, and I regard it, on balance, as more useful than a system with the opposite profile.

10. The claim that no system whose representations are sentences can ever solve the problem, because which facts matter is settled by an agent's practical engagement with its surroundings and not by anything it could be told. Verdict: as an argument, unsound, since 2 and 4 look like counterexamples. As a forecast about how the problem would in fact end up being handled by working systems, I take it seriously, and precisely because of 5 and 6.

My working hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than adopted, is that I am tracking two things: (a) whether the system's burden of establishing that things stay the same grows with how much it knows about the world or only with what it is doing; and (b) where that burden is acceptable, whether mistakes about persistence are ones the system can detect and repair by its own means. Clause (a) is what condemns 1 and admits 2, 3 and 4; clause (b) is what I reach for in 6 and 7. I already suspect (b) is illegitimate, because in 6 and 7 the systems occupy exactly the same epistemic position and differ only in what a mistake costs — and cost is a fact about my purposes, not about the system's treatment of non-change.

What I would like back:

- At most three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically to a new case, with a clause-by-clause account of which of my cases motivates each clause.
- For each candidate, a verdict on all ten cases, flagging every divergence from mine and saying whether the divergence should be repaired by amending the principle or by my abandoning the verdict.
- Three further cases of your own devising on which the candidates rank differently, so that I can see whether my principle is determinate at all. At least one should involve a system of a kind I have not contemplated — for instance one that revises a randomly selected subset of properties, or one whose knowledge of non-change is entirely explicit but extremely small.
- A frank statement of which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated by any reasonable principle and should simply go, and of whether 6 and 7 ought to be merged into a single case with a contextual parameter.
- Whether my descriptions of 4, 8 and 9 correspond to problems the literature treats as distinct from the frame problem proper; I have deliberately described them without using their standard names, and if my glosses are wrong the whole exercise needs redoing.
- Finally, whether "solve" is doing genuinely graded work in this debate, or whether what I call partial solutions are merely well- or badly-engineered solutions, in which case my thesis should be weakened to a claim about engineering trade-offs.

Please do not give me a survey of the known solutions or a history of the controversy — I have that already — and do not supply references or a reading list. I want the reasoning, argued from the content of the cases.
