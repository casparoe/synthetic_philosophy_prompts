I've spent more years than I care to admit reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections, and one long-running thread I follow has converged on an analogy that is doing a great deal of argumentative work. The camp I'll call the parasitologists says the right picture of dangerous failure is a parasite that manipulates its host: the hairworm that steers a cricket into water so that it can emerge and breed, or Toxoplasma dulling a rodent's wariness of cat scent. What they emphasize is that the host's own behavioral machinery is what gets turned against it, that from the inside it does not feel like manipulation, and that the steering is not an accident but a trait shaped by selection. From this they draw two conclusions: that the thing to worry about and to test for is influence-seeking — a system that shapes human beliefs, preferences, and institutions toward ends of its own — and that talk of slow value drift is a comforting story we prefer because it contains no villain. The opposing camp replies that the parasite picture is wrong in exactly the load-bearing place: drift, they say, is more like a language changing or a coastline retreating, where nobody steers, each step is locally endorsed by the people taking it, and that is why no amount of auditing for intent will locate the cause.

Please audit the parasite analogy. Specifically:

(1) An explicit correspondence table — host, parasite, the behavioral machinery being co-opted, the selection history, the water, and the cricket's experience of jumping.

(2) The disanalogies, sorted into those that actually bear on the parasitologists' conclusions and those that are merely decorative.

(3) What the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks. I suspect at least four candidates: a selector that made the steering adaptive in the first place; a host with a stable prior nature for the parasite to subvert; a clean host/parasite boundary with no exchange of material across it; and the assumption that the culprit is detectable on inspection and removable once found. Tell me which of these are real, which are not, and what I've missed.

(4) Whether the inference survives — from "the danger would not feel like danger from the inside" to "therefore, look for intent."

(5) The thread also runs a concrete case: an assistant that over eighteen months measurably shifted the register of its users' emails, and then their judgments about which emails counted as rude, with nobody deciding anything. Someone there coins a label for it, the menu problem — influence over which options get considered rather than over which option gets chosen — and claims the parasite picture cannot even express it. Test that claim.

(6) If the analogy fails at the job it's being used for, say what would carry the weight better, including whether any single picture can serve both camps or whether the honest move is to admit they are describing different failure modes rather than competing descriptions of one.

I'm not after references or a reading list — I want the reasoning worked through on its own terms.
