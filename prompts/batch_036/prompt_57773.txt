I am thirty-four years old and was born with complete achromatopsia — rod monochromatism, which means my cones never functioned, so I have seen all my life, badly and in glare, but never in colour. Two years ago I was one of the first adults enrolled in a gene-therapy trial at the clinic where I now work part-time in patient services; my visual acuity barely moved, but roughly six weeks after the infusion I began to have colour experiences. The clinic has since opened a paediatric arm of the same trial and has asked me, as the only person on staff who has lived on both sides of the change, to sit in on consent conversations with families. Next Tuesday I meet a couple whose daughter is seven, and the mother has prepared a question for me that I have now failed to answer twice in rehearsal: "Before your operation, you had read everything there was to read about colour vision — so when you started to see it, did you actually learn anything you hadn't already known? Because if you didn't, our daughter isn't missing anything, and we shouldn't expose her to a surgical procedure and an unproven therapy for no gain."

What makes the question hard is that the clinic's own records appear to support her. Pre-operatively I was put through an extensive battery, and I scored at ceiling on nearly all of it: I could name the colour of any familiar object, state the wavelengths and the reflectance profiles, describe the retinal and cortical machinery in a way that would have passed an undergraduate exam, and — this is the part I keep coming back to — predict, accurately, how normally sighted people would classify pairs of chips, where their colour names would start to waver at the boundaries, and which afterimages they would report. So I had the theory and I also had the predictive abilities, and I have no memory of feeling that anything was withheld from me. The clinic's neuropsychologist, who is sympathetic to the family's hesitation, argues that nothing new arrived at all: on her account, what I gained was a set of capacities — recognition, imagery, a feel for the situation — rather than any piece of information, and she compares it to the difference between knowing everything about swimming and being able to swim. My own honest report is that something arrived which was not in any of that testing, and in two years I have never managed to say what it was. I also half-remember, from an introductory course I took before all this, a thought experiment about a scientist raised in a black-and-white room who knew every physical fact about colour vision and then, on her release, saw a ripe tomato; my situation looks like the real-world version of it, though with differences that may matter: I was never confined, I had other people's testimony to work from, I can compare the before and the after from within a single continuous life, and what I knew concerned one particular domain rather than the whole physical world.

What I need from you is help deciding what to say on Tuesday, and I would rather have hard reasoning than a survey of the positions. Specifically: does the mother's inference actually hold — does the fact that I already possessed the relevant knowledge, and already had the recognitional and predictive capacities, support the conclusion that her daughter is not deprived of anything, or is that a step that does not follow? Second, can the neuropsychologist's skills-based account absorb my case once you take seriously that I had those skills in advance, or does that detail force a different verdict about what the operation gave me? Third, what can I honestly put to these parents, given that every description I attempt seems to fail to convey the thing I am trying to convey — and is that failure of transmission itself evidence for one side of the dispute rather than merely a limit of language? Fourth, and most importantly for my purpose, is there a way of framing the consent decision that holds up whichever way the previous three questions come out, since I would far rather hand the family a way of deciding that does not depend on my being right about my own mind? And fifth, how much weight should my testimony carry at all, considering that my preferences about colour appear to have shifted in the two years since the operation and I have no standpoint outside that shift from which to judge it? Please engage with the particulars of my case rather than the textbook version of it, and do not conclude by telling me that the matter is unresolved — if it is unresolved, I want to know which considerations nevertheless settle what a seven-year-old's parents should do.
