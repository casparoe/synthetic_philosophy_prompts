I am trying to understand what the criminal jury, as actually constituted in England and Wales, is committed to. The standard defence of the institution is arithmetic in spirit: if each member is more likely than not to reach the correct verdict and their errors are not systematically aligned, then pooling their judgments by majority makes the group far more likely to be right than any individual member, and ever more likely as the panel grows. Twelve heads, on this picture, are not twelve opinions but a single, sharpened instrument for finding things out. Yet the surrounding rules seem designed in a quite different spirit, and I cannot see how they all fit.

Here is the practice as I understand it, in outline:

1. Jurors are selected at random from the electoral roll and are supposed to be lay people; anyone with relevant knowledge of the case or the parties is excluded, and prior expertise is treated as a disqualifying taint rather than an asset. During selection the parties may challenge jurors, and the challenges that matter are aimed at predicted sympathy rather than at competence.

2. Jurors are directed to decide the case solely on the evidence adduced in court. It is a criminal offence for a juror to research the case during the trial: to search the internet, to visit the scene, to conduct an experiment, to look up the law or the rules of evidence, or to ask anyone outside the panel for information.

3. It is also a criminal offence for anyone intentionally to disclose, solicit or obtain particulars of statements made, opinions expressed, arguments advanced or votes cast by jurors in the course of their deliberations. The process by which verdicts are actually produced is therefore, as a matter of law, closed to empirical study.

4. The jury deliberates in secret, is directed to reach unanimity, and is permitted a large majority only after a further period of deliberation. If it cannot reach either, it is discharged and the defendant is retried before a fresh panel; the first panel's failure is given no weight at all, and the second panel is not told what the first one thought.

5. The verdict is a bare finding of guilt on which no reasons are ever given. Jurors need not agree on which of several alternative routes to liability has been made out, provided each is sure that guilt has been.

6. The same legal system runs its appellate courts in panels of three or five, which deliberate at length, publish their reasoning, and record dissenting opinions as a matter of course.

My questions are these.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory. Which of these features presuppose the aggregative, truth-tracking picture — that the verdict's authority derives from the group's reliability as a cognitive device — and which presuppose something else: that the panel stands for the community, that the verdict's authority derives from the fairness of the process rather than the accuracy of the output, or that a collective finding has a standing a mere aggregation of private beliefs could never have? Go through the six features in turn, and for each say what it strictly requires, what it merely sits comfortably with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible theories would license it equally well.

Second, address the tension between the aggregative justification and features 1 to 4. Aggregation of that kind needs independence and better-than-chance competence, but the institution deliberately manufactures dependence (secrecy, an instruction to converge, deliberation until unanimity), deliberately filters the panel on expected verdict rather than on skill, and forbids anyone from checking whether any of this improves accuracy. Is that fatal to the aggregative story, or can it be reformulated — for instance, by claiming that pooling reasons raises each member's competence enough to outweigh the loss of independence? If so, what would that reformulation have to assume about what happens in the deliberation room, and is there any reason to believe it rather than its opposite?

Third, is feature 3 coherent? What does it presuppose about the relationship between a procedure's legitimacy and its demonstrated reliability — that the authority of a verdict cannot depend on facts about how it was reached, or only that those facts must be kept away from the parties? Say whether the prohibition is a mere side-constraint on an otherwise accuracy-driven practice or whether it belongs to a different theory of authority altogether.

Fourth, what does feature 4 presuppose about the epistemic weight of the discharged panel's views? If the jury is a measuring instrument, a second run of the same instrument on the same evidence yielding the opposite reading ought to tell us something about the instrument; treating the first run as void suggests otherwise. And what does the move from unanimity to a permitted majority presuppose about the relative costs of the two kinds of error, and is that consistent with a competence-based defence?

Fifth, consider feature 5. Voting separately on each element of an offence and combining the results can produce a different outcome from voting directly on the offence itself, even where every juror holds exactly the same views throughout. The practice of a bare general verdict, on which jurors need not agree about the route to guilt, seems to presuppose a particular view of what the jury's output is. Say what that view is, what it rules out, and whether it is the view the aggregative justification needs.

Finally, contrast features 5 and 6: two collegial bodies in the same system, one of which treats the recording of disagreement and the giving of reasons as essential and one of which treats both as forbidden. Can a single theory of adjudication underwrite both, or does the contrast show that the jury is not an epistemic device at all? Close by saying concretely what would have to change in courtroom practice if the aggregative justification were adopted officially and taken seriously, and what would have to change if it were abandoned in favour of a purely representative or process-based one.

Please argue this through analytically rather than by reference to the literature, and write your answer in British English.
