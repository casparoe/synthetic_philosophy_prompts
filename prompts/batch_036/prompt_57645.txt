I spent thirty-four years directing a trace-analysis laboratory — mostly heavy metals in environmental samples, mostly the unglamorous work of deciding when an instrument's output may be reported as a detection. Since retiring I have turned to philosophy with more time than training, and I have written a draft essay of about 2,200 words on Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and the closure principle. Below is a compressed version of it, retaining the argument's skeleton. I should be grateful for critical comments of the specific kinds listed afterward rather than general encouragement.

**Operating characteristics: a trace analyst's reading of the tracking account**

§1. In my former trade, a binary discrimination is characterised by two numbers. The hit rate is the probability that the method reports the analyte present when it is present. The false-alarm rate is the probability that it reports presence when the analyte is absent. Neither can be driven to zero without driving the other to one; what is fixed by the physics and the protocol is the discriminability of the method, and what is fixed by the analyst is where on that trade-off curve to sit — the criterion. The criterion is not discovered, it is chosen, and it is chosen by reference to the relative costs of the two error types together with the prior odds. That is the whole content of method validation, and it is why a laboratory report states a detection limit rather than a bare result.

§2. Nozick's account of knowledge, as I understand it, requires that the belief be true, that it be held by way of some method M, that if the proposition were false the subject would not, using M, believe it, and that if it were true the subject would, using M, believe it. Translated into my idiom, the third condition demands a false-alarm rate of zero and the fourth demands a hit rate of one, for the particular method M by which the belief is formed. Two observations follow, and they are the engine of the essay.

First, the two conditions together require a method of unbounded discriminability: no real detector sits at that corner of the curve, and no method of belief-formation known to me does either. Second, because the conditions are indexed to M, discriminability is a property of an instrument-and-protocol pair, and the question of how finely M is to be individuated is exactly the question of whether one is calibrating a spectrometer or a whole laboratory workflow. The familiar complaint that the method clause is underdetermined looks to me like the familiar complaint that an operating characteristic is meaningless until the protocol is specified.

§3. The main claim concerns closure. Suppose p entails q, and suppose the subject's belief that q rests entirely on the belief that p, so that every world in which she believes p is a world in which she believes q. Then, on the probabilistic reading of §2, the false-alarm rate for q cannot be lower than the false-alarm rate for p, and hence sensitivity to q cannot exceed sensitivity to p. In outline: the worlds in which q is false are a subset of the worlds in which p is false; within that subset the belief in q occurs at least as often as the belief in p, since the latter entails the former; and averaging over the remainder of the not-p region can only lower the false-alarm rate for p relative to that subset. So weakening a claim by deduction never improves the operating point, and may degrade it arbitrarily.

If that is right, then closure failure is not a curiosity about painted mules and plaster facades. It is the predictable consequence of pinning the criterion at zero false alarms and then moving to a logically weaker claim: the counterexamples are instances of a general monotonicity property, and any theory built on a fixed, extreme criterion will reproduce them.

§4. The two stock cases read cleanly in this framework. In the zebra case, the discriminating channel (appearance of stripes) resolves zebra from horse but not zebra from cleverly painted mule, so the false-alarm rate with respect to the mule proposition is near one; the tracking theorist is simply reporting a real limit of resolution, and denying closure is a way of refusing to extrapolate a calibration beyond its validated range. In the red-barn variant of the fake-barn case — which I believe originates with Kripke, though I have not managed to locate where he set it down — the stronger claim about a red barn is well tracked precisely because the facades are not painted red, while the weaker claim about a barn is not. Here the extra content in p is carried by a channel of high resolution, which is exactly the configuration in which the inequality of §3 bites hardest. Nozick's grandmother case is the converse configuration: the family's determination to shield her destroys resolution throughout the region where the proposition is false, so no criterion whatsoever can save the belief.

§5. My constructive suggestion is that the criterion should be allowed to move. If the threshold is set, as it is in practice, by the costs of the two error types, then the threshold appropriate to p and the threshold appropriate to its weaker consequence q need not coincide: the costs change when the question changes, and an analyst who reports a trace detection at one limit and a compliance verdict at another is not being inconsistent but is being competent. On such a view the agent who knows p may also know q, because the operative threshold for q is lower, and closure is restored at the price of making knowledge sensitive to what turns on being wrong.

§6. I record two worries of my own. (i) Subjunctive conditionals are not conditional probabilities. In the fake-barn case the counterfactual reading gives a false-alarm rate of one, because the nearest not-barn worlds are facade worlds in which the belief persists; but an average over the whole region in which the object is not a barn — most of which contains no barn-like objects at all — gives a small number. The two readings come apart, and they come apart in the very cases that motivate the theory. If the counterfactual reading governs, my §2 translation is merely an analogy, and §3 has no content. (ii) If instead the probabilistic reading governs, then the tracking account seems to collapse into a species of reliabilism stated with modal vocabulary, and I cannot see what the modal vocabulary is then doing.

**What I should like from you**

1. An accuracy check on §2, §4 and §5's descriptions of the standard material: the four conditions and the role of the method clause, the zebra case, the attribution and structure of the red-barn case, the grandmother case, and my remark in §5 that the alternative built on safety rather than sensitivity escapes the monotonicity problem. Flag anything garbled, overstated, or where I have compressed to the point of error. I have deliberately left dates and titles out; please do not supply them, and do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading, since I have no way of checking what you tell me to read. Your own judgement in your own words is what I want.

2. A rigorous assessment of the §3 derivation. Is the inequality unconditional? I suspect it is not, and that it can be reversed by a suitable choice of method and of worlds — for instance where the method is blind in the region where p is false but q is true, so that the false-alarm rate for the stronger claim is inflated by a stratum in which the weaker claim is never wrongly asserted. Either confirm my suspicion with a worked case of your own construction, or show me where it is mistaken. Separately: given that Nozick's conditions are indexed to a method, is the assumption that every world in which the subject believes p is a world in which she believes q even well formed? If the method for q is not the method for p, the derivation may be comparing operating points on two different curves, in which case §3 says nothing at all.

3. An assessment of §5. Does the floating criterion genuinely restore closure, or does it merely relocate the difficulty to the question of how thresholds are set? Is what I have described in effect a pragmatic account of knowledge attribution wearing laboratory clothes, and if so, does that damage the tracking theory's claim to handle Gettier-type cases without appealing to probabilities?

4. Worry (i) in §6 is, I think, the one that decides whether the essay is worth anything. Please tell me which horn I should take: whether the counterfactual and probabilistic readings can be reconciled by weighting worlds by similarity so that the average is dominated by the nearest region, or whether the divergence I describe is fatal to the translation. Argue it through rather than telling me who has argued it.

5. A structural judgement. The full draft has more of the laboratory material in it than this compression does. Which section is least load-bearing and should be cut? And where does the instrumentation vocabulary do real work, as opposed to merely looking authoritative — I am alert to the possibility that a retired chemist's idiom reads as impressive to philosophers and as naive to chemists.

6. Finally, state in one paragraph the single objection a professional epistemologist would press first, and tell me plainly whether the essay survives it.
