ok so i just spent about four hours arguing with a friend about whether there'd have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler and we got nowhere, again — this is the third time we've tried this and every time we end up in exactly the same place, both of us more annoyed than when we started. I don't want you to tell me who's right about Germany. I want you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, because I've started to suspect it isn't about history at all.

Where we sit. He's the structuralist: Germany in 1932 had Versailles, hyperinflation in living memory, mass unemployment, a weak parliamentary culture, a big nationalist revanchist right and a communist party that he thinks was just as fatal to Weimar as the Nazis were. On his reading some authoritarian, antisemitic, expansionist regime was coming regardless of who fronted it, and Hitler was just the face it happened to get. When I push him on whether the genocide is inside that package he says maybe not the industrialised version, but something in that neighbourhood, because the antisemitism was ambient and there were plenty of men who'd have been happy to run it.

I'm on the other side: the party was barely two and a half percent of the vote in 1928 and nearly died in the mid-twenties, Hitler's specific cocktail of racial pseudoscience plus a personal fixation on the east wasn't a generic output of those conditions, the conservative right — Papen, Hindenburg, the DNVP crowd — was perfectly capable of installing an authoritarian government and would more plausibly have crushed the radicals than handed them the state, and there's a canyon between "Germany turns authoritarian" and "Germany builds extermination camps." I also think he's laundering hindsight into a modal claim.

Here's where it goes sideways every single time. I ask what would change his mind and he points at cliometrics — the Fogel railroads thing, where they actually ran the numbers on a 1890 economy without railroads and concluded railroads weren't indispensable — and says counterfactual history gets done rigorously all the time and produces estimates, so my objection is really just that I don't like his prior. I ask him the same question back and I say a one-shot political counterfactual isn't a railroad: there's no comparison class to compute over, no price data for "authoritarian takeover," and no principled way to hold the rest of the world fixed while you subtract one man and then let everything else carry on unchanged, which is basically the Lewis-style similarity worry about which facts you keep and which you let go. And he says that's just Carr's old dismissal of the might-have-beens dressed up as philosophy, and that Trevor-Roper already buried it.

My working hypothesis, tell me if it's wrong: we aren't arguing about the same proposition. "Would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler" is doing at least three jobs between us — a counterfactual-dependence claim about that specific regime, a probabilistic claim that some regime of that type was more likely than not given the conditions, and a claim about what any adequate explanation of what actually happened has to mention — and we each slide between them without noticing, usually right at the point where the argument gets uncomfortable. Relatedly I think there's a token/type slippage: he's answering a question about a kind of regime and I'm answering one about the particular thing that happened, and we're both saying "Nazi Germany."

So: diagnose it. Is my hypothesis right, or is this a substantive disagreement where one of us is just wrong about the German situation and about how much explanatory work conditions can do? Are we split over facts, over what counts as evidence for a singular historical claim, over how to weigh base rates from a handful of comparator cases against idiosyncratic detail, or over bedrock intuitions about how much room individuals have in history that neither of us could argue for from anything more basic? And is there a hidden formal question in there — like whether a counterfactual in a chaotic, indeterministic system even has a determinate truth value — that's doing all the work while we talk about 1933?

Then tell me what would count as progress, given whatever you land on. A distinction we should be using, a different question we should be asking instead, some result about counterfactuals that would settle the fixed-point problem, a shared comparison class we could actually reason over, or a change in what each of us owes the other evidentially. And if the honest answer is that this one can't be settled and we should stop pretending it can, say that and say why — I'd rather have that than four more hours. Reasons over survey, please, and no list of what people have written about great men.
