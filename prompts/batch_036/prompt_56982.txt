I teach a masters-level course called "Time, Risk, and Rational Choice," jointly listed between my philosophy department and the business school, which spends its second half on the family of arguments holding that a rational agent facing a repeated gamble should maximize the long-run growth rate of her capital rather than its expectation. Section B of my final paper requires one long answer, worth 25 marks, to be completed in about 45 minutes without a calculator. I want that question to be about the Kelly criterion and its rivals, but I cannot use anything resembling last year's version: the question then asked candidates to explain why expected-value maximization recommends betting one's whole bankroll on a favourable repeated coin toss and why that recommendation is nevertheless disastrous, and I received thirty-two scripts that recited, accurately and interchangeably, that expectation ignores compounding, that the growth-rate-maximising stake is the Kelly fraction, and that one should therefore never risk ruin. All three claims were true; none of them engaged with what I had actually set. This year I want the question built so that reciting those slogans scores nothing, and so that the marks go to candidates who can say *which criterion* is being maximised, when two criteria that are usually conflated come apart, and what has to be true of a decision problem for the asymptotic growth criterion to be the right one at all.

To that end I have constructed the following apparatus. Candidates are told: a fund faces a gamble G which it may play as many times as it likes, choosing a stake fraction $f \in [0,1]$ once and holding it fixed for every round; each round, with probability 0.6 the fund's capital is multiplied by $(1+f)$ and with probability 0.4 by $(1-f)$. They are given the following figures and told they may use them without re-deriving them (I have checked all of them myself, so please treat them as authoritative and do not amend or recompute them): for each of $f = 0.1, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.5, 1.0$, respectively, the per-round growth rate $g(f) = 0.6\ln(1+f) + 0.4\ln(1-f)$ is $0.0150, 0.0201, 0.0188, 0.0147, 0.0077, -0.0024, -0.0340, -\infty$; the expected capital after ten rounds relative to the start, $E[W_{10}]/W_0$, is $1.219, 1.480, 1.629, 1.791, 1.967, 2.159, 2.594, 6.192$; the probability of at least doubling within ten rounds, $P(W_{10} \geq 2W_0)$, is $0.046, 0.167, 0.382, 0.382, 0.382, 0.382, 0.382, 0.006$; and the probability of finishing below the starting capital, $P(W_{10} < W_0)$, is $0.367, 0.367, 0.367, 0.367, 0.367, 0.618, 0.618, 0.994$. They are also told that $0.382$ is the maximum attainable value of $P(W_{10} \geq 2W_0)$ over all admissible $f$. The question then presents an excerpt from the annual letter of a fictional endowment — an invention of mine for examination purposes, which should be labelled as such in the question so that no candidate or marker imagines it is a real document — reading: "(i) The fund is a going concern with no terminal date. The question before us is therefore not what maximises our expected capital at some arbitrarily chosen future date, but what maximises the rate at which capital grows along the single path we shall actually travel; under G, that is a stake of 20 per cent. (ii) Let it be clear that this is not the logarithmic utility function in disguise. Our reason for the 20 per cent rule is a fact about how capital compounds through time, not a fact about our tastes. Any agent, however risk-loving, confronts the same arithmetic and is undone by it in the same way. (iii) It follows that staking more than 20 per cent is irrational whatever the fund's objectives. The board's proposal to stake 40 per cent, in order to raise the probability of doubling the endowment within ten years, therefore rests on a confusion: it buys a better chance at an arbitrary milestone at the price of a strictly worse growth rate, and a going concern has no business attending to milestones. (iv) Nor should the board take comfort from the observation that staking everything maximises expected capital. An expectation averages over histories of the fund, only one of which will ever be ours, and the fund does not get to average across its own alternatives. Expected capital is the wrong criterion because the process is non-ergodic; the growth rate is the right criterion because it is the one the fund actually faces." Candidates are asked to evaluate claims (i)–(iv): for each, to say what is right in it, what is wrong, and what would have to be true of the fund's situation for the claim to hold.

What I need from you is the complete examination package. First, the question exactly as it should appear on the paper — with the table reproduced, the instructions to candidates, the mark allocation and the time, and a short rubric telling them what the examiners are looking for, which should make clear that identifying the 20 per cent stake as the Kelly fraction earns no marks in itself. Second, a model answer of about 900 words. I would like that answer to turn on the two features of the data that I take to be the real crux and that I have not spelled out to the candidates: that the target-beating probability is maximised over a whole interval of stakes which straddles the point where the growth rate changes sign, so that a stake of 0.3 attains the same maximum 0.382 as the board's 0.4 while retaining a positive growth rate and thereby dominating the board's proposal on both of the memo's own criteria; and that the jumps in the final column are step-function artefacts of the fact that ten rounds admit only eleven possible wealth outcomes, with the flip between 0.367 and 0.618 falling exactly at the stake where $g(f)$ changes sign — a coincidence the answer should explain rather than merely note. The model answer should also adjudicate claim (ii) squarely: say what is correct in the insistence that no assumption about tastes is being made, and what is incorrect in the further claim that every agent however risk-loving faces the same arithmetic, given that maximising expected logarithmic capital and maximising the almost-sure long-run growth rate select the same stake in this setting and are not the criterion that a fund with non-logarithmic preferences over terminal wealth, or with annual payout obligations, would use. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 that assigns marks to named moves rather than to topics: say how many marks go to each of the four claims, what a candidate must actually say to earn them, which of the three slogans from last year's scripts are to be treated as non-scoring restatement, and where marks are to be deducted for specific errors — for instance, treating "wealth tends to zero in the limit" as equivalent to "likely to lose money over ten rounds," or announcing that staking 40 per cent is irrational because its growth rate is negative without noticing that the same is true at 0.5 while the target probability is identical, or invoking half-Kelly as a prudent compromise when the table shows it all but forecloses the ten-year objective. I would also like the scheme to draw the line between a first-class and a merely good answer by naming at least four moves that only a first-class answer makes, and to close with a paragraph addressed to my second marker explaining how to credit a competent script that reaches a defensible overall verdict by a route the model answer does not anticipate — including one that argues the memo's own framing, which invokes both an endless horizon and a ten-year milestone, is internally incoherent and that this incoherence rather than any arithmetic is its central defect. Please keep the whole package self-contained: no bibliography, no references to the literature, and nothing that a candidate without access to sources beyond the supplied figures and ordinary decision-theoretic reasoning could not work out on the day.
