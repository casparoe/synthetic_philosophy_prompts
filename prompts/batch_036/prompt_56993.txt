write me a short story about Friedman's F-twist — the idea that a theory's assumptions being unrealistic is a virtue rather than a bug, provided it predicts. Not an allegory with philosophers arguing in a seminar though: a proper workplace story where the philosophy only shows up in what people actually do.

The shape I'm after. The protagonist keeps alive a model resting on a flat-out false assumption — make it concrete and technical, e.g. a sediment-transport model of an estuary that assumes the channel is laterally well-mixed, or whatever domain you genuinely know — and it beats a much more honest rival on the numbers. Then someone shows it works for the wrong reason: two errors that cancel. They offer a version with one of them fixed. The fixed version predicts worse, and restoring the accuracy would mean importing a new false assumption that's worse in kind, not just in degree. End with a call being made on the basis of the model, so the falseness costs somebody something.

I want two voices in it: one that says a model earns its keep by predicting, and one that says a model whose errors you cant localise is a model you cant safely extend. Don't settle it — leave it genuinely unclear which of them is right, and don't let either one get a speech that explains the moral. British English please, somewhere around 1300 words.
