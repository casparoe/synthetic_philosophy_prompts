Next month I am giving a colloquium talk in a philosophy of mind series, and I want a question-and-answer preparation sheet rather than another draft of the argument. I set out the position and the audience below in enough detail that you can predict what this particular room will say, as opposed to what a general readership on mental causation would say.

**The position.** I borrow Douglas Hofstadter's image of the careenium — a brain sitting in a vat, its constituent symbols conceived as little balls, simmballs, careening into one another — but I press it further than the image is usually pressed. My thesis is that symbol-level causal claims are not a convenient fiction laid over a neural story that does all the real causing: the simmballs genuinely push the careenium around. The argument runs in four steps. First, causal exclusion presupposes that there is a fixed quantity of causal work available to be allocated between levels, and on no respectable account of causation is causation a substance that can be exhausted. Second, on any interventionist or counterfactual account, a candidate cause must underwrite generalisations that hold across a range of background variation; symbol-level generalisations — that this belief coupled with that desire produces this behaviour — survive neural turnover, pharmacological perturbation, lesion compensation and the slow drift in which cells carry which content, whereas the corresponding neural generalisations hold only against a background of contingencies that the neural description nowhere records. Third, it follows that the neural level is not more causal but differently causal, and in the cases that matter less so: it buys determinacy about the substrate at the price of the modal structure that makes an account explanatory of this effect rather than of a coincidence. Fourth, the physics of the careenium is fully respected throughout; nothing is screened off, and no conservation law is strained, because the claim is about which generalisations carry the counterfactual load.

The paper's most exposed flank, and the place I would most like to be pressure-tested, is that I do not treat this as merely a claim about explanation. I claim that a simmball collision can be a cause of a movement in a sense in which no particular ion flux is a cause of it, even though every ion flux is exactly as physically real as the next. I also claim that a simulation could reproduce the ion fluxes of a careenium down to the last channel and yet host no simmballs at all, since what makes a pattern a simmball is its participation in a large system of interlocking, triggerable, compositional patterns that can be about what is absent. I have no argument for this second claim beyond an appeal to what the patterns must be able to do.

**The audience.** The department is strong in philosophy of neuroscience and philosophy of biology, and nobody there works primarily on mental causation. Two members are committed exclusionists in the Kim lineage: they accept the causal closure of the physical, they think non-reductive physicalism is unstable rather than merely incomplete, and they will run the dilemma that either my symbol-level generalisations are made true by nothing over and above the physical state, in which case they add no causal powers, or by something over and above it, in which case I have quietly abandoned physicalism. Two others work on mechanistic explanation and will press me on whether I have said what a mechanism is, or only observed that good explanations are stable ones; at least one will insist that constitutive relevance is not causal relevance and that I have traded on an equivocation between the two. There is a cognitive scientist of dynamical-systems persuasion who thinks representation talk is what should be eliminated, who will warmly endorse my anti-exclusion arguments about the neural level and then pull the rug out from under my symbol level by pointing out that invariance under perturbation is a property of attractor dynamics and needs no symbols to explain it. There is a graduate student working on whole-brain emulation who will ask what my view says about the simulation case, and who has read my second flank. There are also several analytic metaphysicians who will want to know whether I am using "cause" univocally, whether I have become a causal pluralist, and whether pluralism is an answer or an evacuation of the question.

**What I want from you.** Roughly eight or nine objections that this room will actually raise, ordered by the damage each would do to the paper if I handled it badly. For each: phrase it as that person would phrase it, in their own technical vocabulary and with their own loaded framing — I want to hear the exclusionist press the supervenience dilemma in the way an exclusionist presses it, not in a form I could easily bat away; then give a reply I could deliver aloud in about two minutes; then a frank note on where the reply is soft, or where it depends on a premise this audience does not already share. Please identify which of the objections are one objection wearing several costumes, so that I can answer them together. Mark explicitly the points on which an objector is likely to be right and I should say so and update rather than reply. Tell me what I ought to concede in the first five minutes in order to buy credibility for the rest. Tell me which single reply has to land if the paper is to survive the session. And tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one to which, at present, I have no answer.

Please write the whole sheet in British English.
