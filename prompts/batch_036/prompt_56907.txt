Question 4 (25 marks, from the Coercion and Constraint section):

"A great deal of the argument against torture in ticking-bomb cases is empirical and institutional: permitting it corrupts the agencies that carry it out, brutalises the individual who does the torturing, and in practice spreads well beyond the narrow category of cases that was supposed to justify it. Now suppose a polity deploys an automated system that administers strictly calibrated, non-lethal, non-permanent pain to a detainee under judicial authorisation, with no human being in the room, no human hand on any control, and a complete public audit trail. Assume for the sake of argument that the institutional-spread and agent-degradation objections are fully neutralised in this case. Does the deontological objection to torture survive the substitution? Discuss."

Student's answer:

"The question asks us to suppose away the two strongest practical objections, so what is left has to be assessed on its own terms. That is worth doing, because most writing against torture leans very heavily on consequences: the corruption of institutions, the slippage from the exceptional case to the routine one, the damage done to the people who carry the act out. If we stipulate all of that away, the absolutist is left saying that the act is wrong independently of anything it brings about, and that is a much harder claim to defend.

The first thing to notice is that the machine solves the reliability problem. Ordinary ticking-bomb cases founder because torture is a poor instrument for extracting accurate information: the detainee says whatever stops the pain, and the torturer has no way of checking any of it. Here the system is programmed to stop the moment reliable information is given, and the whole trail is inspectable, so the polity can be confident it has actually got what it needed. That is a real improvement on the human case and the question is right to build it in.

Second, and to my mind more important, the absence of a human torturer means there is nobody in the room who uses the detainee as a mere means. The Kantian objection, if that is what the deontologist is running, needs an agent who treats another person as a tool for their own purposes. A machine has no purposes. It is not choosing, not expressing contempt, not taking pleasure in anything. So the particular wrong the objection identifies — being made into an instrument of another's will — simply does not occur. What remains is only the suffering, and the suffering has been stipulated to be calibrated to the minimum necessary, non-permanent, and no greater in quantity than discomforts we already impose lawfully on detainees, for instance the conditions of prolonged solitary confinement, which few people call torture. Comparing like with like on intensity, the automated case looks no worse.

There is a point on the other side which I should register. The audit trail is public, so the polity is openly declaring that it will do this. One could say the contempt is expressed by the state even though no individual expresses it, and that transparency makes things worse rather than better, since the humiliation is documented rather than hidden. That is a genuine worry. But it shows only that automation changes who the agent is, not what is done — and if what is done is not wrongful in itself, as I have argued, then a change of agent cannot make it wrongful.

On balance, then, I think the deontological objection does not survive the substitution. The empirical strands have been stipulated away by the question, the agent-relative strand has no agent left to attach to, and the suffering strand has been reduced to a level we tolerate elsewhere in the penal system. Absolutism about torture turns out to depend on contingent facts about human beings and human institutions, and once those facts are altered the position loses its grip."

That's the whole thing. The mark scheme is the generic one for the paper: argument, relevance to the question actually set, and precision.
