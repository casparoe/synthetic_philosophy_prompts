I am putting together a graduate seminar week on divine omnipotence, and I want to open it with a real episode rather than with the stone paradox, which is where every reading list on this topic starts. The episode I have in mind is the Parisian syllabus promulgated by Bishop Étienne Tempier on 7 March 1277, three weeks after Pope John XXI asked him to look into reports of heretical teaching in the university, and prepared with the help of a commission of sixteen theologians, Henry of Ghent among them. On his own authority Tempier expanded an earlier, much shorter list into a condemnation of 219 propositions, most of them drawn from what was being disputed in the faculty of arts: the eternity of the world, the unicity of the intellect, astrology, necessitarianism about the will, and a scattering of claims about the reach of God's power. Two of these last matter most for my purposes. One asserts that God could not move the heavens with rectilinear motion, on the stated grounds that a vacuum would remain; another asserts that the first cause cannot make several worlds, and Tempier's own position was explicitly that God could have created more than one world even though revelation tells us he made only one. The preamble reproaches the offenders for saying things they hold to be true in philosophy while conceding they must be denied in theology, and parts of the list were widely suspected of touching positions held by Thomas Aquinas, who had died three years earlier. I want to be clear about what I am not asking for. I know that historians argue about how precisely the syllabus targeted Aquinas, and I know about the long-running and contested thesis that 1277 somehow liberated natural philosophy and helped produce modern science; please leave both of those aside, since I cannot adjudicate them in a seminar and they are not what interests me. I am supplying the facts above so that the analysis does not have to depend on your recalling them, and I would rather have argument than scholarship: no bibliographies, no reading lists, no attributions I would have to check.

What I want is the episode run through the omnipotence paradox and the standard ways of dissolving it, in both directions. First, the mapping. Take the familiar candidate resolutions — the view that omnipotence extends to everything that is possible absolutely, so that whatever involves a contradiction simply falls outside its scope; the view that the paradox is ill-formed because "a stone too heavy for God to lift" fails to pick out a coherent task at all; the move that distinguishes senses of "can," or that treats omnipotence as maximal power indexed across possible worlds rather than as the ability to do anything describable; and the voluntarist response, later spelled out in Descartes's doctrine that God is the author of the eternal truths and could have made it otherwise even about necessity and contradiction. For each, say what it implies about the truth or falsity of Tempier's condemned articles, and about whether those articles were the right things to condemn: does the contradiction-limit view render "God cannot move the heavens in a straight line" straightforwardly false, and if so, is it false for a reason that has anything to do with omnipotence, or only for a reason about physics and the impossibility of a void? Second, the structural mismatch, which is the part I find genuinely puzzling. Not one of the 219 articles is a self-referential paradox of the stone type; they are modal claims about the world — how many worlds there could be, what kinds of motion are possible, whether voids can exist — and the decree's business is what may licitly be taught, not whether a predicate is coherent. Does that fact support the deflationary diagnosis, on which the paradoxes are semantic artifacts with no theological work left to do once you see how "can" behaves? Or does it suggest the opposite: that the real disputes about divine power have always been about which metaphysical and physical possibilities are ruled out and on whose authority, a question the paradox literature almost entirely ignores? Third, tell me what the distinction between God's absolute power and his ordained power does here — whether it resolves the paradox, relocates it, or merely gives the paradox a respectable vocabulary, and whether Tempier is best read as invoking that distinction or as something else, namely an assertion that the ordinary course of nature imposes no constraints on what may be asserted about God. And then a comparison I cannot settle: is Descartes's later voluntarism the consistent extension of the logic of 1277, or a violation of it, given that Tempier's own articles were aimed at people who thought philosophy and faith could deliver contrary truths? What the answer shows, I suspect, is whether "omnipotence" is doing one job in these discussions or two.

Finally, please be disciplined about where the theory is silent rather than making it speak. Some parts of this episode may simply have no bearing on the analytic debate, and if treating a condemnatory decree as a test case for the logic of "can" is itself a category mistake, say so and explain what the category mistake consists in. Where you do think the episode bears on the theory, give me a verdict in the terms the theory itself supplies: is 1277 a confirmation of one resolution, an anomaly for all of them, or a kind of case they never anticipated? And end with the sharpest question I can put to my students — if some of the condemned articles cannot be evaluated as true or false under the contradiction-limit view at all, what does that tell us about whether omnipotence paradoxes are about power, about possibility, or about nothing but the grammar of an honorific?
