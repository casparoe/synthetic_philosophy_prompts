So I've gone pretty deep down a rabbit hole on the Allais paradox over the last couple of months. I work in logistics scheduling, no economics training at all, I just got curious after an argument with my brother about insurance deductibles where I realized neither of us could say whether the other was being consistent. What I keep thinking about now isn't the paradox itself — I've read a dozen explanations of that and I get the common-consequence setup fine — it's the actual episode where the thing first happened, and I want someone to treat that episode seriously as a case study rather than as a puzzle.

Here's what I think happened, please correct me where I'm wrong because I'm piecing it together from memory and half-remembered blog posts. Allais gave out his questionnaire to a room full of economists at a Paris conference on risk in the early fifties — 1952 is the year I have in my head — and the people filling it in included the Americans who had basically built the theory he was attacking, Savage among them. As far as I understand it, most of the room took the certain option in the first pair and the long shot in the second, some people changed their answers when the two pairs were put side by side and they could see the structure, and Savage reacted in a way that amounted to saying he'd have been glad to take the gift. And then, this is the part that fascinates me, the result just sort of sat there for a quarter century without anybody abandoning the axiom — Kahneman and Tversky eventually recast the same pattern as a cognitive bias, Allais got a Nobel in 1988 that everyone agreed was decades late, and in between the Anglo-American textbooks kept teaching the axiom more or less unchanged.

The pairs, as I have them (the original was in hundreds of millions of francs, the versions people quote now are in dollars, I don't think that matters but tell me if it does):

- Pair 1: A is a sure $1 million. B is 89% $1 million, 10% $5 million, 1% nothing.
- Pair 2: A' is 11% $1 million, 89% nothing. B' is 10% $5 million, 90% nothing.

Can you analyze that conference episode through the lens of the independence axiom the way you'd analyze a crisis or a policy decision — mapping first, then judgment? Concretely: who were the players, and what did each of them think the questionnaire was measuring (a test of their preferences, a test of their arithmetic, a piece of theater, a trap)? What exactly did the axiom require of a person sitting in that room with those numbers in front of them, and what did it require of Savage afterwards — is "I'll change my answer once I see the pairs together, but the axiom still stands" a coherent position, and if it is, what does that say about whether the axiom is normative, descriptive, or something that keeps sliding between the two? I also want you to deal with the setup itself: these were enormous hypothetical sums being put to people whose entire professional standing rested on being rational about risk, and I can't tell whether the theory has anything to say about that or whether it just goes quiet. And Allais clearly thought wanting the certainty was not a mistake but a legitimate thing to want — where does that claim live relative to the axiom, inside it, outside it, or is that the actual disagreement?

Then the payoff question. Savage reportedly had a caveat to the effect that his axioms were meant for small, precisely specified worlds and that outside those you'd better check your premises carefully before applying them. Does that caveat rescue the axiom from the Paris room, or does it concede the room? Was a hypothetical questionnaire about millions of francs a small world or a large one? And finally: does this episode count as a confirmation of the axiom, an anomaly it has to absorb, or a case it never anticipated — and did the later history, with the bias framing and the weighting functions and everything since, vindicate Allais or bury him under a different name? Be honest about where the theory has nothing to say rather than making it say something, and if you think my version of the history is off in a way that changes the analysis, tell me that before you get to the analysis.
