I am a doctoral student in the history of economic thought, and this term I am leading the seminar section that follows our philosophy-of-science core. My week is on long-run predictions and the problem of rescue: what it takes for a theory whose predicted event has not occurred to be anything other than a theory that has been falsified. To keep the session off the well-worn tracks, I have built it around Malthus, and I have written up for the students the six defensive moves I think a competent Malthusian can actually make against the charge that the Essay on the Principle of Population was refuted by the last two centuries. What I want from you is an assessment of those six moves as a set — scored, ranked, and compared — rather than a general essay on Malthus or on falsification.

For orientation, the target of the charge is the claim in the anonymous 1798 Essay that population, when unchecked, increases in a geometric progression (Malthus supposed a doubling every twenty-five years) while the means of subsistence increase at best in an arithmetic progression, so that the widening gap must be closed by misery and vice unless births fall. Against this it is said that world population has risen roughly eightfold since 1800 while average real incomes have risen by a far larger multiple, that no general subsistence crisis of the implied kind has occurred, and that fertility in the richest countries declined voluntarily for a century and a half without one. Malthus revised the book heavily for the second edition of 1803 and reissued it four more times through 1826, so some of the moves below are available in his own text rather than supplied by later admirers.

The six moves, exactly as I have stated them for the class:

1. **Tendency, not forecast.** What the Essay asserts is a directional tendency, as in Chapter II's remark that the constant effort towards an increase of population "as constantly tends to subject the lower classes of the society to distress and to prevent any great permanent amelioration of their condition." A tendency claim of this sort is confirmed by showing that the mechanism is real and operative, not by the arrival of the outcome; and permanent amelioration did in fact fail to reach most of the world's labourers for a very long time.

2. **Expired scope.** The arithmetic limit is a claim about land: in an organic economy food comes from acreage and acreage is finite. Once fossil energy, mechanisation, and industrially fixed nitrogen allow yield per acre to keep rising, the scope condition under which the arithmetic ratio was asserted no longer obtains. The theory was not falsified; its domain closed, in the way Newtonian mechanics' domain closed.

3. **The preventive check fired.** Malthus's own taxonomy of checks includes preventive checks operating on the birth rate, and from 1803 onward he allowed moral restraint — late marriage accompanied by chastity before it — as a check capable of averting misery. The demographic transition is then the theory's own escape valve working as described: pressure expressed itself as falling fertility rather than as famine. That is confirmation, not refutation.

4. **Engels' pause.** Between roughly 1790 and 1840 — the interval Robert Allen labelled Engels' pause — British real wages were close to flat while output per head grew rapidly; one standard set of estimates puts the rise in output per head over 1780–1840 at around 46 per cent against a rise in working-class wages of around 12 per cent. That is precisely the predicted signature, productivity gains absorbed by population growth and land scarcity, and the pause ended only once the surplus labour pool was drained. The theory ran on schedule during the half-century in which its conditions obtained.

5. **Patience.** Two centuries is a short test for a mechanism whose natural unit is the generation and whose binding constraints — arable land, fresh water, accessible phosphorus, a stable climate — are planetary stocks rather than annual flows. Declaring the principle refuted now is like declaring a theory of a fault line refuted because nothing has slipped within living memory. The theory may be right but early.

6. **Reformulation.** Restate the principle as follows: population pressure builds until something — technology, institutions, or behaviour — relaxes it. On this statement every conceivable history is compatible with the theory, since whatever happened, something relaxed the pressure.

Here is what I would like, in this order:

(a) Score each of the six moves from 0 to 10 for *content retention*, where 10 means the rescue leaves the theory making every risky claim it originally made, and 0 means it leaves nothing that any possible course of events could contradict. Be prepared to score harshly: a list in which everything lands between 5 and 7 is useless to me for teaching purposes.

(b) For each move, state separately whether the auxiliary it introduces is independently testable — that is, whether it earns its keep somewhere other than in the defence of Malthus — answering yes, partly, or no with a clause of justification.

(c) Rank the six from most to least defensible as a reply to the falsification charge, and for each adjacent pair in the ranking say in one or two sentences what decides the ordering.

(d) Identify which moves can be stacked into a single coherent defence and which undercut one another when combined. I suspect that 2 and 5 pull in opposite temporal directions, and that 3 and 6 trade on the same flexibility, but I want this checked rather than assumed.

(e) Give me one number in the interval from 0 to 1: your probability that the strongest single move, if adopted on its own without help from the others, succeeds in establishing that Malthus was right but early rather than simply wrong.

(f) Close with a short paragraph stating the general condition under which "right but early" is a legitimate verdict rather than a euphemism for "not yet falsified," and say explicitly whether any of the six moves meets it.

Two constraints. The moves are stated in full above, so please do not add references, bibliographies, or reading lists, and do not expand the set unless you judge a seventh move indispensable to the ranking, in which case name it in a single sentence at the very end. And although one of my students will press the point using Popper's criterion of ad hocery and Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts, the scoring and ranking should stand on their own reasoning and should not require knowing those labels.
