Two members of my epistemology reading group spent an entire session arguing about a third member, Jonah, and then asked me to referee. I couldn't decide, so I'm passing it on.

The case under dispute: Jonah is currently about 0.9 confident that a certain argument against free will is sound. Next month he spends a week working closely with a famously charismatic author whose book argues the opposite way, and Jonah confidently expects that by the end of the week he will be down to roughly 0.4. He does not expect any new evidence. A professor has already walked him through what she takes to be a definite flaw in the book's central move, and Jonah found her diagnosis convincing; he simply expects the prose to be overwhelming.

Here is the exchange, more or less as it happened.

(a) Priya said Jonah is already incoherent. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says that if you are certain your later credence in A will be x, your present credence in A should be x. Sitting at 0.9 while certain he will be at 0.4 is a violation, and it exposes him to the two-bet diachronic Dutch book.

(b) Dev replied that Reflection only binds when the expected change is evidential. Jonah expects his shift to be produced by rhetoric, not by information, so his future self is someone he already knows will have been taken in. Deferring to that self is deferring to a mistake.

(c) Priya asked how Jonah knows it will be a mistake. His future self will have everything he has now plus a week of close reading. Refusing to defer is a claim to infallibility — and Reflection is in any case derivable from conditionalization given the assumption that the later self only ever adds to, never loses, the earlier self's evidence, which plainly holds here.

(d) Dev said that derivation is what sinks the principle rather than saving it: it assumes the later credence results from conditionalizing on a richer body of evidence. If the change comes from charisma, the premise fails. And Priya's reading has an absurd consequence — anyone who knows she will be badly sleep-deprived tomorrow ought today to shade her confidence in elementary arithmetic toward whatever she expects her exhausted self to think.

(e) Priya said that is just the price of knowing you will be unreliable, and the sleep case is disanalogous anyway: the exhausted person does not expect her tired self to settle on 2+2=5, only to be sloppy and error-prone, whereas Jonah expects to be genuinely persuaded. Reflection tracks settled conviction, not predicted performance failure.

(f) Dev replied that this concedes the point. A principle that applies only to future selves who are better informed is not a coherence constraint but a heuristic, and once the condition is spelled out Reflection does no independent work: it collapses into conditionalizing on expected future evidence. Besides, Jonah's 0.4 is a prediction about a psychological process, not a report of a future evidential state, and predictions about one's own psychology have not earned that kind of authority.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of (a) through (f), say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and why. Then say who is more right overall about whether Jonah should lower his credence now, and identify anything substantive that both of them missed — including whether Jonah has any legitimate way to handle the coming week, such as declining the invitation or precommitting in advance, that does not itself violate whatever the correct principle turns out to be.
