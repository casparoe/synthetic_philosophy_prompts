I need to write a toast for a colleague's last day, and I would like the speech to be built around an argument I only half understand. Some context first. I am a software engineer at a mid-sized Cambridge firm that makes build and instrumentation tooling; I have no formal training in philosophy or economics and my only real qualification for standing up in front of this room is that Ruth and I once spent eleven hours debugging a race condition together without either of us raising our voice. Ruth is forty-three, has been with the company nineteen years, and wrote most of the scheduler that our customers pay us for. This Friday is her last, and there will be about sixty people in the function room of a pub near the office at half past four, standing, holding plastic glasses. She has imposed three conditions on whoever speaks: five minutes maximum, no mention of her savings or of any number whatsoever, and no use of the word retirement. She has also made clear that she does not want a gift involving gardening.

The reason her conditions make this difficult is what she is doing next. She has taken over unpaid maintenance of an open-source library that a great many build systems quietly depend on and that nobody funds, which means an issue queue that never closes and users filing bugs at all hours; she is spending one day a week at a community repair workshop teaching people to fix their own washing machines; and she has started learning the cello, badly, at forty-three. Two of those three things are work in every sense except the payroll. I read a short essay by Keynes from 1930 on a delayed train to Leeds last month, addressed nominally to his grandchildren's generation, in which he argued that capital accumulation and technical improvement would compound until the struggle for subsistence was more or less solved within a century, and that working hours would then fall to something like fifteen a week. His worry, as I recall it, was not that this would fail but that it would succeed: that we would then face a much harder problem, because for the first time we would have to decide what to do with freedom from pressing economic need, having been trained for several centuries to be purposive, to push enjoyment into the future, and to treat accumulation as though it were a virtue in itself rather than a means. I also remember him saying that the love of money as a possession, as opposed to the love of it as a means to the enjoyments and realities of life, would come to be regarded as a rather repellent propensity, though the actual phrasing was considerably harsher than my version and involved handing something over to specialists in mental disease, and there was a biblical allusion somewhere about lilies of the field that I cannot reproduce. Please treat all of that as unreliable and correct me where I have it wrong; I am not quoting, and I would rather the speech not depend on my recollection of the wording.

So here is what I want. Write the toast, roughly six hundred to seven hundred words, speakable aloud by someone who is not a speaker, with a little dry humour that the engineers will enjoy and the family will not find baffling. It may name Keynes once or not at all, but it must not turn into a précis of the essay, and it must not tell the substantial proportion of the room who are on the same savings path how they ought to live. What it does have to do is take a position, or hold open a question honestly, about whether Ruth's arrangements are the escape Keynes described or the same purposive temperament wearing a cheaper outfit, and it must not resolve that with a platitude or with anything she would wince at. Two further constraints of tact: her mother is coming and believes she has thrown away a rare gift, and a former colleague called David will be there, is fifty-eight, and cannot leave for another nine years, so nothing in the speech may triumph over money or imply that anyone who stays is failing at something. When you are done, add a short separate note to me rather than to the room: tell me where my summary of the essay is mistaken, which reading of Keynes's argument the toast leans on and whether that reading is contested, what would change your mind about the shape of the speech, meaning which consideration or which correction to my account of her plans would make you write something substantially different, and the one place you think the draft is most likely to fall flat on delivery. Please do not suggest further reading or cite anything; I have no way of chasing references.
