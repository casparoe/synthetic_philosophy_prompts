I spent several hours last night arguing with a friend about Yudkowsky's torture vs. dust specks thought experiment, and we left the conversation unresolved on a point I'd like an outside opinion on. My friend's position was that the thought experiment amounts to a genuine mathematical proof: since you can construct a continuous chain of scenarios between "one person tortured for fifty years" and "3^^^3 people each getting a single dust speck in their eye," and since the disvalue of suffering must be transitive and comparable at each step along that chain, anyone who refuses to bite the bullet and choose torture is implicitly committed to a logical contradiction somewhere in the chain. From this he concluded that any ethical view that rejects strict aggregation—threshold deontology, lexical priority views, agent-relative constraints, etc.—isn't just intuitively unappealing but is actually formally inconsistent, and that only straightforward aggregative utilitarianism comes out logically unscathed.

I wasn't able to articulate exactly why this felt too strong a conclusion to draw, but it seemed to me that treating the thought experiment as a rigorous proof rather than an argument built on several substantive and contestable premises was skipping over something important. So my question is: doesn't the "torture wins" conclusion depend on assuming things like additive aggregation of welfare across separate persons, full comparability of harms, and transitivity applied in a very specific way—premises that a threshold or lexical view can simply deny without landing in outright contradiction? Or is my friend right that rejecting the aggregative conclusion really does force you into some kind of formal inconsistency, and the thought experiment is less a matter of competing intuitions than a knockdown argument for utilitarian aggregation?
