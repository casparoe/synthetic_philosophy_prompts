I've spent the last year or so reading rationalist blogs and forums as a hobby -- I never studied any of this formally, I just find it fascinating -- and there's a move people keep pulling that I can't quite get to the bottom of. It goes something like: "two honest, rational people who both know what the other believes can't just agree to disagree; if you're still not converging after talking it through, one of you has to be doing something wrong." I've seen it used to shut down disagreements about everything from geopolitics to nutrition.

It also got used on me directly a few months back, in a work argument about how likely our product launch was to hit its targets. My coworker said that since we'd each laid out our reasoning to the other, my continued skepticism just meant I wasn't updating properly, full stop. When I went looking for where this idea actually comes from, I found it traces back to a theorem by a mathematician named Aumann, whose work on exactly this kind of thing won a Nobel Prize in economics decades later. The whole result seemed to hinge on something called the "common prior assumption," which nobody ever spells out when they're using the slogan to win an argument in the moment, and which strikes me as a strange thing to assume about two people who grew up differently and started reasoning from different places long before either of us saw a single sales projection.

What I'd like from you is not a single verdict but a menu: lay out the different precise claims that "rational people can't agree to disagree" could actually be making, running from a reading so weak it's practically a tautology to one so strong that almost nobody could ever really satisfy its conditions. For each reading on the menu, spell out exactly what work the common prior assumption is doing to make it true, and say whether that assumption is something two ordinary disagreeing people could plausibly be credited with, or whether it quietly smuggles in something much stronger than "we currently have the same information" -- something more like having started out, before any evidence came in, from literally the same place. Then tell me which of these readings are basically truisms once you see them clearly, which are substantive but still defensible, and which sound rigorous but don't really deliver what people want from them -- and in particular, whether any reading on your list would have actually justified my coworker telling me that my continued doubt proved I was reasoning badly.
