**Question.** In *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, Kripke poses a sceptical paradox about meaning using the example of a person asked, for the first time, to compute "68 + 57."

(a) *[8 points]* State the paradox precisely: what fact about the person's past use of "+" would have to obtain for it to be determinately true that she means addition, so that "125" is the uniquely correct answer, rather than some deviant function such as "quus" (where x quus y = x + y whenever x and y are both less than 57, and x quus y = 5 whenever either is 57 or greater)? Why does Kripke think no candidate fact can do this job?

(b) *[7 points]* A natural proposal is that what makes it the case that she means addition rather than quaddition is a fact about her dispositions to respond. Explain Kripke's argument against this proposal, and explain why he treats the objection as principled rather than as merely a practical limitation on finite creatures.

(c) *[10 points]* Kripke says that Wittgenstein's response to the paradox is a "sceptical solution" rather than a "straight solution." Explain this distinction, state what the sceptical solution says about the status of statements such as "Jones means addition by '+'" once the sceptic's negative point is granted, and explain what work the notion of a community is doing in it. Briefly indicate how Kripke connects this discussion to the private language argument of *Philosophical Investigations* §§243ff.

**Student's answer.**

(a) The sceptic asks what fact about my past use of "+" determines that, in computing "68 + 57" for the first time, I should answer "125" rather than "5." Since I have, by hypothesis, never before added two numbers as large as 57, my past linguistic behavior is compatible with my having meant the ordinary addition function, but it is equally compatible with my having meant a deviant function, quus, which agrees with addition for every pair of numbers below 57 but yields 5 whenever either number is 57 or greater. Nothing in my finite history of past applications can favor one hypothesis over the other, and the same argument can be run again for any candidate fact I might cite (a rule recited to myself, a mental picture, and so on), since any such item is itself open to the same range of deviant interpretations. The sceptic's challenge is therefore not really about arithmetic at all: it is the question of what could make it the case that I meant one function rather than another, when both are equally compatible with everything about my past.

(b) The obvious next move is to say that what makes it true that I mean addition is that I am disposed to answer "125" (and, more generally, disposed to produce the outputs of the addition function whenever queried). Kripke argues that this cannot be the fact we are looking for. First, my actual dispositions are finite: there are sums involving numbers too large for me ever to compute, so my dispositions run out before the function does, and an appeal to an idealized, infinite disposition merely replaces one fiction with another. Second, and more importantly, even where my dispositions are well defined they can misfire: I can be careless, make an arithmetical slip, or answer while distracted, and a bare disposition to respond has no way of distinguishing performance error from the correct application of a different rule that I actually meant. Drawing that distinction would require already knowing what I mean, which is exactly what was in question. Kripke takes this second point to show that the problem is not merely practical: dispositions are descriptive facts about how I would respond, whereas meaning is normative, a matter of how I should respond, and no amount of idealizing the descriptive facts closes that gap.

(c) Because every candidate fact fails in this way, Kripke says that Wittgenstein does not attempt a "straight solution" that would answer the sceptic by producing the missing fact and showing his premises false. Instead he offers a "sceptical solution," modeled on Hume's treatment of causation: one that concedes there is no such fact, but argues that our ordinary practice of making and relying on meaning-attributions is nonetheless perfectly in order. Once the sceptic's negative point is granted, statements such as "Jones means addition by '+'" are not made true by any fact about Jones considered on his own; rather, the community's agreement with Jones's use of "+" is what constitutes his meaning addition and what makes such attributions true, since agreement with the community just is the practice in which talk of meaning addition is embedded. This is why the discussion connects to the private language argument: a solitary individual, considered apart from any community, has no one to agree or disagree with him, so there is nothing that could constitute his using a sign correctly or incorrectly, which is exactly Wittgenstein's conclusion that a private language, in the relevant sense, is impossible.
