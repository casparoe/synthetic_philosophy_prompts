Ok so I'm an SLP and a big chunk of my job is building AAC systems (picture boards, tablet apps, whatever) for nonverbal or minimally verbal kids -- autism, cerebral palsy, apraxia, the whole range. There's a running argument on my team, me vs a couple of the ABA-trained aides, about whether a kid pressing a symbol actually *means* it or whether we've just built a really elaborate pigeon-peck that happens to get reinforced -- is "more" more to them, or is it just a button that produces juice. I stumbled on Stevan Harnad's symbol grounding problem recently (the old worry that a system which only defines symbols in terms of other symbols, like a dictionary that only ever points to more words, can never actually get to meaning) and it's basically our argument except about computers instead of kids. He also had this proposed fix -- ground the basic symbols in stuff the system can actually perceive and categorize, then build the complicated stuff out of combinations of those -- which sounds a lot like how we're supposed to build AAC vocabulary, nobody on my team has just ever put it in those terms.

Can you turn that into an actual decision procedure I can run, in a session or across a few weeks, for judging whether a given symbol is grounded enough that we should build on it (combine it with others, use it to teach a new one) versus whether we're just chaining rote behavior that'll fall apart the second the context changes. I need the questions in order, what counts as good enough evidence at each step, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision over to my clinical judgement because no test is going to settle it. It has to be usable by someone with zero philosophy background -- I need to explain it to an aide in about five minutes and be able to defend whatever we decide in an IEP meeting.

Run it on two real-ish cases: a 6 year old who presses "more" across totally unrelated activities (swinging, bubbles, singing, tickles), and a 9 year old who nails a 4-symbol string for "want juice please [name]" every single time but has never once used "want" or "please" with anything else. I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell me something about the second kid that the team doesnt want to hear, which is fine, I'd rather know. Last thing -- be straight with me about what this procedure can never actually settle, because my hunch is there's a real philosophical wall here and not just a practical one, and I dont want you smoothing that over just to give me a tidier answer.
