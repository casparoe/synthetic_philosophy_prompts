I have no formal training in philosophy, but a disagreement at work a while back over whose fault a missed deadline really was sent me down a rabbit hole of reading about whether failing to do something can count as a genuine cause of what happens next. Almost everything I have found on this uses some version of the same setup: someone was supposed to water a plant, didn't, and then the point gets made that a stranger on the other side of the world who also didn't water it seems to pass the very same test for being a cause, which can't be right. I understand the puzzle, but I would like to see it worked through on a case that is not already worn smooth from overuse, so that the theories are doing the work rather than being recognized from a summary I have already read.

Please construct a thought experiment meeting this specification. Set it in an ordinary, present-day workplace or household -- not a hospital ward, a beach with a lifeguard, or anyone's houseplants, since those are the settings every treatment of this topic reaches for. The case must involve exactly three people, all present, all equally capable of performing one and the same preventive action, and all equally aware that it needs doing. Arrange things so that each of the three, taken individually with the other two's inaction held fixed, passes the ordinary counterfactual test for causing the eventual harm: if that one person alone had acted, the harm would not have occurred. Exactly one of the three, and only one, must have been given the job of performing that action beforehand, through an actual assigned role, contract, or explicit prior agreement -- not through being more skilled, closer at hand, or in any way more able than the other two, who must match the duty-bearer in every other respect. In the scenario, nobody acts, and the harm occurs. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

What I am after is whether two theories of causation really do come apart on a case like this for principled reasons, or whether any apparent difference between them is just smuggled in by how the case is described -- so please do not simply hand me verdicts, show how each one follows from applying the theory to the facts you have stipulated. After the scenario, walk through: (1) what a plain counterfactual theory of causation -- on which one thing causes another just in case the second would not have happened without the first -- says about each of the three people's inaction, and whether that theory has any resources for treating the three differently from one another; (2) what a rival theory, on which an omission is a cause only when the outcome is a departure from what would normally or was expected to happen given people's roles (call this the expectation theory), says about each of the three, and why its verdict differs from the counterfactual theory's verdict in this case; (3) which single feature of the case is doing the work of separating the three people, with confirmation that it is not secretly a difference in skill, proximity, or counterfactual strength; (4) what happens to both theories' verdicts if the assigned duty is taken away from everyone, and separately, what happens if that same duty is extended to all three; and (5) the strongest move available to someone committed to the counterfactual theory who wants to resist the conclusion that all three people equally caused the harm.

Once you have done all of that, give me a structured summary as a single JSON object using exactly this schema: a field "scenario_label" (string) holding a short name for the case; a field "candidates" holding an array of exactly three objects, one per person, each with "role_description" (string, a short phrase identifying who they are in the scenario), "has_assigned_duty" (true or false), "counterfactual_theory_verdict" ("cause" or "not a cause"), "expectation_theory_verdict" ("cause" or "not a cause"), and "ordinary_judgment" ("cause" or "not a cause", meaning how you expect most people to judge the case before doing any theorizing); a field "differentiating_feature" (string) holding one sentence naming what actually distinguishes the three people; a field "removal_and_addition_test" (string) holding one or two sentences on what happens to each theory's verdict when the duty is stripped from everyone and when it is extended to everyone; a field "counterfactualist_rebuttal" (string) holding the strongest reply available to a defender of the counterfactual theory; and a field "bottom_line" (string) holding a single sentence on what the case shows about the relationship between the two theories.
