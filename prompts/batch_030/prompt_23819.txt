So I fell down an Augustine rabbit hole a while back and it turns out his actual position on lying is way more extreme than the watered-down version people usually mention. He wrote two whole treatises on it, On Lying and then years later Against Lying, and that second one exists because a guy named Consentius asked if it was ok to fake being a heretic in order to infiltrate and expose real heretics, and Augustine said no, not even for that. His final position across both is that literally every lie is a sin, full stop, even one that saves somebody's life. He's even got a list ranking lies from worst to least bad -- lying about religious doctrine is the worst kind there is, a lie that saves someone from being caught or killed sits way down near the bottom -- but "least bad" is not the same as "fine," and he has to do real work explaining away the part of Exodus where the midwives lie to Pharoah to save the Hebrew baby boys and God rewards them for it. His answer is that they were rewarded for the mercy, not the lie.

I can't stop running this up against Huckleberry Finn, because Huck lies constantly through that whole book and Twain never treats the lying itself as the interesting problem. The two scenes that match Augustine's exact test case are the smallpox lie, where Huck scares off the men hunting for Jim by convincing them there's smallpox on the raft, and the much more famous scene later where he tears up the letter turning Jim in and decides he'll go to hell instead. Both are lies told purely to keep someone from being caught, which is exactly Augustine's category -- but what eats at Huck is whether helping Jim escape is the sin, never whether lying about it is.

Can you actually read the novel against Augustine's real position here, not the pop version of "is lying ever ok"? Does Huck Finn work as a genuine counterexample to "no lie is ever justified," or does it only look that way because the book's real moral fight is happening on a different axis entirely and the lying just comes along for the ride? Does it change anything that Twain fills the book with people who never technically lie -- the preachers, the feuding families, the whole slaveholding town -- but who he clearly thinks are doing something worse than Huck ever does? I want the honest version, including where Augustine's framework actually gets tested by this book versus where I'm just forcing a connection that isn't really there, and whether the novel shows you something about lying that the standard "murderer at the door" thought experiment doesn't.
