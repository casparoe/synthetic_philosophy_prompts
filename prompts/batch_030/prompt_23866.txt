I sit on the advisory board of a foundation that makes effective-altruism-style grants across global health and long-run causes such as institutional reform and catastrophic-risk reduction. Every funding cycle, its program officers face the same problem: consequentialism tells them to rank proposals by expected total consequences, but they are clueless about most of those consequences beyond a short horizon. Write a decision procedure a program officer, not a philosopher, could run on a proposal before it goes to the funding committee: the questions to ask in order to tell simple cluelessness (unmodeled effects that wash out symmetrically across the live options) from complex cluelessness (reasons pulling in different directions that don't net out); what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure stops and returns no ranking; and where it instead hands the call to the committee, because what's left is a question of values rather than a further fact. Run it on two live grants -- a global-health intervention with well-modeled near-term effects, and a policy-advocacy grant meant to shift a long-run institution -- and don't soften the verdict if the foundation's favored long-termist bet is the one that fails.
