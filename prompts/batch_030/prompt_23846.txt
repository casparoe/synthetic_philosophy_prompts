I spent my working life as a physicist, and it is only since retiring that I have read through the medieval debates on causation. Al-Ghazālī's argument in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa -- that fire does not itself necessitate the cotton's burning, and that what we call a cause is merely the customary occasion for God's action -- seems to me, theology aside, correct in what it denies: we never observe a necessary connection between events, only an unbroken regularity, and every law I trusted in the lab was, in hindsight, a description of that regularity and nothing more. What would count against this deflationary view of causal necessity? I should like to know which of Ibn Rushd's objections to the fire-cotton case still bite once restated without an Aristotelian theory of essences behind them; whether a modern powers ontologist has any real purchase here, or whether even a Humean about laws of nature -- who reaches the same denial of necessity without invoking any deity -- simply inherits the same difficulties; and whether the position, its theology removed, has been rendered proof against any possible observation. Which of these would a committed occasionalist concede as a genuine problem, and which would they dismiss as more of the same custom?
