I am at work on a novel whose plot turns on travel into one's own past, and before I settle the internal rules the book will obey, I want to think through the actual philosophical standing of the grandfather paradox rather than simply choosing whichever convention is most convenient for the story.

Here is the debate as I understand it, and where I would like you to adjudicate rather than merely survey. David Lewis argued that the paradox rests on an equivocation in "can": in the classic case, the time traveler has what it takes, in the ordinary sense, to kill their grandfather -- steady hand, loaded gun, clear shot -- but relative to the full set of facts about a single, already-fixed past, one in which grandfather demonstrably lived to father the traveler's parent, success is simply not compossible with those facts, so something mundane always intervenes. Critics grant that this removes any outright contradiction, but they press a further worry: that the sheer reliability of the interventions -- the gun that jams every time, the ill-timed cramp, the passing stranger -- amounts to a run of coincidence so improbable that it demands its own explanation. A second way out, far more popular in the genre I write, relocates the killing onto a branching timeline or parallel universe, so that a grandfather dies, but not the one whose survival actually produced the traveler. Lewis's own view was in effect that this changes the subject: what has been described is no longer travel to one's own past, but travel to someone else's. Physicists who take closed timelike curves seriously -- general relativity does permit them, in certain solutions -- have their own analogue of Lewis's constraint in Novikov's self-consistency principle; Hawking took the more deflationary view that nature may forbid such loops from forming at all, a suggestion he called the chronology protection conjecture. I would like all of this kept separate from the bootstrap paradox -- the puzzle of a loop with no origin, rather than one threatening contradiction -- since the book may eventually need a position on both, and I do not want to assume that settling one settles the other.

Write me an essay that weighs these positions and comes down on one side, rather than a catalogue that leaves every option standing. Is backward time travel to a single, non-branching past a coherent notion that survives the grandfather paradox, or does it only appear to survive by way of a maneuver that quietly empties it of what made it interesting in the first place -- and is the branching-timeline alternative actually a rival answer to that question, or a different and lesser one wearing the same name? A verdict along the lines of "logically consistent, but only by a maneuver whose costs are worth stating plainly, and branching timelines do not address the same puzzle" is a fine shape for an answer, but so is a cleaner verdict on either side. What I want is your own reasoning, not a reading list.
