My doctoral advisor is retiring after thirty-eight years teaching philosophy of religion at her seminary, and I have been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. She is a wholehearted believer in penal substitutionary atonement, and she is also the person who introduced two generations of her advisees to David Lewis's short, needling paper on the subject, "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" -- his observation that we are perfectly content letting a stranger pay our fine, since the debt is discharged and nobody feels cheated, but much less content letting a stranger serve our prison sentence or stand in front of the firing squad in our place and calling the matter settled, since punishment in the retributive sense is supposed to fall on the guilty party specifically and on nobody else. She used to put the question to her first-years more bluntly than Lewis ever did in print: would you let a stranger take a parking fine for you? Obviously. Would you let a stranger take your execution, and consider the books balanced? Obviously not -- so which one is the cross supposed to be? She never gave anyone a tidy way out of that question, and so far as I know she still doesn't have one herself.

Roughly half the room at the dinner will be former advisees and philosophy-of-religion colleagues who spent office hours arguing with her about exactly this, and who will know precisely what is being invoked the moment it comes up; the other half will be the congregation she has quietly served as a lay theologian for just as long, plus siblings, in-laws, and a couple of retired colleagues from the practical-theology side of the school, none of whom has ever heard of the philosopher David Lewis and will assume C. S. Lewis is meant. Please write the toast: about five minutes read aloud, warm rather than argumentative, built around one moment precise enough that the specialists know exactly which puzzle is being invoked and smile, but that never tips into a lecture on theories of punishment and never strands the half of the room for whom this is simply a beloved teacher's retirement party. It should be honest that she never resolved the question she spent decades asking, and it should manage to make that unresolved quality feel like the most loving thing about her rather than a flaw the toast has to explain away.
