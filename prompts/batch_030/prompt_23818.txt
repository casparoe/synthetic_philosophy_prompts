I have drafted a short essay on Dennett's "Quining Qualia," built around his coffee-taster thought experiment, and I would like your comments before I develop it further.

Dennett asks us to imagine two long-serving coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, whose job for years has been to certify that a coffee brand's flavor never changes, and who each eventually confess that they no longer enjoy the coffee they once loved. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly as it always did; what has changed is his own palate for judging it, sharpened by years of professional tasting, so that a flavor he once prized no longer satisfies him. Sanborn says the opposite: his standards of what counts as good coffee have not moved at all, but something in his tasting apparatus has degraded, so the coffee no longer tastes the way it used to — if it did, he insists, he would still love it. Dennett's point is not simply that we, from the outside, cannot tell which of the two has diagnosed himself correctly. It is that neither Chase nor Sanborn can settle the question about himself either, since both stories are compatible with the same memories, the same behavior, and the same present experience. If qualia were the directly and incorrigibly apprehensible properties the tradition takes them to be, this is exactly the kind of question — has the taste changed, or only my reaction to it? — that first-person access should trivially resolve. That it cannot, even in the most favorable case, is Dennett's argument that the fourth of his four defining marks of qualia (ineffability, intrinsicality, privacy, and direct apprehensibility) is empty, and that the whole category should be eliminated rather than clarified.

Here is where I want to press the argument further, and where I am least sure of my footing. A defender of qualia might retreat from cross-time comparisons altogether, conceding that memory is too coarse an instrument to track whether the quale or only the reaction to it has shifted over six years, while still insisting that at any given moment there is some fact about how the coffee tastes to Chase right then, independent of that instant's relation to six years ago. Call this the synchronic retreat. I do not think it succeeds, but I can only gesture at why: even Chase's momentary report seems already to involve an act of recognition or comparison — calling the present taste "the same taste" is itself a reactive, judgment-laden act, not a passive reading-off of some antecedently individuated quale that is only afterward, and separably, liked or disliked. If that is right, the quale/reaction distinction does not merely fail across time; it never had a determinate application even at an instant, because no description of "the taste itself," prior to and independent of some act of classification, is on offer. But I am not fully convinced this is more than a restatement of Dennett's conclusion dressed up as an argument for it.

I would like comments on three things. First, whether my reconstruction of the Chase and Sanborn case, and of exactly what it is supposed to establish (something about apprehensibility specifically, not about qualia wholesale), is accurate to Dennett's argument. Second, whether the synchronic retreat is really closed off for the reason I give, or whether there is some way of individuating "the taste itself" independently of any reactive or comparative act that I am overlooking. Third, whether the essay's conclusion should be the strong one Dennett wants — that qualia are an incoherent posit, full stop — or only the weaker one that qualia, even if real, cannot do the epistemic work of grounding first-person authority that motivated positing them in the first place; tell me which conclusion you think makes for the more interesting and better-supported essay.
