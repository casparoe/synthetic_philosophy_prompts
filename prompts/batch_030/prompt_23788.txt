So somewhere along the way I turned into a genuine epiphenomenalist about qualia -- the view that phenomenal properties (the felt redness of red, the painfulness of pain, whatever you want to call it) are real, irreducible features of experience that get produced by brain states but don't themselves do any further causal work, not on behavior, not on anything else downstream. It grew on me because it seemed like the only position that lets you keep the causal closure of the physical AND take the hard problem seriously, without either denying qualia outright, Dennett-style, or rewriting physics to make room for two-way mental causation like an interactionist. My philosophy of mind professor mentioned in passing that this runs straight into something called "the paradox of phenomenal judgment," and when I went and looked it up I found Chalmers devotes a whole section of The Conscious Mind to it, plus a pile of replies on both sides (Shoemaker's review of the book, an older paper by Anthony Rudd, a recent Synthese piece arguing the paradox isn't even real) -- but none of them seem to agree on how bad it actually is, or whether its even the strongest thing going against the view.

Here's what I want from you: tell me what would actually count against my epiphenomenalism, and how badly. Give the paradox of phenomenal judgment its full due first -- spell out exactly what would have to be true of my own introspective reports for the objection to really bite, and whether there's a version of it an epiphenomenalist just cannot answer, as opposed to a cost they can absorb by biting a bullet. Then don't stop there: throw in whatever else belongs on the list (the worry about how natural selection could ever favor creatures whose qualia do nothing, the self-knowledge and reference problems, the parsimony argument that the view should just collapse into full-blown illusionism about consciousness) and rank the whole lot from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which of these a committed epiphenomenalist -- Frank Jackson circa "Epiphenomenal Qualia," not the guy who recanted later -- would actually grant as a real cost, versus which he'd just shrug off. And also: does the view predict anything that physicalism or interactionism doesn't, or is it observationally identical to its rivals from the outside -- in which case is there really anything left that could count against it, or has it just been defined into an unfalsifiable corner? And is that a special problem for epiphenomenalism, or just what you should expect from a metaphysical view at this level to begin with?
