Ok so I've been reporting on professional gamblers and quant traders who size their bets using the Kelly formula -- stake a fraction of your bankroll set by your edge over the odds, instead of just shoving everything onto any bet with positive expected value -- and I keep getting stuck on a philosophical knot underneath the finance-nerd argument. Ed Thorp used Kelly sizing to beat blackjack and then ran one of the first quant hedge funds the same way. Here's the puzzle: a straightforward expected-value maximizer, reinvesting every round, should go all-in on every positive-edge bet, since the all-in bet has higher expected value at every step than the smaller Kelly bet does -- but doing that means you're certain to eventually loose one of those bets, and the moment you do you're wiped out completely, no matter how good your edge was. Kelly bettors don't usually defend the smaller stake by saying "I'm risk-averse" -- they say you have to ask what happens to one bankroll over time, not what happens on average across a crowd of parallel yous. Paul Samuelson spent years attacking exactly that move (he once wrote an entire paper trashing it using only words of one syllable), insisting it's nothing but expected-utility maximization for someone who happens to have logarithmic utility. But the physicist Ole Peters says it's actually the ensemble-average side that needs justifying, not the time-average side, and a researcher named Scott Garrabrant has generalized the whole pattern into what he calls "geometric rationality," which he admits can conflict with the standard axioms of rational choice.

So what does Kelly betting, defended the way its actual practitioners defend it, strictly presuppose about rational choice under risk? Does it need Peters's or Garrabrant's story to make sense, or is Samuelson basically right that it's log utility wearing a long-run costume, in which case anyone without log utility is arguably betting wrong by doing it? Also: does betting "half Kelly" instead of full Kelly, purely to hedge against not being sure of your true edge, quietly smuggle ordinary risk-aversion back in and undercut the whole "this was never about risk attitudes" pitch? And if Samuelson is simply correct, does anything actually have to change about how these outfits place their bets, or is the whole dispute just different justifications for the same numbers?
