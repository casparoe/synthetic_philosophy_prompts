I'm part of a small reading group working through Quine this term, and we just spent a session on "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." When we reached the final section, where Quine describes the totality of our beliefs as a man-made fabric that touches experience only along the edges, with logic and mathematics sitting further toward the interior and therefore costlier — though never strictly impossible — to give up, our discussion leader reached for Neurath's image of sailors who must rebuild their ship plank by plank while still at sea, never able to dry-dock it and start over with a clean design. She used the boat to argue that Quine's holism really does dissolve the analytic/synthetic distinction rather than merely relocating it: since any plank, however central, can in principle be pried up and replaced given enough compensating repairs elsewhere, there is no privileged set of planks that count as analytic in the old sense of "true no matter what." One of the other regulars pushed back, insisting that the boat analogy smuggles the distinction right back in through the hull: a ship under repair still has to preserve something like its keel and overall shape at every stage, on pain of sinking outright, and that structural invariant is just analyticity wearing a disguise.

I'd like help thinking this through properly. First, lay out the mapping in the boat analogy as precisely as you can — a table of correspondences is fine — covering what plays the role of an individual plank, the keel or hull, the open sea, the shipwrights doing the repairs, the never-available dry dock, and a plank being thrown overboard for good. Then do the same, more briefly, for the rival "web of belief" image (periphery, interior, the tribunal of experience) that Quine invokes in the same section, and say whether it holds up better or worse under the same pressure. Finally, tell me where the boat analogy earns its keep for Quine's actual argument and where it either breaks down or quietly imports something he can't afford: does the ship's need for structural continuity really reintroduce a principled analytic core, or does that objection trade on a difference — between "revisable in principle" and "revisable without cost" — that Quine already has the resources to draw without giving up his holism? Either way, I want to know whether either image, once pushed on, still licenses the conclusion that there's no tenable line between analytic and synthetic statements, or whether the metaphors were doing more persuasive work than argumentative work all along.
