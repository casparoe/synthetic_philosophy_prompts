I spent my whole career as an evolutionary biologist, so game theory was never a stranger to me, just always the biologist's flavor of it - replicator dynamics, evolutionarily stable strategies - never the rational-choice, philosopher's flavor. Since retiring I've been reading around in decision theory for fun and fell down a rabbit hole on the centipede game and backward induction, partly from stumbling on that whole Aumann-Binmore back and forth from the 90s. So here's a question I want a straight answer to. Take the plain vanilla case: a finite number of rounds, common knowledge of exactly how many, no repeated play, no reptuation effects, both players fully rational and that fact itself common knowledge between them. The standard line is that backward induction gives the one and only rational solution, so both players take the small pot on move one and the whole thing unravels before it starts. I want your actual credence that this is right, not "it depends which solution concept you like" - a number. And I want it decomposed: how much rests on common knowledge of rationality even being a coherent thing to suppose, given the argument needs you to reason about what a rational player would do at a node which that very rationality should make unreachable in the first place; how much rests on backward induction being the correct solution concept at all, against some rival like forward induction or a resolute, decide-once-for-the-whole-game approach; how much rests on the finite, commonly known horizon doing all the real work, given cooperation turns out rational once the horizon is merely unknown or reputation effects are let back in; how much rests on "rational" even having a determinate meaning once you've built in unlimited common knowledge among idealized agents no real person could ever be; and anything else you think belongs on the list. Give each piece a rough number, show me how they combine into your overall figure, and tell me which piece that figure is most sensitive to. One thing though, just write it as plain paragraphs, like a letter - no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no little tables, I want to actually watch the pieces connect rather than have them handed to me pre-sorted into boxes.
