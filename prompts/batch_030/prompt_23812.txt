Write a ghazal of at least nine couplets on the hinge of Nietzsche's genealogy of morality: the moment one word does opposite work in two mouths. Let "good" be the radif, its qafia rhyme falling just before it each time, and keep every couplet as self-contained as the form demands. Some couplets should speak in the voice that names itself good first, out of sheer distance and self-delight, with "bad" trailing in afterward as a pale, unbothered afterthought; others should speak in the voice that must invent something to hate before it can call itself anything, so that its "good" exists only downstream of an "evil" soaked in a rancor the first voice never needed. Someone who knows the text should be able to sort the couplets by voice unprompted, and feel, in the closing couplet, that one voice could not exist without the other to resent. If the form's insistence on independent couplets seems to fight this argument rather than carry it, or fitting Nietzsche to a refrain feels like cleverness standing in for insight, say so rather than forcing a poem that only pretends to need this shape.
