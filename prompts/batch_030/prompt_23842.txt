ok so this has been living in my head: the "Battle of Wits" scene in The Princess Bride (Vizzini vs the Man in Black, the two goblets of wine, all the "inconceivable" business) gets trotted out constantly as a cute pop-culture example of game-theoretic reasoning, the "I know that you know that I know" stuff. I want you to actually take that seriously instead of just nodding along, using the real apparatus -- common knowledge in the Lewis/Aumann sense, common knowledge of rationality, the whole backward-induction mess that shows up in things like the centipede game.

quick recap in case the details are fuzzy: Westley claims to have poisoned one of two goblets while Vizzini's view was blocked. Vizzini then talks himself through a nested chain of "a clever man would put the poison in his own goblet, but he'd know I'd expect that, so--" all while secretly distracting Westley and swapping the goblets himself. He picks, they drink, he dies laughing, and then Westley reveals to Buttercup that it didnt matter at all -- both goblets were poisoned, since he'd spent years building up an immunity.

so here's what I actually want: is what Vizzini's doing in that monologue really reasoning under common knowledge in the technical sense, or is it just him privately running nested guesses about Westley's psychology that never actually become mutual knowledge, let alone common knowledge, between them (nothing is ever publicly observed or communicated, Westley just stands there)? does that distinction actually matter for whether Vizzini's argument is even valid on its own terms, seperately from the fact that the premise turns out false? also -- his chain of "he'd know that I'd know that he'd know" never bottoms out, he just arbitrarily stops and decides. is that the same kind of fragility people like Stalnaker pointed to in Aumann's common-knowledge-of-rationality argument for backward induction (fine in form, but needing some non-obvious stopping point, or breaking down off the equilibrium path)? or is that analogy a stretch?

and then there's the actual twist: Vizzini isn't just wrong about which cup is poisoned, he's playing a totally different game than the one he thinks he's in -- he assumes symmetric stakes (whoever picks wrong dies), when Westley actually has a dominant strategy no matter what, because of the immunity. is there something real here about how common-knowledge / rationality arguments presuppose common knowledge of the game itself, not just of each other's rationality, and this scene accidentally nails exactly how that assumption can fail? give me a reading that's honest about where the scene's comedy is doing genuine epistemic work and where it's just a fun "gotcha" that doesn't actually track the formalism -- don't just tell me it's a cute illustration of game theory, push on it.
