A friend of mine and I spent a long evening arguing about theories of causation and never quite settled anything. She holds that Woodward's interventionist account of causation — causal claims understood in terms of what would happen to an effect under a hypothetical, surgical intervention on the cause, with everything else held fixed — represents a genuine advance over Lewis's older counterfactual theory, since it replaces vague talk of "closest possible worlds" with a precise manipulationist semantics and thereby escapes the counterexamples that plagued Lewis's account. I argued that this overstates the contrast: on Woodward's own framing, interventionism is itself a species of counterfactual theory, so the real question is not whether it escapes the counterfactual framework but whether restricting attention to interventionist counterfactuals actually solves Lewis's problems rather than just relocating them.

Trying to settle the point, we turned to a concrete test: the standard "late preemption" scenario in which Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle. Suzy's rock arrives first and shatters it; Billy's, thrown with equal accuracy, would have shattered the bottle a moment later had Suzy not gotten there first, but instead sails through the space where the bottle used to be. We agree that Suzy's throw caused the shattering and Billy's did not, but neither of us could cleanly derive that verdict from our own preferred theory. On the Lewisian side, a simple chain of stepwise counterfactual dependence cannot be made to reach from Suzy's throw to the shattering at all, so something further is required. On the interventionist side, the natural test — would the shattering have differed had Suzy not thrown — has to be evaluated under some contingency about which other facts (notably, facts about Billy's rock) are held fixed, and it is unclear what licenses one choice of contingency over another without simply building in the answer we wanted in advance.

Could you write a dialogue between two equally capable characters — one committed to Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation, the other to Woodward's interventionist theory — who take up exactly this dispute? I would like them to start from our disagreement about whether interventionism is a genuinely distinct theory of causation or a refinement within the counterfactual family, then work through the rock-throwing case in enough technical detail to show precisely what each theory must add to get the intuitive verdict right, and to press each other on whether the needed addition is a principled development of the theory or an ad hoc patch designed after the fact to fit the example. Each character should take up the specific objections the other actually raises rather than simply restating a prepared position, and neither should have an easy time of it — I would like to come away genuinely unsure which of them has the better of the exchange.
