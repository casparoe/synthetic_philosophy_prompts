Before I look at the replies Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument has provoked, I want the requirements list drawn up independently of them. A condition is luminous, in Williamson's sense, when, whenever it obtains, one is thereby in a position to know that it obtains; the KK principle -- if one knows that P, one is in a position to know that one knows P -- is luminosity applied to the condition of knowing itself. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of introspective self-knowledge must meet if it is to adjudicate, rather than simply presuppose an answer to, whether luminosity and KK actually hold. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. At a minimum, an adequate account has to: respect the real asymmetry between the confident, non-inferential access we seem to have to our own current sensations and moods and the access we have to external, empirical matters, without inflating that asymmetry into infallibility; say what, if anything, is wrong with the sorites-flavored reasoning Williamson runs against the luminosity of feeling cold -- that a gradual warming from freezing to hot cannot contain a sharp instant at which one starts being in a position to know one no longer feels cold; take a stand on whether the margin-for-error condition that plausibly governs ordinary inexact knowledge (of a tree's height, say, or a crowd's size) also constrains self-knowledge, and say why self-knowledge is or is not exempt; keep the distinction between a state's merely obtaining and one's being in a position to know that it obtains from collapsing in either direction -- neither into a dogmatic infallibilism nor into a skepticism that denies any privileged access at all; and explain both why KK seems so intuitively compelling, and useful in formal epistemic logic, and why it might nonetheless fail. Flag explicitly any pair or larger subset of these requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which of them you would give up -- that tension, if genuine, is the result I am most interested in, since I take Williamson's argument to amount to showing that one such combination fails. Then, briefly, say how three contenders fare against the list: Williamson's own safety-theoretic view, on which luminosity and KK both fail for every non-trivial condition; a Cartesian view that preserves strong luminosity by denying that introspective judgments about current phenomenal states can ever be mistaken; and Shoemaker's constitutivist argument from the impossibility of self-blindness, which rejects the 'inner sense' model of introspection that Williamson's argument seems to presuppose.
