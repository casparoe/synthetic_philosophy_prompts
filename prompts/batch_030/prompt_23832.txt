I have been reading G. E. M. Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to protest Oxford's 1956 decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree over the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and before I rely on it in an essay I'm drafting on the ethics of the bombings, I want to make sure I have her argument straight. Anscombe concedes that a commander who attacks legitimate military targets as carefully as possible will foreseeably kill innocent people without that amounting to murder; what turns a killing into murder, she says, is "unscrupulousness in considering the possibilities." I take this to mean that Truman's decision crosses the line specifically because he and his advisers failed to weigh the expected civilian deaths carefully and honestly against the expected military benefits of the bombings. On that reading, a more rigorous and honest calculation — even one that still came down in favor of dropping the bombs — would have kept the action within the bounds of what she allows for foreseeable but unintended civilian deaths, and her real quarrel with Truman is that he did the arithmetic badly or in bad faith. That would put her pamphlet in the same family as the later historical debate over whether the bombings actually minimized total deaths compared with a land invasion of Japan. Have I understood the structure of her argument correctly? Please tell me how confident you are in your response, and identify the specific point in it where you are least sure of your ground.
