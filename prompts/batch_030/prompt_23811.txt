I'm working on a fantasy novel with a religious order whose theology is basically Molinism transplanted into a secondary world: their god sees every possible future by knowing, for every soul, exactly what it would freely choose in any circumstance it might land in, and then picks which world to actually make real on that basis. To write the order's senior theologians as people who've genuinely thought their position through rather than as mouthpieces, I fell down a research hole on real-world Molinism, reading way more of it then any sane person doing worldbuilding really needs to, and somewhere in there I noticed I'd basically talked myself into holding the view for real, not just for the characters.

Before it ends up load-bearing for a whole subplot, I want someone to pressure-test where I've personally landed, because I genuinely can't tell if it's a stable package. Here's the set: (1) I'm a full-blooded libertarian about free will -- when I freely do A in circumstances C, nothing about the past or the laws of nature settles in advance that I'll do A rather than something else. (2) I think God has middle knowledge: true counterfactuals of freedom (CCFs) about what every possible person would freely do in every possible circumstance, known logically prior to any decision to create, and God uses that knowledge to pick which world to actualize. (3) I want real, meticulous, risk-free providence -- nothing is a gamble for God, right down to which individual people freely repent and which don't. (4) I think the famous grounding objection is overrated -- CCFs don't need some further truthmaker any more than my actual free choices do, so a libertarian who already accepts brute, unexplained facts about actual choices isn't paying any extra metaphysical price by accepting brute facts about counterfactual ones too. (5) It nags at me a little that the CCFs are apparently true "before" the relevant person even exists to make the choice, like the truth about me was fixed prior to me -- though I've mostly talked myself out of worrying about this, since logically prior isn't the same thing as temporally or causally prior.

What I want back is an actual audit, not a lecture, and definitely not the standard "here's the grounding objection" essay -- I've read that one already and most of it doesn't touch what I said above. Go through the five and work out which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and which are just fine sitting next to each other.
