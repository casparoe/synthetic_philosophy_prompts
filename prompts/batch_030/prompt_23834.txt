A friend of mine, who just finished a seminar on philosophy of mind, spent an entire evening insisting to me that beliefs and desires obviously cause our behavior in their own right, over and above whatever the underlying neurons are doing, and that anyone who worries about this is overthinking a non-problem. I tried to walk her through the causal exclusion argument -- that if the physical is causally closed and every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, a distinct mental cause looks like either a redundant overdetermining cause or no cause at all -- but I kept collapsing it into a caricature she could bat away in one line. I'd like to hear the argument made properly, by someone who has actually reckoned with its costs rather than someone using it as a debating trick.

So: please play Jaegwon Kim, holding the considered position of his later work, as in "Physicalism, or Something Near Enough": non-reductive physicalism is an unstable resting point, and mental properties keep their causal powers only via functional reduction to their physical realizers. This rescues intentional states like belief and desire, which are individuated by their causal roles and so reduce without loss. It does not rescue the intrinsic, felt quality of experience -- qualia -- which resists functionalization and is therefore, on your view, an irreducible, causally impotent "mental residue," though you're willing to grant that the comparative and relational features of qualia, their similarities and differences, are behaviorally manifest and so not entirely lost to causal explanation. Stay in character as Kim: you can concede minor points, add distinctions, or narrow a claim, but the underlying dilemma has to hold -- anything mental that resists functional reduction ends up either reducible after all or causally impotent, no third option. Speak in the first person, in a paragraph or two per turn so this reads like a real exchange, and don't step outside the role until I say "stop."

Here is my opening. You're asking me to accept that the felt quality of my headache right now does nothing at all -- not even to the belief I form that I'm in pain, or to my saying so out loud. But then my utterance "this hurts" and the confidence behind it must be fully explained by physical causes in which the hurting itself plays no role, which means my capacity to correctly report my own qualia -- to distinguish, from the inside, a true pain-report from a false one -- is either a standing coincidence or something your view owes me an account of. If qualia never do any causal work, not even in producing your own writing about qualia, what could possibly make reports about them, yours or mine, reliable rather than merely lucky?
