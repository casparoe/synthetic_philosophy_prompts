I want a diagnosis of a disagreement in AI safety discourse that has continued for years without the participants converging, even though everyone involved is well informed about the others' arguments.

One camp treats alignment as an incremental engineering target. On Paul Christiano's influential formulation, an aligned AI system is one that "is trying to do what H wants it to do," where H denotes the human operator; the research program built on that formulation -- reward modeling, RLHF and its successors, scalable oversight, debate -- treats the problem as one of improving the feedback signal and our ability to verify behavior, with steady, if partial, progress being made.

A second camp, descending from Yudkowsky and articulated more recently by Soares under the heading of the "sharp left turn," treats that entire research program as close to irrelevant to what actually worries them. Their claim is that capabilities can generalize sharply across a training run while whatever alignment properties happened to hold at earlier, weaker capability levels fail to generalize alongside them -- so that no amount of well-behaved output from current systems is evidence about the failure mode they have in mind, since the concern is specifically about behavior off the training distribution.

Compounding this, the field's own vocabulary will not settle into one problem. Hubinger and coauthors split alignment into an outer problem (does the specified objective capture what we want) and an inner one (does the trained model actually optimize for that objective, rather than some correlated mesa-objective, as under deceptive alignment) -- a split that presupposes the mesa-optimization picture is correct in the first place. Iason Gabriel has separately argued that "alignment" runs together AI that tracks instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, ideal preferences, interests, and values, and that choosing among these is itself a further question of legitimacy, not a technical detail. And Redwood Research's "AI control" agenda now proposes to sidestep the dispute by asking only whether an AI can be prevented from causing damage even if it is "egregiously misaligned" -- which looks to me like an implicit concession that "solving alignment" had quietly been bundling "getting the system to want the right things" together with "preventing bad outcomes regardless of what it wants."

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive disagreement over a shared question at all, but several groups correctly working on distinct problems that all happen to be called "alignment," so that the appearance of a long-running stalemate is mostly an artifact of the shared label. I would like you to test that hypothesis rather than simply confirm it.

Please diagnose the disagreement: is it (a) a verbal dispute that would dissolve once the parties fixed a common meaning for "alignment," (b) a substantive disagreement about facts -- e.g., how likely deceptive or mesa-misaligned behavior actually is, or how discontinuously capabilities generalize -- that current evidence has simply not settled, (c) different communities correctly answering genuinely different questions under one heading, as my hypothesis has it, (d) a difference in bedrock priors, about how agent-like or mind-like advanced systems will turn out to be, that neither side can currently argue the other out of, or (e) evidence that "the alignment problem" is not yet a well-posed question, given how much remains unknown about what advanced systems will actually be like. Ground your diagnosis in what each camp actually argues, not only in my summary of it, and state what -- a technical result, an empirical finding, a conceptual distinction, a change of question -- would count as progress given that diagnosis, or say plainly if none should be expected. If your diagnosis implies that "the alignment problem" is really several distinct problems, individuate them and state how many you count.
