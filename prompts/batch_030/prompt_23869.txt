I've been testing my gut reactions to a run of cause-and-effect scenarios, and I want to know whether one principle explains them. Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle; Suzy's arrives first and shatters it, and Billy's equally accurate throw arrives a split second later, hitting nothing but flying glass. My verdict: Suzy's throw caused the shattering, even though the bottle would have shattered at virtually the same instant had she not thrown at all. While a desert traveler naps before a crossing, one enemy bores a small hole in his canteen; later, unaware of the hole, a second enemy poisons the water. It all drains out through the hole before he can drink, and he dies of thirst, untouched by the poison. My verdict: the hole-borer caused the death, not the poisoner. A boulder comes loose and rolls toward a hiker, who spots it, ducks, and survives. My verdict: the rolling caused the ducking and the ducking caused the survival, but the rolling itself did not cause the survival. Finally, I forget to water my office plant during a two-week trip, and it dies. My verdict: my not watering it caused its death -- yet I don't think every other person on Earth who also failed to water it that week is thereby a cause too, even though it's equally true of each of them that the plant would have lived had they watered it.

Is there one precisely stated principle of causation, or a short list of rival candidates, that captures all four verdicts, with each clause tied to the case that forces it? If none succeeds fully, tell me which verdict has to be given up, and why. Then apply whichever principle survives to a case I haven't judged: a train approaches a fork, and a signalman throws a switch, sending it down the left track rather than the right; the tracks reconverge further on, so the train reaches its destination at the same moment either way. Did throwing the switch cause the train's arrival?
