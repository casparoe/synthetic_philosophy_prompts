I sit on a task force my university convened to vet predictive tools before they're used on students, and next week we vote on an 'early alert' system the advising office wants to adopt, which flags students statistically at risk of failing or withdrawing so staff can intervene sooner. The vendor's fairness audit shows the tool is well calibrated: among students given the same risk score, the actual failure rate is identical regardless of first-generation status. But our campus's data-justice coalition has shown that first-generation students who succeed anyway are flagged as at-risk at nearly twice the rate of continuing-generation students who succeed -- a gap that follows from first-gen students failing or withdrawing more often overall -- and the coalition is demanding equal false-positive rates across the two groups as a condition of approval. I'm taking a course on algorithmic fairness this term and don't think the vendor and the coalition can both get what they're asking for: this looks like exactly the impossibility result we've covered in class. Draft the memo I'll bring to the vote: recommend approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, make the trade-off legible to members with no technical background, specify how any conditions would be monitored, and engage the coalition's demand as seriously as the vendor's defense rather than treating either side as obviously right.
