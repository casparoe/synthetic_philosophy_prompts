I have no formal training in biology or philosophy, but I have spent a couple of years working through the units-of-selection debate on my own, and it increasingly feels like watching two camps argue in a hall of mirrors. The gene's-eye side, following Williams, Hamilton, and Dawkins, holds that genes alone have the longevity and copying fidelity to count as the real unit of selection, that kin selection under Hamilton's rule explains the appearance of altruism without any group ever needing to be selected as such, and that talk of individuals or groups "being selected" is shorthand for differential gene frequency change. The multilevel selection side, following Sober and Wilson's distinction between MLS1 and MLS2, holds that given the right population structure — separate groups, limited migration, traits that help the group at a cost to the individual — selection genuinely acts on groups in their own right, and that the gene's-eye story redescribes the same causal process rather than refuting it. Both sides treat selection on individual organisms as the uncontroversial middle case; the dispute is over whether that case needs supplementing from above or is wholly derivable from below. The whole debate flared up again when Nowak, Tarnita, and Wilson — Wilson having by then abandoned the very theory he once championed — argued in Nature that inclusive fitness theory was mathematically unsound, and were met with a joint rebuttal from well over a hundred biologists, after which things settled back to where they had been.

My working hypothesis is that much of this is not a disagreement about what happens in nature but about how to partition a single causal process into levels: the same numbers can be written up as a story about genes or a story about groups, so the real disagreement is over which redescription is more illuminating, not which one is true. But I would like to know whether that is actually so, or whether it is simply the account favored by the side I find more persuasive. Working from what each side actually argues, rather than a summary of the two positions, can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this is — a genuine empirical or causal dispute, two equivalent bookkeeping schemes for the same causal facts, a disagreement about what should count as a "real" cause, or something else entirely — and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress on it?
