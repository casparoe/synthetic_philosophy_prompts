In the decision-theory unit of my economics elective, my teacher introduced the Ellsberg paradox to motivate why some theorists model beliefs using "imprecise probabilities" — ranges or sets of probabilities — rather than a single number. She used the classic two-urn illustration: Urn A holds exactly fifty red and fifty green balls, while Urn B holds a hundred red and green balls in a ratio nobody knows, and when people are offered a bet on either color, they reliably prefer to bet on Urn A no matter which color they pick, even though standard expected-utility theory says the unknown ratio shouldn't make a difference. She wrapped up the lesson by saying that this is basically the formal version of the old saying "better the devil you know than the devil you don't," and moved on to the next topic before I could ask what exactly she meant by that.

The proverb had already been rattling around in my head for a different reason: my uncle used the same line the week before to explain why he was turning down a job at a fast-growing startup to stay at his stable, unglamorous company, which felt like a much more personal, temperament-driven kind of reasoning than anything involving urns and unknown ratios. That contrast is what I'd like help with. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single verdict, the different precise claims "better the devil you know than the devil you don't" could actually be making — for instance, that it's just ordinary risk aversion dressed up in folksy language and has nothing to do with ambiguity as such; that it's tracking exactly the preference for known over unknown, imprecise probabilities that the Ellsberg paradox isolates; that it's a much stronger claim that one ought always to plan for the worst case whenever probabilities are imprecise; that it's a purely psychological remark about the comfort of familiarity with no rational force at all; or that it's simply a false generalization that the unfamiliar option is usually the worse one? For each reading, say whether it's a triviality, a substantive and defensible position, or a false claim, and tell me which reading, if any, actually backs up my teacher's use of the proverb and which backs up my uncle's, since I suspect they're not the same one. I gather that working decision theorists still disagree about some of this, including about whether it is even rational to treat known risk and unknown or ambiguous probabilities differently in the first place, so please keep what's genuinely settled separate from where you're taking a side that not everyone in the field would grant you.
