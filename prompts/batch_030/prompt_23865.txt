In my school's philosophy club we've been discussing the philosophy of time, and I recently read Kurt Gödel's 1949 essay written for a volume honoring Einstein, where he describes a rotating-universe solution to the equations of general relativity containing closed timelike curves -- paths that would let a traveler return to a point in their own past. Gödel argues that even if our own universe isn't rotating like this, the mere fact that such universes are compatible with the laws of nature already undermines the idea of objective temporal passage in any universe, including possibly ours, since it would be philosophically unsatisfactory for so basic a metaphysical fact to depend on the contingent arrangement and motion of matter. This claim has been heavily criticized, typically by objecting that we simply do not seem to inhabit a rotating, time-travel-permitting universe, so its mere possibility elsewhere shouldn't unsettle our confidence that time genuinely passes here. I'd like you to construct the strongest possible defense of Gödel's claim against exactly this objection.
