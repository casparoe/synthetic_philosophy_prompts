I'm writing my quals essay on Dennett's "Quining Qualia" and keep getting stuck on the Chase and Sanborn bit. Quick version: Chase says the coffee tastes exactly like it used to, he just doesn't enjoy it anymore (stable qualia, shifted standards); Sanborn says his standards never budged, it's the taste itself that changed for him (shifted qualia, stable standards). Dennett's move is that no test -- behavioral, physiological, or even their own most careful introspection -- can tell these two stories apart even in principle, and he wants that to show there's no fact of the matter rather than just an unknown one. Everyone's stock objection is that undetectable-in-principle isn't the same as nonexistent, so he owes us a bridge premise from the epistemology to the metaphysics that he never quite states outright. Can you actually supply that premise and defend it -- not just a blanket verificationism that would prove way too much elsewhere, but the specific reason this case (unlike, say, the number of hairs on Caesar's head at death, which we just cant access) earns the stronger, eliminativist conclusion? Then tell me the worst bullet that defense has to bite.
