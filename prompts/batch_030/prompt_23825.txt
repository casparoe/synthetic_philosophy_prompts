I'm an economics PhD student working on choice under uncertainty. Whenever I ask whether Ellsberg's urn experiments really force us to abandon a single subjective probability distribution, my advisor waves the question away with 'better the devil you know than the devil you don't,' and takes this to settle that ambiguity aversion is nothing but risk aversion, or at most a rational hedge against an adversarial urn-setter. Neither is adequate: the textbook pattern of preferring the known 50/50 urn on bets for either color can't be rationalized by any single probability for the unknown urn, whatever the bettor's risk attitude, and the same people happily bet on ambiguous propositions when it's their own judgment, rather than a stranger's urn, that's in question. Lay out the distinct precise claims 'better the devil you know' could be making about this choice, from platitudes compatible with ordinary expected utility, through defensible departures from a single prior, to claims the experimental record actually contradicts. Tell me which of these she's probably running together, and which reading, if any, would actually let her set the puzzle aside rather than answer it.
