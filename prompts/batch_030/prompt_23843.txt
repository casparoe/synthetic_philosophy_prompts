My game theory reading group did Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" theorem last week and someone turned it into an arguement I can't shake, since the conclusion seems obviously false. (1) Rational agents are Bayesians: they only shift credence by conditioning on evidence. (2) Two rational agents start out with the same prior over the possible states of the world (the common prior assumption), since nothing but evidence should be able to make two correct reasoners differ -- so the only way their posteriors can end up different later is if they've each received different evidence. (3) Aumann's theorem: if it's common knowledge between them what each one's posterior on some event is, those posteriors must be equal. (4) Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis showed you don't need to actually share your evidence to reach common knowledge -- honestly trading your current posterior back and forth is enough, since each announcement is itself evidence, and in a finite state space this converges to common knowledge (hence agreement) in finitely many rounds. (5) So two honest, rational Bayesians with a common prior can't talk about something for long and still end up stably, knowingly disagreeing about it. (6) But obviously there are tons of smart, sincere, informed people who've argued something -- ethics, politics, the very same pile of evidence -- for years without converging. (7) So either none of those are really two honest rational Bayesians talking to each other (always some lying, hidden evidence, or bad reasoning going on), or one of (1), (2), or (4) is false.

Biting the first half of (7) feels nuts -- it means every long, sincere disagreement you've had with a smart friend is secretly proof someone was lying or inept. But (3) and (4) are real theorems, not vibes, so which of (1), (2), or (4) would you reject, and what does it actually cost us? I've got a hunch it's (2) but don't honestly know the bill, so talk me out of it if I'm wrong.
