I'm in a philosophy of technology seminar and our professor mentioned in passing that John von Neumann is one of the acknowledged partial models for Peter Sellers' character in Dr. Strangelove -- the wheelchair, the Manhattan Project pedigree, the Mitteleuropean accent all get pointed to, though everyone agrees he's not the primary influence -- that's usually pinned on Kahn or von Braun instead. That sent me off to actually read von Neumann's own 1955 Fortune essay, "Can We Survive Technology?", and his actual argument in it is alot more sober than the movie character would suggest: his point is basically that the postwar crisis isn't about any one scary technology, it's that we've run out of the geographic and political "room" that used to absorb the shocks of industrial change, that you can't inhibit just the "bad" technologies without inhibiting basically everything since they're too tangled together, and his actual prescription at the end is just "patience, flexibility, intelligence" -- not a recipe, just those three qualities, on the theory that they've gotten humanity through comparable messes before.

What I'd love from you is a reading of Dr. Strangelove against that actual essay, not against the pop-culture caricature of von Neumann as a bomb-loving mad genius. Where does the film's satire -- the War Room, the fail-safe apparatus, the Doomsday Machine and Strangelove's own point that it's worthless if kept secret, the gleefully horny mineshaft ending -- actually land a hit on the substance of what von Neumann argued, and where is it really just going after Kahn, or Teller, or the general culture of RAND-style strategic thinking instead? I'm especially curious whether the film, maybe by accident, dramatizes a real soft spot in von Neumann's own answer: his "we'll manage it on patience and judgment" line quietly assumes human judgment stays in the driver's seat, and you could read the movie as one long joke about formal, game-theoretic rationality -- the exact tradition von Neumann himself helped found -- shoving human judgment out of the room before anyone notices. Does the film actually know it's making that argument, or would I be the one making it up and pinning it on the film? Walk through specific scenes rather than staying at the level of generalities, and end by telling me how confident you are in the reading overall, plus which single claim in it you think is the shakiest or most contestable.
