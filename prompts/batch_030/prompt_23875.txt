For a graduate proseminar on philosophy of mind, we read Norman Malcolm's Dreaming back to back with Dennett's 1976 reply, "Are Dreams Experiences?," and most of my classmates came away with a flattened version of the disagreement — "Malcolm thinks dreams aren't real, Dennett thinks they are" — that doesn't survive a careful reading of either text. Malcolm's actual argument is narrower and stranger than that. Because a sleeping person cannot check, judge, or report anything while still asleep, he holds that there is no criterion, beyond the waking impression, for the content, duration, or even the bare occurrence of a dream during sleep; from this he concludes that treating a dream as a time-extended experience happening during sleep is a grammatical confusion rather than an empirical hypothesis we simply lack the evidence to confirm. He also runs a second, separate line: that "sound asleep" and "in some state of consciousness" are logically exclusive by ordinary usage, so the received view is self-contradictory on its face, quite apart from the verification point.

Dennett doesn't just reassert the received view against this. He grants that the naive picture may well be wrong about the timing, and to make that concession vivid he leans on the nineteenth-century researcher Alfred Maury's old report of dreaming an elaborately building narrative about the French Revolution that ends with his own execution by guillotine — only to wake and find that the headboard of his bed had fallen across his neck, the kind of case people have used to suggest the whole dream might have been composed in an instant at the moment of waking rather than lived through in real time. Dennett turns this into his "cassette theory": dreaming, on this proposal, might be nothing but an unconscious composition-and-loading process, with the only conscious episode being the replay on waking. But he also concedes, quite explicitly, that no dream report — including the dreamer's own, then or later — could in principle distinguish his theory from the received view it was meant to rival.

That concession is what I'd like a dialogue to dig into, because it looks to me as though Dennett has quietly bought back, as an empirical hypothesis, exactly what he refused Malcolm as a piece of grammar. Write an imagined face-to-face conversation between Malcolm and Dennett, set not long after Dennett's paper appeared, in which Malcolm presses him on precisely this: if Dennett's own alternative to the received view is admittedly unverifiable from the inside, hasn't he granted the substance of Malcolm's argument while merely relabeling it? I want Dennett's real best answer to that specific challenge, not a general defense of the cassette theory, and I want Malcolm to keep pressing rather than let a distinction that doesn't do the needed work end the exchange there. Somewhere in the conversation, have Dennett go on the offensive too, pressing Malcolm on whether his "sound asleep excludes consciousness" premise is doing any argumentative work beyond stipulating the answer he wants. Neither of them should concede more than the argument actually forces, and I'd rather see their strongest moves against each other's positive proposals than a rehash of objections to the naive view they both already reject. I'd like it to end with the two of them agreeing on what, precisely, is still in dispute between them, if anything.
