I'm a retired experimental physicist -- condensed matter, mostly low-temperature stuff -- and somewhere along the way I built more feedback control loops than I care to count, just to keep cryostats and lock-in amplifiers behaving themselves. Since retiring I've finally had time to get properly into philosophy of mind, and I've gotten talked into computationalism: the view that cognition really is computation in some substantive sense, not just "everything can be described computationally if you squint hard enough." I already like the view, which is exactly why I want you to try to take it apart rather than defend it.

So: what would actually count as evidence against computationalism, ranked from the stuff that would really sink it down to the stuff that's merely awkward? Here's where I keep getting stuck, so engage with these directly rather than staying general. First, theres Putnam's old result (and Searle's crack about his own wall implementing WordStar) that on a naive mapping account of implementation, more or less any physical system implements more or less any finite automaton -- which threatens to make "the brain computes X" true of rocks too, and hence empty. Does Chalmers' fix, requiring the mapping to respect the right counterfactuals rather than just the actual sequence of states, rescue a real claim here, or does it just push the problem back a step? Second, there's Maudlin's Klara/Olympia pair, where a machine that's just replaying a recorded sequence of states is supposed, on pain of denying supervenience on physical activity, to be exactly as conscious as the machine that "really" computed that sequence live -- if our gut says Olympia obviously isn't conscious, is that telling against computationalism, or just against a sloppy version of it? Third, van Gelder's point about the Watt governor, which hits close to home given how many control loops I've built myself, that some regulatory processes are better modeled as continuous dynamical systems than as computations over discrete representations at all -- is that a real rival, something that actually predicts something different, or just a dispute about vocabulary given that dynamical systems can themselves be simulated computationally?

And the question underneath all of it: computationalism these days comes with so many riders -- doesn't have to be digital, doesn't have to be classical or symbolic, connectionist and predictive-processing versions all count too -- that I wonder if its been stretched until nothing could actually count against it anymore. Is that true, and if so, is it a real problem, or is "cognition is computation of some kind" allowed to be that unconstrained, the same way physics isn't hurt by "physical systems obey some dynamical law" being unfalsifiable? Be honest with me too about which of the three things above a computationalist whose work you respect would actually concede has some real force, versus which they'd wave off as attacking a version of the view nobody holds anymore. And for what it's worth, personally a clean empirical result would move me a lot more than another thought experiment, so if one of these actually cashes out that way, don't bury it at the bottom of the list.
