I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a piece about paradoxes of self-knowledge, and the one I keep coming back to is the surprise examination paradox (also known as the unexpected hanging): a teacher announces that an exam will fall on exactly one day of the coming week and that, on the morning it happens, the class won't yet be able to say for certain it's that day — and the class seems to reason validly, step by step from Friday back to Monday, that no day can possibly satisfy this, and yet a teacher who simply gives the test on, say, Wednesday appears to surprise everyone without any trouble. I first met this puzzle as an undergraduate and assumed it was dispatched easily enough by saying you can't trust a five-step chain of inductive reasoning; digging back into the literature for this piece, I've found that logicians who all agree the conclusion is absurd disagree sharply, and have disagreed since the 1950s, about which step in the argument actually fails, or whether any single step is even the right place to look.

Rather than write another explainer that states the puzzle and shrugs, I'd like an interactive branching quiz for the web version of the piece, aimed at readers who are curious and patient but have no training in logic or philosophy. It should start from someone who agrees the class's reasoning looks valid at every step and yet is sure the conclusion is false, then walk them through a short sequence of concrete questions about their own diagnosis of the argument, each offering two or three concrete options, until they land on a named position in the literature — so they discover which solution they actually hold rather than picking a label they recognize. Build it as a decision tree of somewhere between four and seven branch questions, each phrased so a reader could answer it without already knowing what it leads to (no question should just ask "do you think this is a genuine paradox?"). I'd like the leaves to include at least: Quine's diagnosis, Kaplan and Montague's verdict that the announcement is a genuine self-referential paradox in the family of the Liar, Sorensen's blindspot solution, Williamson's margin-for-error account and what it does to the KK principle, and at least one solution that locates the trouble in the two-agent, public-announcement structure of the scenario rather than in a single reasoner's private deductions. Work out the logical space first — what are the possible places to lay blame, and which combinations of answers to your questions are actually available — before you hang names on the leaves; note any combination nobody in the literature seems to occupy and say briefly why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored), and flag any two leaves your tree can't cleanly keep apart.

Because I'll be presenting this quiz to readers as a fair map of a real disagreement, tell me at the end, in a few sentences, how confident you are in the tree as a whole, and single out the one branch question you're least sure actually forces the split you're using it for — the one where a sharp defender of two different camps might honestly give the same answer.
