I'm a research engineer on a brain-computer interface team that implants microelectrode arrays in the motor cortex of people paralyzed by ALS or brainstem stroke. Until recently our systems decoded attempted speech: the residual neural activity produced when someone physically tries, and fails, to speak. This year we moved to decoding inner speech directly -- sentences a person merely imagines saying, with no attempt at articulation, transcribed straight into text. It works well enough that we had to add a deliberate "unlock" step, because the decoder kept transcribing interior monologue participants had never intended to share. This has sent me back to Wittgenstein's private language argument, which I filed away as a student as an ingenious but purely academic argument about sensations and beetles in boxes. It doesn't feel academic anymore.

I'd like your best reconstruction -- argued from the actual moves Wittgenstein makes in Philosophical Investigations §243-315, not a popularized gloss on them -- of what he would say about what we're doing. A few places I want you to actually get into, rather than wave at:

1. In the diary case (§258), the trouble is supposed to be that the private linguist has no criterion of correctness for "S," so that, in Wittgenstein's words, "whatever is going to seem right to me is right." Does a neural decoder supply exactly the missing criterion, turning what looked like a private avowal into a public, checkable practice -- or does it just add one more symptom that can itself diverge from what the person "really" meant, leaving Wittgenstein's point completely untouched?

2. When a participant protests that the decoded sentence isn't what they meant to think, is that the genuine is-right/seems-right dispute Wittgenstein says the private linguist can never have with himself -- meaning something philosophically important has actually changed -- or is it just an engineering disagreement that, on his view, has nothing to do with the private language argument at all?

3. Apply the beetle-in-the-box passage (§293) directly. Whatever is in the box is supposed to drop out as irrelevant to what the word means, because no one can check anyone else's box against their own. Our decoder is a way of checking the box from outside. Does that show these contents were never private in Wittgenstein's sense to begin with, so that none of this troubles him -- or is there something in his view that still resists calling the decoder's output "the sentence the person was thinking," prior to any decoding at all?

4. Wittgenstein insists he is not a behaviorist (§304) and treats the grammar of "criteria" versus "symptoms" as unstable rather than fixed (§354). Given that instability, is there a principled Wittgensteinian answer to whether a decoder's output should count as a criterion or a mere symptom of what someone is thinking -- or would he say that this is exactly the kind of question his later philosophy is meant to dissolve rather than answer?

I'm not asking for a survey of what commentators have said about him; I want the position worked out on its own merits, including anywhere you think it's genuinely indeterminate what he would say, and why.
