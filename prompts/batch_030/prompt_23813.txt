I am at work on a novel that follows three generations of a family who founded, and still direct, a walled refuge that offers the people who come to it relief from having to decide things for themselves — what to believe, whom to marry, how to raise a child — in exchange for their obedience. Each generation must justify the arrangement on its own terms, and my drafts keep giving them the same rationale in different costumes. The novel is built as a long argument with Dostoevsky's "The Grand Inquisitor," so what I need is a map of the logical space that poem actually opens up, rather than the familiar two-sided summary of it as "freedom versus happiness."

Please lay out a taxonomy, as a branching tree, of the positions available on the Inquisitor's indictment of Christ. Label each branch with the specific claim that divides the positions beneath it, phrased so a character could affirm or deny it without already knowing which side of the argument that answer places them on — not "paternalist" versus "libertarian" as free-floating tags. Build the tree from the poem's own materials: the claim that only "miracle, mystery, and authority" can hold the conscience of the weak; the split between "the elect," who can bear a freely chosen faith, and the mass who cannot; and the Inquisitor's account of his own order as the "hundred thousand martyrs" who take on the "curse of knowledge of good and evil" so everyone else can be happy.

At minimum the tree must separate: someone who accepts the Inquisitor's diagnosis of ordinary human weakness but rejects his remedy as a lie regardless of its comforts; someone who accepts both diagnosis and remedy, since a happiness produced by administered miracle is no less real for being administered; someone who denies the diagnosis, treating the burden of freedom as unevenly distributed rather than universal; and someone for whom the Inquisitor's self-portrait as a martyr is not fully credible, even to him. Aim for six to eight leaves in total — fewer, and I suspect real disagreements are being merged; more, and my readers won't be able to tell characters apart. For each leaf, give a working name (an existing philosophical or theological label if one genuinely fits, otherwise say none does), the commitment that makes the position hardest to hold without hypocrisy, and how a character holding it would respond to Christ's silent kiss at the poem's close, the one moment every position must answer without the cover of argument. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly keep apart, and say why.
