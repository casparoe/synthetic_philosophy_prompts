I've been down a rabbit hole on Ian Hacking's 'looping effects' idea -- classifying someone with a mental disorder can change how they see themselves, which changes what the category covers, and round it goes (his favorite case is multiple personality diagnoses exploding once the category, the talk shows, and the specialist clinics all existed together). Before I read anymore takes on this, lay out the requirements properly: what would a classification of mental disorders actually have to get right to count as more than a glorified billing-code list -- something defensible as tracking real kinds, not just handy clinical bookkeeping? Give me the desiderata -- the cases it has to handle (comorbidity being the norm not the exception, categories that boomed and went bust like hysteria and multiple personality, categories defined out of existence like homosexuality was, symptom clusters that shift across cultures and decades), the distinctions it can't blur (reliability vs validity vs mere clinical usefulness, a kind that just sits there vs one that loops back on the people in it), and any established findings it can't contradict. Give the case behind each requirement, say which are genuinely non-negotiable versus just what one camp wants true, and flag it if any two requirements can't both be satisfied -- that's the bit I'm most curious about. Also say which single requirement does the most damage to the most popular approaches. Briefly scoring the DSM's approach since 1980 and something like RDoC against the list is fine too, but that part's optional.
