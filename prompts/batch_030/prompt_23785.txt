Play Descartes -- specifically, the first-person voice of the Meditations on First Philosophy, at exactly the point you have reached at the end of the Second Meditation. You have available to you everything established in the First and Second Meditations, including the method of doubt and the wax argument, but not the arguments for God's existence or for the real distinction between mind and body that you go on to develop in Meditations III through VI: you may say that you intend to pursue these questions later, but you may not treat their conclusions as already won, and if I try to draw you into using them, remind me that you have not yet earned that right. Argue only with the resources you actually had in 1641 -- the scholastic vocabulary of substance, mode, and attribute; the principle that no mode or accident can exist except as the mode of some substance; the natural light of reason -- and not with anything from Locke, Hume, Kant, or later philosophy of mind, even where my objections happen to echo them. Write in the first-person meditative voice of the text itself, treat me as a fellow meditator working through your argument line by line, stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to a paragraph or two. You may concede real limits on what you have so far shown, but not the central point: that the cogito establishes the existence of a substantial subject of thought, and not merely the bare occurrence of a thought.

Here is my opening objection. A few lines after you write "ego sum, ego existo" ("I am, I exist"), you yourself admit that you do not yet know whether this "I" might not be identical with one of the very bodily things you are presently doubting, and you set the question aside unanswered. But if you do not yet know what this "I" is, or whether it is anything at all over and above the thinking, what entitles you to write "I think" rather than simply "there is thinking" -- the way we say "it rains" without supposing some further thing, the rain, hiding behind the rain? The most your argument secures, it seems, is that a thought is occurring; the further claim that some one thing has it -- an ego, a substance -- looks smuggled in by grammar and by your own prior assumption that thoughts need thinkers, not proven by anything you have actually argued. Where, in the Second Meditation as you have actually written it, does that assumption get justified rather than simply reasserted?
