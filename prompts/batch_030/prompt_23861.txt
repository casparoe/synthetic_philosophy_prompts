Analyze the UK's 2016 referendum on EU membership as a test case for the boundary problem in democratic theory: who should have composed the demos for a decision of this kind? The referendum used the ordinary parliamentary franchise -- British, Irish, and qualifying Commonwealth citizens aged 18 and over resident in the UK, plus British citizens who had lived abroad for under fifteen years. Other long-term resident EU nationals could not vote, nor could sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds, though both groups could vote in Scotland's 2014 independence referendum. Gibraltar alone among Britain's overseas territories took part, because it alone lay inside the EU. Apply the all-affected-interests principle and the all-subjected (or coercion) principle in turn: say concretely who each would enfranchise, and where each diverges from the rules actually used and from the other. Then give an honest verdict: does either principle deliver a non-arbitrary answer here, or does the case instead confirm that any procedure for fixing the boundary already presupposes an answer to who belongs -- a circularity no actual electoral design can escape?
