I have a friend who has spent the last few years immersed in the wild-animal-welfare literature — the strand of animal ethics concerned with whether and how humans should intervene in nature to reduce suffering, rather than only in farming or research settings. Over time I've watched her react to a series of real and proposed interventions, and her verdicts have been consistent in some ways and puzzling in others:

1. She strongly approved of the decades-long oral-bait rabies vaccination campaigns that eliminated fox rabies across most of Western Europe, calling it one of the clearest wins available in this whole area.
2. She opposed a regional cull of invasive grey squirrels undertaken to protect native red squirrels from squirrelpox, a virus greys mostly carry without symptoms but which is often fatal to reds. She agreed the cull would probably help the red squirrel population, but said that doesn't settle it: the grey squirrels being killed still matters, and a probable benefit to a different population doesn't simply cancel that out.
3. She opposed supplemental winter-feeding stations for an overabundant deer herd, on the grounds that feeding predictably lets the population overshoot its food supply and sets up a larger, slower die-off from starvation within a few winters, even though the feeding itself harms no one at the time.
4. She supported a gene-drive program to suppress a mosquito population, on the grounds that it pre-empts enormous numbers of short lives that mostly end in starvation, predation, or disease within days of hatching anyway.
5. She opposed lethal wolf culls proposed to protect livestock and deer, on the grounds that removing wolves mostly substitutes death by starvation or disease for death by predation — what wildlife biologists call compensatory mortality — rather than reducing how much suffering actually occurs, so the wolves would be killed for no real gain.
6. She supported immunocontraceptive vaccine programs of the kind already used on some deer herds, as a way of controlling population growth that neither kills anyone now nor sets up a worse crash later.

Here is a new case she hasn't yet responded to. A small, high-fenced deer research herd — fully enclosed, with no contact with wild deer and no risk of the disease escaping the enclosure or of new cases entering it — has just had every one of its animals test positive for chronic wasting disease: the prion disease, invariably fatal and with no vaccine or treatment, that kills over many months through progressive neurological wasting. Every animal in the enclosure will die of it regardless of what anyone does; the only question is whether that happens gradually, over the next year or two, or all at once, quickly and painlessly, this week. There is no wider ecosystem at stake and no real uncertainty about how things play out either way.

Do you think she would support culling the herd? I'm less interested in the answer itself than in reconstructing the rule she seems to be running: I suspect at least two distinct principles fit everything above equally well, so please lay out the candidates, say what each implies for the new case, and give me your best guess together with your reasons for preferring it. Please also name a further case — real or hypothetical — that would separate the candidates more cleanly than this one might. Finally, tell me what would most change your mind: which fact about the case, or which step in your own reasoning, you are least confident of, such that if it came out otherwise you would expect the opposite verdict from her.
