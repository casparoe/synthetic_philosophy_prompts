I built a defense of indirect utilitarianism against Williams's integrity objection for my school's ethics bowl team, and now I think it backfires. (1) Williams says it's "one thought too many" for a husband to save his drowning wife via the thought that it's permissible, rather than simply that she's his wife -- needing the permission shows he's alienated from the source of his own action. (2) An indirect utilitarian can grant this: the criterion of rightness needn't double as anyone's decision procedure, so the theory can just sanction a standing disposition to love one's spouse, leaving the husband's actual thought simply "she's my wife." (3) But that disposition is sanctioned only as long as it maximizes utility, and must yield once the sums change. (4) So the permission hasn't been removed by going indirect, only relocated to the disposition's standing rationale -- the alienation moved up a level. (5) But every disposition any theory could ever recommend has some further rationale explaining why it's good, so by the same reasoning no one's ground projects, under any theory, could ever be genuinely their own. (6) That can't be right: it would make Williams's own standard for integrity unsatisfiable by anyone, which guts the objection instead of vindicating it. Which step would you reject, and why did it look fine at first? I'd like to keep (1) if possible -- tell me what that costs.
