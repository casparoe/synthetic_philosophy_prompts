I'm a novelist, and I keep running into a line that people deploy as though it settles a question rather than merely naming one: Coleridge's "willing suspension of disbelief." It came up most recently at my critique group, after three readers told me, independently, that they had cried at the scene where my protagonist's brother dies -- a death I built, timed, and rewrote across eleven drafts, and which each of them would tell you, if asked directly, never happened to anyone. What unsettled me more is that it happened to me too: proofreading the chapter months later, thinking about nothing but commas, I felt the same pull. When I said this seemed strange -- that grief this specific could attach to a fact everyone in the room, including its author, knew to be invented -- the most experienced writer among us cited the suspension of disbelief as though that closed the matter.

I don't think it does, and I'd like your help seeing why -- or seeing that it does after all. What are the distinct, precise claims "the willing suspension of disbelief" could actually be making about what happens when someone responds to a scene they know to be invented, laid out from the most trivial to the most radical? I can imagine candidates at both ends: that the phrase names nothing more than a fact about behavior -- we don't act on our disbelief, we don't call for help or set the book down in protest -- and, at the other extreme, that it names a real if momentary reversal of belief, so that for the length of the scene the death is actually taken to be true and then un-taken. What I can't tell is whether either survives scrutiny, whether there's a defensible position between them, or whether "suspension" and "disbelief" are even the right words for whatever is actually going on. For each reading you can distinguish, say whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive claim that's actually defensible, or a claim that falls apart once you examine it closely -- and in particular, say whether it's even coherent to knowingly hold a belief and suspend it at the same time. Then tell me which reading, if any, would genuinely explain what happened in my critique group and at my own desk, and which readings only rename the mystery while sounding like they've solved it. Set this out as a list of readings rather than an argument for a single one, the way I mean to bring it back to the group the next time someone reaches for that phrase as though it ended a conversation instead of starting one.
