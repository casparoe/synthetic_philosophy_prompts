I teach a graduate seminar on theories of causation, and I'm setting the single essay question for this term's take-home final (a 72-hour window, student answers capped at 2,500 words). Every year a good fraction of students describe Woodward's interventionist theory as though it simply swapped out Lewis's counterfactuals for something else -- "manipulability" instead of "dependence" -- and treat the two views as rivals in the way the counterfactual theory and a causal-process theory are rivals. Half of them have clearly gotten this from skimming the SEP entry on causation and manipulability without reading past the introduction. I want a question this year that makes that slogan impossible to lean on: it has to force students to say exactly what Woodward keeps from Lewis's semantics for counterfactuals and what he changes -- surgical intervention on a structural equation, versus overall similarity of worlds -- to say whether his theory is even meant to be reductive, and then to use the standard case of late preemption (Billy and Suzy each throw a rock at a bottle, and Suzy's gets there first) to show that sharing a counterfactual pedigree doesn't stop the two traditions from handling the case differently, and at different costs.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of around 650 words that hits everything a strong answer needs to hit, and a marking scheme built around the specific moves that separate an A-range answer from a merely competent B-range one. In particular, I'd like the scheme to state explicitly: whether the student correctly explains why Lewis's original 1973 account (causation as the ancestral of counterfactual dependence) copes with early preemption but not late preemption; whether they can explain how the structural-equations/interventionist tradition tries to recover the right verdict for late preemption by holding some variables fixed at non-actual values while testing others; whether they notice that this move, left unsupplemented, also certifies the preempted backup (Billy's throw) as a cause, which is the wrong result; and whether they can say anything about what blocking that over-generation costs a theory that has already conceded it isn't reductive. For each of these points, please also flag the specific wrong turn a weaker answer tends to take -- for instance, students who think the two theories differ over whether counterfactuals are involved at all, rather than over which counterfactuals and what grounds them. No need for a reading list -- I'll add my own references to Lewis, Woodward, Hitchcock, and Halpern and Pearl afterward.
