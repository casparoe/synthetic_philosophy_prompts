My friend who's really into AI-safety/longtermist stuff keeps bringing up the Doomsday Argument at dinner and it's honestly starting to get to me. The basic move: treat your own birth rank among all humans who will ever live as though it's a random sample, run the Bayesian math, and you get a real update toward the species ending sooner rather than later, purely from the fact that you exist now rather then at some other point. I went and read around it a bit -- Carter apparently floated the idea in a lecture in the early 80s, Gott published his own version in Nature using the Copernican principle, Leslie wrote a whole monograph defending it, and Bostrom is the one who tried to actually formalize the reasoning (calling it the self-sampling assumption, SSA) and who spent a lot of time on what he named the reference class problem.

That's the part that's been bugging me: nobody in this debate seems to disagree about the arithmetic once a reference class of observers is fixed. All the real disagreement is over which class is even the right one -- all humans ever, humans since agriculture, all observers of any kind, observer-moments rather than whole observers -- and different, individually reasonable choices spit out wildly different, sometimes absurd conclusions. The main rival camp uses a self-indication assumption (SIA) instead, weighting hypotheses by how many observers they predict, which cancels the doomsday update but generates its own absurdity, the "presumptuous philosopher": two physics theories equally supported by actual evidence, and you're apparently rationally required to bet on whichever one implies vastly more observers exist, purely because you exist at all. Forty-plus years on from Carter's original lecture, none of it has converged an inch.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really? A real fact about which reference class is privileged that's just genuinely hard to pin down, a technical gap in the probability theory that more careful formal work could close, a sign that self-locating probability stops being well-defined once you leave toy cases with clean bounded populations, or the SSA and SIA camps quietly answering two different questions while using the same vocabulary? Please lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence, no preamble and no restating my question back to me, and then back it up with what each side is actually committed to, and tell me straight whether anything -- a proof, a new thought experiment, a reformulated question -- could break the stalemate, or if this is just permanent.
