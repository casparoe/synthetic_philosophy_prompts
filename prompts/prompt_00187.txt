I spent much of last night arguing with a friend (who works in cognitive science, so I assume she knows what she's talking about) about the split-brain literature, and I'm still stuck on what seems like the obvious moral upshot. Her position was that once the corpus callosum is severed, the patient simply becomes two separate people sharing one skull and one body, each with its own continuous stream of consciousness running in parallel at all times, and that the famous experiments (Sperry, Gazzaniga, etc.) prove this conclusively. If that's right, it seems to raise a genuinely new problem for personal identity and responsibility: if a split-brain patient's left hand (controlled by the "silent" right hemisphere) does something harmful while the talking left hemisphere disavows any knowledge of it, whom exactly do we hold responsible, given that there are now two distinct subjects of experience coexisting in one body full-time?

What I want to understand is how philosophers of mind actually parcel out responsibility in cases like this, given that the disunity of consciousness has been experimentally demonstrated to be total and permanent rather than confined to specific test conditions. Is there a standard view on how we should individuate the two agents for purposes of blame or punishment, and has anyone proposed a legal framework that takes the "two persons" picture seriously?
