I've been trying to work out, without much formal training, what the Humean and anti-Humean sides actually disagree about when they talk about laws of nature. The part that keeps me stuck is the difference between a regularity and a law when the world is so small that there is almost nothing else to compare it to. Imagine a finite universe containing exactly three point particles, A, B, and C, and no other fields, forces, or objects. Throughout its whole history, the distance from A to B is always exactly twice the distance from B to C, and whenever A moves to the left, B moves to the right at half the speed. There are no other events and no background that could explain the pattern.

Could you write a short dialogue between a Humean about laws of nature and an anti-Humean about laws of nature, both equally competent and charitable, who are trying to decide whether this toy universe contains a genuine law governing the motion of A, B, and C, or whether it contains only a brute regularity? The Humean should not simply say that laws are summaries of what happens; they should explain how a best-system or pattern-based account would treat the case, including the trade-off between simplicity and strength. The anti-Humean should not simply say that laws are mysterious extra stuff; they should explain what it would be for the regularity to be grounded in a law, and why that matters for counterfactuals or for the possibility of a different history. I'd like the dialogue to contain real back-and-forth, with each person pressing on the other's actual answer, rather than a list of positions.
