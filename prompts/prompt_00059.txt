I've been reading L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience for a seminar on decision theory, and I'm stuck on how her argument is supposed to unsettle standard rational choice frameworks. The core case—deciding whether to become a parent, or whether to undergo some experience like military combat or a religious conversion that changes your preferences and your epistemic access to what the outcome is like—seems to break the usual model where you evaluate options by simulating their expected value against your current preferences. If I can't epistemically access what it's like to be the "post-transformation" version of me, and if that transformation also revises my values, then it's unclear what work deliberation is even doing beforehand.

What I'm trying to pin down are the most important implications of this for how we should think about major life decisions more broadly, beyond just Paul's canonical examples. Does this mean standard expected utility reasoning is simply the wrong tool for a whole class of first-personal, identity-altering choices, and if so, what (if anything) replaces it—authenticity-based reasoning, revealed-preference-after-the-fact, some kind of deference to testimony from people who've already transformed? I'm also curious whether this has knock-on effects for adjacent debates, like personal identity over time, the rationality of regret, or how much weight we should give to our past selves' judgments versus our current or anticipated future selves' judgments. My friends and I disagree about whether this is a genuinely novel problem for decision theory or just a dressed-up version of old worries about preference change, so I'd appreciate a treatment that takes seriously the possibility that it's more radical than that.
