I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size aerospace startup, and I've just been offered a spot on the crew for the first permanent Mars colony — software lead for the settlement's life-support systems. It's a one-way contract: no return flights are scheduled for at least twenty years, and by most estimates the physiological and psychological effects of long-term low gravity and isolation are irreversible. My partner and I have been going back and forth on this for weeks. She keeps asking me to imagine what daily life would actually be like there, but I can't form a clear picture, and even veterans of long-duration ISS missions admit that their sense of what mattered to them shifted in ways they never anticipated once they were actually up there.

I keep trying to model this as an ordinary cost-benefit decision — weigh the career opportunity and scientific significance against the risks to my health, my relationships, and everything familiar about life on Earth — but something about the situation resists that approach. I can't be sure my current preferences are the right ones to use in evaluating an outcome I'll only experience after being changed by the very process I'm evaluating, and I can't fully trust the reports of people who've already been transformed by similar experiences, since their "before" selves are no longer the ones doing the reporting. Is there a rational way to make a decision like this, given that I can't know in advance what it will be like or who I'll become on the other side of it, and any testimony I gather is necessarily filtered through people whose perspective was itself reshaped by the choice in question?
