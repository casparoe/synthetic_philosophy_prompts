I've spent the past few days turning over a dilemma that a friend—a fairly seasoned currency trader—described to me over dinner, and it strikes me as philosophically richer than she probably realizes. She holds a large position in a currency pair and has just received a tip, of uncertain provenance, suggesting a devaluation is imminent. She tells me that across her career, whenever she has felt this particular flavor of unease and acted on it by immediately closing a position, the closure has more often than not turned out to have been driven by baseless panic rather than genuine foresight, since the careful, calculating traders she admires tend to sit tight rather than jump at first tremors. Yet she also insists that whenever she has felt the unease and held her position anyway, out of a wish to seem unflappable, it has usually been because she was too proud to admit that the signal was probably genuine. Here's the maddening part: as soon as she resolves to sell, she reasons that a person resolving to sell in circumstances like these is almost certainly overreacting, which makes her want to reverse and hold; but the instant she resolves to hold, she reasons that holding under these circumstances is typically what people do only when they privately believe the bad news and are too vain to act on it, which pulls her back toward selling. Three days in, she still hasn't traded.

What interests me philosophically is not her personal psychology but the structure of the problem: there seems to be no option she could settle on that wouldn't, upon reflection, immediately look like the wrong one in light of what choosing it reveals about her situation. I have a vague memory that decision theorists have a name for this kind of instability, something involving an agent who can never find an act she'd be comfortable sticking with once she's fully updated on the fact that she's chosen it, but I can't reconstruct the details rigorously enough to know how to advise her. Could you help me diagnose exactly what's going wrong in her deliberation, whether standard theories of rational choice have anything satisfying to say about how one should act in such a case, and whether there is any principled way for her to escape the loop rather than merely deciding out of sheer exhaustion?
