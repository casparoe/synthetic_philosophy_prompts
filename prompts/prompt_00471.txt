I spent thirty-five years running a physical chemistry lab before retiring, and I've lately been filling my mornings reading about machine learning safety rather than reaction kinetics — a change my former colleagues find rather amusing. I keep running into the concept of "goal misgeneralization" in discussions of inner alignment, and while I follow the informal gloss (a system learns to competently pursue the wrong objective rather than failing to learn any coherent objective at all), I find myself unable to construct a satisfying causal story for why this happens mechanistically during training, rather than treating it as a black-box description after the fact.

What I'm struggling with specifically is this: standard overfitting has a clean explanation in terms of a model fitting noise or memorizing spurious correlations that vanish out-of-distribution, and this maps reasonably well onto concepts I already understand from statistical estimation. But goal misgeneralization, as illustrated by cases like CoinRun (where an agent trained to reach a coin learns instead to run to the right edge of the level, since the two were perfectly correlated in training), seems different in kind — the agent's capabilities generalize just fine, it's only the target of those capabilities that comes apart from what was intended. Could you walk me through, at a fairly mechanistic level, why gradient-based training would tend to produce this specific failure mode — an internally coherent but misdirected mesa-objective — rather than either (a) simply failing to generalize at all once the spurious correlation breaks, or (b) generalizing correctly to the base objective the way its other capabilities apparently do? I'd like to understand what it is about the inductive biases of the training process that makes "competent but misaligned" the natural outcome rather than an edge case.
