So I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and a work conversation about how to desribe what our LLM "thinks" sent me down a rabbit hole on Dennett's intentional stance. I've been trying to apply it to the models we work with and I wrote up some half-baked notes that I'd really appreciate a sanity check on, because I keep feeling like I'm missing the forest for the trees.

My rough understanding is that Dennett gives you three stances you can take toward a system: the physical stance (treat it as a physical object and model it with physical laws), the design stance (treat it by its design description, i.e. what it was built to do), and the intentional stance (treat it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, and predict its behavior by attributing those). The intentional stance is the one that's supposed to kick in when you don't understand the internals.

Here's where I'm stuck. For an LLM, the design stance feels like it should be the "correct" stance in principle, since we literally know how it was built (a neural net trained to predict the next token). But that design description doesn't actually let me predict what it'll output, so in practice I fall back to the intentional stance and start saying "the model believes X." Except the intentional stance is supposed to require the agent to be roughly rational, and a model that hallucinates constantly doesn't seem rational at all. So I'm not sure whether the intentional stance even applies to these things, or whether attributing false beliefs to them is just a sign I'm using the wrong stance. Could you tell me where I'm going wrong, and specifically whether there's a cleaner way to think about when it's legitimate to attribute beliefs to a system like this?
