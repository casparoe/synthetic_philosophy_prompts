I'm the chair of the graduate committee for a PhD student who's proposing a dissertation that defends a version of the Principle of Alternative Possibilities against Frankfurt cases. The student's specific move is to argue that Frankfurt cases don't work because in them the agent's "inability to do otherwise" is only a counterfactual one — it's the inability that would obtain if the agent had decided differently — and the student thinks that only "actual" inability, the kind that obtains in the actual sequence of events, defeats responsibility. I was discussing this with my co-advisor last week and she thinks the student is just stipulating away the problem, but I think there's something real going on here, esp. if the student can show that the "actual-sequence" response is not just a stipulation but a genuine philosophical move with consequences for how we think about the relationship between control and responsibility.

The comittee is split: two members think this is a defensible and interesting line, one thinks it's a nonstarter, and I have to write the memo before Thursday's meeting. Draft the memo: recommend whether to approve the proposal as is, with revisions, or reject, and engage with the strongest version of the student's position rather than just saying "Frankfurt cases have settled this."
