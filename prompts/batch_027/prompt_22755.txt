I'm taking an AP philosophy class this spring, and we've been working through a unit on perception, and I've been trying to sort out something that a few of us keep arguing about in study group. We're all on the same page about the milder point — that what we pay attention to and what we expect can shape what we notice, so that if I'm told to look for a red car I suddenly seem to see red cars everywhere, and I understand this is what people mean by "top-down processing." Where I get stuck is the term "cognitive penetration," which keeps coming up in the reading, and I can't tell how it's supposed to be different from the top-down effects we already grant. Suppose I genuinely believe that the sky is bluer than it really is, and as a result it looks bluer to me. Is that a real case of my belief altering what I perceive, or is it just that I'm attending more to the blue, or that I'm describing my experience differently when I report it? I keep going back and forth on whether there's any principled line there at all.

I also want to understand why the distinction matters in the first place. I've heard people say it's tied to the older idea that perception is "modular" or "encapsulated" — supposed to be insulated from what we believe and expect — but I don't quite see the stakes. Could you walk me through where the line between an ordinary top-down effect and a genuine case of cognitive penetration is supposed to run, and what's actually at issue if perception turns out not to be modular after all?
