Question: Tolstoy defines art as the intentional communication of feeling. Explain the core of this definition, why Tolstoy rejects beauty as a criterion for judging art, and what follows for works pursued for art's sake.

Student's answer: Tolstoy's definition holds that art is an activity in which a person, having experienced a certain feeling, wishes to transmit it to others and does so by means of external signs — words, tones, form. A work succeeds in proportion as the feeling passes over from the artist to the receiver; this transmissibility is what Tolstoy calls infectiousness, and it serves as the touchstone both for whether something is art at all and for its degree of excellence. Tolstoy rejects the aesthetic theory of beauty for a simple reason: it is circular. It declares good whatever is beautiful and beautiful whatever is good, so that in the end it amounts to nothing more than the approval of a privileged class; since beauty cannot be objectively defined, it cannot function as a standard. Two further points follow. First, the feeling transmitted may be any feeling whatsoever — grief, boredom, or the pleasure of a well-constructed fugue — for Tolstoy's criterion is the accuracy of the transmission, not the moral quality of the content. Second, the artist need not himself be moved by what he produces: what matters is that the signs he arranges — a melody, a brushstroke, a turn of phrase — effectively produce the corresponding feeling in the receiver, much as a well-designed instrument produces a given tone. The consequence for art for art's sake is that such works are exposed as what Tolstoy calls clever art: their difficulty and self-sufficiency do not transmit a feeling to an ordinary listener, and so, on Tolstoy's own criterion, they fail to be art in the proper sense.
