So I've been a volunteer moderator for about four months on a small urban foraging forum, the kind where people post photos of mushrooms and plants they found and ask if they're edible, with a verified species database behind the scenes. The rules were written by the founder years ago and are locked until the annual vote in like eight months, so I can't just change anything. One of the rules is that posts containing factually false claims about edibility or toxicity get removed immedately, since people actually cook what they read here and a bad claim can get someone hurt. The auto-moderation bot checks claims against the database and flags anything that contradicts it.

A long-time member who's always been a good-faith poster (retired biology teacher, hundreds of helpful identifications) posts a single sentence: "This post is false." The bot can't resolve it and just keeps re-flagging it as an error, and it's been sitting in the queue for three days. The thing is, every action I can take under the rules seems to undermine itself: if I remove it, I'm treating it as false, but then its claim is wrong, which makes it true, so I removed a true post. If I leave it up, I'm treating it as not false, i.e. true, but then its claim is right, so it is false, so I'm keeping a false post. One of the other mods says I should just ban the user for abusing the queue, but the founder, who's semi-retired from the forum, says the rules are the rules, delete it and dont overthink it. My friend, who's in a philosophy department, says this isn't a word game and that there's a whole way of thinking about logic built around exactly cases like this, and that the real question is whether one unresolvable contradiction like this "infects" the whole rulebook or not.

What do I actually do with the post right now (delete, leave, lock it, ban, something else)? And is my friend onto something, or is the "one contradiction breaks the whole system" worry just logician's paranoia? I'm not a logician so I'd appreciate an explanation that doesn't assume I've read a textbook.
