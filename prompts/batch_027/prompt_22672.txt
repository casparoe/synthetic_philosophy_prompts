So I've been deep in the mechanistic interpretability rabbit hole for a while now (I've been reading the Alignment Forum and the various interpretability posts), and there's this one distinction I keep failing to nail down. We have these two main ways of poking at a model's internals: probing, where you train a little linear classifier on the activations to read out some property, and activation steering, where you find a direction in activation space and add it in to nudge the model's behavior. And the whole framing in the literature is that if you can find a clean direction for, say, negation or some register-like property, then the model "represents" that concept.

But I keep feelin like there's a real gap between "I can pull this lever and the model behaves differently" and "the model actually carries a representation of the concept in its state." Can you walk me through that gap? Like, what's the actual reason — philosophical or technical — why a steerable direction doesn't automatically mean the concept is represented? And then there's the superposition thing, where a single direction might be a mix of several concepts, which makes the "this direction = this concept" picture even shakier. I want to understand whether "representation" is doing real work here or whether it's just a metaphor we've stopped interrogating, and whether there's a version of the argument where a clean steerable direction does count as a representation, or whether that's just a non-starter.
