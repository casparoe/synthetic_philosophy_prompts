I have just retired from a career in physical oceanography, and I have recently begun reading philosophy of science seriously. One distinction that keeps coming back to me is the contrast between counterfactual accounts of causation and interventionist or manipulability accounts, and I am not sure how much of it is merely a difference in formal language and how much actually changes what a scientist is entitled to say. I am trying to prepare a careful talk for a local science society on climate attribution, and I want to know the most important practical and epistemic implications of adopting the interventionist view rather than the counterfactual view in a field where controlled experiments are impossible.

In particular, I would like you to explain how the two approaches differ on the status of observational regression coefficients, on claims about causal levers such as emissions policies, and on variables that no agent can realistically manipulate, such as latitude, topography, or deep-ocean stratification. Please focus on the substantive, non-obvious consequences for causal inference and public explanation, including where the choice between the two approaches can change the answer and what a cautious scientist should say when the distinction is doing real work rather than merely redrawing boundaries.
