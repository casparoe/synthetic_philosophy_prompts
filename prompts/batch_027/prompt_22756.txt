My company recently tied our customer-support team's quarterly bonus to the share of tickets resolved within 24 hours. I'm a manager in that org, so it's my problem to sort out. At lunch, Ravi, who does our data analysis, and Dana, who runs operations, argued about whether the metric is sound, and I couldn't tell who was right. I've written down what they said, roughly.

Ravi: This is a textbook Goodhart trap. The second people's pay depends on the 24-hour rate, they'll close tickets early, mark things "resolved" that aren't, or nudge customers to end the chat. The number goes up while real resolution stays flat or drops, and the metric stops measuring what it's supposed to.

Dana: You're conflating two things. If agents game it, that's them optimizing the wrong objective — the metric isn't broken, their incentives are. And the gaming is actually useful: it shows exactly where the metric and the goal have diverged, so we can patch the metric with a re-open rate or a follow-up check. Abandoning metrics because they're gameable is like refusing to use any measurement because measurements can be fudged.

Ravi: "Patch the metric" assumes there's a stable target to measure against. But "the customer's problem is actually solved" is fuzzy and high-dimensional. Any scalar you pick to stand in for it will diverge from it under optimization pressure, because the pressure selects for the gap. It's not that our measurement is sloppy; it's that the target itself is a proxy for something you can't reduce to a number.

Dana: "Can't reduce to a number" is doing all the work, and it's a strawman. Every metric is a proxy — even revenue or uptime are proxies for "the business is healthy." The question was never whether a metric is a perfect mirror of reality; it's whether it's a good-enough proxy that, on net, points people the right way. Your standard would condemn every metric, including ones that clearly work.
