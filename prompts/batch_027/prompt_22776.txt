The question:
Explain the difference between the standard (computability-theoretic) Church–Turing thesis and the physical version, sometimes called the strong Church–Turing thesis or the Church–Turing–Deutsch principle. For each, say whether it's best understood as a conceptual or definitional claim or as an empirical claim, and what it would take to refute it. Finally, some philosophers argue that the physical version is closer to a conceptual truth than to a substantive empirical claim — what is the best version of that argument?

The student's answer:
The standard Church–Turing thesis says that a function is computable by an effective method if and only if it is computable by a Turing machine. I think this is best understood as a conceptual or quasi-logical claim rather than an empirical one. Its support is that every model of computation people have proposed — the lambda calculus, recursive functions, and so on — turns out to be exactly as powerful as the Turing machine, and nobody has ever found a model that goes beyond it. So it's more like a definition of what "effective" is supposed to mean.

The strong version, the Church–Turing–Deutsch principle, says that anything that can actually be physically realized or computed in the real world is Turing-computable. Unlike the weak version, this one is an empirical claim about the universe, so in principle it could be refuted.

I think the strong version is actually false. Quantum computers refute it: Shor's algorithm lets a quantum computer factor large integers exponentially faster than any classical computer can, so there are physical computations that a Turing machine simply cannot perform.

That said, even setting quantum computers aside, there are "hypercomputation" proposals — Malament–Hogarth spacetime being the main example — where an observer can, in a finite amount of their own proper time, read off the result of a computation that has been running for an infinite amount of time, which would let you solve the halting problem. If any of those were physically realizable, that would be a clean refutation of the strong thesis.

Finally, some philosophers argue that the strong thesis is closer to a conceptual truth than to an empirical claim, and I think they're right, because the strong thesis holds for every model of computation we've found, just like the weak thesis does, so it's really just the same conceptual point restated in physical language.
