I've been reading a lot of old effective-altruism and longtermist discussion lately, and it keeps circling back to Henry Sidgwick's closing dilemma in *The Methods of Ethics*—the one where he reasons that if the universe is infinite, then our present actions make practically no difference to the total sum of good, so we are, as he puts it, almost indifferent to the fate of the universe, while if it is finite the calculus is straightforward but the horizon is bounded. What I find confusing, and what I've been arguing about with friends who read a lot of the discounting literature, is how a Sidgwickian who takes the universe to be genuinely infinite and who is committed to hedonistic utilitarianism would reason about *pure time preference*—that is, the part of the social discount rate that is not just the opportunity cost of capital (the Ramsey "golden rule" term, where you discount because a dollar invested earns a return) but pure impatience, a rate that says a unit of good now is worth more than an identical unit later for no productive reason.

Could you work out, as charitably and specifically as you can, what Sidgwick's own position on pure time preference would be under the assumption that the universe is infinite? I'm particularly interested in three sub-questions. First, whether the "almost indifferent" conclusion he reaches in the infinite case actually *undermines* any positive discount rate, or whether it only shows that the *absolute magnitude* of future good is swamped, leaving a *rate* question still open. Second, how he would treat the distinction between a discount rate justified by the productivity of capital (which he, as the author of a treatise on political economy, would presumably take seriously) and a pure rate of impatience, and whether his hedonism—where pleasure and pain are the only intrinsic goods and are, on his view, roughly fungible across persons and times—gives him any principled reason to prefer the present over the future at all, or whether consistency pushes him toward a rate of zero. Third, and this is where I think the real tension is: if he takes the universe to be infinite and the sum of good to be infinite in both directions, what does "maximizing" even mean for him, and does his dilemma force him to abandon the very utilitarian calculus that the question of a discount rate presupposes, or can he salvage a local, present-centered decision rule? I'd like the answer to engage the actual structure of his argument rather than just say "he would be a longtermist" or "he would discount"; I want to know where his own premises pull him, and where they run out.
