I have only recently begun reading philosophy, after a long career as a physical chemist, and I find myself stuck on a figure who strikes me as closer to my own kind than most philosophers: Otto Neurath, the economist and philosopher of science who, from around 1925 as director of the Museum of Society and Economy in Vienna, developed what later came to be called ISOTYPE, the International System of Typographic Picture Education, originally known simply as the "Vienna Method" of pictorial statistics. His aim, as I understand it, was to build a wordless, systematic, and in principle culture-free picture language that could communicate empirical and statistical facts directly to citizens, bypassing the distortions of ordinary prose and of partisan rhetoric. I can see the appeal of the idea from a scientist's standpoint: if we can agree on a common visual grammar, we should be able to agree on the facts, and argument would be reduced to checking the pictures against the data.

What keeps me up at night is the objection that this project quietly fails at its own goal. A picture language is not a passive window onto the world; it forces every phenomenon into a chosen set of units, scales, and categories, and those choices are never innocent. The very act of quantifying, of deciding what a household, a worker, or a ton of output shall be, and how one picture-symbol shall stand for how many, seems to smuggle in exactly the classificatory and even ideological assumptions that Neurath wanted to eliminate. And there is a further worry that I cannot quite resolve: if the language is genuinely wordless and culture-free, how can it carry the qualitative, evaluative, and historical content that most of what we care about in social life actually has? In short, I suspect there is a deep tension between Neurath's empiricist, positivist aspiration to a neutral language of facts and the inescapably interpretive, value-laden character of any symbolic system that represents a society.

I would like you to write an essay that weighs this controversy and arrives at a defensible verdict rather than an even-handed shrug. Take seriously both the case that Neurath's picture language can, at least in principle, deliver a genuinely objective and universally intelligible medium for empirical communication, and the case that it cannot, because every such medium encodes the very choices it claims to set aside. Where should the balance fall, and what would it take for the stronger position to be right? I would be especially grateful if you could say something specific about the role of quantification and about the wordless ideal, since those are the two points on which I feel I am most likely to be talking past myself.
