For my seminar on the history of moral philosophy, I'm working on an assignment that asks us to project a historical thinker onto a contemporary controversy. We've covered Russell's position on marriage and sexual morality — his attack on marriage as an institution bound up with property and the subordination of women, and his defense of sexual freedom as a matter of personal autonomy. The task is to predict what he would have made of an issue that did not exist in his lifetime: whether the state should legally recognize non-monogamous and polyamorous partnerships, a practice often labeled group marriage. Please reason from Russell's actual philosophical commitments rather than from a generic modern liberal stance, and be explicit about the places where the assumptions of his own period — about the family's social function, about gender, about the proper role of the state — might lead him somewhere a present-day reader would not expect.
