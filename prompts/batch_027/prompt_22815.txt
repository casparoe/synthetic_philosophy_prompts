I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I'm putting together a long-form piece on logical uncertainty — the problem of how a reasoner should assign probabilities to mathematical statements (say, "the 87,653rd digit of π is a 7") that it has not yet had the computational resources to settle. I've been working through the introduction and abstract of Garrabrant, Benson-Tilsen, Critch, Soares, and Taylor's "Logical Induction" (arXiv:1609.03543), and I keep getting stuck on the precise sense in which their core criterion is supposed to generalize the old "Dutch book" argument from probability theory. The key passage I'm circling is this:

"These properties and many others all follow from a single logical induction criterion, which is motivated by a series of stock trading analogies. Roughly speaking, each logical sentence φ is associated with a stock that is worth $1 per share if φ is true and nothing otherwise, and we interpret the belief-state of a logically uncertain reasoner as a set of market prices, where P_d(φ) = 0.5 means that on day d, shares of φ may be bought or sold from the reasoner for 50¢. The logical induction criterion says (very roughly) that there should not be any polynomial-time computable trading strategy with finite risk tolerance that earns unbounded profits in that market over time."

Two things in that last sentence are doing more work than I can currently make sense of, and I'd be grateful for help untangling them. First, why does the criterion single out polynomial-time computable trading strategies rather than just any computable strategy — what would go wrong if you dropped the efficiency restriction? Second, why is the target "unbounded profits over time" rather than a one-shot guaranteed loss, as in the classic Dutch book setup? I suspect these two restrictions are load-bearing for making the criterion both non-vacuous and actually satisfiable by a real algorithm, but I can't quite see the argument, and I'd like to understand it well enough to explain it accurately to a general (but numerate) readership.
