My grandfather died on Tuesday, three days shy of his ninety-fourth birthday. He was a corn and soybean farmer his whole life, and in his last few years he'd taken up reading Cicero's On Old Age — I think a friend of his gave him a paperback, and he read it twice, which for a man who usually just read the seed catalog is a lot. He was not a philosopher and never pretended to be one, but he genuinely, stubbornly liked being old, and he kept coming back to two things in that book. One was the idea that the old have a kind of power over the young that the young are actually jealous of, and he used to say that to the grandkids to our faces, grinning. The other was the bit about the mind being like a field you keep working even when your body can't — he put it in his own words as "you don't stop farming just because your back goes, you just farm a different crop."

He left a note for me, the one grandkid who'd do this, asking that I give his eulogy at the service Saturday and not make him out some poor old man who was "suffering through" his last years. He said, and I'm paraphrasing, that he didn't want anyone pitying him, and that if I was going to talk about age I should talk about it the way the old Roman in the book did, not the way the eulogist at my cousins dads funeral did, which was all about how he'd been "fading." The problem is the room will be mostly family and farm neighbors, none of whom have read a word of Cicero — except maybe the one guy who gave him the book, who'll be there and who definitely has — and a couple of the neighbors will be a little drunk by the end, so I can't just lecture them. But I also don't want to throw away the two ideas he actually cared about, because those were the ones he lived by. Can you write me that eulogy? It should run about three or four minutes, it should comfort people who are genuinely sad and not philosophers, and it should use those two ideas from the book in a way that a farmer would nod at and a person who'd read it would recognize.
