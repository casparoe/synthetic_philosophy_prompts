I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm in a book club that's been doing a stretch of older tech-adjacent writing. Last week someone brought up a 1955 essay by John von Neumann, "Can We Survive Technology?", published in Fortune, and it kind of wrecked the usual vibes of the group. The essay opens with "The great globe itself is in a rapidly maturing crisis—a crisis attributable to the fact that the environment in which technological progress must occur has become both undersized and underorganized", and the whole argument is basically that our problem isn't a lack of resources or ingenuity but that the organizational structure of civilization (states, borders, command chains) is too small and too slow for the scale of effects we can now produce. I've been stuck on the framing of the title the whole week: the question is not "can technology survive?" but "can we, the people, survive our own technology?" which is a much weirder and more pessimistic claim than it first reads, and I suspect he was treating technological progress as something almost outside of political control, more like a weather system than a policy lever.

I want to get a handle on what he'd make of problems that only became real after 1957, so I was thinking of three test cases. First, modern LLMs and agentic systems: code that writes code, systems that can partially modify their own tooling, and the fact that the "operators" of these systems are often a handful of companies rather than states. Second, orbital debris and the space economy: the physical environment is now genuinely undersized in a way he might have found almost poetic, since low orbit is a finite commons with no borders and no obvious authority. Third, gene drives and synthetic biology: capabilities that can propagate themselves in the biosphere with no off switch, which feels like it mixes his two failure modes (undersized and underorganized) into one. How do you think he'd map each of these onto his undersized/underorganized distinction, and would his answer to the title question have gotten more or less pessimistic given what's happened since he wrote it? I'm curious if he'd see any of these as problems that are actually more solvable than he assumed, or if he'd argue that self-improving technology just moves the crisis faster.
