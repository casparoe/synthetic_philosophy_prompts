I've been dragged back into philosophy of science by the rationalist blogs (I swear im done with it after the whole "map is not the territory" phase, but here we are), and I ran into a thing at a dinner party that I still can't resolve. Two of my friends, one a physics grad student and one a philosophy major, got into it about underdetermination, and they were both pretty smart and both pretty stuborn, so I'm not sure who's right. It started with the physics grad student (Marcus) saying that the classic case of Mercury's perihelion is not actually a case of underdetermination. He pointed out that the 43 arcseconds per century residual could be explained either by General Relativity or by positing a hidden planet, Vulcan, inside Mercury's orbit, and that both fit the data. But then he said that Vulcan was just a bad ad hoc hypothesis, a desperate fudge, and that General Relativity wasn't just a fit to Mercury's perihelion but actually predicted the bending of light, the gravitational redshift, and the precession of binary pulsars. So he invoked the "no miracles" argument: it's a miracle that a false theory would predict so many independent phenomena, so the evidence does determine the theory, and it's GR. The philosophy major (Dana) pushed back by saying that Marcus was smuggling in the "no miracles" argument, which is just a restatement of realism, not an independent argument. She pointed out that even GR could be underdetermined by a deeper theory, and that Quine's web of belief shows that you can never test a single theory in isolation, only the whole web. So underdetermination is built into the structure of science, not a bug.

Marcus responded that the Duhem-Quine thesis is too strong, that of course you can't test a single sentence in isolation but you can test a theory against a mostly-settled background, and that the "no miracles" argument is a genuine inductive argument, not a restatement. He said the track record of science is a meta-empirical argument that's independent of any particular theory. Dana said that was circular, that Marcus was using the success of simple theories to justify a prior that simple theories are true, and then using that prior to justify believing in the current simple theory. She said the history of science is full of "elegant" theories that turned out to be wrong, so the "no miracles" argument is just an expression of a metaphysical preference, not evidence. Marcus said it wasn't circular, that the track record was evidence that's independent of any particular theory, and that the "no miracles" argument was a genuine empirical argument.

I honestly couldn't tell who was right, and I keep thinking about it. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about each specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd like a verdict on each move, not just who wins overall.
