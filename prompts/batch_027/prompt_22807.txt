I'm teaching a small section on experimental philosophy this term and two of my grad students got stuck in a debate at our study session that I honestly cant referee. They both accept the basic asymmetry — that people are much more likely to say an agent intentionally brought about a harmful side effect of a decision than a beneficial one, in cases where the agent foresaw the side effect, thought of it as a mere byproduct, and went ahead with the decision anyway — so that part isn't in dispute. Their fight is over what the asymmetry shows.

Mara argues it shows that our concept of intentional action itself is morally contaminated. Her reasoning is that in the harm case and the help case the agent's mental states are roughly the same — same foreknowledge, same indifference — and the only thing that changes is the valence of the outcome, so the only thing that could drive the asymmetry is that moral evaluation is literally built into the concept. Intentional, on this view, is doing normative work rather than descriptive work. Dev pushes back and says this is the old contamination story and that its not needed: people are running a causal-inference process, and when we learn that an agent brought about a bad outcome, we infer she must have endorsed it or believed in it in a way we don't infer for a good outcome. So the difference, he says, is in the folk's inference, not in the concept — the two cases differ in the evidence, not in the criterion.

Mara's reply is that the inference story just collapses back into the contamination story, because the very fact that bad outcomes count as evidence of the relevant mental states is itself a moral premise — we expect bad things to be done by people who intended them and good things to be done by people who didn't — and that expectation is normative, so normativity is still doing the work, just one layer down. Dev says that proves too much, because almost every folk-psychological inference is normatively flavored (we expect liars to lie, for instance) and nobody says that makes our concept of belief normative.

Can you adjudicate this for me? I'd like a point-by-point verdict on who's right about which specific claim, and what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular whether the inference account really is a separate hypothesis from the contamination account or just a relabeling of it, and whether there's a diagnosis of the asymmetry that neither of them has even considered.
