I'm a software engineer by trade with no formal philosophy training, and I've been reading Plato's *Meno* on the side because a colleague kept pointing me to the passage where Socrates tries to say what knowledge is that a mere true belief is not. Last week, two people in my reading group got into a fairly heated argument about whether knowledge is actually worth more than a true belief, or whether the difference is an illusion, and I found myself unable to tell which of them was on firmer ground.

Let me lay out the exchange. One of them, Dana, started from the text: she said Socrates' point is that a true belief which doesn't amount to knowledge is unstable and can simply drift away, which is why he compares true beliefs to the statues of Daedalus, said to wander off, whereas knowledge is anchored, held in place by an account, by reasoning out the cause. So, she argued, knowledge is worth more than a lucky true belief because it is stable, and you can actually rely on a stable belief and build on it, whereas a mere true belief might evaporate the next day; a stable belief is plainly more valuable to you than an unstable one, all else being equal. The other, Marcus, pushed back and said that Dana's stability point quietly concedes his whole case: the only reason the anchoring is good is that it keeps the belief true, and if the extra ingredient in knowledge, the account or the justification, is valuable only in so far as it produces or preserves truth, then its value is entirely absorbed by the value of the true belief itself, so knowledge is not intrinsically worth more, just a true belief plus a mechanism for not losing it, and the mechanism has no value of its own. Dana replied that this misreads the situation, because the stability and the account are valuable in their own right, not merely as a means to truth, and that having a belief you can explain and that will not waver is a genuinely different and richer state of mind, so it is not absurd to think a stable belief has value even on its own terms. Marcus then came back with what he saw as the decisive point: if stability has value independent of truth, then a stable false belief would have to be valuable too, which is nonsense, and the same trouble hits the appeal to understanding, since you can understand a false theory just as well as a true one, so understanding does not single out knowledge either. They both ended up saying the other was going in circles, and I honestly could not referee it.
