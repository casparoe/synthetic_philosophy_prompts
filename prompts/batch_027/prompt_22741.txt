I'm putting together a long-form piece for my ideas section on how a handful of old philosophical thought experiments keep getting redeployed in new arguments, and I keep circling back to Putnam's Twin Earth and the semantic externalism it was designed to establish — the broad claim that the content of my thoughts and the meaning of my words are partly fixed by my surroundings and by my linguistic community, rather than being located entirely inside my head. A reader recently pushed back on a paragraph I wrote, insisting that externalism does not merely leave Descartes' cogito standing, as Putnam himself suggested, but quietly corrodes it. I've been turning that over, and I'm now inclined to think the reader is onto something: if the very concepts the cogito leans on — "I," "think," "am" — are themselves partly constituted by an external, social-linguistic order, then a subject who is radically severed from that order might fail to possess the concepts needed to entertain the cogito in the first person the way Descartes meant, which is a kind of threat the original argument was never supposed to have to answer.

Could you steelman and then argue that position for me, in some depth and with a clear structure? I'd like you to make the case that semantic externalism genuinely undercuts the first-person, a priori certainty of the cogito, and to do it in a way that engages the strongest reply — the one that says the self-referential character of the cogito, the fact that it amounts to my entertaining, right now, this very thought, makes it immune to exactly the externalist revision that Twin Earth applies to empirical terms like "water." I don't need you to resolve the debate or tell me I'm right; I want a serious, well-organized argument for the more unsettling reading, the sort of thing a philosopher committed to externalism would have to grapple with, and that I could use to give my reader a real sense of what's genuinely at stake.
