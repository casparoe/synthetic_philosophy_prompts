I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene in my new book: a group of friends at a dinner party, one of them mentions a new restaraunt, and by the end of the evening everyone has agreed to go, even though a couple of them privetly thought it looked overrated. I want to get the psychology right, and I keep reading that this is an example of an information cascade, but I've also seen the exact same behavior called rational herding, and I'm not sure those are the same concept or whether I'm just using two names for one thing.

Can you compare information cascades and rational herding as concepts? I'm interested in the mechanism-level differences: does a cascade strictly require people to move in sequence and rationally ignore their own private signals once the public history gets heavy enough, whereas herding can also happen in a simultaneous game or be driven by payoff considerations (like the venue actually being more fun the more people go — my scene might or might not have that)? And what does each one imply about how wrong the group can end up being — I recall something about cascades being fragile and sometimes forming on the basis of just a couple of early actions, and herding being associated with overreaction to news or volatility in the economics literature, but I'd like a cleaner side-by-side comparison. Ideally, end with a note on how I should tell which one my characters are actually exhibiting, since the scene hinges on whether the later compliers are rationally ignoring their own taste or just following for payoff reasons.
