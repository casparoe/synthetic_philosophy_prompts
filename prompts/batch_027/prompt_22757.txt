I'm in a philosophy of language seminar this term, and our readings on slurs have left me stuck on what seems to me a simple consequence of the semantic view. I've been assuming that if a slur's meaning includes a social-attack or pejorative component, then every token of the word should carry that component in the same way that an ordinary insult does, regardless of whether the speaker intends to insult. On that view, a professor's quoting the N-word in a lecture should still semantically express an attack on the target group, since the word's meaning hasn't changed. But in many such cases no one is obviously insulted, and the word seems to function more like a neutral example. That makes it look to me as though the offensive force of slurs must be entirely pragmatic, and that any account which puts the pejorative in the semantics is simply mistaken.

If I am right, what is the remaining job for semantic theorizing about slurs, given that the same word can be used offensively in one context and apparently innocuously in another? Or am I missing something about how quotation, metalinguistic use, or context affects the semantic contribution of the word? I would like to understand why the non-offensive classroom use is not, by itself, decisive evidence against semantic accounts.
