My uncle died on Thursday, and I'm giving his eulogy on Sunday at a small church, with roughly forty people in the pews: most are family, who knew him as a quiet, meticulous restorer at the city's art museum for forty years, but a handful of curators and dealers will be there too, because a few of them knew his secret. After his death we found the basement: more than thirty forgeries of Old Master paintings, made over the last fifteen years, never sold, and by the judgment of the two people in the art world who have examined them, indistinguishable from the genuine works. When I finally worked up the nerve to ask him why he kept making them, he said only, "Because I wanted to know if I could."

I am not an art person, but I have been turning over a question I cannot resolve, and I think the eulogy must confront it rather than pretend it is not there: if a forgery is indistinguishable from the original, what exactly is the difference between them, and does that difference matter? If it is only the history — the original was made by the hand that made it, and the copy was not — then his paintings have no real value, and his secret life was a waste. If the difference is something more than that, then what is it, and can it be stated in a eulogy without turning into a lecture? I do not want to comfort the family by pretending the forgeries were the genuine article, and I do not want to insult the few experts in the room by pretending the question of what an original is has no answer.

Please write the eulogy, for about five minutes when read aloud. It should honor him as a person first — the father, the restorer, the man who mixed his own pigments — and let the question of fakes and originals do the real work in the middle of the speech: it should arrive at an honest answer to what his forgeries were worth, an answer that a family member who knows nothing about art can feel in their chest, and that a curator would not roll their eyes at.
