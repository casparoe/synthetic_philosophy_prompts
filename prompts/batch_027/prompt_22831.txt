Assignment question:
(1) Describe the "hypoxia" case and explain why the higher-order evidence in it does not bear directly on the first-order proposition G.
(2) Does Aisha's higher-order evidence rationally affect her first-order credence in G? State your view and the puzzle it generates.
(3) State the Reflection principle (Elga 2010) and explain whether it settles the case, and if so how.
(4) State the Enkratic Principle and explain whether the possibility of higher-order defeat is compatible with it.

Student's answer:
(1) Aisha's first-order evidence E (gauges, dials, and maps) supports G to degree 0.99, and on that basis she forms the belief G. Her higher-order evidence is that she is at an altitude where pilots performing these calculations are only correct half the time, so she may be hypoxic. The higher-order evidence does not bear directly on G — whether she has enough fuel is independent of whether she is hypoxic — but it does bear on her reliability as a reasoner.
(2) I think yes, the higher-order evidence should affect her first-order credence, and it should drop it substantially, because her total evidential situation now includes the fact that her own reasoning may be compromised. The puzzle is that her first-order evidence still strongly supports G, yet she seems rationally required to be considerably less confident.
(3) The Reflection principle, introduced by Elga, says that upon learning what the rational credence function is, one should adopt its opinions; formally, for any proposition q and any credence function π, P(q | P = π) = π(q). Applying this, Aisha should set her credence in G to the rational credence she believes she ought to have, which given her 50% reliability is 0.5. So Reflection settles the case in favor of dropping to 0.5.
(4) The Enkratic Principle says it is never rational to believe p while believing that one should not believe p. But the hypoxia case is a case of higher-order defeat: Aisha's first-order belief G is defeated by her higher-order evidence that she may be irrational. Since higher-order defeat is possible, the Enkratic Principle must be false, because Aisha is rationally required to keep believing G (her first-order evidence supports it) while also believing she should not believe G. This is the standard rationale for the "rational akrasia" position defended by Christensen.
