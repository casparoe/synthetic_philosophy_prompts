Question: "Explain the bootstrapping problem and the related notion of 'easy knowledge' in epistemology. Then explain why the problem is supposed to threaten reliabilist accounts of justification, and whether that threat is limited to externalism or whether it also reaches evidentialist internalism. (10 points.)"

Student answer: "Bootstrapping is the problem that we can't use a method to check whether the method is reliable, because using it to check it already assumes it works. For example, if I look out at Hume's field to confirm that my vision is reliable, I'm just using my vision to check my vision. This is sometimes called easy knowledge, the idea that we can get knowledge for free just by using a source to verify itself. The problem is especially nasty for reliabilism: if my belief-forming process is reliable, then my belief that the process is reliable is itself produced by that reliable process, so it's justified, and I end up with knowledge that my vision is reliable without ever having independent evidence for it. But I think the problem is really just a problem for externalism. Evidentialist internalists are safe, because they only count a belief as justified if there's a reason the subject can actually access, and when I use my eyes to check my eyes there's no such accessible reason, so the internalist just has to say I don't have a justification for trusting vision. So the internalist can block the bootstrapping move by pointing out that I lack a reason, while the externalist is the one who gets stuck."
