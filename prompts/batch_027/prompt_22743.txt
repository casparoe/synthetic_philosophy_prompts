I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and on the side I sit on the grants panel of a small social-science foundation. This cycle we have two competing proposals for our methodology prize, and the panel is deadlocked, so I've been asked to draft the written recommendation we'll circulate before the vote. What makes the case unusually clean is that both proposals study the same empirical phenomenon — a housing-market panic — and they disagree only about the right level of explanation. The first, from a behavioral economist, builds an agent-based model of individual borrowers, lenders, and rating agencies, each with their own incentives and information constraints, and treats any explanation that invokes market-level entities as causes as a category error; the author's real test, as I read it, will be whether the micro-level rules can be shown to generate the macro-level panic rather than merely being fitted to it. The second, from a sociologist of finance, treats "market frames" and "professional norms" as autonomous explanatory variables that causally shape what individuals can perceive and do, and argues that the individualist model will misfire at the crucial moment because it cannot represent the shared interpretive structures that made the panic possible in the first place.

We have money for exactly one prize. A philosopher of social science I talk to keeps insisting that the real dispute is less about ontology — do markets exist as entities in their own right? — than about methodology — what is the right unit of analysis for a causal explanation? — and I think that is the crux the memo should get at, because it determines how the panel should weigh the two proposals' respective vulnerabilities: the holist's exposure to the reification or "pluralistic fallacy" worry, and the individualist's exposure to the aggregation and level-of-analysis worry. Please draft the panel's decision memo: recommend which proposal to fund — or, if you judge that to be the wrong call, recommend funding neither or both, with a clear principled reason — and in doing so engage the strongest version of the losing side's worry rather than restating the textbook individualism-versus-holism contrast. The memo should also give the panel concrete, applicable criteria for future cases, not just a verdict on these two.
