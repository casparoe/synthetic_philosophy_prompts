I spent thirty years as a physical chemist, and since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of science with an amateur's persistence; underdetermination of theory by evidence is the one problem I cannot get past. Write a dialogue between two philosophers in a seminar room, each trying to convince the other. One defends the strong view that underdetermination is a genuine and devastating obstacle to scientific realism: because any theory can be shielded from refutation by adjusting its auxiliary hypotheses, no body of evidence ever uniquely determines which theory is true. The other is a scientific realist who concedes that many theories fit the same data but holds that this merely logical underdetermination is not the real threat, because scientific practice breaks the tie with non-empirical virtues and because the no-miracles argument supplies positive grounds for realism. I want the exchange to turn on the specific point that the realist's appeal to virtues such as simplicity or coherence is itself underdetermined—that which virtue to trust is a matter of background assumption, so the move merely relocates the underdetermination one level up rather than dissolving it—and I want the realist to answer that point on the merits rather than dismiss it. Keep both speakers sharp and competent, with real back-and-forth; neither should be a strawman.
