I run a small, student-run philosophy podcast at my school, and I'm putting together an episode on decision theory for next month. My guest is a philosopher who has written in favor of evidential decision theory, roughly the view that you should choose the act with the highest expected value, where the probabilities of the outcomes are computed by updating on the evidence that you are about to perform that act. I have read enough of the surrounding debate to know that her position is not reducible to one-boxing in Newomb's problem, and I want the interview to reflect that rather than spend the whole hour retelling the one-box, two-box story.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual content of evidential decision theory? I would like at least one question on the smoking-lesion case, where someone is told that smoking is a strong indicator of lung cancer in her population only because a shared genetic lesion causes both the urge to smoke and the cancer, so that smoking does not actually cause cancer in her, and on how she thinks evidential decision theory should handle it without yielding advice like "never smoke." I also want at least one question on whether her theory can distinguish a causal correlation from a merely evidential one, or whether it treats every correlation the same way, and at least one question on what she takes to be the real lesson of Newomb's problem: is it about the predictor's reliability, about the agent's ability to decide freely, or about something else entirely? For each of these, please also give me a follow-up that is keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so I can push the conversation along if she lands on the standard reply.
