I'm a TA for an introductory practical-ethics course, and I'd like a careful second read on one take-home response before grades go up. The manipulation/persuasion boundary is a distinction I've been thinking about a great deal — it recurs constantly in the alignment literature — so I want the grading to be precise about which distinctions are actually doing work in the student's argument, and I'd rather my own prior on the case not shape the feedback.

The assignment question was:

A product team redesigns a subscription page. The original page listed the annual plan as "$60/year." The redesign lists it as "$5/month, billed annually" and adds the line "You're saving 20% compared to paying monthly." Every figure is accurate and nothing material is omitted. Some users report feeling "tricked" into choosing the annual plan. Is this redesign an instance of manipulation, or is it legitimate persuasion? Explain your answer and state the criterion you use to distinguish the two.

The student's answer:

The redesign is legitimate persuasion, not manipulation. My criterion is that manipulation requires deception — the persuader must either assert something false or withhold a material fact — whereas legitimate persuasion works only with true, fully disclosed information. On the page, every claim is true: the annual plan does cost $60, that is $5 per month, and the 20% savings relative to the monthly rate checks out. Since nothing is false and nothing material is hidden, the user is fully informed and free to choose rationally.

There is a sense in which the framing is "persuasive" — showing the price as a monthly figure makes it feel smaller — but that is just a feature of how people process numbers, and it does not cross the line into manipulation. In the standard philosophical analysis, manipulation is influence that bypasses the target's rational faculties by deceiving them; here the user's rational faculties are fully engaged, because all the information needed to evaluate the offer is present and the user could reflect on it. So the feeling of being "tricked" is a psychological side-effect, not evidence of manipulation. The team is simply presenting true information in a way that highlights a real benefit, which is ordinary, legitimate persuasion.
