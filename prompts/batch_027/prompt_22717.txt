I'm writing a piece on how philosophers of science and the people who actually build causal-inference tools tend to talk past to each other, and at a panel I was covering last month two of the speakers got into a real tangle over what causation even is. I'd like to know who was right about what before I put either of them on the record, so I'd be grateful for a referee.

The philosopher kept defending the counterfactual idea: to say X caused Y is to say that, had X not happened, Y would not have happened. He thought that was basically the whole story and worked for ordinary cases. The statistician, who does causal inference for a living, said it falls over on the standard counterexamples — picture two buckets of water dumped on a dry tent at the same time, each one enough on its own to soak it. Take away either bucket and the tent is still soaked, so by that test neither bucket caused the soaking, which is plainly wrong. He said his interventionist, do-operator framework handles it. The philosopher then turned it around: fine, so in that same two-buckets case, if you intervene and remove bucket one, the tent is still soaked by bucket two — your do operator gives the exact same wrong answer as my counterfactual, you've just renamed the problem. The statistician said the fix is to hold the other cause fixed or to lay out the whole causal model explicitly, and that you can't even state the bare counterfactual without already deciding what else you're holding fixed, so he's just being explicit. The philosopher replied that that's a lot of extra machinery you have to get right and the counterfactual is simpler and good enough most of the time, and the statistician said causation is model-relative anyway, so making the model explicit is a feature, not a bug.

Could you walk through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And is there anything both of them are missing about how the two accounts actually fit together?
