I was re-reading an old forum thread about the centipede game the other day and it got me going down a rabbit hole on backward induction, and now I'm stuck on something that feels small but I just cant shake. The standard story is that if both players are rational, backward induction shows player 1 should grab the pot right at the start, so both of them end up with the worst possible payoff, and everyone calls this the "backward induction paradox." But I don't actually get why this is a paradox. Backward induction is just... doing the math from the end of the game backwards. Each player is just correctly predicting that the other will do what's in their interest at each step. So why is anyone surprised that the "rational" outcome is bad? Isn't the whole "paradox" just that we had this naive expectation that rational players would cooperate, and the game is just showing that expectation was wrong? Like, it's a disappointment, not a paradox. You just learn not to expect rational agents to be nice to each other.

Or am I missing something? I keep seeing people on the forum and in papers treat it as if there's a genuine logical problem with the backward induction argument itself — like the argument is secretly circular, or it's using some notion of "rationality" that doesn't actually apply at the off-equilibrium nodes, or there's some weird work being done by the common knowlege of rationality assumption. But to me it just looks like a valid argument with an unflattering conclusion. Can someone sort out for me: is there a real paradox here, or is "the backward induction paradox" just a fancy name for a disappointment? And if there really is a problem with the argument, what exactly is it?
