I'm reading David Lewis's short paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (originally published in Philosophical Papers in 1997) for a seminar on philosophy of religion, and I want to write a short essay that does more than summarize it. Lewis's argument is built around an analogy: a guilty offender has been justly sentenced to punishment, and an innocent friend, out of devotion, voluntarily bears the punishment in the offender's place. Lewis's upshot is that we find such a thing absurd or repugnant in ordinary life, and that we should therefore be suspicious of the Christian doctrine of penal substitution, which says that God punishes the innocent Christ in the place of guilty sinners.

Write a 1,200–1,500-word essay that (a) reconstructs the structure of Lewis's analogy-based argument as carefully as you can, including the specific objections he develops from it — in particular the double-jeopardy point (the punishment being paid twice over, by the friend and by the offender, who is still punished in being condemned and stripped of the friend's devotion) and the disproportion point (the innocent suffering far more than the guilty deserve); and (b) decides whether the argument succeeds. You may defend Lewis's conclusion, or you may try to rescue penal substitution from it — for instance by arguing that the analogy misdescribes the doctrine, that the analogy is inapplicable because God is not a third party who punishes himself, or that the intuitions Lewis is eliciting are not the ones that should decide the case — but whatever you do, you must land on a verdict and defend it rather than ending on a both-sides note. In doing so, address the strongest possible version of the opposing position, not a caricature.
