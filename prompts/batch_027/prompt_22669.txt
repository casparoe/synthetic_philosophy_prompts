I got into a pretty heated argument with my friend last night about methodological individualism vs. holism in social science, and we went around in circles for like two hours without either of us budgeing. He's a committed individualist: any social phenomenon is either really just a bunch of individual people doing things and making decisions, or it's a sloppy way of talking that should get unpacked into individual-level claims, and he thinks the holists are smuggling in a spooky "social will" that isn't really there. I was arguing the other side — that things like norms, institutions, and the "culture" of a place are real group-level things (the Durkheimian idea that social facts are external to and coercive over the individual, roughly) that actually do causal work, and that if you flatten everything down to individual decisions you lose the explanation. We couldn't really settled it, so I thought maybe a story would do what argument didn't.

Can you write a short story that actually dramatizes this methodological tension instead of just talking about it? I want it set in one specific, small, closed-off place — a town, a co-op, a monastery, a village, whatever — where there's this one persistent, slightly strange custom or institution that everyone in the place participates in, and that no single person can point to as the one who "started" it. A newcomer arrives (a sociologist, an anthropologist, a data analyst, a journalist — pick one) and tries to figure out why everyone does it, and as the story unfolds it should keep yanking in both directions: at some moments the custom looks like it's fully explained by each person's own individual self-interest and choices, and at other moments it looks like the custom itself is this independent entity that's shaping and constraining the individuals. I want the ending to stay genuinely ambiguous about which level is "real" — please don't just resolve it in favor of the individualist or the holist. Keep it to a few pages, make the custom concrete and a little weird, and let the philosophy come out through what actually happens rather than through characters lecturing each other.
