After thirty-odd years in experimental nuclear physics, I retired two years ago and, much to my family's amusement, have taken up philosophy — specifically the parts of it that still smell of the laboratory: epistemology, the philosophy of probability, and decision theory. A former postdoc of mine, who now teaches a graduate seminar on statistical reasoning under uncertainty, asked me to serve as a second reader on one of his problem sets while he was away at a conference. One question concerned imprecise probabilities, and I have set out below the question as I posed it and one student's attempt at it. I want to be fair, but I do not want a confident, well-phrased answer to slide past a real fault in it. Could you give me a careful, point-by-point assessment — for each part, whether the student is right, wrong, or partially right, and where the answer is faulty, exactly what the fault is and how a careful grader should weigh it — together with a single grade out of ten and the specific marginal comments I should write on the paper? I would rather you err on the side of the careful grader than the generous one, though I do not want you to be pedantic about mere wording.

The question read as follows: "An urn contains only red and blue balls. You are told nothing except that the proportion of red balls, p, lies in the interval [0.4, 0.6]. Let K be the credal set of all Bernoulli distributions with P(red) in [0.4, 0.6]. Consider two tickets: X pays one dollar if a randomly drawn ball is red and zero otherwise; Y pays one dollar if the drawn ball is blue and zero otherwise. (a) Give the natural extension (the coherent price interval) for the ticket X. (b) Your colleague argues that because the interval in (a) is symmetric about 0.5, the framework really assigns probability 0.5 to red, and that 0.5 is the unique coherent probability one should use. Assess this claim. (c) Your colleague further claims that the lower prevision of the combined ticket X + Y equals L(X) + L(Y). Compute the lower prevision of X + Y and say whether the additivity claim is correct."

The student's answer was as follows: "(a) The lower prevision is L(X) = inf over P in K of P(red) = 0.4, and the upper prevision is U(X) = sup over P in K of P(red) = 0.6, so the natural extension is the interval [0.4, 0.6]. It is coherent to buy X at any price up to forty cents and to sell X at any price of at least sixty cents. (b) I agree with my colleague. Since the interval is symmetric, the midpoint 0.5 is the unique point that the coherence constraints actually select, and it is the least-committal choice available; for all practical purposes one should simply use 0.5. (c) Because the lower prevision is linear, L(X + Y) = L(X) + L(Y) = 0.4 + 0.4 = 0.8."
