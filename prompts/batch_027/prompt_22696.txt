I've been reading a lot about time-inconsistent preferences lately — the hyperbolic discounting literature, and the whole "Ulysses contract" and precommitment apparatus that comes up so often on the rationalist forums — and it has me wondering whether it is really the same phenomenon that the philosophers mean by akrasia, or whether we have been quietly conflating two different things. I raised it at a dinner with two friends, and they got into it in a way I could not referee. One, let's call her Mara, keeps insisting that hyperbolic discounting is not akrasia at all; the other, Dev, thinks it is actually a cleaner case of akrasia than the stock "I meant to go to the gym" examples.

Here is roughly how it went. Mara opened by saying that akrasia, properly understood, is acting against one's own *synchronous* judgment: at the very moment of action, one judges A to be the best thing to do and then does B. Hyperbolic discounting does not produce that. At any given moment the discounting agent's preferences are internally consistent — given where it is on the clock, it genuinely judges the immediate option to be better, and there is no gap between its judgment and its action. What changes is the judgment itself, as time passes, in exactly the way that someone who prefers coffee in the morning and tea in the afternoon does not have a weakness of will but simply has time-indexed preferences. So what we have been calling "weakness of will," she said, is really just ordinary, unremarkable preference change over time, and the label "akrasia" has been doing a lot of work it cannot support.

Dev pushed back on two fronts. First, he said the synchronous-judgment criterion is too narrow and quietly assumes the very thing in dispute. The hallmark of the hyperbolically discounting agent is that it *knows* its future self will flip: at the planning stage it judges that it would be better to follow the plan, and at the moment of temptation it does the impulsive thing anyway. So there is a violated judgment after all — just a diachronic or higher-order one, a judgment about what the agent ought to do given what it knows about its own future behavior. Second, he said that the move "I override my past self all the time, that is just how I change my mind" is doing too much work. Changing one's mind in light of new evidence is one thing; being systematically captured by a transient, steeply-discounted impulse is another. The signature of the hyperbolic agent, he said, is precisely that it would *pay to precommit* — it would prefer to be a creature that does not have this preference-reversal property, but it cannot bring itself to be one. That "I know I should commit but I cannot" is, he argued, the very signature of weakness of will, not its absence.

Mara conceded that the agent would pay to precommit, but said that fact does not make it akratic; it only makes the agent's preferences *expensive*, in the way that a person with a strong but mistaken fear of flying is paying a cost. And she returned to the multiple-selves framing: if each time-slice is treated as a distinct agent, then the present slice is not violating any of its *own* judgments at all — it is just a different agent making a different choice, the way I can rationally refuse to honor a promise my younger self made. Dev replied that the multiple-selves framing does not dissolve the problem, it only relocates it. If the present slice is a different agent, then it is *harming* a future slice it genuinely cares about, which is a form of akrasia at the individual level; and the fact that the agent cannot "just make a plan and stick to it" is exactly the weakness of will that Mara wants to deny. They were still going when I had to leave, and I honestly could not tell who was more right. So please go through their exchange and tell me, move by move, who is right about which specific point — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
