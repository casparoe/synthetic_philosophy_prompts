I spent most of my career as a physicist, and Gauss has always been one of the figures I most admire — not merely for his contributions to electromagnetism and number theory, but for the disciplined way he insisted that mathematical claims be backed by genuine constructive content, and for his well-known impatience with the treatment of the infinite as though it were a completed object. It is only in retirement that I have begun to read seriously about the foundations of mathematics, and I have recently come across Brouwer's intuitionism, which strikes me at first glance as a natural, if considerably more radical, descendant of the constructive sensibility that Gauss embodied.

I would be very interested in your considered reconstruction of how Gauss might have received Brouwer's program, had he lived to see it. In particular, I am curious about two points. First, would Gauss have endorsed Brouwer's rejection of the law of excluded middle for propositions about infinite domains, or would he have regarded that move as going beyond the constructive standards he himself applied? Second, how would he have reacted to the more idiosyncratic devices of mature intuitionism — the free choice sequence, the constructive reconstruction of the continuum, and the associated claim that classical analysis proves more than is constructively warranted? My own suspicion, offered only as a starting point, is that Gauss would have sympathized with the constructive core of the program while resisting its more radical departures, but I would welcome a reasoned argument rather than a bare verdict, and I am happy to be corrected.
