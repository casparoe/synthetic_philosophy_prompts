Compare the St. Petersburg paradox (a fair coin is flipped until the first heads, with payoff 2^n if the first heads lands on the nth flip, so the expected value diverges to +∞) with the Pasadena paradox (payoff (−1)^{n+1} 2^n with probability 2^{−n}, so the expected value is the Grandi series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ⋯, which is not merely infinite but undefined). In what sense is the Pasadena paradox the more fundamental of the two for expected utility theory? In particular, explain why the standard responses to St. Petersburg — bounding utility, invoking finite wealth, or switching to a different decision rule — do not straightforwardly carry over, and whether any generalized notion of a 'fair price' for the Pasadena game, such as one obtained by Cesàro or Abel summation or by grouping the outcomes into blocks that each contribute zero in expectation, can be defended as the correct answer.
