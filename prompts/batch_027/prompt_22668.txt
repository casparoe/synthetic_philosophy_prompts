I'm working through the judgment-aggregation literature for a social-choice reading group (I mostly came to it via the social-choice corner of the rationalist blogs, so I'm a bit behind on the finer points), and two members of the group, Marina and Dev, got into a real fight over how to escape the discursive dilemma — the one where three internally consistent judges each endorse a pair of premises and a conclusion that follows from them, yet majority-voting each proposition separately yields a majority for both premises and a majority *against* the conclusion. I'm reporting this as a bystander and I genuinely don't know who's more right. The exchange ran roughly like this. (1) Marina argued the group should vote on the premises and then derive the conclusion, because the conclusion is a logical function of the premises and a deliberative body that is bound by what follows from its own beliefs is doing the whole job; to let the conclusion be voted on independently is to license a group that is committed to a self-contradictory set of beliefs, which she took to be the deepest possible failure a rational group can commit. (2) Dev replied that this assumes the conclusion is derivative, whereas empirically and psychologically it usually isn't — judges and ordinary agents form the verdict first and then construct the reasons, so if the conclusion is the primary attitude, conclusion-first voting is the *honest* procedure and premise-voting is just ex post rationalization laundered through a fake derivation; on top of that, the stakes actually attach to the conclusion, not to the intermediate propositions. (3) Marina answered that even if individuals form conclusions first, the point of a court or a deliberative body is to be bound by logic rather than to mirror the psychology of how its members happened to arrive at their views, so she accused him of describing how irrational agents form beliefs and then proposing to institutionalize that irrationality; the derivation is precisely what makes it a collective judgment rather than a popularity contest over the headline. (4) Dev came back with two points: first, the "derivation" is not neutral, it is a choice of which proposition counts as basic, and he is not obliged to accept her choice; second, there is a strategic problem, because if the rule is premise-first, a judge who cares only about the outcome can vote strategically on the premises to push the derived conclusion their way, whereas sincere voting on the conclusion is blocked — so premise-first is manipulable in exactly the way that matters, and conclusion-first at least lets people register their real bottom-line commitment. (5) I then told them both about the standard impossibility result in this area (List and Pettit, 2002), which says there is no aggregation procedure that simultaneously satisfies the minimal desiderata — unrestricted domain, individual and collective rationality, and systematicity — and about the follow-up possibility theorem (List, 2003), which shows the only way out is a domain restriction of a particular structural kind, so I asked whether "just pick which end to vote on first" is actually a solution or merely a choice of which inconsistency to hide. (6) Marina conceded the point and said it was therefore a tie between the two orderings, and that the genuine fix is the domain restriction, which she was willing to accept because it at least tells you not to aggregate over the proposition structures where the dilemma is live; Dev objected that the domain restriction is doing enormous hidden work, that it tells you in advance which questions you are not permitted to ask of a group, and that this is a substantive epistemic and political loss rather than a free lunch, so he would rather keep the unrestricted domain and simply be explicit that we are choosing a priority ordering and living with the fact that it is a choice, not a theorem.
