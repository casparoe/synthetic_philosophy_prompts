Examination question:
In the Philosophical Investigations, Wittgenstein's treatment of the "private language" is often discussed under two headings. First, in §256 he characterizes a private language as a language which "describes my inner experiences" and as one "which only I myself can understand." Second, in the diary example someone writes "S" in his diary whenever a certain sensation recurs, and the claim is made that there is "no criterion of correctness" for the use of "S."
(a) Distinguish the two characterizations in §256 and explain why the second is best treated as a disputed consequence of the first rather than as part of the definition.
(b) Outline the "Russellian" route by which one might try to derive the incomprehensibility of a private name from the privacy of its referent, and explain why this route fails, referring to the token/type distinction.
(c) In the diary example, what exactly is the diarist supposed to be unable to do, and how should one distinguish the "memory-scepticism" reading of the "no criterion of correctness" point from the "meaning-scepticism" reading?

Student's answer:
(a) In §256 Wittgenstein characterizes a private language by two features: it describes my inner experiences, and it is a language which only I myself can understand. These are related but not identical. The first — that the language names immediate private sensations — is the definitional core. The second — that no other person can understand it — is a consequence, but as Craig and others have argued, it is a disputed consequence; it should be separated from the first and treated as something requiring independent argument rather than as part of the definition. It is therefore better to take the first feature as what makes a language "private" and to regard the second as a claim that must be established.
(b) The Russellian route begins from Russell's acquaintance principle: to understand a proposition in which the name of a particular occurs, one must already be acquainted with that particular. If a private name refers to a token sensation — a particular that occurs only in the diarist's mind — then no one else can be acquainted with it, and so no one else can understand the name. This would yield the incomprehensibility result (the second §256 feature) from the privacy result.
The route fails, however, because Wittgenstein takes the sensations in question to be types, not tokens (cf. §258). Russell himself holds that two people can be acquainted with the same property or type, including properties of private objects. So even if the diarist is acquainted with a certain type of sensation, that is no barrier, on Russell's own view, to others also being acquainted with it. The acquaintance requirement, which blocks understanding of a name of a token, does not block understanding of a name of a type. The Russellian argument therefore cannot derive incomprehensibility from privacy. Since this route is unavailable, Wittgenstein's real argument must be the criterion-of-correctness argument, and the Russellian consideration is merely a digression.
(c) In the diary example the diarist is supposed to be unable to remember the connexion right in the future. This means, on the natural reading, that he has no criterion for whether he is correctly applying "S" to the sensation he now has: he cannot check that the sensation before him is the same as the one he originally named. This is the memory-scepticism reading of the "no criterion of correctness" point: the diarist might simply misremember which sensation was the one he called "S." The meaning-scepticism reading, by contrast, holds that there is no fact of the matter about the meaning of "S" at all, independent of the diarist's memory. Either way, the upshot is that the use of "S" cannot be governed by a standard of correctness, which is what makes a private language impossible.
