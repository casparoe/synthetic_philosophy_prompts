The assignment read as follows: "Leibniz holds that no two distinct objects can share all their properties (the identity of indiscernibles). In a 1952 paper, Max Black offered a counterexample: imagine a universe containing exactly two identical iron spheres, each one mile in diameter, at rest, the same temperature, and fixed two miles apart, with absolutely nothing else in the universe. Black argues that the two spheres are numerically distinct yet share all their properties, so the principle is false. (a) Does Black's example succeed? (b) A defender of the principle replies that the spheres do differ: the first sphere bears the relation 'being two miles from' to the second sphere, while the second sphere bears it to the first, so they differ in the relations they bear. Evaluate this reply. (c) What is, in your view, the strongest objection to Black's example, and does it succeed?"

The student's response was:

"(a) Black's example is meant to refute Leibniz's principle, and I think it largely succeeds. The two spheres share every intrinsic property — same diameter, colour, temperature, composition, and so on — and because the universe contains nothing else, there is no third object or external feature that could tell them apart. So we appear to have two numerically distinct objects with all the same properties, which is exactly what the principle says is impossible. Black has, then, shown that Leibniz's Law is false.

(b) I don't think the defender's reply works. The relation 'being two miles from' is symmetric, so the property of being two miles from the other sphere is one that both spheres have: the first is two miles from the second, and the second is two miles from the first. A property that two objects share cannot distinguish them, so this does not rescue the principle.

(c) The strongest objection to Black's example, I think, is that the spheres do in fact differ in their spatial locations. The first sphere is at one place and the second sphere is at a different place, two miles away. Location is a genuine property, and since the two spheres occupy different locations, they do not after all share all their properties. The example therefore fails, and the identity of indiscernibles survives."
