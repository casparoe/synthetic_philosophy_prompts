I've just finished a long and fairly technical argument with a friend about the problem of logical omniscience, and we ended up at an impasse over the so-called "slow reasoner" case. My friend keeps insisting that the standard solution is perfectly adequate: the knowledge operator in epistemic logic is only meant to range over idealized, deductively closed agents, so a real, finite agent who has not yet carried out a derivation simply is not the sort of thing the operator is about, and there is no paradox to resolve. I've come away thinking that this move quietly concedes the point, and I want to see the other side argued properly rather than deflected.

Please argue that the slow reasoner problem is a genuine counterexample to the closure principle—the principle that if an agent knows p and knows that p entails q, then the agent knows q, which in modal logic is the axiom K—and that the correct response is to reject closure outright, i.e. to give up the K axiom, rather than to restrict the domain of the knowledge operator to idealized agents or to explain the case away in terms of resource-boundedness. In making the case, please be precise about what exactly is being rejected and what is being preserved: in particular, whether giving up K also forces us to give up other standard principles such as the thesis that knowledge entails belief or the positive-introspection axiom, and what the resulting weaker modal system looks like. And please address the two objections my friend pressed hardest. First, that if we reject closure we lose the ability to model even ordinary reasoning, since the agent would then "not know" trivial consequences of what it knows, which renders the knowledge operator useless for anything beyond tautologies. Second, that the slow reasoner's failure to know the conclusion is better explained by a failure of justification, or by the agent's finite computational resources, than by a failure of closure per se—that is, that the problem lies with the agent, not with the logic. I'd like the argument to stand on its own without appealing to any particular published resolution.
