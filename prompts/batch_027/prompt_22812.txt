I am writing a novel in which a present-day philosopher of logic revisits F. H. Bradley's regress and the problem of the unity of the proposition, and I want to get Bradley's likely reaction to a move that post-dates him. The move is the standard formal-semantic treatment of predication: the predicate is said to be satisfied by the subject, or, equivalently, the proposition is given a definite logical form and truth-conditions, and this is held to be enough to make 'a is F' a single thought rather than a mere aggregate — so that no independent 'relation of predication' has to be posited, and Bradley's regress, which only bites on relations, is supposed to lose its grip. On Bradley's own principles, how would he have answered this? Would he treat satisfaction, or the logical form of the proposition, as a genuinely non-relational unifier that dissolves the regress, or would he regard it as relocating the very problem the regress exposes — the satisfying or relating item itself needing a further relation to do the work — and so as just one more appearance within the one Absolute?
