I've been going down a rabbit hole on Grice's implicature stuff lately — mostly old LessWrong threads and a few scattered papers — and I keep thinking the whole "what's said vs. what's implicated" distinction is begging to be a story. So here's what I'm after: a short story, maybe 1500-2000 words, set in a near-future where the courts have adopted a rule that people are only held legally responsible for the explicit content of their utterances — the literal sentence meaning — and explicitly not for conversational implicatures. The protagonist is a defense attourney who's built a whole practice around this loophole: her clients get coached to communicate only through flouted maxims and particularized implicatures, so they've never literally commited to anything.

The case she's defending: her client, in a heated argument, said to the plaintiff "Of course I'll be there for you — I'd never let you down," and now the plaintiff is suing, claiming that was a binding promise. The defense's line is that the literal content is just a constative assertion about the client's character and intentions, not a performative promise, and that the "promise" reading is at best an implicature — and a cancelled one at that, since the client knew full well they were going to bail (so the maxim of quality was flouted, not observed). I want the climax to turn on the precise Gricean machinery: is the commitment the explicit content or a detachable implicature, does the flouting cancel the implicature, and what does "speaker meaning" even amount to here when the speaker's intentions are at war with their words?

I'd love for the ending to stay genuinely ambiguous about whether the client "meant" what they did, and for the story to actually get the concepts right — the Cooperative Principle, the maxims, flouting, detachability — instead of just hand-waving "people don't always mean what they say." Feel free to give the plaintiff a philosopher friend who keeps asking annoying questions, and make the judge someone who's only half-convinced that implicatures are a real legal category.
