I am a third-year philosophy PhD student working on the extended mind thesis, and I have been trying to find a narrative scenario that genuinely discriminates among the positions rather than merely illustrating one. Please write a short story, roughly 800 to 1,500 words, that works as such a test case. The protagonist is a field ecologist with early-onset amnestic deficits who has built an elaborate external system: a red notebook, cross-referenced index cards, and fixed routines of transcription that she follows without critical reflection. The notebook is load-bearing for her professional life and for her sense of who she is. The story must reveal, only in its final scene, that the notebook has been tampered with: someone has subtly altered entries she had trusted, and one of the altered entries has already led her to act. The philosophical content should be carried entirely by the plot and the prose, not by the protagonist lecturing, but the scenario should be constructed so that the reader can feel which conditions would be doing the work: availability, reliability, trust, and the question, in the spirit of Adams and Aizawa, whether mere reliable coupling is enough for the notebook to be part of her mind at all, rather than only causally involved in her thinking. The ending must remain genuinely ambiguous between the reading on which the tampering is a violation of the person and the reading on which it is a violation of a tool; do not resolve the question, and do not have any character state a philosophical position outright.
