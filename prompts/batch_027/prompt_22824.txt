So I'm in the middle of drafting this novel set on a small island that just got out of a civil war, and the faction that won is now sitting down to write a constiution, and I want one of their leaders to give a speech justifying the new order by appealing to Pascal. I've been reading the "Justice and Force" fragment in the Pensées, the one where he says "justice without force is impotent; force without justice is tyrannical," and ends with "not being able to make what is just, strong, one made what is strong, just." And I'm getting a little tangled up on what he's actually claiming, because I keep flip-flopping on whether he's describing what actually happens to law (i.e., that the right we end up following is just the right of whoever won, dressed up as justice) or prescribing what law ought to be (that the strong should be brought under the constraint of genuine justice). These feel like two different moves and I can't tell if he's doing both at once.

I guess the specific thing I'm stuck on is his argument about the original seizure of property — the idea that the first taking of something is really an act of force, and that this first act somehow establishes a right that everyone then recognizes, so that all our laws ultimately trace back to that original act of force rather than to any real standard of rightness. If that's right, then it sounds like he's just saying might makes right, which feels like a weird thing for a character in my novel to defend, and also like a weird thing for a moralist like Pascal to actually beleive. But then he also talks about this demand that the just be made strong and the strong be made just, which sounds like a genuine requirement, not a description of what happens. So how do these fit together? Is that demand something that's actually achievable, or is it more of an ideal we can see we're always falling short of, or is it just a way of naming the gap between what is and what ought to be? I'd like to understand this well enough that I can write the leader's speech as a plausible (even if ultimately wrong) reading of Pascal, rather than just putting "might makes right" in his mouth.
