I retired from statistics a few years back (I spent most of my career on signal detection and sensor fusion, so I think about these things in terms of noise and averaging), and I've recently gotten into the philosophy of collective decision-making, or at least the economics of it. I cant quite see why two results I keep running into seem to be in direct tension. On one side is the Condorcet jury theorem: if you have a crowd of voters, each with a private signal that's a little better than a coin flip, and the signals are independent, then majority rule gets more and more reliable as the crowd grows, and the chance of the right answer goes to 1. That's just the law of large numbers to me, and I'm comfortable with it. On the other side is the classic information cascade model (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, Welch, that sort of thing): people make a binary decision one at a time, they can see what everyone before them did, and once a cascade kicks in, each new person rationally ignores their own private signal and just copies the crowd. And the punchline is that the cascade's accuracy doesn't keep improving as you add more people—it plateaus at something below 1, so you can have arbitrarily large crowds making a decision that's only a little better than a coin flip.

What I keep failing to internalize is why the setup changes things so completely. In both cases we've got agents with private signals that are a bit better than chance, so intuitively I expect the noise to average out in both. So could you compare the two models for me, and be really precise about the information structure? Specifically: what exactly does the combination of sequentiality and public observability of actions do to the independence of the signals, and why does that kill the averaging effect that the jury theorem depends on? And I keep hearing that once a cascade starts, it conveys no new information—can you make that claim precise? Is the idea that the crowd's action freezes the posterior probability at some value, and that's why the accuracy stops improving? I'd like a real account of the mechanism, not a hand-wave about people being herd animals.
