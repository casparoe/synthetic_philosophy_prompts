I'm preparing material for a discussion on the aggregation of tiny harms, and I keep returning to a version of the torture-versus-dust-specks case that I think exposes a tension the standard presentations tend to smooth over. The setup is the familiar one: you must choose between (T) torturing one person and (D) causing a single grain of dust to enter the eye of each of a very large fixed number of people — say a billion — where each dust speck is a genuine but trivial harm, not something so negligible that it fails to count as a harm at all. I'd like a short dialogue between two people who disagree about which option to choose, but I want the disagreement to be a specific, structured one rather than the generic "aggregatist versus non-aggregatist" contrast.

The first speaker should be a total utilitarian who is fully committed to aggregation: holding that each dust speck contributes some positive amount of disvalue, they should accept that once the number of dust specks is large enough, the total disvalue of (D) exceeds that of (T), so that the option which minimizes total harm is in fact to carry out the torture. The second speaker should work from a person-affecting principle — on which one outcome is better than another only if it is better for at least one person and not worse for any person — and should make the point that, on this view, (T) and (D) are not ranked against each other at all: (D) is better for the person who would otherwise be tortured, but it is worse for each of the billion who would receive a dust speck, so neither option is better than the other. I want the dialogue to take that incomparability seriously rather than treating it as a cop-out. The total utilitarian should press the objection that "incomparable" is not itself a decision — in the actual situation someone either is tortured or a billion people get dust in their eyes — and that a moral theory which never yields a verdict in a live case is not doing its work; the person-affecting theorist should answer by supplying a tie-breaking rule for cases of incomparability (for example, a lexical priority on avoiding the worst individual outcome, or a rights-based constraint against torture) and by defending the claim that incomparability reflects a genuine feature of the case rather than a defect in the theory. I'd also like at least one exchange in which they confront the fact that the total utilitarian's own verdict depends on the number of dust specks being above the precise threshold at which their summed disvalue overtakes the disvalue of the single torture, a threshold that turns on a ratio of harms that in practice no one can measure, and whether that epistemic fragility undercuts the apparent decisiveness of the "just add them up" answer.

Please keep both speakers competent and fair to their own positions, with genuine back-and-forth in which each responds to the other's actual points rather than restating their own, and aim for roughly six to ten exchanges.
