I've been thinking about David Hume's essays on money and trade, and I keep coming back to what looks like a tension between two of his arguments. In one essay he argues that a sudden increase in a nation's money supply benefits industry in the short run: wages and the demand for labor rise before prices fully adjust, so for a time there is more employment and more real wealth. In another he argues that trade surpluses self-correct: the inflow of gold raises domestic prices, exports become uncompetitive, imports become cheap, and the money flows back out. But if a trade surplus injects money into an economy, and that injection is what produces the short-run benefit, doesn't the very mechanism that corrects the surplus also destroy the benefit? Write a dialogue between two well-read friends trying to settle this: one insists that Hume's short-run argument undermines his long-run argument, and the other insists the two are perfectly consistent. Both should make their strongest case and respond directly to each other's actual points, and the dialogue should be allowed to end without the tension being fully resolved.
