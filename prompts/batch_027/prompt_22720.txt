I've been thinking about something called the "wrong kind of reasons" problem, which I came across while reading about value. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that some things that seem like reasons to value something are actually of the "wrong kind" — for example, the fact that something is widely praised is not, on the standard view, a genuine reason to value it, but rather a reason to *believe* that it is valuable. I've been trying to understand whether this distinction is really a substantive philosophical point or just a way of talking. I'm particularly confused about how we're supposed to distinguish between a "wrong kind" reason and a "right kind" reason in a principled way, rather than just by intuition. Is there a clear criterion, or is the whole idea just a way of capturing a vague intuition? I'm also not sure whether the problem is specific to *value* or whether it generalizes to other normative concepts, like "reasons to believe something is true" or "reasons to be motivated to act." I'd appreciate a clear explanation of the conceptual issue, ideally with some examples that make the distinction feel less ad hoc.
