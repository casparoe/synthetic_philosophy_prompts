I am working on an essay about Aquinas's view on tyrannicide, and I keep running into a tension that I cannot quite resolve, so I would like you to argue for one particular reading of it. Aquinas insists, as a general rule, that killing an evildoer is lawful only for someone who exercises public authority, and that a private individual may not simply take the law into his own hands. Yet when he turns to tyranny he says it is praiseworthy to kill the tyrant in order to free the country, and he even appeals to Cicero's defense of the men who killed Caesar, who were ordinary private citizens rather than officials. Please argue that the right way to make these two claims consistent is to hold that a private person's killing of a tyrant is lawful only in so far as it counts as an exercise of public authority on behalf of the whole community, rather than as a private act of revenge or self-defense. In doing so, explain why the tyrant's ruling for his own private good rather than for the common good is what allows the community to assume that public role for itself, and address why this reading is superior to the alternative that Aquinas is simply granting a private right of resistance.
