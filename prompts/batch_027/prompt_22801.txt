I'm taking a graduate course in formal epistemology and decision theory this term, and we spent a week on the expected value of information, where the instructor sketched what's usually called Good's theorem: that for a standard expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of any available information is non-negative, since the agent can always choose to ignore the signal and behave exactly as it would have without it. I found the one-line proof — that the informed optimum weakly dominates the best uninformed action once you take expectations over the signal — to be almost suspiciously clean, and I've been trying since to figure out whether the theorem is as general as it looks or whether it quietly depends on assumptions that fail in interesting cases.

The cases that keep coming back to me are (i) information that is in some sense about the agent's own future choice, of the kind that shows up in Newcomb-style problems, where learning what you will do seems to change the payoff landscape rather than merely refine beliefs about a fixed world; (ii) strategic settings in which acquiring information can be a liability because it changes what other rational players expect you to do; and (iii) the way the result looks different if you reason causally about your own actions versus evidentially about them. I'm not sure these are genuine counterexamples to Good's result or merely cases where the "you can always ignore the information" maneuver is no longer available — for instance, in the self-referential case I can't even be confident that "ignore the information" names a well-defined strategy, since the act of deciding to ignore it may be what the information is about. I can't tell whether the right verdict is that the theorem is trivially true but uninteresting, that it is a deep and robust fact about rationality, or that it is an artifact of a narrow model of agents with a fixed action set and a well-behaved probability measure.

Please write an essay that takes up this controversy and actually reaches a conclusion. I want you to lay out the standard argument for the non-negativity of the value of information and state precisely which assumptions it uses, then weigh how seriously each of the three kinds of cases above threatens those assumptions, and finally commit to a verdict about the scope and significance of Good's theorem — whether it is a universal principle, a theorem about a specific decision-theoretic idealization, or something in between — and defend that verdict against the strongest objection.
