Two friends of mine got into a long argument at a dinner party about the idea that even small physical things have something like a tiny experience of their own, and, if that is true, how that could ever add up to a person having a single, unified experience. I have been reading about this on my own, so I followed along, but I could not tell who was making the better points.

One of them, Ben, said the standard objection is overblown. If every particle has a tiny experience, then a brain is just a vast collection of them, and the total is my experience, the same way a glass of water is just a vast collection of water molecules. There is nothing mysterious about a whole being made of its parts.

Clara, the other, said that is exactly where it goes wrong. A collection of water molecules is still just molecules; nothing new has been added, and the glass is not a collection of many glasses. But a collection of experiences, if it is an experience at all, has to be one experience, not many. And saying to just add them up does not even state what the addition would be, because all we know what it is like to be is a single subject, not what it is like to be a sum of subjects.

Ben replied that maybe there is no single experience to be explained at all. My brain might be more like a city of micro-minds, and I am just the society. A city is made of people and is not one person, so the same way.

Clara said that if that is his answer, then the city itself has to be the one having the experience of the dinner party, and he has just moved the problem up one level. And if instead he says that I am nothing over and above the micro-minds, then he still has to explain why it seems to me like one thing to be me, rather than like many things.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Can you go through their exchange and say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
