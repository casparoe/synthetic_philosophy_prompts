Let me set up the background: the paradox is the claim that if you assume every true statement is in principle knowable, you can formally prove that every true statement is actually known, which is absurd; so the assumption must be false.

Friend A said the paradox is airtight and really does show that some truths are forever beyond our grasp. The logic is valid, so the only escape is to reject the starting assumption.

Friend B said that's a misreading. The trick, he argued, is that the proof considers a statement like "the earth is old, and no one knows the earth is old." It then applies the same assumption — that anything true is knowable — to this new statement. But this statement is self-undermining: the moment anyone comes to know the earth is old, the "no one knows" part turns false, so the whole thing can never actually be known. Friend B's point was that the assumption "anything true is knowable" was supposed to apply to ordinary facts, not to these self-undermining constructions, so the proof is just exploiting a gap in the wording rather than revealing a real limit on knowledge.

Friend A replied that the self-undermining nature is exactly the engine of the proof, not a flaw in it — the whole reason the contradiction arises is that the statement can't be known, and that's precisely what forces you to give up the assumption. If you just carve out the weird statements, you've already assumed the conclusion.

Friend B said that's not assuming the conclusion, it's noticing that the original assumption was always meant to be modest — a claim about ordinary facts — and that the paradox only looks powerful because it stretches "knowable" to cover things it was never supposed to.
