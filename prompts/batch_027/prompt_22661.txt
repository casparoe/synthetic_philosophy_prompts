I retired a few years ago after a long career in experimental physics, and I have recently taken it upon myself to read a little philosophy for pleasure. I find the epistemology of belief particularly engaging, because it is so close to the kind of reasoning I did in the lab. In that spirit I have been trying to work through the Dutch-book argument, and I believe I understand its broad shape, though I am stuck on a detail that I increasingly suspect is the crux of the whole matter.

As I understand it, the argument is supposed to show that an agent whose credences fail to satisfy the axioms of probability can be offered a set of bets on which the agent is guaranteed to lose money. But here is where my picture breaks down. I keep imagining that the bookie must be loading the dice in some way — that each individual bet must carry a small edge in the bookie's favor, rather as a casino charges a vig, and that these little edges are what accumulate into the agent's certain loss. If the mechanism is really that, then the argument seems to be about the bookie's cleverness, or about the house edge, rather than about anything defective in the agent's beliefs. After all, an agent with perfectly sound beliefs could still lose money to a resourceful bookie over a long enough stretch.

So I would be very grateful if you could tell me whether my picture of the mechanism is correct. If it is not, I would like to understand what the argument is actually doing, and in particular how it manages to establish something about the rationality of the agent's credences rather than merely about the bookie's skill. I have the feeling that the answer turns on a subtle point about what it means for a single bet to count as fair, but I cannot quite pin down what that point is.
