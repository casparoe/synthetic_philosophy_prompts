I cover science and ideas for a living and I'm working on a long-form piece about how vague language quietly wrecks real decisions — like when conservationists have to declare a species "extinct" even though nobody can survey every last square meter of the planet, or when a court has to rule on whether a car was "abandoned." In my research I kept hitting this subfield of philosophy on vagueness, and I'm stuck on the difference between two of the main positions: epistemicism and supervaluationism. I know the rough shape — epistemicism says vague terms have sharp boundaries that we simply can't know, while supervaluationism says a vague statement is only true if it comes out true under every admissable "precisification" of the term — but I don't really get how the two diverge in practice, or why a philosopher would committ to one over the other.

Could you write a dialogue between a committed epistemicist and a committed supervaluationist, both of them sharp and well-read, in which they try to talk each other out of their view? I want real back-and-forth — each one should respond to the other's actual strongest points, not just recite their own talking points. Please ground it in a concrete case so it doesn't float in the abstract; the "is this species extinct yet?" question works well for me, or if you think a classic one like a heap of sand is cleaner, use that instead. And I'd really like them to land on the genuinely technical sticking points — higher-order vagueness, the objection that supervaluationists just relocate the vagueness into "admissibility," how each side defuses the sorites paradox, and the like — rather than staying at the surface. They don't have to end up agreeing, but I want a reader to come away seeing why this is a live, hard debate and not two people talking past each other.
