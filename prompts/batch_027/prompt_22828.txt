I'm taking a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century aesthetics this term, and we've just reached the section of Schopenhauer's *The World as Will and Representation* where he turns to music. I've been reading the English translation my course uses, and I keep hitting a passage I can't quite get straight, so I'd like help unpacking it rather than a general summary of what Schopenhauer "means by" music. Here is the chunk I'm stuck on:

"Music is as immediate an objectification and copy of the whole will as the world itself is, indeed as the Ideas are, the multiplied phenomenon of which constitutes the world of individual things. Therefore music is by no means like the other arts, namely a copy of the Ideas, but a copy of the will itself, the objectivity of which are the Ideas. For this reason the effect of music is so very much more powerful and penetrating than is that of the other arts, for these others speak only of the shadow, but music of the essence."

My confusion is this. Earlier in the book Schopenhauer makes the Ideas the "objectivity" of the will and the world of individual things the "multiplied phenomenon" of the Ideas, so the Ideas already look like an objectification of the will, one step removed from it. But in this passage he says music is "as immediate an objectification and copy of the whole will as the world itself is, indeed as the Ideas are," and yet also "by no means like the other arts, namely a copy of the Ideas." I can't tell whether he's placing music at the same level as the Ideas (both copies of the will) or above them (a more direct copy, since the Ideas are already a "multiplied phenomenon"). If music is a copy of the will in a way the Ideas are not, what exactly is the difference in kind between the two objectifications? And once that's clear, how does it follow that the other arts, which do copy the Ideas, therefore only "speak of the shadow"? I'd like you to work through the will–Ideas–individual-things hierarchy and then locate music within it, rather than just restating that music "expresses the will directly."

I also noticed that he sets the whole thing up against Leibniz's view that music is only an "unconscious exercise in arithmetic in which the mind does not know it is counting," and I'm not sure whether he's rejecting the mathematical account wholesale or merely saying it's insufficient. If you could say what the precise disagreement with Leibniz is, and how it bears on the shadow/essence point, that would help a lot.
