So this came up at our retro after we shipped a new feature on our dev-tools platform, and two of my colleagues got into it and I couldn't refere. Background: adoption spiked hard after a handfull of early users posted about it, and then basically everyone who'd been on the fence just turned it on without really trying it first.

Dana (our PM) said it's a textbook informational cascade — in the classic model, each person gets a noisy private read on whether the thing is good and also watches what everyone else did, and once enough people have adopted, the public record outweighs any single person's private assessment, so the rational move is to ignore your own hunch and just adopt. Marcus (an ML engineer) said no, that model assumes everyone knows the exact signal precision and that everyone else is doing the same Bayesian updating, i.e. common knowledge of the whole setup, and our users are obviously not computing log-likelihood ratios, they're just pattern-matching on "everyone else has it," which is social proof, not rational inference, so the model is describing a world that doesn't exist. Dana shot back that that objection applies to every rational-choice model, so it doesn't single out cascades, and the point is the mechanism, that public information can rationally swamp private information, whether or not anyone's doing explicit calculus. Marcus then said the part that's actually doing the work in the model is the absorbing cascade, where once it starts it never ends because everyone after just follows, and that's the most unrealistic part, since real cascades break when a bad review or a bug shows up. Dana said the absorbing thing is just an artifact of the irreversibility assumption, i.e. once you adopt you can't un-adopt, and if you allow reversibility or even a small chance someone acts on their own signal the cascade can unwind, so the basic model is just a limiting case. Marcus dropped one more before I stepped in: the model is purely informational, about what a choice reveals about the world, but a lot of real adoption is about the feature being more useful the more people use it, i.e. payoff interdependence, which is a different kind of cascade the model doesn't cover, so even in the best case it only explains part of what we saw.

I genuinely don't know who's more right. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall, and then say what both of them are missing?
