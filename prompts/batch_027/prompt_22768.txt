I was discussing this with a colleague in economics the other day and I came away a bit confused. We were talking about the social discount rate and the Ramsey rule (r = δ + η·g), and I keep seeing peope in the climate literature treat the pure rate of time preference δ as if it's the moral core of the whole thing — the part that's doing the ethical work of deciding how much we care about future generations. But when I actually plug in the numbers, it seems like most of the discount rate comes from the η·g term, not from δ.

So I'd like you to argue that the pure rate of time preference is actually doing less moral work than its advocates and critics both seem to think, and that the real ethical debate is about η and g — and about the pure vs. impure nature of discounting more generally. I'm especially interested in the 'fiction' of pure time preference: the idea that all real discounting is impure (arising from mortality risk, opportunity cost, uncertainty, changing tastes, and so on) and that pure time preference — caring less about a good just because it's later — is a mistake that the Ramsey rule bakes in as if it were a genuine preference.
