I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm writing a short essay for a reading group on distributed systems that meets monthly. The topic is the discursive dilemma from the judgment aggregation literature (Pettit 2001; List and Pettit 2002), which I keep coming back to because it looks exactly like a failure mode of our code review board: three reviewers each vote on whether a proposed change introduces a security vulnerability (p), whether it degrades performance (q), and whether it should be blocked (p∧q, where I model the blocking decision as the conjunction for simplicity), and the board's official finding is the propositionwise majority on each item. Below is my current draft. I'd like comments in three categories: first, on accuracy — which of my technical claims are defensible, which are too strong, and which are flat-out wrong; second, on the "escape routes" section, where I suspect I have mischaracterized the literature; and third, on interest — one or two suggestions for making the piece more engaging for an engineering audience that has never heard of judgment aggregation.

Draft: Our review board's problem is a textbook instance of what Pettit calls the discursive dilemma. Each reviewer is individually consistent: each votes on p, on q, and on the conjunction p∧q in a logically coherent way. But majority rule applied proposition by proposition can yield a collective judgment that is inconsistent: a majority holds p, a majority holds q, and yet a majority rejects p∧q. The collective "judge" is logically incoherent even though every individual judge is not. The key result here, due to List and Pettit, is an impossibility theorem: no aggregation rule that respects unanimity, is anonymous, and is propositionwise independent can guarantee a consistent collective judgment on agendas like this one. My reading is that this is essentially Arrow's theorem with extra labels — a voting paradox in disguise — and that it shows majority rule over logically connected propositions is inconsistent whenever the agenda contains logically related propositions. The obvious engineering fix is agenda control: the board should vote only on the conclusion (should the change be blocked?), or else the agenda should be fixed in advance to a set of propositions that cannot generate an inconsistency. I argue that this is the only realistic escape route, and that the other options discussed in the literature — premise-based voting, supermajority thresholds, and the like — are either dictatorial or merely move the problem.

Given my background, I expect I am wrong about the scope of the impossibility theorem and about the status of the escape routes, so please treat the draft as a set of claims to be checked rather than a settled account. In particular: does the theorem really cover any agenda containing logically related propositions, or only specific agenda structures (and if so, what does the relevant structure look like, and is my three-proposition example an instance of it)? Is it fair to call the result "Arrow's theorem in disguise," or do the two frameworks admit genuinely different escape routes? And does my dismissal of premise-based voting and supermajority rules hold up, or is there a version of one of them that genuinely resolves the dilemma at a cost I should be naming explicitly?
