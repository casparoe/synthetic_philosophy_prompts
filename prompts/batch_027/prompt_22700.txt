I'm a third-year graduate student in practical ethics, and I'm going to be moderating the Q&A for a panel at a conference on global justice next month. I've been reading the panelist's new book over the past few weeks, and I find her handling of the demandingness objection a bit underdeveloped — she argues that the duty only extends to "modest" sacrifices, not to all of our surplus income, but she never really says what counts as "modest" or who gets to draw that line. I also find her response to the arbitrary distance objection a bit too quick; she's been known to deflect it with the phrase "it's a matter of practical efficacy, not of moral kind," which I think papers over a real problem.

The panelist is defending the view that the drowning child thought experiment establishes a strict moral duty, not merely a supererogatory one, to aid distant strangers whose deaths we could prevent at a modest cost to ourselves. I'd like a sequence of five to seven questions that press on the strictness of the duty specifically. I want at least one question on whether the duty to save the nearby drowning child is of the same moral kind as the duty to give to effective charities, one on the negative-versus-positive-duty distinction and whether the strictness of the duty depends on classifying the case as one of omission rather than commission, one on what would count as a "modest" sacrifice and who gets to draw that line, and one on the arbitrary distance objection. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and end with a question about what empirical or philosophical evidence could count against the strictness of the duty.
