I've been following a reading group on moral uncertainty, and two members got into a long, good-faith argument about whether you can actually run expected-value calculations over competing moral theories. I sat on the fence, so I'd like you to referee.

M started from the view that moral uncertainty is handled the same way as ordinary uncertainty: assign each candidate theory a credence and pick the action that maximizes expected value across the theories you might be right about — expected utility applied one level up, from uncertainty about outcomes to uncertainty about the right value function.

R objected that this only works when the theories are commensurable. On most routine actions they agree, so no problem. But on the cases where they diverge, a rights-based theory may hold that an action is simply forbidden, attaching no numerical value to it, while a utilitarian assigns a number. You can't take a probability-weighted average of a number and a bare prohibition, so the expected value isn't hard to compute there — it's undefined.

M answered that the incommensurability is a defect in the theories, not in the meta-level procedure. At the meta-level, he said, you're not averaging the theories' values; you're comparing your own credence-weighted options, and that comparison is always well-defined.

R pushed back on the definition itself: the expected value of an action is a sum of value times probability over outcomes, and value has to live on a single numerical scale. If one of the theories in your probability distribution doesn't supply numbers, the sum doesn't exist. So the calculation is undefined in exactly the cases where you'd need it.

M then conceded the edge cases are hard, but argued that's a reason to apply the method carefully, not to abandon it: on the large majority of decisions the theories agree, the expected-value calculation works cleanly, and that's where most of your actual choices fall.

R closed by saying that conceding the hard cases doesn't rescue the method, because the entire point of the exercise is to guide you on the hard cases, and a tool that is undefined precisely where it's needed is, for its intended purpose, broken.

Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
