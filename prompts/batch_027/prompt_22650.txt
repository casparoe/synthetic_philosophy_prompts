Two people in my ethics seminar got into it after we finished the unit on negative utilitarianism, and I honestly couldn't follow who was winning.

Mara said negative utilitarianism is basically just utilitarianism with the sign flipped, so it inherits all the same problems. Her example was the repugnant-conclusion thing: on NU, a world with a huge population whose lives are slightly negative is worse than a world with just a few people in agony, and she thought that was intuitively wrong, which for her showed NU is no better off than the theory it's trying to fix.

Jon said that was exactly backwards and was actually the whole point. He said the repugnant part of the original conclusion is that a vast population with barely positive lives beats a smaller one with great lives, and NU flips it so a vast population with slightly negative lives is worse than a few people in agony, which is the intuitive, non-repugnant direction. So the thing Mara was calling a bug was NU's main selling point, and she was just objecting to the intuitive conclusion.

Mara came back with the suffering offset. She said if happiness can't offset suffering, which is the core of NU, then NU has to say a world with one person in moderate pain and everyone else in bliss is worse than a world with no pain but a lot less bliss, because only the pain counts. She thought that made NU weirdly insensitive to overall quality of life, like it only ever looks at the downside.

Jon replied that that's not a bug, it's the point: the offsetting idea, that enough happiness can cancel out suffering, is exactly what lets classical utilitarianism justify massive suffering if it produces enough joy, and NU denies that happiness is a good at all, so there's nothing to offset with. He added that NU doesn't have to say happy lives are bad, just that they're not a reason to trade away suffering, so her downside-only complaint was overstated.
