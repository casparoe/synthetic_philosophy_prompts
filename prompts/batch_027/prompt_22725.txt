I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a longform piece about why we treat some moral actions as heroic and others as merely expected, and I've been reading enough ethics to get confused about supererogation. Here is a draft paragraph I want to use: Supererogatory acts are usually described as things that are good to do but not required. The problem is that this definition makes the category sound purely negative: an act is supererogatory only if it fails to be obligatory. But many of the cases we care about, like donating a kidney to a stranger or rushing into a burning building, seem to depend on the social and institutional backround. A doctor in an emergency room may have a duty to act that a passerby does not, and a passerby's act can look heroic partly because the hospital system has not assigned them that duty. I want to argue that supererogation is not just a leftover category of morally optional good acts, but a way of tracking where our practices of role, expectation, and praise are doing work. If we made everyone a hero, heroism would disappear, but not because the action became less good; rather, because the background norm shifted.

Could you comment on whether this is a defensible way to frame supererogation, or whether I'm smuggling in a mistake by treating non-obligation as the main thing? I especially want to know if the kidney and burning building examples are bad in ways that would weaken the point, and if there is a cleaner in-the-weeds distinction I should be making between moral permissibility, obligation, and the kind of moral credit we give to people who do more than they have to.
