I'm giving a speech at a restorative justice circle this Saturday. The person who was wronged is my sister, and she's asked me to speak on her behalf because she finds it too hard to look the person in the eye. The room will have my sister, the person who did the wrong, a facilitator, and a few of our family and some community members who've been part of the process. The wrong was a long one — years of financial manipulation by a family member, the kind of thing that leaves you feeling like you can't trust your own memory of what actually happened.

I want the speech to do something specific: I want it to hold the line that my sister is allowed to be angry, that her anger isn't something to be "managed" or "resolved" by the person who did the wrong, but I also want it to leave a door open — not forgiveness exactly, but the acknowledgment that the person who did the wrong is genuinely trying to understand what they did. I was reading Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" last week and I keep comming back to this idea he has about the participant stance and the objective attitude, how the reactive attitudes are these second-personal things directed at the person rather than just at their behavior, and how even when you step back and excuse someone there's still this residual resentment that doesn't fully go away, some leftover that doesn't fully resolve. I want the speech to be honest about that leftover, not to pretend it's all clean and settled.

Can you write it? It should be maybe three to four minutes long, and it should land as something my sister would actually be okay with hearing, not as a philosophy lecture. I don't need it to name-drop Strawson or anything, but the idea should be doing the work underneath.
