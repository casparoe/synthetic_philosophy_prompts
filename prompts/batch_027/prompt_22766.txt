I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and my dissertation is on panpsychism and the combination problem. I've been spending the past few months on what I take to be the less-discussed "top-down" variant of the combination problem — the argument, developed most explicitly by Goff, that if you try to dodge the standard bottom-up subject-combination problem by positing a single conscious cosmos whose experience is then localized into the experiences of its parts (the cosmopsychist move, in the tradition of Strawson's realistic monism), you don't so much dissolve the problem as relocate it: you now have to explain how the one macro-subject's experience gives rise to the many differentiated, localized micro-experiences of its parts. I keep running into colleagues who treat cosmopsychism as the "easy escape" from the combination problem, and I'm not convinced that's right, but I want to make the case more carefully before I commit to it in my writing.

Could you describe the most important implications of this top-down combination problem for the panpsychist program as a whole? In particular, I'd like you to think through three things. First, whether the top-down problem undermines cosmopsychism as a way of avoiding the combination problem, or whether it merely shifts the burden of proof onto the cosmopsychist to supply a combination mechanism. Second, what it implies about the choice between bottom-up and top-down strategies — that is, whether the combination problem is best understood as a structural problem about the relation between levels of conscious subjects rather than as a problem specific to a particular direction of composition. And third, how it bears on whether panpsychism can be a genuinely constitutive account of macro-consciousness or whether it is forced into a merely explanatory role, offering at most a grain of truth about the fundamental nature of reality. Please be specific and avoid rehashing the standard bottom-up subject-combination and quality-combination problems, which I already understand.
