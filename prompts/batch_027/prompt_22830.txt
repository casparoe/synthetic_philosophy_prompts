I'm writing a short blog post explaining the Dutch book argument for probabilism, and two grad students in my study group got into it about whether the argument actually justifies the probability axioms or just restates them. I was the one taking notes, so I don't have a side. Here's the exchange:

(1) Rhea said the argument is circular. To even set up the bets you have to treat my credences as my fair prices, and that already assumes betting is the right way to cash out a degree of belief. You've smuggled the conclusion into the setup, so "incoherence" is just what you get after you've decided what incoherence means.

(2) Sam said that's not circular at all. The point is that I'm rationally required to be willing to buy and sell each bet at my own price, and if I refuse then I've just admitted I don't really have a credence in the first place. And the loss doesn't depend on anything about the world; the bookie wins whether the coin lands heads or tails. It's a pure inconsistency in my evaluations, like being willing to pay two dollars for a one dollar bill.

(3) Rhea replied that that willingness is exactly the assumption doing all the work. Why should I be willing to bet at my credences? My 0.7 might just be a planning number, not a price. And in any case, in the real world I can see the book coming, so I'll just decline the third bet and the book never closes. Your argument only catches a person who takes each bet in isolation and never reconsiders, which is precisely the kind of agent the argument is supposed to be about.

(4) Sam said declining the third bet just means I've changed my mind, and if I now treat the whole package as fair then I've already conceded the additivity constraint; I just discovered it by feeling the money. And the isolation thing is a feature, not a bug, because the argument targets what it's irrational to hold all at once, not my sequence of decisions.
