Two friends of mine, who are both into computer science and philosophy, got into an argument at a study group about the Church–Turing thesis. One of them, who leans more toward the philosophy side, said: "The Church–Turing thesis is a theorem. Church and Turing proved that the λ-calculus and Turing machines are equivalent, and that any effective procedure can be captured by a Turing machine. So it's as settled as any mathematical result." The other, who is a bit more careful, replied: "No, that's not quite right. The mathematical part — the equivalence of the λ-calculus and Turing machines — is a theorem. But the Church–Turing thesis, which says that every effectively computable function is Turing-computable, is not a theorem, because 'effectively computable' is not a precisely defined mathematical concept. It's a physical or empirical claim about what kinds of computation are physically realizable." The first one pushed back: "But Turing's whole argument is that any machine that could do what we call 'computing' is equivalent to a Turing machine. That's a proof, not a conjecture." The second one answered: "Turing's argument is a plausibility argument, not a proof. It depends on the informal notion of 'effective procedure,' which is not mathematically defined. So the thesis is a physical claim that could in principle be refuted by a hypercomputer." The first one said: "That's too strong. The thesis is a mathematical claim about the limits of computation, not a physical claim." The second one replied: "Actually, it's both. The mathematical part is the equivalence of formal systems. The physical part is the claim that the human mind, or any physical system, can only do Turing-computable things. And the latter is an empirical claim." I was in the middle of it and couldn't tell who was right. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall — and also say what, if anything, both of them are missing?
