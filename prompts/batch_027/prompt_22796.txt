Question: (a) State the core claim of phenomenal conservatism. (b) Explain why cognitive penetration of perception is a problem for it. (c) Present one response to the problem that does not simply deny that penetration is empirically possible.

Student's answer:
Phenomenal conservatism holds that if it seems to S that p, then S thereby has at least prima facie justification for believing p. The problem for the view comes from the possibility that experience can be cognitively penetrated, i.e., that its content can be shaped by the subject's prior beliefs, desires, and so on. If that's possible, then a subject with an unjustified belief can have that belief alter her experience so that it seems to her that p, and then use the seeming to justify the very belief that did the distorting. Justification for experience becomes hostage to the prior mental state that shaped it, and the view can't block this bootstrapping.

Siegel's Angry Jack case illustrates the point: Jill's unjustified belief that Jack is angry causes her to see him as angry, and her experience then justifies retaining the belief -- a circular loop. The upshot is that penetrated experiences are unreliable sources of information, so they fail to provide justification on the reliabilist grounds that phenomenal conservatism relies on.

One response that doesn't deny the empirical phenomenon is the perceptual learning response: cases like the expert radiologist who just sees a nodule on an X-ray look like penetration, but are better explained as slow, long-lasting changes to the perceptual system from training. The expert's experience is the output of a tuned perceptual system, not of the online influence of a belief, so the epistemic problem doesn't arise. This is in contrast to McPherson's color case, where she argues that the subject's belief directly changes the phenomenal character of the experience, wich is exactly why her case is a threat to phenomenal conservatism.
