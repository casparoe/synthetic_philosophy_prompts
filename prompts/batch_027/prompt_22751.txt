I'm working on a long-form piece about the history of utilitarian thought, and I keep returning to a puzzle about Bentham that I haven't been able to resolve. At a recent conference, two of my colleagues — a legal historian and a historian of ideas — got into a fairly heated exchange about it, and I was too busy taking notes to weigh in. I'd like your help working through it.

The legal historian argued that Bentham's position on sexual morality is straightforwardly consistent with his utilitarianism. In the "Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation," Bentham explicitly treats the pleasure of consensual same-sex acts as a real component of the overall-happiness standard he proposes, and he argues that punishing such acts is a clear violation of that standard because it produces pain without any offsetting social benefit. From this, the legal historian concluded that Bentham was, in effect, one of the earliest serious philosophers to argue for the decriminalization of homosexuality, and that his argument is a direct and unambiguous application of his own framework.

The historian of ideas pushed back hard. She pointed out that Bentham's personal life — his well-documented attraction to men, which he met with a practice of strict self-restraint — shows that he didn't actually live by the sexual liberty he was arguing for. She argued that this isn't just a biographical curiosity but a genuine philosophical problem: if Bentham's own framework for weighing pleasure against pain, applied to his own case, would have concluded that his repressed desires were producing net pain, then either his framework is inapplicable to its own author, or he was using it as a rationalization for a personal failing he couldn't bring himself to acknowledge. The legal historian replied that this is a genetic fallacy — Bentham's personal life is irrelevant to the validity of his public arguments — and that conflating the two is exactly the kind of ad hominem reasoning Bentham himself warned against.

I couldn't get a word in. Can you walk through their exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relationship between a philosopher's personal practice and the force of their arguments?
