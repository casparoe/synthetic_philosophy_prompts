Two friends of mine, both analytic philosophers, got into a long argument over dinner about Molinism — specifically, about whether the so-called "counterfactuals of freedom" are coherent. I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I followed the shape of the exchange but not every technical move, and I'd like a careful adjudication. Here is roughly how it went.

Iris defended the Molinist position. God's "middle knowledge," she said, is knowledge of counterfactuals of freedom — statements of the form, "If God had actualized the circumstance in which S was tempted, then S would freely have resisted." She was careful to insist that the truth of such a counterfactual does not mean S is causally determined to resist. The truth-maker, she said, is a primitive "transworld" fact about S's character: which way S would go, as a matter of who S is across possible worlds. S's freedom, on this view, is untouched, because freedom is the absence of causal compulsion, not the indeterminacy of the counterfactual.

Owen objected that this is incoherent. He appealed to the standard Stalnaker–Lewis semantics for counterfactuals: a counterfactual "if P, then Q" is true only if, in the nearest possible worlds where P holds, Q holds. If S is genuinely free in the relevant circumstance, he said, then there are possible worlds in which S is in that circumstance and does not resist; and if any of those worlds is among the nearest, the counterfactual is simply false. So, he argued, the counterfactual of freedom is either false — in which case God has no middle knowledge of free acts and Molinism collapses — or true, in which case S's act is fixed across the nearest worlds, which is determinism in disguise.

Iris replied that Owen was smuggling in the very semantics that was in dispute. The counterfactual, she said, can be true by virtue of S's stable character or disposition without that character acting as a causal determinant. She offered an analogy: "If I had been offered the job, I would have taken it" is true because of my preferences, yet those preferences do not causally force my action; I could still have declined. So, she said, a counterfactual can track what a free agent would do without thereby making the act unfree.

Owen answered that the analogy actually concedes his point. In the job case, he said, there is a stable fact about what I would do precisely because my preferences are fixed and I act on them; the "would have taken it" is fixed by my character, which is exactly the kind of character-determination he is worried about in the Molinist case. And he added that libertarian freedom requires the act itself to be a genuine novelty, not fixed by prior character; if character fixes the act, it is not free. So, he concluded, the Molinist counterfactuals either track something deterministic or they have no truth value at all.

They ended without resolving it. Could you go through their exchange and say who is right at each step — not just who wins overall — and, if there is a point both of them are missing, point it out?
