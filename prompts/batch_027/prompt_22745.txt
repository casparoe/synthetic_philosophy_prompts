I'm a backend software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been teaching myself the recent literature on metaphysical grounding by working through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry and a few of the papers it points to. I've drafted a short essay for a personal blog in which I try to make sense of the subject by mapping it onto something I know cold, dependency graphs in build systems, and I'd be grateful for your comments on it. I'm especially unsure about one interpretive claim and one accuracy point, which I flag at the end.

The essay's argument runs like this. On the standard view, grounding is a relation between facts such that the grounded fact holds in virtue of the grounding fact, and the relation is taken to be irreflexive, asymmetric, and, with a few caveats, transitive. If you grant those three properties, the grounding relation is a strict partial order, which means the whole structure of reality can be drawn as a directed acyclic graph: put an edge from fact A to fact B whenever A is grounded in B, and the fundamental facts sit at the leaves, the nodes that have no outgoing edge because they are not grounded in anything more basic. In my software analogy, fundamental facts are like base libraries or leaf dependencies, the things that themselves depend on nothing, while non-fundamental facts are like higher-level packages built out of them; a concrete example I lifted from the encyclopedia is that the fact that a bowl is brittle is grounded in the ionic bonding of its atoms, so the brittleness is the higher-level package and the bonding is the base library. From there I make two claims. The first is that fundamentality is not an intrinsic property of a fact but a purely relational status, namely that a fact is fundamental just in case it is a leaf node in the grounding graph, with no extra property of fundamental-ness doing any work. The second is that, given the first, priority monism, the view associated with Jonathan Schaffer that the cosmos as a whole is the single fundamental fact in virtue of which everything else exists, comes out as the claim that the grounding graph has exactly one leaf. But a dependency graph with a single leaf is structurally unnatural in software; real systems almost always have many independent base libraries, and forcing everything to hang off one is a code smell. So on this picture priority monism is an unnatural, gerrymandered structure, and the more natural default is a pluralist graph with many fundamental facts and many leaves.

So my questions are three. First, on accuracy: is my reconstruction of the standard axioms correct, and does the well-known caveat that transitivity is not as secure as the other two properties, which I think Rosen has pointed out, actually threaten the DAG analogy, or do most working metaphysicians simply assume transitivity and the analogy is safe? Second, on the first claim: I suspect I am overstating things when I say fundamentality is purely relational, because I have a vague sense that there is a live debate over whether fundamentality is a substantive, intrinsic property rather than just a node's position in the ordering, but I cannot pin down the objection. Could you tell me whether that fundamentality-as-a-property objection is real, and how it bears on both my relational claim and my single-leaf argument against priority monism? And third, more broadly: is the dependency-graph framing a genuinely illuminating way to think about grounding and fundamentality, or does it systematically mislead, and if so, where does it first break down?
