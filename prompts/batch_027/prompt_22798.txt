Okay so I've been messing around with this side project of mine where I'm trying to build a little "update your confidence" calculator, and I finally hit a case that breaks my brain. I get the basic stuff: if you learn a fact for sure, you just condition on it, no big deal. But what if the evidence itself is fuzzy? Here's my exact scenario. I'm checking a light bulb out of a batch where 5% are defective. I've got a tester that, when it's working, correctly sorts good vs. defective 90% of the time. But I'm not totally confident the tester is calberated -- I'd put it at like 80% that it's working right, and 20% it's just spitting out random good/defective calls (50/50, no info at all). The tester says "good." Now how do I update my belief that the bulb is actually good?

I came up with four candidate ways to do it and I can't tell which is the legit one. (1) trust_it: ignore the calibration worry, assume the tester is working, and just do plain Bayes with the 90% accuracy. (2) average_posteriors: work out the posterior assuming the tester is working, and seperately assuming it's broken, then take 0.8 x working + 0.2 x broken. I have a vague feeling this one has a fancy name, maybe Jeffrey conditionalization or "probability kinematics" or whatever, but I could be making it up. (3) flatten_test: first average the tester's accuracy across the working and broken cases to get one effective (noisier) test, then do plain Bayes on that single test. (4) keep_base_rate: decide the reading is too untrustworthy to matter and just stay at 0.95.
