Write a dialogue between a Jain anekāntavādin and a logician who is a strict defender of the law of non-contradiction, taking as their case the claim that the jīva (soul) is both finite and infinite. The logician should press the charge that if "finite" and "infinite" are genuine contraries, then asserting both at once is incoherent, and that Jain many-sidedness either silently abandons classical logic or is really a rule about the limits of our statements rather than a claim about reality. The anekāntavādin should answer that the apparent contradiction dissolves once the respects (naya) are specified—so that "syād asti" and "syād nāsti" are two qualified assertions, not one contradictory one—and should argue that this is a substantive claim about the real modes (paryāya) of substance, not mere epistemic modesty. Both speakers must be fully competent and must engage each other's actual points, and the exchange should close with the disagreement sharpened rather than settled, particularly over whether the doctrine is ultimately metaphysical or epistemic.
