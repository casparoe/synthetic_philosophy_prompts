I got into a long argument with a friend of mine last night about Benjamin Franklin's whole moral perfection project, the thing in his autobiography where he decides he's going to reach a point where he never commits a fault at all, writes out his thirteen virtues (temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquillity, chastity, humility), and then builds himself this little book with a page for each virtue, ruled in red ink with a column for each day of the week, so that every evening he can mark a little black spot for each fault he caught himself committing against the virtue he's been focusing on. The plan was to give one virtue his full attention for a week at a time and then move to the next, running through all thirteen in a single course and doing four courses a year, and he compared it to weeding a garden one bed at a time instead of trying to pull every weed at once.

My friend is a psychology grad student and she kept hitting me with the situationist critique, saying the whole enterprise is basically dead on arrival because a stack of studies, Milgram, the good Samaritan experiments, that sort of thing, show that what you do is driven way more by the situation you're dropped into than by any stable character trait you've supposedly built up over time, so the idea that you can grind out a durable habit of virtue and then just coast on it is naive. She called Franklin's chart a fancy placebo. I kept pushing back, saying he was actually ahead of the curve because his method was all about engineering the routine and the environment, not just wiling himself to be a better person, and that the evening self-examination was the whole point, not a crutch. We went at it for a hour and a half and I genuinely could not tell who had the stronger argument.

So I want to turn it into a question: if you could sit Franklin down and walk him through the modern situationist case against virtue ethics, the idea that character is not a reliable predictor of behavior and that the situation does most of the work, what do you think he'd actually respond? Would he abandon the project, or double down on it? And would he read his own chart-and-evening-examination method as a form of situation design that already got ahead of the situationists, or as exactly the kind of character fantasy they're trying to debunk? I'd really like an answer that engages with how his method actually worked in practice, not a general Franklin-was-a-pragmatist hand-wave.
