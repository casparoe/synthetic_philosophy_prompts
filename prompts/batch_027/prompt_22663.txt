I understand the Kelly criterion at the surface level: on a fair-odds bet with a positive edge, stake a fraction of your bankroll equal to that edge, and this choice maximizes your long-run geometric growth rate. What I want to understand is a subtler point about what happens when your estimate of the edge is wrong. Please explain intuitively why the criterion is asymmetric in this respect: why does there exist a range of overbets — staking more than the true Kelly fraction — that leads to almost sure ruin, i.e., your wealth decaying to zero with probability one, even though each individual bet still has positive expected value? And why does this asymmetry have to do with the fact that the right objective is expected log wealth (the geometric growth rate) rather than expected wealth? I want the gut-level intuition, not the full derivation.
