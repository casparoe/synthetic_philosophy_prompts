I'm putting together a reading list for a graduate seminar on the question of whether large language models are agents, simulators of agents, or something else entirely, and I keep coming back to a puzzle that I suspect rests on a confusion I haven't managed to pin down. The standard "simulator" objection goes roughly like this: an LLM is not itself an agent, but a simulator of one; it reproduces the behavior of an agent without being an agent. But if the simulation is behaviorally perfect — if the model's outputs are, in every observable respect, indistinguishable from those of a genuine agent — then it seems that the "simulator" and the "agent" are the same thing, at least in every respect that the behavioral criterion of agency can capture. So the "simulator" objection looks to me like a distinction without a difference, and I can't see what work it is supposed to be doing.

I was discussing this with a colleague of mine last week, and they insisted that the objection is not about behavior at all, though I haven't been able to get them to say exactly what it is about. I've been assuming, perhaps wrongly, that the objection has to be cashed out in behavioral terms, since behavior is the only thing we can observe from the outside. But I'm not sure that assumption is right. Could you help me understand what the "simulator" objection is actually claiming, and whether it really is a distinction without a difference, or whether there is a sense in which the internal causal structure of the model — as opposed to its behavior — is doing the work of the objection?
