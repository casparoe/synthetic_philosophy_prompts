I've just had a long, inconclusive argument with a friend over Nāgārjuna, and I'd like an essay that actually settles the point between us. The question we keep circling: when Nāgārjuna applies the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) — the fourfold rejection of the alternatives that something exists, does not exist, both, and neither — to the nature of phenomena, is he asserting a positive doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā), or is he only running a reductio against whatever position his opponent commits to? The essay should weigh the two main readings: the dialectical (prasaṅgika) reading, on which emptiness is a conventional instrument for dismantling views rather than a thesis to be held, against the reading that treats the claim that all is empty as a genuine metaphysical claim. Then it should confront the self-refutation objection — if everything is empty, is emptiness itself empty, and does that groundlessness infect the tetralemma's own force — and bring in the standard Madhyamaka replies, including the point that emptiness has no svabhāva of its own and is itself empty, so that it is not a view to be defended. Weigh the arguments on both sides, then reach and justify a clear verdict: is the tetralemma self-refuting, self-consistent, or something more subtle than either?
