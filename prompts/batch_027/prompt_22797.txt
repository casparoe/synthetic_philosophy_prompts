I'm in a political philosophy seminar this term and half the discussion keeps collapsing into "a constitution is just a Ulysses contract for a whole society," and I keep wanting to push back but I can't get my argument into a form that survives contact with the class. I want to argue that constitutional precomitment is different in kind from a Ulysses contract, not just a bigger version of one. My rough thought is that a Ulysses contract only works if the agent's preferences are basically stable over time — the future self who's tempted to jump overboard is the same person who tied himself to the mast, just in a moment of weakness — so the contract is a workaround for a temporary lapse, and it's legitimate because the "real" you, the one with the stable preferences, is the one who set it up. A constitution, though, is built to bind across a genuine change in preferences: it's meant to constrain future legislatures and majorities who might have entirely different, maybe even better-informed or more legitimate, preferences than the framers.

So I think evaluating a constitution the way you'd evaluate a Ulysses contract — "does it bind you to what you really want?" — is just asking the wrong question. A constitution seems good precisely when it binds us against what we or our successors want, because it's encoding something deeper like the rule of law or rights that should constrain even a future, possibly wiser self. Can you argue this for me, and in particular steelman the obvious counter — that a constitution is just a Ulysses contract where the "stable" self is the founding or constituent self instead of an individual — and then tell me why that counter doesn't actually dissolve the difference I'm trying to draw?
