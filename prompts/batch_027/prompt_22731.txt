I'm working through Simmel's Philosophy of Money for a reading group and I keep getting stuck on this paragraph in the first part. Here it is:

"Only money, in terms of its pure concept, has attained this final stage; it is nothing but the pure form of exchangeability. It embodies that element or function of things, by virtue of which they are economic. It does not comprehend their totality, but it does comprehend the totality of money."

I get the general idea that money is some kind of neutral medium that lets you compare otherwise incommensurable values, but I'm not really sure what he means when he calls money the "pure form" of exchangeability rather than a thing with content. And the last line is the one that really throws me: why does money "comprehend the totality of money" but not "their totality"? What's the difference between the totality of a particular thing and the totality of money? I'd appreciate someone walking me through what he's actually arguing here, and whether the form/content distinction is doing real philosophical work or if it's just obscure phrasing. (I also have no idea what "this final stage" is pointing at, since the paragraph doesn't seem to set it up.)
