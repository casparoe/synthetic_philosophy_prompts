In my formal epistemology seminar this term, we've been working through Aumann's 1976 "Agreeing to Disagree" paper, and I keep getting stuck on how it would sit with earlier subjectivist accounts of probability, especially Ramsey's. Ramsey's "Truth and Probability" essay is usually credited as one of the founding texts of the Bayesian/subjectivist tradition, since he grounds degrees of belief in coherent betting behavior rather than in any logical relation between propositions (contra Keynes). But Aumann's theorem depends on the common prior assumption plus common knowledge of posteriors to derive the striking result that genuinely rational Bayesian agents cannot knowingly agree to disagree. This seems to require exactly the kind of shared, quasi-objective starting point that Ramsey's radical subjectivism about credences might resist.

Given what we know of Ramsey's actual epistemological commitments — his coherentism, his skepticism toward Keynes's logical theory of probability, and his emphasis on individual psychological states of belief rather than any impersonal standard of rationality — how do you think he would have reacted to Aumann's theorem had he lived to see it? Would he have accepted the common prior assumption as a reasonable idealization, or dismissed it as smuggling back in the very objectivism about probability he was trying to avoid? And relatedly, how might he have interpreted the massive amount of real-world persistent disagreement among apparently rational, well-informed people: as evidence that we lack common priors, that common knowledge of posteriors essentially never obtains, or that something else is going on that the formal model doesn't capture?
