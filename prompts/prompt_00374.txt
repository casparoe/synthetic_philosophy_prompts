I sit on my university's research integrity committee, and a case has come before us that I can't stop turning over in my head. Two junior colleagues in the same department independently cut the same corner in their empirical work: both reported preliminary regression results in grant applications as though they were based on a fully cleaned dataset, when in fact the data processing was still incomplete and somewhat haphazard at the time of submission. In one case, the eventual fully-processed data confirmed the original results almost exactly, and nothing more came of it. In the other case, the completed analysis reversed the key finding, a co-author noticed the discrepancy, and it became a formal misconduct inquiry that could affect the colleague's tenure case. The underlying decision-making and level of care exercised by each researcher, as far as I can reconstruct from emails and lab notebooks, was essentially identical.

Several committee members want to treat these as fundamentally different cases: one a non-issue, the other a serious integrity violation. I find myself uneasy with this, since it seems like we would be punishing one colleague and exonerating the other for something that was, at the point of decision, outside either of their control. But I also recognize that outcomes have to matter somehow in institutional judgments, since we can't just evaluate intentions in a vacuum. How should I think through this tension when I sit down with the committee next week, and is there a principled way to decide how much weight the eventual result should carry relative to the identical underlying conduct?
