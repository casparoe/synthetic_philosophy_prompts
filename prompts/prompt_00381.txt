I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language working through the secondary literature on Kripke's Wittgenstein for my dissertation chapter on rule-following. I keep hitting the same wall: the dispositionalist response to the skeptical paradox seems initially plausible (meaning "plus" is just having a disposition to answer "125" to "68+57"), but once you add the idealizations needed to handle the finitude objection—infinite capacity, ceteris paribus clauses ruling out fatigue, miscalculation, and so on—it starts to look like the normativity objection (Kripke's point that dispositions describe what I would do, not what I ought to do) becomes even harder to escape rather than easier. I want to see this tension actually argued out rather than just gestured at in a survey article.

Could you write a dialogue between a committed rule-following skeptic (channeling Kripke's Wittgenstein) and a sophisticated dispositionalist who has read Kripke's objections carefully and refuses to give easy ground? Set it as an actual back-and-forth over a concrete case—say, the skeptic asking the dispositionalist to explain why "68 + 57 = 125" is the correct answer rather than the "quus"-based "5"—and have them work through at least two rounds: first the finitude problem and the idealization move, then the normativity problem once idealization is on the table. Each side should respond to what the other just said (no talking past each other or strawmanning), and the dispositionalist should get real, non-trivial resources to draw on (e.g., teleosemantics, ceteris paribus clauses, appeals to natural selection or learning history) rather than folding immediately. The dialogue doesn't need to resolve neatly—I'd rather it end by sharpening exactly where the disagreement bites than by having one side concede.
