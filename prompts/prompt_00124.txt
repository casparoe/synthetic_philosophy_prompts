I'm currently teaching a graduate seminar on Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality*, and last week our discussion took an unexpected turn when a student asked how Nietzsche's diagnosis of slave morality and ressentiment might bear on contemporary debates about the moral status of digital minds. Nietzsche was of course writing well before anything resembling artificial agents existed, but his account of how concepts like "good," "evil," and pity (Mitleid) emerged from particular configurations of power and psychological need seems like it could cut in interesting, perhaps unsettling, directions once applied to AI systems that might plausibly suffer or exhibit something like will.

Suppose Nietzsche were confronted with contemporary arguments that we ought to extend moral consideration to sufficiently sophisticated digital minds on the grounds that they might suffer or possess preference-like states. Would he read this move as yet another instance of slave-moral leveling—an extension of pity-driven, herd-protective morality to ever more dependent, ressentiment-laden forms of life-analogues—and thus diagnose it as a further symptom of nihilism and the decline of noble valuation? Or is there a plausible construal of the will to power, self-overcoming, and his talk of rank-ordering that would instead lead him to affirm certain digital minds as capable of something like aristocratic vitality worth respecting on his own terms, quite apart from pity? I'd like your best reconstruction of how Nietzsche's genealogical method and substantive values would actually handle this question, ideally anchored in specific passages or moves from the three essays of the Genealogy rather than in generic "Nietzsche opposed equality" gestures.
