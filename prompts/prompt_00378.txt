I've spent the last few weeks reporting on the effective altruism movement for a feature on longtermism and existential risk philanthropy, and I keep running into a technical objection that none of my sources have been able to explain to my satisfaction. Several philosophers I've interviewed mention Nick Bostrom's work on "infinite ethics" as a serious problem for the whole longtermist project, and as I understand it, the argument goes like this: if the universe is spatially or temporally infinite, it already contains an infinite amount of value (and disvalue) scattered throughout it. Since any finite action — say, preventing a pandemic or funding AI safety research — can only ever add a finite quantity of value, and finite quantities are mathematically swamped by infinite ones (infinity plus any finite number is still just infinity), doesn't this prove, in a fairly rigorous way, that our actions are worth exactly zero in the grand scheme of things? It seems to follow that the entire case for prioritizing the long-term future collapses, since utilitarian calculations that are supposed to justify longtermism can't actually distinguish between a universe with our efforts and one without them. Is that a fair reading of what Bostrom and others in this literature are actually arguing, or am I missing something about how philosophers who work on this problem think about comparing infinite quantities?
