So I had this whole thing happen in office hours yesterday that I couldn't quite settle. A student was working through On Certainty and got really stuck on hinge epistemology—specifically the question of whether hinge propositions are unjustifiable *in principle* or just *in practice*. She was arguing that Wittgenstein's hinge propositions genuinely fall outside the whole justification game. Like, she said it doesn't even make sense to ask whether "I have hands" is justified; it's more like asking whether English is justified using the letter 'e'. These aren't things that get justified or unjustified, they're just what we *do*.

Another student sitting in on the meeting thought she was missing something. He said hinge propositions are just things we're epistemically stuck on, not things that are fundamentally unjustifiable. Yeah, we can't actually justify them right now, he granted, but that's contingent—it's not some deep fact about justification itself. He even pointed out that what counts as a hinge might differ from person to person, or culture to culture, or time to time. "If *that's* true," he asked, "then how can you say they're unjustifiable in principle?" She came back: "Okay, fine, maybe different people have different hinges, but *for any given person*, those hinges are unjustifiable. So the in-principle distinction still holds." He wasn't satisfied: "But then Wittgenstein would be making a claim about the structure of justification that we can't even test. If I genuinely can't justify my hinges, how do I know I'm failing at something impossible rather than just something I'm not actually doing?"

So work through this exchange point by point. Is there a real philosophical disagreement here, or are they just using the same words differently? For each move they make—who's actually right about what? And what, if anything, is each of them getting wrong or missing?
