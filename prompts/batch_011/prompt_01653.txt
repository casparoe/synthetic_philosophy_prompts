I determined to oppose the proposal to give Mr. Truman an honorary degree here at Oxford. Now, an honorary degree is not a reward of merit: it is, as it were, a reward for being a very distinguished person, and it would be foolish to enquire whether a candidate deserves to be as distinguished as he is. That is why, in general, the question whether so-and-so should have an honorary degree is devoid of interest. A very distinguished person will hardly be also a notorious criminal, and if he should chance to be a non-notorious criminal it would, in my opinion, be improper to bring the matter up. It is only in the rather rare case in which a man is known everywhere for an action, in face of which it is sycophancy to honour him, that the question can be of the slightest interest.

I have been puzzling over what Anscombe is trying to establish here. On the surface, she seems to be arguing that honorary degrees are not really about merit at all, so objections to giving Truman an honorary degree on moral grounds seem categorically confused. But then her argument makes the matter morally relevant in a very specific case—when someone is notorious for a bad action and honoring them would constitute sycophancy.

My question is this: what is the actual logical structure of Anscombe's position? Is she arguing that honorary degrees are indeed outside the realm of moral evaluation in general, and Truman simply happens to be the exception that proves the rule? Or is there something deeper going on—something about the relationship between honor, public reputation, and collective moral responsibility that makes this argument work? And crucially, does her distinction between "a very distinguished person" and "a person known everywhere for an action" carry the philosophical weight she seems to think it does? I'm asking because the passage seems to shift between a descriptive claim about what honorary degrees typically are and a normative claim about when they become morally problematic, and I'm not entirely sure those two moves are compatible with each other.
