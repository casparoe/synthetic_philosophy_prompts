I'm currently teaching a graduate seminar in philosophy of science, and we've been working through various formulations of the underdetermination thesis. Most presentations in the literature depict underdetermination as a straightforward empirical phenomenon: multiple distinct theories can be fully consistent with the same body of evidence. Yet what strikes me is how deeply philosophers disagree about the philosophical implications. Some argue that underdetermination reveals a fundamental limit on what empirical evidence can justify us in believing, thereby constraining legitimate claims about scientific knowledge. Others contend that once we properly incorporate considerations like explanatory power, simplicity, and the rational adjudication of auxiliary hypotheses, underdetermination ceases to pose a serious challenge to scientific realism. I suspect the disagreement may be more terminological than substantive—that the parties are disagreeing about how to frame and delimit the problem itself rather than about the underlying empirical facts. Write a dialogue between two competent philosophers exploring this tension: one who treats underdetermination as revealing a fundamental epistemological problem for scientific realism, and another who argues that standard theory-choice criteria adequately address cases of apparent underdetermination. Have them genuinely engage with each other's conceptual commitments and assumptions rather than simply restating their respective positions.
