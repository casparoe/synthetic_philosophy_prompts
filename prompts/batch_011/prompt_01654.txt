Two people in my debate club got into an argument about what makes preferences rational. One said, "If you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, you're vulnerable to a money pump. Someone could sell you A for a dollar, trade it for B for another dollar, trade that for C for another dollar, then trade C back for A for another dollar. You'd end up where you started but three dollars poorer. So rational preferences must be transitive." The other objected: "That assumes I'd accept all of those trades. But a rational person would notice the pattern and refuse some deals. My preferences might be non-transitive, but I can still be rational by not getting pumped." The first responded: "You're missing the point. The money pump shows that non-transitive preferences are intrinsically vulnerable to exploitation. That vulnerability is what makes them irrational." The second said: "But vulnerability only matters if I actually get exploited. And I won't, because I can see it coming. This shows I need to pay attention to my decisions, not that my preference structure is irrational."

Who is right about what the money pump argument actually demonstrates? What aspects of the disagreement involve genuine confusion?
