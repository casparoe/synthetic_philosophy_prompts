I'm teaching a unit on political philosophy and trying to give my students a concrete case to work through. One of them brought up the fact that we use animals in medical research—specifically primate testing for neurology work. Their intuition was pretty straightforward: "Well, we require strict ethical oversight for human medical trials. So why don't we just apply the same standards to animals?"

At first I was ready to brush past it, but the more I thought about it, the less obvious the answer became. We don't allow people to be used in ways that violate their rights, even if the benefit to others would be massive. But we don't seem to think animals have those kinds of rights in the first place. Yet it also seems totally wrong to say animals don't matter *at all*—like, you shouldn't torture them just for fun. So where's the line between "has interests that matter morally" and "has the kind of rights that can't be overridden by aggregate benefit"?

My hunch is there's a principled way to think about this—something like the distinction between having rights vs. having interests. But I can't quite make it stick. If animals have interests that are real and morally considerable, why are we allowed to sacrifice those interests for human benefit when we absolutely wouldn't do that to humans? Is that just realpolitik, or is there solid reasoning underneath that explains why the two cases really are different?

How would someone work through this systematically?
