Let me finalize the Nozick prompt:

I'm teaching a unit on political philosophy and trying to give my students a concrete case to work through. One of them brought up the fact that we use animals in medical testing all the time—specifically primate testing for neurology research. Their intuition was: "Well, we use humans in medical trials too, but we have strict ethical requirements. Why don't we just apply the same standards to animals?"

The more I thought about it, the less obvious the answer seemed. We don't let people consent to being used in ways we think violate their rights, even if the benefit to others would be massive. But we don't seem to think animals have those kinds of rights in the first place. On the other hand, it seems weird to say animals don't matter *at all*—like, you shouldn't torture them for fun. So where's the line? 

My sense is there's a principled way to think about this—some philosophers would point to something like the difference between having rights and having interests. But I'm fuzzy on exactly how that's supposed to work and whether it actually holds up. If animals have interests (which seems hard to deny), what does it mean that we're allowed to sacrifice those interests in pursuit of human benefit, when we *wouldn't* be allowed to do that to humans? Is that just practical realism, or is there a deeper principled reasoning underneath?

I'd love to see how someone would work through this systematically.
