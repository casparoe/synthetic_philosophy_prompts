I'm writing my dissertation on epistemological responses to disagreement, and I've been wrestling with a puzzle at the heart of the peer-disagreement literature. I believe I've isolated a response that may avoid some of the core dialectical tensions, though I'm uncertain whether it ultimately succeeds. Let me lay out the problem and my proposed solution for your consideration.

The puzzle, briefly: Suppose you have justification J for believing some proposition p, grounded in your evidence E. You then discover that a generally reliable source believes ~p. The difficulty is this—either your original justification J survives this disagreement intact (which seems to make you epistemically arrogant or dogmatic), or the source's mere assertion somehow undermines J itself (which is puzzling, since J was supposedly adequate justification before you learned of the disagreement). We appear trapped between these two equally unsatisfying positions.

My proposed resolution turns on distinguishing justification from what I'll call "justification for decisive action or high confidence." The core idea is that the source's disagreement doesn't undermine J as a justification per se, but it does undermine J's capacity to support very high confidence or consequential action. Under this view, you can consistently maintain that J is a genuine, adequate justification while acknowledging that the disagreement creates rational pressure to moderate your confidence or to refrain from actions based on p. This seems to avoid the appearance of dogmatism without committing us to the counterintuitive claim that learning about disagreement simply erases prior justification.

I'd appreciate your thoughts on whether this actually resolves the tension or merely relocates it. Are there cases where the distinction between "justifying a belief" and "justifying high confidence in a belief" collapses or becomes untenable? And does introducing this multi-level framework create more conceptual complications than it resolves?
