I was discussing revealed preference theory with a friend who teaches microeconomics, and we got stuck on something. We were discussing the classic cases of apparent preference reversals—like Tversky's experiments where people make different choices depending on how options are framed—and I asked whether revealed preference theory predicted such phenomena should be impossible. He said no: revealed preference theory is about *choices*, not preferences. When choices violate transitivity, it means the revealed preferences are intransitive; the theory doesn't rule this out. I suggested this made the theory trivial—it seems to say "people reveal preferences through their choices, and whatever they choose reveals their preferences," which can't be falsified. He countered that I was confusing the empirical observation (preferences can be intransitive) with the definition (preferences are what choices reveal). The theory is falsifiable because we can predict *which* choices people will make under specific conditions, even if the preference orderings turn out intransitive. He gave an example: if we observe someone choosing A over B in one context and B over A in another, revealed preference theory says they have intransitive preferences, which is a meaningful prediction about behavior in other contexts.

But I pressed him: doesn't that move the theory beyond mere description? He's now claiming intransitive choice patterns will persist across contexts. He admitted this is a further claim, not entailed by pure revealed preference theory, but argued it's a reasonable empirical hypothesis—if someone's preferences are truly intransitive, we'd expect instability. I then asked whether he was effectively admitting that underlying preferences—something independent of individual choice events—must actually exist, and that revealed preference is just an imperfect window onto them. He said that's not quite right: preferences *just are* dispositions to choose, so intransitive dispositions are still real preferences. But if preferences are dispositions, I objected, then surely the disposition can be thwarted by contextual factors and presentation effects. So a choice doesn't always reveal the underlying disposition. He said this concedes too much—presentation effects might alter choice, but they don't *hide* a separately existing true preference; they're part of what makes the person's preference what it is.

Who's right about these specific steps, and what assumptions might both of them be missing?
