Two philosophers were debating a scenario I'm working into a character's crisis of conscience. The character would consent to this arrangement: if a fair coin lands heads, she will be awakened once on Monday and released; if tails, she will be awakened on Monday and again on Tuesday, with her memory erased between the two. Upon waking, she will know neither what day it is nor what the coin produced.

The first philosopher argues she should assign a credence of 1/2 to heads. The coin was fair before she slept, and the mere fact of waking provides no new evidence about an event that has already occurred.

The second replies: "From her subjective vantage point upon waking, she must find herself at one of three equally probable moments: Monday-given-heads, Monday-given-tails, or Tuesday-given-tails. Two of those moments correspond to tails. By self-locating reasoning, her credence in tails should therefore be 2/3."

The first objects: "That logic presumes each moment of consciousness is a separate observer. She remains one continuous person. Her waking was certain to occur no matter what the coin showed—it cannot be evidence."

The second counters: "You're denying that self-location can ever be informative. But it must be. When she asks which temporal point she now occupies, that question has factual content. Surely knowledge of one's location in time constrains rational belief in any consistent framework?"

At each stage of their exchange, who has the stronger logical position? What, if anything, is each philosopher overlooking?
