I've been writing this character who becomes convinced by consequentialist ethics, and I keep running into something the standard demandingness critiques don't quite capture. Everyone says consequentialism is too demanding—you have to sacrifice everything for the greater good. But here's what's nagging me: does that objection *show* consequentialism is wrong, or does it just show that our moral intuitions recoil from it? And maybe our intuitions are unreliable here, since we're all basically self-interested and would benefit from others not sacrificing everything. So the objection feels a bit circular.

There's also this thing bugging me: a theory saying 'maximize welfare' isn't the same as saying 'spend every moment deliberating about global utility.' Maybe that's confusing what the theory demands of you with what it demands you *think about*. A good consequentialist agent could mostly operate on intuition and habit, only bringing out explicit calculations in hard cases. So I'm not sure demandingness really bites.

I've got scattered notes on this but I can't tell if I'm onto something real or just confused about the dialectic. What's your take—am I misunderstanding the objection itself, or is there something to the distinction I'm drawing?
