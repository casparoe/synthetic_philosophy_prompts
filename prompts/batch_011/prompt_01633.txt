I've been reading Sartre's work on freedom and bad faith as part of a broader interest in how people rationalize their choices, and I'm running into what seems like a fundamental logical problem with his framework.

From what I understand, Sartre's core thesis is that humans have radical freedom—we are essentially free to choose who we are and what we do at every moment, and there's no escaping this fundamental freedom. Yet he also extensively discusses bad faith: the phenomenon of people systematically deceiving themselves about their freedom, pretending they're bound by circumstance or social role, acting as though their choices are determined by factors beyond their control.

But this seems to create a contradiction. If we're truly radically free in Sartre's sense, and if we're aware of this freedom (which we must be, since it's fundamental to our nature), then bad faith seems logically impossible. The moment someone is in bad faith—pretending they're not free, or claiming their situation determines their choices—aren't they necessarily making a conscious, free choice to maintain this pretense? And wouldn't that immediate awareness of the free choice undermine the entire self-deception? How can Sartre consistently claim both that we're radically free and that we successfully deceive ourselves about that freedom? Doesn't genuine self-deception about our freedom seem to require an ignorance of something we cannot possibly avoid knowing?
