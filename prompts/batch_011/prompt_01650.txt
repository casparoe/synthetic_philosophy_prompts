Two engineers at my company debated the ethics of mentoring junior colleagues. One argued that in-depth mentoring—career guidance, detailed technical knowledge-sharing—should remain supererogatory: praiseworthy but not obligatory. He worried that team norms were shifting it toward required, which he felt was unfair to those prioritizing their own work. His colleague disagreed: when you recognize someone is struggling and could help without significant cost, an obligation exists. It's no longer merely supererogatory. He objected that this collapses the boundary. There will always be people who could benefit from mentoring; if obligation tracks "could help at low cost," obligations become unlimited. She acknowledged that unlimited potential need doesn't create unlimited obligation, but insisted that the absence of substantial cost should matter ethically: when helping costs almost nothing, declining becomes harder to justify morally. He countered that "cost" includes protecting your own time and boundaries, which itself carries ethical weight.

Who is right about what specific points here?
