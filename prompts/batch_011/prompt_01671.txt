I've been wrestling with a question about causation and omissions that came up in conversation with some friends. We were discussing a case where a lifeguard fails to throw a rescue buoy to someone drowning, and the person dies. Someone said "the lifeguard caused the death by failing to act," but then others pushed back, saying that's not really right—there's no positive event or action that the lifeguard caused; rather, the lifeguard simply failed to prevent something. This got me thinking: when we say an omission caused something, are we talking about causation in the same sense as when we say an action caused something? Or are we using the word "cause" in a different, more attenuated way?

I've read a bit about this and found out there are genuinely different philosophical positions on the matter. Some people argue that omissions really can be causes—that the failure to act stands on equal metaphysical footing with positive events or actions as a cause. Others argue that only positive events can be causes, and that when we talk about omissions causing things, we're really just talking about how the absence of a certain action allowed other causes to produce the effect.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers with these opposing views. Have them discuss the lifeguard case and perhaps other examples, with each trying to show why their account of causation is superior. The conversation should feel like they're genuinely engaging with each other's arguments, not just restating their positions. Try to bring out the real philosophical tensions between the two views—what exactly is at stake depends on which view is correct?
