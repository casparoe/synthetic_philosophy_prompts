I was researching mental representation for a novel I'm working on, and while discussing the topic with friends, one of them—a philosophy graduate student—mentioned teleosemantics as a solution to the problem of naturalizing intentionality. I became intrigued but also skeptical, and found myself drawn into a disagreement that neither of my friends could resolve.

The philosopher contended that mental content is determined by evolutionary function: my belief about tigers counts as *about* tigers because my cognitive systems evolved to track tigers. This anchors content to something naturalistically respectable and avoids circularity. The engineer objected: "But that divorces content from what I'm actually thinking about. If I misapply a tiger-tracking mechanism to my dog right now, surely my thought is about my dog, not tigers. And if you say it's about tigers but false, you're treating intentional content as hostage to ancient evolutionary history rather than to present reality."

The philosopher replied: "Misrepresentation is indeed possible—that's how falsity works. But intentionality itself must be explained in non-circular, naturalistic terms. You're trying to appeal to what you intend to think about, but that's precisely what we're trying to explain."

The engineer pushed back: "So you're saying the content of my thoughts could be radically divorced from what I consciously intend and from what I'm actually causally interacting with? That seems to empty the word 'content' of meaning."

Neither conceded any ground. Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point—who has the stronger position at each turn, and what is each side perhaps overlooking?
