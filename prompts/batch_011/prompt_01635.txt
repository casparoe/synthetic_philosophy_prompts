I'm working through some early twentieth-century philosophical texts for my dissertation on the relationship between law, morality, and politics. I've encountered a passage that's been puzzling me from Gottlob Frege's political diary (an obscure private text written in 1924, near the end of his life). The relevant entry is from March 22nd, and it concerns the proper role of judges in relation to wealth:

"Justice is the foundation of law. The fair judge is not permitted in any way to take into account the wealth of one of the parties in a lawsuit. The rich are not permitted, because they are rich, to have any special privilege over the poor, but also, on the other hand, the poor are not permitted, because they are poor, to have any special privilege over the rich. Religion, however, gladly places obligations on the rich which favor the poor. But these religious obligations are not legal obligations. If they are not held clearly separate from one another, religion can endanger the law, and therewith the state."

This passage is interesting to me because Frege seems to be drawing a very sharp distinction between the domain of law (where strict impartiality regarding wealth is required) and the domain of religion (where partiality toward the poor is appropriate). My question is: How does Frege's framework here relate to modern concerns about the relationship between justice and equality? In particular, I'm wondering whether his strict neutrality principle for judges would survive scrutiny from contemporary perspectives that argue judges inevitably bring their values to their interpretations. Is he perhaps articulating something important that contemporary theory has lost sight of, or is he being naïve about the possibility of judicial impartiality? How would you interpret what he means by saying that religion "can endanger the law" if not properly kept separate?
