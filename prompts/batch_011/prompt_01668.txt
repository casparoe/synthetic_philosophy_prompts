I'm working on a novel where my protagonist discovers something troubling about a mentor she's trusted her entire life. She becomes convinced the mentor betrayed her, and when I'm writing the scene where she tries to convince her best friend of this, I realize I need to understand something about how people actually justify their beliefs philosophically. Like, she points to specific incidents. Her friend asks "but how do you know those incidents actually mean betrayal and not just misunderstanding?" So she offers more reasoning to support that interpretation. But then logically, *that* reasoning can itself be questioned. How does it ever stop?

I keep thinking there should be three ways out of this loop, and I've seen philosophers mention them, but I can't quite articulate them clearly enough to use them in the novel. One seems to be that some beliefs can just be foundational—you know them without further justification, which stops the questioning but feels kind of magical. Another seems to be about coherence—maybe it's OK if all her beliefs just hang together consistently, even if each individual one can be questioned. The third involves accepting infinite justification chains, which obviously can't be right. I need to understand which of these (if any) actually works, and why the others fail, because my character has to find some way to live with her conviction while being epistemically honest about how she got there.
