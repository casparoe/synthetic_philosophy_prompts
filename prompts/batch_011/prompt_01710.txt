Two friends of mine got into a heated discussion about Molinism. One defended it as elegantly solving the omniscience-free will problem: God possesses middle knowledge of what free creatures would choose under any circumstances, allowing divine planning without determining anyone's choices. The other objected: if God already knows what you'd freely choose in a given situation, doesn't that situation compel you to make that choice? The first replied that God's knowledge doesn't cause the outcome; knowing something doesn't make it happen. The second pressed: the real problem isn't causation but grounding. If God's knowledge is grounded in facts about your nature and circumstances, and those facts are fixed, then there's only one outcome God could know—so what remains of freedom? The first contended that libertarian free will doesn't require being able to do otherwise when God's knowledge correctly tracks what you'd actually choose in those actual circumstances. The second said this just redefines freedom to fit Molinism rather than showing the view actually preserves it. Both felt the other had simply missed the heart of the matter. Can you work through their exchange and say who's right about what at each step—not just who wins the overall dispute? What might they both be overlooking?
