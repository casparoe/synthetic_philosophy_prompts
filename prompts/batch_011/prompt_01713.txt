I spent thirty years as an experimental biochemist, and like many researchers in the field, I developed fairly reliable instincts about which experimental approaches would work and which would founder. That gut sense was cultivated through repeated successes—it usually felt trustworthy to me, even when I couldn't articulate precisely why.

Recently, somewhat to my embarrassment, I decided to audit myself. I went back through my old notebooks and collected roughly three hundred instances where I'd made a confident initial judgment about experimental design, and I compared these against the actual outcomes. The result was humbling: my confident first impressions were right only about 55% of the time. When I was less confident initially, my accuracy dropped further still. Statistically, my instincts barely outperformed random guessing.

Yet here is what troubles me philosophically: I find I cannot bring myself to trust this statistical conclusion, even though I generated it myself and can find no error in the analysis. When I encounter a new problem, my gut judgments still feel compelling and authoritative. The evidence about my own unreliability, by contrast, feels strangely abstract and distant—almost as if it were about someone else's track record entirely. This creates an odd bind: I have strong evidence that my initial judgments are unreliable, yet I still cannot seem to discount them appropriately when I'm making new judgments.

I'm newly engaged with philosophy and I suspect there's a coherent way to think about this predicament. Is my situation genuinely troubling from a rational standpoint? And if so, what would philosophers say about someone who can't seem to act on evidence against their own judgment, even when they fully accept that evidence as correct?
