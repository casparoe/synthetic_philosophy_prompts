G. E. Moore observed in his 1942 "A Reply to My Critics" that something odd occurs when we consider assertions of the form "I went to the pictures last Tuesday, but I do not believe that I did." Moore noted that while these words are not nonsensical on their own and the situation they describe could actually occur in reality, there is nevertheless something absurd about asserting them. In later discussions of the puzzle, he also observed that this absurdity disappears entirely in the past tense (when we say "I went to the pictures, but I did not believe that I did") and when we shift to other people (when we say "He went to the pictures, but he does not believe that he did").

The puzzle Moore identifies is: if the content of what I assert—the proposition expressed by my statement—is perfectly consistent and could well be true, why exactly is it absurd to assert both parts of it at the same time in the present tense? What makes the first-person present-tense case so different from the past-tense and third-person versions when all of them express logically consistent propositions?
