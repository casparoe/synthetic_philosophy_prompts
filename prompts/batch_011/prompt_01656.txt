I've been reading Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics for my philosophy class, and I'm working through his discussion of justice in exchange in Book V. I found this passage where he's explaining how money works:

"Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together (for if men did not need one another's goods at all, or did not need them equally, there would be either no exchange or not the same exchange); but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma)--because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless."

He also says later: "Money, then, acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them; for neither would there have been association if there were not exchange, nor exchange if there were not equality, nor equality if there were not commensurability."

I'm confused about something. Aristotle seems to be saying that demand is what fundamentally holds things together and enables exchange. But then he also says that money is what makes goods commensurate and makes exchange possible. These seem like they might be saying the same thing, but he presents them differently—one as "the unit in truth" and one as a "representative." Can you help me understand what the actual difference is between what demand does and what money does in his system? Why does he need both concepts if they're both supposed to be the basis for exchange?
