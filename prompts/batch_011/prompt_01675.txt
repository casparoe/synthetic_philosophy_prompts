So I just got in this big thing with a friend about whether we should say that LLMs "have beliefs." The friend kept going back to like, "but we can predict the model's behavior perfectly well if we treat it as belief-having, so what's your actual problem?" and I knew my reaction was right but couldn't quite articulate it without sounding like I was being mystical about "genuine" beliefs.

Here's what I actually want to argue: Make the case that predictive success should NOT be enough to justify attributing beliefs to an AI system, even when the intentional stance works perfectly. The key is that beliefs aren't just "whatever makes accurate predictions"—they're supposed to function in a particular way: as stable representations that get used in reasoning and flexible decision-making. An LLM generates accurate predictions through statistical pattern-matching over training text, which is a totally different operation. Attributing beliefs to it is like saying a very sophisticated thermometer "believes" the temperature—technically useful but missing what beliefs actually are.

Can you construct a real argument for this position that doesn't end up resting on something hand-wavy or circular? I'm genuinely not sure if I'm pointing at something ontologically important or just being weird about definitions.
