I've been attending a study group where two colleagues recently engaged in a disagreement I haven't been able to resolve myself.

One colleague made an argument about cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions. When researchers present moral dilemmas—the trolley problem, for instance—across different cultural contexts, responses vary considerably. Some cultures emphasize aggregate welfare; others prioritize personal agent responsibility or relational duties. She argued this variation demonstrates that philosophical intuitions are not reliable guides to objective moral truth, but rather reflect cultural construction.

Her colleague objected: "You're conflating variation with unreliability. Mathematicians in different historical periods held incompatible geometric intuitions; they weren't unreliable, merely untrained. Moreover, your argument assumes objectivity requires cross-cultural convergence. But perhaps moral understanding is mediated by lived experience or cultural particularity in ways that make agreement unlikely even among reflective practitioners. This wouldn't render moral facts any less objective."

The first responded: "Then what makes them objective? If permanent disagreement is compatible with moral objectivity, you've emptied the concept of content. There must be a way to distinguish genuine knowledge from mere cultural preference."

He countered: "The distinction is whether one accesses something real. Historical facts are objective, yet historians from different traditions interpret them through distinct interpretive frameworks. Objectivity requires access to reality, not a culture-transcendent single description. You're building a specific epistemological requirement into the definition of objectivity itself."

The exchange went no further. Who has the better argument at each stage, and what might both parties be overlooking?
