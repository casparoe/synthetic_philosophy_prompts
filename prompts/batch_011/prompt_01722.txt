I was discussing information cascades and herding behavior with a friend who works in behavioral finance. They kept emphasizing that cascades are a primary driver of market irrationality and instability, points where people systematically ignore their private information and follow others instead. This seemed to them like a clear case of irrational behavior that could be mitigated through better financial literacy and awareness.

But I found myself questioning the characterization. As I understand the standard explanation, individuals rationally infer from others' observable choices that those others possess valuable private information. Given what each person can observe, their decision to follow the cascade seems perfectly reasonable. My friend acknowledged this logical point but insisted I was missing something crucial—that the irrationality lies elsewhere, not in the individual decisions. Yet they struggled to articulate exactly where.

I've been puzzling over this since. If information cascades involve individually rational responses to genuinely limited information, in what sense are they actually irrational? Or is the genuine problem not irrationality at all, but rather something about how individually reasonable choices can aggregate into collectively inefficient or unstable outcomes? I'd appreciate a rigorous explanation of what specifically goes wrong in cascades, and why—or whether—we should characterize them as failures of rationality.
