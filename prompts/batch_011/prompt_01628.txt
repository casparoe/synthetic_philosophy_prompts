In my metaphysics course, we've been discussing causation by omission—the philosophical claim that the absence or failure of some event can sometimes be a genuine cause of an outcome. Most of the contemporary literature I've read emphasizes skepticism about this view: philosophers argue that when we say an omission caused something, we're really identifying some positive causal factor instead (negligence, inattention, a breach of duty, etc.). However, I find this skepticism too quick. I'd like you to construct a sophisticated argument that omissions can be genuine causes, one that doesn't simply retreat to talk of positive surrogate causes.

In particular, consider how our standard counterfactual analysis of causation seems to apply to omissions just as well as to positive events: we say X caused Y if, counterfactually, had X not occurred, Y would not have occurred. This holds for omissions too—for instance, if a lifeguard's failure to notice a swimmer caused the swimmer to drown, it seems correct that had the lifeguard noticed him, he would have lived. Yet defenders of the skeptical view seem to assume, without sufficient argument, that this counterfactual dependence can only ground genuine causation when it involves positive facts. What metaphysical or philosophical assumptions drive this asymmetry, and should we really accept them? Try to identify where the skeptic's position might actually rest on undefended commitments about the nature of facts, events, or causation itself.
