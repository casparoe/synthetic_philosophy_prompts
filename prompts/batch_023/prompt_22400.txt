I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I'm a student rep on my hospital's neuroethics comittee. We have a case that's got me a bit stuck: a 41-year-old woman had surgery years ago to disconnect the two hemispheres of her brain because of intractable epilepsy. She speaks with the left side, but her right hand can point to cards and make choices even when she doesn't seem to notice. In one test, a word is flashed to the side of her visual field that reaches the non-speaking hemisphere, and her right hand points to the matching picture, but she verbally says she didn't see anything and doesn't know why her hand chose it. In a meeting about a proposed pacemaker, she says she doesn't want it, but her right hand points to the 'yes' card, and a few minutes later she says she has no idea why her hand did that. The neurologist says she passes the usual verbal capacity assessment, but the conflict makes us uneasy. Her brother says the hand is 'her' and should count, while her sister says the speaking part is 'her' because it has access to her memories, voice, and relationships.

We need a short written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting. Please recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame: for example, whether to treat the verbal report as decisive, whether to require a procedure for resolving the conflict, whether to defer to a surrogate, or some other workable rule. It should engage both siblings' positions seriously, say what kind of evidence we should collect before deciding, and include a default rule if the evidence remains inconclusive. Don't just say 'we need more data' or hide behind the legal definition of capacity; I want something the committee could actually vote on.
