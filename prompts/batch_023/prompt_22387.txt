I have been thinking on my own about what it would mean for a policy change to be a safe Pareto improvement, and I have written the following short draft. Please comment on whether the central definition is coherent, whether it is genuinely different from an ordinary Pareto improvement, and whether the bridge example supports the conclusion. Please also say whether the essay is interesting as a starting point, or whether it mostly restates a familiar distinction. I am especially unsure about the move from expected harm to harm in any plausible scenario, and I would like suggestions for making that part clearer or more defensible.

Draft:

A safe Pareto improvement is not merely a change that makes everyone at least as well off and someone better off. Because we cannot know the future with certainty, I think it should require that, for every person, there is a reasonable bound on how much worse off they could become under any plausible state of the world, and that at least one person is better off in every plausible state. For example, suppose a town plans to replace an old bridge with a stronger one. The new bridge would benefit most residents by reducing commute times, but if a rare geological event occurs, the construction might trigger a landslide that harms a few nearby homes. If the expected benefits outweigh the expected harms, a standard cost-benefit analysis might approve the project. Under my definition, however, the project is not a safe Pareto improvement unless the design is changed so that no plausible scenario makes any resident worse off, or unless the affected residents are fully compensated in every such scenario. My claim is that this is a more honest standard for public policy, because it treats the possibility of harm as a reason to redesign rather than merely to discount.
