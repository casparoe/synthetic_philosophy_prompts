I am a PhD student in philosophy of science. Two of my research-group colleagues had a debate I could not resolve. (1) Alex said the pessimistic meta-induction is the strongest case against scientific realism: past successful theories such as phlogiston, the luminiferous ether, and Newtonian mechanics had false ontologies, so we should expect our successful quantum field theory and general relativity to be replaced with false ontologies. (2) Sam replied that the pessimistic meta-induction is a bad induction, because it compares unlike cases: older theories were less empirically successful, less mathematically unified, and less embedded in later theory, whereas current theories have explanatory and predictive success that is hard to explain if their core entities are wholly fictional. (3) Alex answered that success is not a reliable guide to truth, because a theory can be pragmatically fit for prediction without being true, and historical cases show that success can survive radical reinterpretation. (4) Sam conceded that success is not proof of truth, but said it gives a non-accidental explanatory link: if a theory is very successful and its structure is preserved in later theory, we have positive evidence for some approximate truth, not a reason to assume total failure. (5) Alex then said that structural realism is just a retreat: it preserves “structure” but leaves the term vague, and historically even mathematical structures have been revised, so it does not answer the pessimistic meta-induction. (6) Sam replied that vagueness is not fatal if structural realism is understood as a methodological claim: we should retain what survives across theory change, and the pessimistic meta-induction only shows that unqualified entity realism is risky. Please adjudicate move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is clearly wrong, defensible, or clearly right, with a brief justification, and then say what both sides are missing.
