I'm currently taking a seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and I've been trying to make sense of a dispute over whether rational credences should generally defer to objective chance. In particular, I'm interested in Lewis's Principal Principle, which says that if an agent knows the objective chance of a proposition is c and has no inadmissible evidence, then the agent's credence in that proposition should be c. I find the principle compelling in simple cases, such as a known-fair coin or a radioactive atom with a known decay probability, but I'm less sure how it should apply when the agent has evidence about the physical process that seems to be neither part of the chance nor logically independent of it. For example, suppose the agent knows that the objective chance of a particle decaying in the next hour is 0.01, but also has a reliable, non-chance-based prediction—perhaps from a hidden-variable theory or a sophisticated detector calibration—that the particle will not decay. The difficult question is whether the agent should still set her credence at 0.01, or whether the prediction counts as inadmissible evidence that blocks deference to chance.

It should also consider whether the principle is best understood as a substantive rational norm, a constraint on how chance enters Bayesian updating, or merely a heuristic for cases in which the agent lacks relevant non-chance evidence.
