I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I keep finding the common prior assumption in Aumann's agreement theorem harder to pin down than the standard summaries suggest. Many presentations state it as though the agents must literally have the same probability distribution over the original states of the world, but that seems either too strong or potentially misleading, because the theorem appears to depend more on the structure of what is publicly known about the agents' information and updating than on a simple shared prior over the world.

Please argue for the following position: the common prior assumption is best understood not as requiring identity of priors over the original state space, but as requiring a common prior over a richer epistemic state space that includes the agents' information partitions, signal structures, and rationality conditions. In your argument, explain why this richer-space reading preserves the force of Aumann's result, why it avoids making the theorem vacuous by allowing arbitrary priors over the original world, and what it implies for cases where agents have different background theories or different likelihood functions but publicly know each other's rationality and updating rules. Please be careful to distinguish the common prior condition from mere mutual knowledge of rationality, and from the weaker requirement that agents' posterior probabilities become common knowledge after sufficiently many rounds of public announcements.
