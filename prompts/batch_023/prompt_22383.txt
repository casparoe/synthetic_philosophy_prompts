I'm trying to write remarks for a memorial for my former colleague, a psychiatrist-philosopher who spent most of her career working on Hacking's looping effects in psychiatric classification—how a category like depression, resilience, or recovery doesn't just describe people but feeds back on them, so that the people "with" the category change, and then the category has to be revised. She died suddenly, and the room will be a mess of her family, a few clinicians, and a handfull of philosophers who will immediately hear the trap I'm in.

I want the speech to do the work that her work did: it should let us say something true about who she was without turning her into a stable object, a diagnostic case, or a professional legend, and it should make the looping point feel like a way of mourning rather than a footnote. The bit I keep missing is the second-order point: it's not just that "depressed" or "resilient" people act differently, but that the way we talk about her in the room will change what it means to have known her. Please write it as a 4-5 minute eulogy, with one concrete anecdote placeholder if needed, that comforts the family without pretending that our categories are innocent, and that acknowledges that even my telling her story now will loop back on us and change what she was to us.
