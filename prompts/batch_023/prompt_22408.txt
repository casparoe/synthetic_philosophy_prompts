I am drafting a short essay for a seminar on social choice theory and would like comments on a particular interpretation of Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives. My working claim is that IIA is best understood as a constraint on agenda sensitivity rather than merely as a technical condition that makes the proof work. On this reading, if a social welfare function ranks x above y for a given profile, then it should continue to rank x above y whenever the profile is changed only in ways that leave every individual's relative ranking of x and y unchanged, even if the set of available alternatives is expanded or reduced. I want to argue that this is what gives the theorem its philosophical force: it rules out aggregation rules in which the social judgment about one pair of alternatives is allowed to be influenced by the presence or absence of a third alternative that is not itself at issue.

My worry is that I may be conflating IIA with a broader anti-manipulability requirement, or with the idea that social preferences should be stable under irrelevant changes of description. Could you comment on whether this characterization is accurate, where it is too strong, and whether I should add a minimal example with three alternatives and three voters to show a non-dictatorial rule that satisfies Pareto efficiency but fails IIA? I would especially like feedback on whether the phrase “agenda sensitivity” is precise enough, or whether it risks confusing IIA with the failure of many voting rules to be invariant under the addition of spoilers.
