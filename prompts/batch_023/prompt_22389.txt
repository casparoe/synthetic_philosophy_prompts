I'm in an AI alignment course this semester and we've been doing inverse RL for inferring human preferences, and I'm stuck on a point that seems basic. I read that the reward function you recover from demonstrations is only identified up to a monotonic transformation, which makes sense if you think of it as a utility function. But then why do inverse RL papers still treat the learned reward as a scalar utility and compare its numeric values across states, like saying the person 'prefers' one state to another because its reward is higher? Isn't that exactly the mistake decision theory warns about, since utility is only ordinal? And if all the method can really give you is an ordering over states, why bother representing it as a scalar reward at all? Isn't the whole approach secretly assuming that the numerical reward is the thing being inferred, rather than just the preference order?

I keep thinking maybe the scalar is just a computational trick, but I can't seem to seperate the 'reward as model' part from the 'reward as preference' part. I feel like I'm missing whether the issue is just notation or whether inverse RL is making a stronger claim than the data support.
