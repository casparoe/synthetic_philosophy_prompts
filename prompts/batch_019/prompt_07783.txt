I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory, and this term we devoted a session to one of the stranger arguments in that literature: evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL), still sometimes called by its original name, multiverse-wide cooperation via correlated decision-making, after researchers such as Caspar Oesterheld who worked it out in detail. The argument runs, roughly, as follows. If the universe or multiverse is spatially infinite, or otherwise contains vastly many regions permanently outside one another's light cones, then there almost certainly exist civilizations, and perhaps artificial agents, whose reasoning is correlated with ours, not because we could ever signal to or be causally influenced by them, but because similar starting conditions tend to produce similar decision procedures, much as two people who reason identically will reach the same answer to a puzzle without ever meeting. Because your choice is then evidence about what these correlated but permanently unreachable agents choose, in exactly the way that in Hofstadter's twin prisoner's dilemma your cooperating is evidence that your psychological twin cooperates too, an evidential decision theorist can conclude that it is rational to unilaterally moderate your own values toward some compromise with the unknown, unverifiable values of these other agents: in effect, trading with parties you will never meet, message, or catch defecting.

One of my students asked, after we had spent an hour formalizing all this, whether anyone had actually tried to dramatize the argument rather than just prove things about it, and I could not think of a good example. I would like you to have a go. Please write a short story, in the neighborhood of 800 to 1,500 words, built around the moment when someone, or some institution, has to decide whether to act on ECL reasoning. You might set it among researchers deciding whether to build this kind of reasoning into a powerful institution or artificial agent; or in the ruling council of a civilization, human or otherwise, debating whether to ratify a revised, more cosmopolitan set of values purely on the strength of an argument that yields no observable evidence either way; or wherever else you judge the tension comes alive best.

What I most want is a story that makes a sharp, skeptical reader feel why someone might stake real, present-day resources on the reasoning and preferences of agents who can never be seen, thanked, or double-crossed, rather than one that simply narrates the theory at the reader. Please also keep the decision theory honest: the story should not quietly slide into ordinary cooperation between parties who can actually communicate or verify each other's existence, since the entire philosophical bite of ECL lies in cooperation that has to survive without any of that.
