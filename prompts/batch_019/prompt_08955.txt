I'm taking an epistemology seminar this term, and this week's discussion-section handout turned into a whole thing in my study group over text. The case: Priya is on the waitlist for an oversubscribed seminar and keeps refreshing the registration portal to see if a spot opened up. Normally the page pulls live from the registrar's database and is about as reliable as these things get. She refreshes and it says "Enrolled." This is true -- she is in -- but only because of a caching bug: the page had frozen about ninety seconds after the registrar actually bumped her from waitlisted to enrolled, and then just kept serving that same frozen snapshot to anyone who loaded it during the two-hour outage window. If the freeze had hit two minutes earlier, the page would still be showing "Waitlisted," which was accurate up to that point.

Two people in the group, Maya and Chris, would not let this go. Maya's opening claim was that this is obviously a Gettier case: Priya has a justified true belief (checking the portal is normally about as reliable as a method gets) but doesn't know she's enrolled, because whatever actually produced the belief in that moment -- a frozen cache -- wasn't tracking her enrollment status at all. Chris said reliabilism handles this fine without giving up knowledge: the relevant process is "checking the registration portal," which is reliable the vast majority of the time, and a two-hour glitch shouldn't disqualify the method any more than an occasional floater in your eye disqualifies vision as a reliable process. Maya replied that Chris was just helping himself to a convenient grain of description -- call the process "checking the portal, ever" and it looks great, call it "reading a frozen cache during the outage" and it looks terrible, and nothing about reliabilism tells you which description is the right one, so it can't settle the case without begging the question (apparently this is called the generality problem, which we'd literally just covered in lecture). Chris shot back that the same objection boomerangs onto Maya, since "reading a frozen cache" is just as gerrymandered a description as his was, chosen precisely because it gives the verdict she wants, and that some tolerance for local malfunction has to be built into any workable reliabilism or basically no process ever counts as reliable. Maya's last word was that there's a real asymmetry here and not just question-begging: if the freeze could easily have hit a couple minutes earlier, Priya would have ended up with the exact same confident belief while it was still false, and that's what's doing the work, not how you happen to describe the process.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's actually right at each step, not just who you'd side with overall? I'm especially curious whether Maya's "generality problem" move really damages Chris's reliabilism or just shows both of them are choosing descriptions to suit themselves, and whether there's a way of defending reliabilism here that neither of them thought of.
