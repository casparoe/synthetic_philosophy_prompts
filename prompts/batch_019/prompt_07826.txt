So for extra credit in my intro-to-CS class (my teacher lets us do a "tech history" writeup instead of one homework set, it's kind of a whole thing) I picked John von Neumann, and I went down this whole rabbit hole reading about an essay he wrote for Fortune magazine in 1955 called "Can We Survive Technology?", like two years before he died. His big point is that technology sped up so much that stuff which used to only cause local damage now automatically turns into a whole-planet problem, because the actual physical size of the Earth is basically the limiting factor now, not our tools or how fast news/people/effects can travel. And then the part that really got me: he specifically says "just ban whichever technology seems scariest" isn't a real fix, because useful and dangerous tech are too tangled up together to cleanly seperate (his example is how classified vs. open science never actually stays seperated for more than a few years, it always leaks back together). So his actual answer to how you survive is basically that progress has no cure, and all we get forever is "day-to-day...opportunistic measures, a long sequence of small, correct decisions" — which felt like a weirdly deflating answer from the guy everyone calls the smartest person of the 20th century.

Heres what's really bugging me though: my friend who's obsessed with AI safety stuff pointed out that this basically already dooms "differential technological development," which is this approach some AI safety people push now where you deliberately speed up safety/alignment research while slowing down the riskiest capabilities research — because that's just a fancier version of the "separate the good tech from the bad tech" move von Neumann already shot down in '55. And apparently he ALSO told his friend Ulam, separately, that accelerating technology was heading toward some kind of singularity past which human affairs as we currently understand them basically couldn't continue anymore, which is such a wild thing to have said in the 1950s.

So genuinely, what do you think von Neumann would say if you showed him today's AI safety debate — stuff like people calling for a pause on training bigger models, or treaties to restrict the riskiest capabilities work while boosting alignment research? Does his own logic from the 1955 essay basically force him to call that a doomed non-solution, or is there some way to reconcile "no cure for progress, only small opportunistic decisions" with actually being pro-pause? I genuinely can't tell if he'd land as an accelerationist, a doomer, or just some kind of fatalist about the whole thing.
