I have been reporting on how moral philosophers handle rescue dilemmas, and the exchange I keep returning to is the disagreement between John Taurek and T. M. Scanlon over whether numbers should ever decide who gets saved. Both reject the utilitarian move of simply summing welfare across people, yet they end up in different places. Write a short, sharp dialogue between a committed Taurekian and a Scanlonian contractualist debating a case in which a rescuer can save either one stranger or a group of five others, but not both, stipulating that there is no further morally relevant difference between the two options. The Taurekian should favor deciding by some equal-chance procedure, such as a coin flip, on the grounds that each person's claim is separate and is not enlarged simply because there happen to be more claimants on one side. The Scanlonian should hold that once individual claims are of equal strength, their number can still break the tie in favor of the larger group without this amounting to aggregation. Each should press the other on the mechanics of their proposal rather than on its intuitive appeal alone: does a coin flip really take the five claims as seriously as the one, and is deciding by numbers at the tie-breaking stage really different from summing them? Keep both characters equally sharp, and have them genuinely respond to one another rather than talk past each other.
