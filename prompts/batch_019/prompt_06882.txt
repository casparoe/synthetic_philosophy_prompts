A friend and I spent an evening arguing about the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents and never resolved it. Their view: once a tiling agent replaces proof-theoretic self-verification with a probabilistic, calibration-based notion of reflection — handing off control to a successor whenever it has arbitrarily high credence, rather than a proof, that the successor's reasoning is sound — the obstacle dissolves, since Löb's theorem constrains what a system can prove about its own soundness schema, not what it can rationally believe about it. My view: this move doesn't dissolve the regress but relocates it, since the coherence conditions that let a probabilistic reflection principle escape contradiction (by settling, Tarski-style, for approximate rather than exact self-reference) must themselves be underwritten from outside the system, and nothing in the construction makes that externally imposed trust any less question-begging than the proof-based demand it replaced. Please make the strongest case you can for my side: argue that trading provability for calibrated credence does not overcome the Löbian obstacle but only relocates the regress by one level, and take on directly the best reply available to them — that epsilon-bounded approximate reflection is still genuine progress precisely because exact reflection was never achievable even in principle.
