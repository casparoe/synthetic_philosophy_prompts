Throughout my career as an experimental physicist, I took for granted that a good explanation shows an event to be an instance of some general law, given the relevant initial conditions -- what philosophers call the "covering-law" (or deductive-nomological) model of explanation. Since retiring, I have been reading in the philosophy of history and came across Hempel's attempt to extend this same model to history, treating ordinary historical explanations as incomplete "explanation sketches" standing in for a covering-law argument -- a proposal that met serious resistance from philosophers such as Dray and Walsh, who favor a "narrative" mode of explanation instead. I can restate both positions, but I do not yet feel the force of the disagreement: it is not obvious to me why an explanation of, say, why a particular revolution succeeded, could not in principle be cashed out as a very complicated, largely implicit, covering-law argument. Could you explain what narrativists think a narrative supplies that a covering-law explanation, however elaborated, cannot -- what real explanatory work is done by devices such as colligation -- and then give your own assessment of whether the dispute marks a genuine difference in the logical structure of historical versus scientific explanation, or is really just a disagreement about how explicit the underlying generalizations need to be made?
