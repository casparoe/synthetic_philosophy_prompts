At a philosophy-of-mind reading group that grew out of a local rationalist meetup, two regulars I will call Owen and Nadia spent an evening arguing over Kim's causal exclusion argument, and a week later I still cannot decide who had the better of it.

(1) Owen opened with the argument itself: if the physical world is causally closed and every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, then a mental property that is merely realized by (not identical to) some physical property has no causal work left to do -- non-reductive physicalists, he said, are stuck choosing between reduction and epiphenomenalism.

(2) Nadia answered with a proportionality move: a pigeon trained to peck at red pecks at whatever shade of red it is shown, so the cause of the peck is the determinable "red," not the specific wavelength, even though the wavelength alone was physically sufficient. Mental properties, being multiply realizable in just this way, are not rivals to their realizers for causal credit -- they screen off the extra physical detail as noise.

(3) Owen replied that this only shows "red" is a good category for explaining and predicting pecks, not that it names a cause over and above the specific wavelength that struck the retina that day; Nadia, he said, was conflating causation with causal explanation.

(4) Nadia pushed back with Woodward's interventionist theory of causation: a property counts as a genuine cause if intervening on it changes the effect, and across the relevant interventions -- holding everything else fixed, swapping which realizer is present -- it is the mental property, not the extra physical detail, that the effect actually tracks.

(5) Owen's closing move was that no such intervention is really available: you cannot vary the mental property while holding its realizer fixed, since the mental property is nothing over and above the realizer, so any "intervention" on the mental level just is a redescribed intervention on the physical one.

They were interrupted before either conceded anything. Could you adjudicate moves (1) through (5)?
