I spent thirty-odd years doing biomedical research before I retired, mostly running clinical trials, and one of the eternal fights on every trial I worked on was whether to stop now or gather one more round of data before deciding anything. So a few months ago, when a friend from my new philosophy discussion group brought up Good's theorem on the value of information (this is the same I. J. Good who cut his teeth as a codebreaker under Turing at Bletchley Park, small world), it felt like something I'd been informally rediscovering my whole career without ever having the clean, formal version of it. The theorem, as I understand it, says a Bayesian decision-maker who updates properly on new cost-free evidence and then acts to maximize expected utility can never end up worse off in expectation for having looked first, only better or exactly the same as if they hadn't looked at all.

What's been rattling around in my head ever since is all the fine print that makes that result a lot less obvious than it sounds at first: the fact that its a claim about expectation and not about any particular draw of evidence, the fact that "cost-free" is quietly doing a huge amount of work, and the fact that the whole guarantee can apparently flip once your in a genuinely strategic situation with another mind reacting to what you now know. I'd love a joke built out of that material, one where the actual punchline depends on one of those wrinkles rather than being some generic "nerds love more data" gag, so that it gets a real laugh out of someone who's sat through a stats or decision theory seminar, and mostly a blank stare from everyone else. Think you can pull that off for me?
