Over the past year I've gotten mildly obsessed, purely as a hobby, with a puzzle from game theory called the centipede game, even though I've never studied philosophy or economics formally and am mostly just working through papers and lecture notes I find online in my spare time. For anyone unfamiliar: two players alternate moves, and at each turn the player to act can either "take," ending the game and pocketing slightly more than half of a pot that has been growing the whole time, or "pass," which shrinks their own current share a little but grows the total pot for the next round, up to some fixed final move where the game ends automatically (it's called "centipede" because of how the game tree looks when you draw out all the little alternating branches). The textbook solution, reached by backward induction, is that the very last player should always take, so the second-to-last player should anticipate that and take one move earlier instead, and so on all the way back to the first move — meaning two perfectly rational players should never cooperate even once, and should end the game immediately for the smallest possible pot. What surprised me is that Robert Aumann apparently proved this conclusion follows quite generally just from assuming it is common knowledge that both players are rational, which seems like a very modest premise to be doing that much work. I've also come across writers, Ken Binmore among them (and I think something similar is in Philip Reny's work, though I'm less sure I've understood him correctly), who argue that Aumann's proof, whatever its mathematical merits, doesn't actually settle what a rational player should do in the game, since it says nothing about how a player ought to revise their beliefs about their opponent once they find themselves at a point in the game that "common knowledge of rationality" was supposed to guarantee would never be reached.

What I'd like is for you to construct the strongest version you can of the argument that backward induction is not, in fact, the rationally mandated way to play the centipede game, and I'd like the argument to engage directly with Aumann's proof rather than sidestep it. In particular, please don't rest the case on the observation that real people cooperate in practice — I already know that, and on its own it isn't an objection to what rationality requires in principle. Instead, build the argument around the idea that a player who reaches, say, move 30 of a 100-move game has thereby acquired information bearing on what they should now believe about how their opponent will play the remaining moves, and that no version of "common knowledge of rationality" can coherently tell that player both that this situation should never arise and what they are rationally obligated to believe once it has. If, after doing this as rigorously as you can, you think the argument ultimately doesn't succeed against an Aumann-style defense, you're welcome to say so — but I'd like to see the strongest case made first rather than a quick verdict.
