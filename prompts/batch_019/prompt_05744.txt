I'm on my high school's ethics bowl team, and one of the cases we're prepping this year involves a drone operator who suspects (but can't fully confirm) that a strike order is hitting the wrong house. Our coach had us read part of Walzer's *Just and Unjust Wars* plus a handout on Jeff McMahan's objections to it (the whole jus ad bellum vs jus in bello split), and it turned our study session into chaos because two of my teammates, Diego and Naomi, got into this huge argument in the library that I honestly can't stop thinking about.

It started when Diego said the "moral equality of soldiers" thing is basically correct: an ordinary soldier who follows the rules of war (doesn't target civilians, doesn't abuse POWs, etc) isn't a murderer even if his country started the war unjustly — that's literally why we don't put random enemy POWs on trial for murder, only the leaders who started the war get judged for that. Naomi said that's mixing up two different questions: whether we should punish someone, and whether what they did was actually okay. She said McMahan's whole point is that an unjust soldier can be excused for killing (because he was lied to, indoctrinated, whatever) without it being true that his killing was justified the same way a soldier defending against aggression is — excuse and justification aren't the same thing, even if they lead to the same "don't put him on trial" conclusion. Diego's comeback was that this is unrealistic: soldiers can't independently verify whether their side's cause is just before they fight, they get fed propaganda and bad intel, and refusing orders mid-war can get you shot by your own side — so its not fair to hold soldiers to a moral standard they have no real way of passing. Naomi said that's exactly the excuse argument and she thinks McMahan would just agree with it, soldiers often shouldn't be blamed much — but insisted that still doesn't make the killing itself okay, those are separate questions. Diego then said okay, but if that's true, doesn't it make the actual laws of war kind of pointless, since stuff like symmetric POW protections is built entirely around treating both sides' soldiers the same regardless of cause? Naomi said she read that McMahan deals with this too, by separating the "deep morality" of who's really permitted to kill whom from what the law/policy should say, and he thinks the law should stay symmetric anyway for practical reasons (soldiers can't verify causes in the moment, symmetric rules keep some restraint and reciprocity going) even though he thinks that symmetric law doesn't match the deeper moral truth. Diego said that feels like a bit of a cop-out, because if the "deep" morality never actually shows up anywhere in how anyone is treated, he's not sure what work it's even doing — and that's basically where they left it when the bell rang.
