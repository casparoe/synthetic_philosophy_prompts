I don't have any formal training in philosophy or computer science, but I've always been a bit fascinated by Alan Turing, and a few weeks ago I decided to stop reading summaries of his work and go straight to his original 1950 paper where he lays out the "imitation game" that everyone now calls the Turing test. I found a copy online and worked through it slowly over a couple of evenings. Most of it matched what I already knew from documentaries and articles, but I was genuinely thrown by a section near the end where Turing works through a list of objections to his own proposal, because one of them is literally titled "The Argument from Extrasensory Perception." In it, he treats the statistical case for telepathy as more or less overwhelming, and he even proposes that, to run the imitation game properly, you might need to seat the human and the machine in a specially shielded "telepathy-proof room" so that a psychic interrogator couldn't unfairly single out the human.

I brought this up with a friend who works in software, fully expecting her to tell me I'd misunderstood something, but it turned out she had never even heard of this part of the paper either. The more I sit with it, the more it feels like a genuine problem rather than a quirky footnote: if one of the founders of computer science thought extrasensory perception was a live enough possibility that it needed its own shielded room, then the imitation game was never really the clean, purely behavioral test of intelligence it's remembered as today; it was quietly resting on the assumption that ordinary people might have paranormal powers that a machine could never be credited with, and the shielded room was just a patch over that assumption rather than a genuine fix. Doesn't this mean that the modern "Turing test," as people still invoke it, has a piece of discredited pseudoscience built into its foundations? And if Turing himself didn't fully trust his own test to rule out ESP, shouldn't that make us doubt whether passing the imitation game was ever really supposed to be good evidence of genuine machine thought?
