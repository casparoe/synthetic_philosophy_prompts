ok so I went down a rabbit hole this week that started with an ACX comment thread about moral circle expansion and ended with me actually reading Descartes instead of just absorbing the meme version of him. Everyone (me included, until now) summarizes Discourse on Method Part V as "Descartes thought animals are unfeeling machines," but the actual passage that's supposed to be the source of that doesn't obviously say anything about feeling at all. Here's the bit (pulled this translation off wikisource so forgive any clunky phrasing):

"...if any such machines had the organs and outward shape of a monkey or of some other animal that lacks reason, we should have no means of knowing that they did not possess entirely the same nature as these animals; whereas if any such machines bore a resemblance to our bodies and imitated our actions as closely as possible for all practical purposes, we should still have two very certain means of recognizing that they were not real men. The first is that they could never use words, or put together other signs, as we do in order to declare our thoughts to others. For we can easily conceive of a machine so made that it utters words, and even that it would utter some in response to bodily actions which cause a change in its organs, but not that it should arrange them in various ways so as to reply appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, as even the most stupid human beings can do. And the second means is that, although such machines might do many things as well as, or perhaps even better than, any of us, they would infallibly fail in certain others, by which we would discover that they did not act through knowledge, but only through the disposition of their organs. For while reason is a universal instrument that can be used in all kinds of situations, these organs need a specific disposition for each specific action, from which it follows that it is morally impossible for a machine to have enough different organs to make it act in all the contingencies of life in the way our reason makes us act."

So my question: does this argument actually get you to 'animals don't feel pain,' or does it only get you to 'animals don't reason / use language'? Both tests are about flexible, general-purpose thought, not (obviously) about sentience. Feels like theres a whole extra step -- from "no reason" to "no feeling" -- that this passage just doesn't take. Is that step made somewhere else in Descartes (letters maybe?), or is the "unfeeling machine" reading more of a popular flattening of a narrower argument? Would love a close reading of exactly what the passage does and doesn't commit him to.
