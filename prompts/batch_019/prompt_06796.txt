I'm working on a piece about how today's fights over "political art" keep recycling an argument the Harlem Renaissance already had, in far sharper terms than we usually give it credit for. In June 1926, W. E. B. Du Bois told the NAACP's annual conference that "all art is propaganda and ever must be," arguing that Black artists were obliged to marshal beauty in the service of racial uplift and against caricature. Two and a half years later, in the debut issue of Harlem magazine, Alain Locke answered him in an essay titled "Art or Propaganda?," insisting that protest art traps its audience inside the very posture of grievance it means to escape, and that the artist's deeper obligation is to individual expression and universal appeal, not racial image management.

Write a dialogue of around 500 words in which Du Bois and Locke fight this out over a real test case: Claude McKay's novel Home to Harlem (1928), full of unrepentant hustlers, cabarets, and drifters, with no uplifting moral in sight, which Du Bois himself denounced. Have each press the other's genuine weak point. Does Locke's art-for-its-own-sake quietly assume a freedom from politics that no Black artist in 1928 actually has? Does Du Bois's propaganda standard end up policing Black life almost as tightly as the caricatures he's fighting? Don't let either man simply concede the point or land a clean final blow.
