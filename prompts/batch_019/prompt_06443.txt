My friend and I had a long argument last night about Hobbes's insistence that civil philosophy could be made as rigorous as geometry, the one science he regarded as entirely beyond doubt. He convinced me that because Leviathan is explicitly built on the model of Euclidean demonstration, its central argument, from the miseries of the state of nature to the necessity of an absolute, undivided sovereign, carries the very same demonstrative certainty as a geometric proof, provided one grants Hobbes's initial definitions. If that's right, though, I don't understand why historians of political thought still treat the move from the state of nature to absolute sovereignty as one of the most contested steps in the book, rather than as settled as, say, the Pythagorean theorem. Shouldn't a genuinely geometrical argument leave no room for that kind of ongoing dispute?
