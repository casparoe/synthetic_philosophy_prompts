I work as a software engineer, and over the past year I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole reading about statutory interpretation, mostly triggered by recurring legal fights over the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) — the 1986 U.S. law that makes it a crime to access a computer "without authorization" or in a way that "exceeds authorized access." That language has been stretched over the decades to cover things far removed from what most people would call hacking, from Aaron Swartz's mass-downloading of journal articles over MIT's network to garden-variety terms-of-service violations. The case that really pulled me in was Van Buren v. United States (2021), where the majority (Barrett writing, in what I recall being a 6–3 decision with Thomas, Roberts, and Alito dissenting) adopted a narrow, textualist "gates-up-or-down" reading of the statute: either you're technically entitled to access a given file or system or you're not, and misusing information you were otherwise allowed to see doesn't count as "exceeding authorized access." Part of the majority's reasoning was that the government's broader reading would turn an enormous amount of ordinary, harmless computer use into a federal crime. The dissent, from what I gathered, put more weight on what Congress plausibly meant to prohibit, even if that meant reading the statutory language a bit less literally than the majority did.

Thinking about this case made me want to know how Jeremy Bentham would have reacted to the whole textualism-versus-purposivism split, since his own jurisprudence seems to pull in both directions at once. On one hand, he was ferociously hostile to judges exercising interpretive discretion beyond the enacted text — his attack on Blackstone in A Fragment on Government, his lifelong campaign for comprehensive codification, and his writings on what he called "nomography" (the art of drafting laws precisely enough that courts would have nothing left to interpret) all seem motivated by a desire to eliminate exactly the kind of freewheeling "what was the legislature really trying to achieve" reasoning that purposivists rely on. On the other hand, his entire theory of legislation rests on the principle of utility, and he was preoccupied with proportionate punishment and with keeping vague, overbroad criminal statutes from handing excessive discretion to prosecutors and judges — concerns that, on their face, seem to favor exactly the outcome the Van Buren majority reached, just not by the majority's chosen method.

So here's what I'd like your help with: based on what you actually know of Bentham's writings on law and legislation, rather than the simplified "codification guy" version of him you sometimes get in intro discussions, how do you think he would have approached a case like Van Buren? Would he have landed with the textualist majority, sided with the dissent's more purpose-driven reading, or rejected the terms of that whole opposition in favor of something distinctively his own? And more broadly, do his commitments actually resolve into a coherent position here, or does he just hold two principles that happen to point in different directions depending on the case — in which case, would it be a mistake to claim him as a forerunner of either side of the modern debate?
