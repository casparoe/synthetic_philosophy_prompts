In a seminar on normative ethics that I'm taking this term, one of my classmates recently argued that act utilitarianism can be refuted using nothing more than well-established facts about computational complexity, and I haven't been able to find the flaw in the argument. The reasoning went roughly as follows: identifying the action that truly maximizes aggregate welfare in any realistic situation requires comparing the total welfare produced by an enormous number of alternative actions and their downstream consequences; formally, this is structurally identical to the combinatorial optimization problems that theoretical computer scientists classify as NP-hard, for which it is widely believed (though not proven) that no algorithm can find the exact optimal solution in polynomial time as the size of the problem grows. Since act utilitarianism defines the right action as whichever action actually maximizes aggregate welfare, and since we now have strong theoretical grounds for thinking that no procedure, human or mechanical, could reliably identify that action except in toy-sized cases, my classmate concluded that act utilitarianism fails as a criterion of right action in much the same way a scientific theory would fail if it turned out to make no determinate predictions at all.

Given that this argument seems to rest on solid results from computer science rather than on any of the usual contestable claims about moral psychology or intuitions, why do so many contemporary utilitarians continue to treat act utilitarianism as a live, serious theory of the right, rather than as a position that complexity theory has, in effect, already refuted, in much the same way a proof of inconsistency would refute a theory in any other domain?
