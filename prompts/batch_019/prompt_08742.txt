I have been working through Hobbes's Leviathan on my own, and the chapter on persons, actors, and authors has stayed with me: his rule seems to be that an act carries genuine responsibility only if an "author" stands behind the "actor" who performs it, and that anything lacking reason in its own right -- he gives children, the insane, and even inanimate things like a bridge or a hospital as examples -- can be represented as acting but can never itself be an author, so some existing owner or guardian must answer for it instead. What I cannot work out is what Hobbes would have said about a machine-learning system whose specific decision in a given case was never willed or foreseen by the programmer, the owner, or anyone else involved, so that there is no obvious candidate to "own" the act in his sense, even though the system is not reasoning the way a natural person does either. This looks to me like an old-fashioned version of what is now sometimes called a "responsibility gap." Reasoning strictly from Hobbes's own concepts of authorization and covenant, rather than importing later ideas like strict liability or corporate personhood, would he have concluded that such acts simply lack an author, or would his framework have forced him to pin authorship on some existing person regardless -- and if so, on whom, and by what argument?
