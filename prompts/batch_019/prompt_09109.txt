Okay, this is going to sound like a weirdly specific rabbit hole, but here goes. I'm a CS PhD student, but I talked my way into cross registering for a grad seminar on 17th century natural philosophy this term (long story, don't ask), and the last two sessions were entirely about Hobbes's decades-long feud with John Wallis over geometry. The guy spent something like the last twenty years of his life insisting he'd squared the circle and trisected the angle, getting publicly demolished by Wallis over and over in pamphlet after pamphlet with titles I can't even keep straight anymore, and he never backed down once. What actually hooked me wasn't the math, which was apparently just bad, it was why he cared so much: for Hobbes, geometry isn't just one science among others, its basically the only domain where humans get total certainty - the kind of thing people call "maker's knowledge" - precisely because we generate the objects ourselves through definitions involving motion: a line is the path traced by a moving point, a circle is what a line sweeps out when it pivots around a fixed end, and so on. Physics, by contrast, he treats as merely "hypothetical," since we didn't make natural bodies and can never be sure we've hit on their actual causes.

So here's what's been rattling around in my head ever since: what happens to that whole picture once you get to Bolyai, Lobachevsky, and Riemann in the 1800s, who show that if you just swap out Euclid's parallel postulate for a different one, you get other perfectly rigorous, perfectly constructible geometries? Part of me thinks this should vindicate Hobbes - if certainty comes from construction, and there's more than one consistent thing you can construct, that's not a problem for his epistemology, it basically confirms it. But another part of me thinks it should wreck him, since he was also a committed materialist who thought geometry tracks the actual extension of actual bodies in actual space, and I'm pretty sure it never crossed his mind that "the" space bodies sit in might not be Euclidean - so doesn't that side of him need there to be exactly one true geometry, not a menu of equally legitimate ones? I don't want the standard "this is what refutes Kant's synthetic a priori" answer - I already know that one, and Hobbes isn't Kant, there's no talk of forms of intuition here, just nominalism, materialism, and motion. Could you actually work through, based on what he says about definitions and demonstration (De Corpore especially), which way his own commitments would pull him, and whether he'd end up flatly contradicting himself? And only if you've got room left, I'm also curious whether he'd have despised or secretly admired Hilbert's later move of stripping geometry of all intuitive content, since that's almost the exact opposite direction from where Hobbes wanted to go.
