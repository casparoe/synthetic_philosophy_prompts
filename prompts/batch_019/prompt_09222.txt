My grandma died last week, she was 84, and for almost forty years she ran a little wildlife rehab out of her garage — foxes, owls, the occasional very unhappy heron. To basically everyone in her town she was just "the animal lady." What most of them don't know is that for the last ten or fifteen years, mostly because I kept sending her stuff from my program, she got completely converted to the wild animal suffering view — the idea that nature isn't the peaceful idyll everyone defaults to but is mostly parasitism, disease, predation and enormous amounts of pointless death (in a lot of r-selected species something like 99% of offspring die within days of being born), and that this is a real moral catastrophe even though nobody agrees on whether or how we should intervene. She was not shy about this. I'm told she once, gently but very audibly, corrected a minister at another rehab volunteer's funeral for saying the woman was "back in nature's embrace now."

I'm her grandkid, and also a philosophy PhD student working on animal ethics largely because of her, and I got asked to give the eulogy this Saturday. The room is going to be maybe ninety percent rehab-centre volunteers and extended family who think about nature in the totally standard Bambi way, plus a handful of people from the wild-animal-welfare research world (the kind of people who know Oscar Horta and Brian Tomasik's stuff cold) who will absolutely notice if I cave and reach for the comforting version of nature she spent fifteen years arguing against. Could you write the eulogy for me? Five minutes or so, needs to genuinely comfort a room full of non-philosophers, can't misrepresent what she actually believed, and definately can't lean on any "returning to nature" imagery to close it out — I need something else to land on.
