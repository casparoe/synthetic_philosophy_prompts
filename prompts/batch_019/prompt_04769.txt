I spent about thirty-five years running a lab on foraging behavior in birds — patch-leaving decisions, delay-of-gratification choice tasks, that sort of thing — and hyperbolic discount curves were just a tool we fit to pigeons' and starlings' choice data, no more philosophically loaded than a growth curve. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy for fun, and its a bit disorienting to run into the very same curves in a totally different context: economists and psychologists like Ainslie use hyperbolic discounting to explain "weakness of will," while philosophers going back to Aristotle treat weakness of will (akrasia) as this much older puzzle about how someone can judge, all things considered, that X is better than Y, and then go and do Y anyway. Davidson's paper on this is the one I keep circling back to — his move is that the akratic person doesn't change there mind, they just don't act on it, which he treats as a specific, narrow kind of irrationality rather than as any change in what's valued. This has actually gotten urgent for me because a little philosophy reading group I joined is starting on self-control and addiction next month, and I'd rather go in with my head straight than just repeat whatever line I half-remember from a podcast.

What I can't work out on my own is whether Ainslie's story actually explains Davidson's puzzle or quietly swaps in a different one. On the discounting picture, the smaller-sooner reward's value only overtakes the larger-later reward's value as it gets close in time, so isn't the "weak-willed" chooser there just following their current, actual preference, rather than defying a judgment they still hold at the moment of choice? That seems importantly different from Davidson's "clear-eyed" akratic, who acts against a judgment that hasn't budged one bit. So: could you compare the two accounts properly and tell me, with the reasoning laid out, whether hyperbolic discounting is a genuine explanation of Davidsonian akrasia, an explanation of some other everyday phenomenon we've been lumping in with it (relapse, procrastination, breaking a diet), or something in between? I'd rather you stay focused on that specific question than hand me a broad survey of the weakness-of-will literature — though a line or two on where Aristotle himself wavered on whether akrasia is even possible would be a welcome bit of context if it actually bears on the answer.
