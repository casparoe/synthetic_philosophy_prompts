I'm teaching a graduate seminar on self-knowledge this term, and we just got through Williamson's case against the KK principle (if you know P, you're in a position to know that you know P). The way it's always presented -- in the textbook we're using, in every lecture I've seen on it, honestly in my own slides too -- is via his general anti-luminosity argument: take a condition that's obviously present in one case and obviously absent in another, chain the two together with a long series of cases that are pairwise indiscriminable to the subject, then use a margin-for-error principle (knowledge requires safety across nearby indiscriminable cases) to derive, absurdly, that the condition must hold all the way along the chain. The go-to illustration is always the same: somebody sitting outside from early morning to noon, feeling cold at the start and clearly not cold by the end. My students can now recite that example in their sleep, but I don't think half of them could actually tell you how it gets you all the way to a problem for KK specifically, as opposed to just showing "feeling cold" isn't luminous.

Can you help me out with two things? First, give me a fresh example of the anti-luminosity argument in a totally different domain -- not another gradually-changing-physical-stimulus-plus-perceptual-discrimination case, I want something further from the template -- laid out with the two end-cases and the indiscriminability chain between them spelled out explicitly. Second, and this the bit I actually care about: show how that very same structure, run with "knows that p" as the condition instead of whatever your example uses, gets you an actual violation of KK rather than just another luminosity failure. I want something I could put in front of the class that makes the move from "general anti-luminosity" to "KK in particular" obvious, since that's the part I keep ending up hand-waving through.
