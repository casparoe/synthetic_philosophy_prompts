I've been reading Hannah Arendt's essay on lying in politics on my own time, not for school or anything, and it actually started an argument at dinner. The trigger was a city council member a friend of mine used to work for -- for two years he's told everyone he was against a big development project "from day one," but my friend still has his old emails showing the guy was pushing hard for it before it got unpopular. When someone waved the emails at him in a meeting, he didn't even flinch, he just said "I have always opposed this," like he's fully convinced of it now.

Two other friends, Dana and Marcus, argued over whether that counts as lying.
(1) Dana: obviously yes, there's literally a paper trail proving the opposite of what he's saying.
(2) Marcus: Arendt's whole point is that lying requires the liar to know the truth he's hiding -- if this guy has actually rewritten his own memory by now, he's not lying when he says it, he's just wrong, and calling an honest (if very convenient) delusion a "lie" blurs a distinction that matters.
(3) Dana: the delusion kicked in exactly when it became useful to him, so letting "I fooled myself too" get him off the hook is way too generous -- that's just lying with a extra step.
(4) Marcus: fine, the timing's suspicious, but if every self-serving false belief counts as lying then the category basically swallows itself, and he's pretty sure Arendt treats the self-deceived propagandist as a different, scarier case than an ordinary liar, not as secretly the same thing.
(5) Dana: "different and scarier" still isn't "not lying" -- Marcus is just letting the guy hide behind a description of his own psychology.

Who's actually right at each step, (1) through (5), and is either of them misreading what Arendt thought lying actually requires? Also curious what you think both of them are missing.
