My grandmother Ruth died nine days ago at eighty-six, and I've been asked to give her eulogy at the funeral this Thursday. She ran a fabric shop for most of her life, but for almost twenty years she also sat in on a philosophy-and-Torah discussion group at her synagogue, and it was there, she told me once, that she first worked out what she called "the hiddenness problem": the thought that a genuinely loving God would not leave sincere, willing people unable to believe, and that such people plainly exist — which she thought was a harder problem for faith than the usual problem of evil. I only learned years later, in an undergraduate philosophy course I almost didn't take, that this has a name and an author, Schellenberg, and a whole literature I now realize she had basically reconstructed from scratch at her kitchen table. I work in software now and hadn't thought seriously about any of this in a decade, until this week.

What made her unusual is that she never used the argument to talk herself out of belief; she used it to describe her own devotional life. She was fond of hester panim, the older, more scriptural idea of God's "hiding of the face," and in her last years she'd settled on tzimtzum — the kabbalistic idea that God withdrew to make room for the world — not as an answer to the hiddenness problem but as the most honest description of it she'd found: a reason the hiddenness might be built into things rather than a fixable oversight. Going through her apartment this week, I found some version of this argument annotated into the margins of four different books, and a line in her own handwriting, tucked inside one of them, essentially instructing whoever gave her eulogy not to say she'd "gotten her answer" or was "finally seeing God's face" — on the grounds that this would erase the one thing she'd actually spent her life doing, which was staying faithful without that answer.

I want to honor that, but the room on Thursday won't make it easy: her sister and most of her generation will want, and deserve, the ordinary comfort of a Jewish funeral; my cousins have never heard of hester panim and shouldn't have to; and three women from her discussion group, who argued with her about exactly this for two decades, will be sitting together near the front and will know at once if I get her actual position wrong in either direction — into a pious certainty she didn't have, or a despair she didn't feel. I'm not even sure I share her belief, only her sense that the problem underneath it is real. Could you write me a eulogy, about five or six minutes long, that can do right by all of them at once?
