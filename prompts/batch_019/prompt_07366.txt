A couple of people in my forecasting group got into a long back-and-forth after our last meetup and I can't stop replaying it. We're the kind of group that tries to put actual numbers on stuff -- geopolitical events, whether a given clinical trial will succeed, that sort of thing -- and this time the trigger was Mia refusing to give one single number for whether a drug trial would read out positive by year end. She said her honest state was "somewhere between 15% and 45%, and the studies I've seen don't let me narrow it further," because the existing evidence pointed in genuinely different directions and picking one number out of that range would just be inventing precision that isn't really there. Owen said that's not a forecast, it's a refusal to forecast, since she still has to act on it somehow -- place an actual bet, decide how to allocate the group's play-money portfolio -- and whatever she actually does will reveal some implicit number, so she might as well just own up to it instead of hiding behind a range.

Mia's comeback was that acting under a range doesn't require collapsing it to a point at all: she'd decline a bet on the trial succeeding at 40 cents on the dollar, and she'd also decline the opposite bet (that it fails) at 10 cents, and there's nothing incoherent about turning down both, since neither one is clearly good given what she actually believes -- that's just what having a spread instead of a point means. Owen shot back that this is exactly the problem: if she'd turn down every bet on both sides across that whole range, then in practice she never trades at all, and an "imprecise probability" that never cashes out in action is really just a fancy way of dodging the discipline that makes forecasting worth doing in the first place. Mia said the alternative is worse -- forcing out a made-up point estimate doesn't add any real information, it just hides the fact that the extra precision was invented, and invented precision isn't more rational than honest imprecision. Owen wouldnt let it go though: he said two forecasters with different point numbers can at least be checked against each other later and one of them shown to be wrong, while a forecaster who deals only in ranges can basically never be caught out, which smells less like epistemic humility and more like a way to avoid ever being on the hook.

Neither of them backed down and it kind of fizzled out unresolved. Could you go through this move by move and say who's actually right at each step, not just who wins overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are just missing? Also curious whether you think there's a genuinely principled answer to this or whether it's a spot where reasonable, technically-informed people just land differently.
