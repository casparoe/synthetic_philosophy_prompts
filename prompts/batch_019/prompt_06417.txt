And here's the answer I have to grade:

"(a) Urmson's target is the idea, which he associates with the Kantian / common-sense tradition, that every act sorts into obligatory, forbidden, or merely permissible/indifferent. He points out that this leaves no room for cases like a soldier throwing himself on a grenade to save the rest of his squad — we don't think he was failing in his duty if he hadn't done it, so it's not obligatory, but it's obviously not indifferent either, since it's exactly the kind of act we praise as heroic. So Urmson thinks we need a fourth category, the supererogatory, for acts that are morally good and praiseworthy but not required, to capture cases like this. Urmson actually coined the term 'supererogation' for this category, since ordinary moral vocabulary didn't really have a word for 'good beyond duty' before his paper.

(b) The paradox is this: if morality requires you to bring about the best outcome available to you, then whenever one act is better than some other act you could've done instead, the better act becomes the one you're required to do — there's no room left for an act to be better than the alternative while still being merely optional. But that's exactly what a supererogatory act is supposed to be: better than the alternative, yet optional. So maximizing consequentialism seems to rule out the very possibility of supererogation, even though ordinary moral thinking is pretty confident that this kind of optional heroism exists.

(c) Yes, satisficing consequentialism solves this. Since satisficing consequentialism gives up the idea that there's a fixed line between obligatory and forbidden acts and just ranks acts as better or worse than one another, 'supererogatory' simply ends up picking out the acts near the top of that ranking. Once there's no threshold separating required acts from merely good ones, there's nothing paradoxical left about an act being better than its alternative but still optional — the optionality just falls out of there being no obligatory/forbidden line to violate in the first place."
