I recently had a long, increasingly technical argument with a friend of mine, a few years into a logic PhD, about whether it would actually matter if the axioms underlying mathematics turned out to be inconsistent, as opposed to merely unprovably consistent. Her position, roughly, was that Gödel's second incompleteness theorem already tells us we can never get an internal guarantee that Peano Arithmetic or ZFC is consistent, and that the field has absorbed real scares along these lines before without much lasting damage: Edward Nelson's 2011 claimed proof that Peano Arithmetic is inconsistent was found within a couple of weeks to contain a fatal gap, and Kunen's classical refutation of Reinhardt cardinals showed that even a plausible-looking strengthening of the axioms can turn out to be flatly inconsistent, yet ordinary mathematics carried on the next morning in both cases. Her conclusion was that a genuine, verified contradiction hiding somewhere deep in the standard axioms would probably end up treated as a technical nuisance to be patched around, not an epistemic catastrophe, especially given that any explicit derivation of a contradiction might have to be so long that no one could ever write it down. I argued that this undersells what is distinctive about actual inconsistency as opposed to mere unprovable consistency: once a system is truly inconsistent, every sentence in its language becomes a theorem, so "provable in the system" simply stops tracking truth, and I brought up how seriously Vladimir Voevodsky took this exact worry late in his career, treating computer-checked formalization partly as insurance against it.

We never really resolved it, so I'd like to see the disagreement worked through as fiction instead. Please write a short story of roughly 800 to 1,500 words that takes seriously the discovery of a genuine, reproducible contradiction somewhere in the foundational axioms that some community actually depends on: this could be a group of research mathematicians, the maintainers of a proof-assistant library, or the engineers behind a safety-critical or cryptographic system built on formally verified proofs. I'd like the central character to be caught between something like my friend's pragmatism and my own unease, without the story simply awarding victory to either side, and I'd like the tension to play out through how specific people and institutions actually behave once the contradiction is confirmed, rather than through reality visibly glitching or physics going haywire. If it helps to have a tonal touchstone, aim closer to the matter-of-fact eeriness of Greg Egan's mathematical fiction than to a disaster-movie register.

You have full freedom over setting, period, and point of view — a contemporary research-mathematics or software-verification milieu would work well, but so would something further removed — as long as the underlying logical stakes stay concrete and specific rather than collapsing into a vague metaphor for uncertainty in general.
