I trained as an evolutionary biologist and did not begin reading philosophy seriously until after I retired. My closest colleague of thirty years retires next month, and I have been asked to give the toast at her dinner. For three decades we have argued about what it means for a trait to have a function. It was only this past year, reading the philosophical literature for the first time, that I discovered we had been rehearsing, without ever having the vocabulary for it, the dispute between Cummins-style causal-role accounts of function, which she has always defended, and etiological, selected-effects accounts, which I have always defended. Half the room will be philosophers of biology who will notice at once if I misstate either position; the other half will be family and former students who have never heard either phrase. Please write the toast: approximately five minutes long, built around one biological example in which the two accounts genuinely diverge rather than one in which they happen to agree, fair enough to her position that she could not reasonably object to it, and affectionate enough that no one present mistakes it for a conference talk - without allowing either of us to actually win the argument.
