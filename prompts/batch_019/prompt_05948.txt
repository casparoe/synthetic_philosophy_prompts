And here is my classmate's answer, submitted as a single block of text with the parts labeled:

"(a) For Kant, an action has genuine moral worth only if it's done 'from duty,' not merely 'in accordance with duty.' His shopkeeper example makes the point: a shopkeeper who doesn't overcharge a naive customer is doing the right thing, and it looks honest, but if he's only refraining from overcharging because a reputation for fair dealing is good for business, he's acting from self-interest, not duty. He's done nothing wrong, but the action has no moral worth. Only the shopkeeper who wouldn't overcharge even if it cost him customers, because he recognizes honesty as a duty, acts with moral worth.

(b) Two objections say this makes Kant's ethics psychologically distorting. Stocker's point, sometimes called the 'one thought too many' problem, is that if a friend visits you in the hospital and, when thanked, says 'I'm just doing my duty,' that's worse than if they'd said nothing about their motives at all — it suggests they don't really care about you as a friend, only about being a good moral agent in the abstract. This is meant to show that Kantian ethics, taken at face value, drives a wedge between being a good person by the theory's lights and being a good friend, spouse, or parent.

(c) Herman's response is that the motive of duty acts as a 'limiting condition.' On her reading, this means that for an act to have moral worth, the thought 'this is my duty' has to be the agent's real motivating thought in the moment — affection or sympathy can accompany the act, but duty has to be doing the actual work, front and center, or the act doesn't count. This lets Herman insist that Kant was right all along that duty must be what moves the dutiful agent, while conceding that this thought doesn't have to be the only thing going on in the agent's head.

(d) Markovits's coincident reasons thesis is a further step away from Kant: an act has moral worth just when the agent's motivating reason is the same reason that actually makes the act right, whatever that reason is — so a person who helps a friend because the friend needed help, and that fact is also what makes helping the right thing to do, acts with moral worth, even though 'the friend needed help' isn't a specifically moral consideration. This is meant to accommodate cases where someone does the right thing for good, sympathetic reasons without ever thinking in terms of duty or moral rightness at all."
