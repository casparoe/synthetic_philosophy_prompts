I sit on the ethics advisory panel that reviews our regional council's predator-control operations under New Zealand's Predator Free 2050 programme. Next month the panel has to recommend whether to renew, modify, or decline consent for the next multi-year aerial 1080 (sodium fluoroacetate) operation across one of our forest park blocks — a drop designed to knock down possum and rat numbers, and stoats along with them, so that kiwi chicks and other native birds stand a better chance through the next breeding season. Over the weekend this turned into a long, genuinely unresolved argument with an old friend who volunteers for an animal-welfare organisation. Her objection wasn't the familiar complaint that poison harms the bush; it was that possums, rats, and stoats are sentient mammals that typically die slow, convulsive deaths from 1080, and that the panel can only treat this as an acceptable cost because we let the administrative label "invasive pest" do work that only facts about an animal's capacity to suffer should be doing. She also pointed out that none of these species are native here in the first place — they exist in New Zealand, in the numbers they do, because of choices earlier generations of settlers made, which she thinks strengthens rather than weakens our obligations to them now. My reply, that leaving the predators alone doesn't reduce suffering but simply redirects it onto the chicks and eggs they would otherwise take, didn't seem to fully meet her point, since it doesn't explain why our hands need to be the ones doing the killing rather than staying out of it altogether.

Could you draft the short memo, no more than two pages, that I will circulate before the panel meets? It should recommend one specific course of action on the upcoming consent decision, attach a concrete timeframe to whatever you recommend — whether that means renewal, staged review, or phase-out toward less acutely painful methods — and be written for an audience of ecologists and council staff rather than philosophers. Most importantly, please don't let the memo settle the question by simply restating the biodiversity case or noting that these animals aren't native; I would like it to take seriously the possibility that sentience, rather than species membership or population status, is what actually grounds an animal's moral claims on us, and to work out what follows for this particular decision if that possibility turned out to be basically right.
