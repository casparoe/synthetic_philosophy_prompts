My father is turning eighty, and I've been asked to give the toast at his birthday dinner. For as long as I can remember, he has run his life by an actual, literal version of Benjamin Franklin's "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection": the same thirteen virtues, a weekly chart, a small mark for every day he catches himself failing one. A handful of my philosophy colleagues, old friends of the family, will be at the table, and they've heard me argue in print that this kind of daily bookkeeping can't really produce virtue in the Aristotelian sense -- at best it produces a merely "continent" person, someone who wins the daily fight against temptation through effort and record-keeping, never the genuinely virtuous person who wouldn't feel the pull the other way to begin with. Everyone else at the table just thinks the chart is a sweet, slightly funny habit of his, and has no idea any of this is contested. Write the toast. It should run about five minutes, built around one moment that lands as a real joke for the philosophers and as a real tribute for everyone else, and it needs to stay warm rather than clever, without quietly abandoning the argument I've actually published.
