Since retiring from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have been devoting my mornings to metaphysics, and I keep returning to the old puzzle of the statue and the clay. A sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue; for as long as the statue exists, it and the lump occupy the same place and are composed of exactly the same particles, identically arranged, which tempts one to conclude that they are simply the same object. Yet the lump could survive being squashed back into a ball while the statue could not, and the lump existed before the statue did, so by Leibniz's Law it seems they cannot, after all, be strictly one and the same thing. Two responses to this puzzle strike me as the most carefully developed: Lynne Rudder Baker's constitution view, on which the statue and the lump are numerically distinct objects joined by a relation of constitution that Baker insists is not identity, and the four-dimensionalist treatment associated with David Lewis and Theodore Sider, on which each is a distinct aggregate of temporal parts that merely overlaps the other for the period during which they coincide, rather as two roads can share a common stretch. The second picture appeals to the old physicist in me, since it echoes how one draws worldlines through spacetime, but I do not entirely trust that instinct. Could you compare the two views on their philosophical merits: how each reconciles the statue and the lump's apparent sharing of all their matter with Leibniz's Law, what cost each incurs in doing so, and which, in your judgment, offers the more satisfying resolution of the puzzle?
