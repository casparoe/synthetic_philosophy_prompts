Over the past few weekends I have been building a small side project: a position-sizing module for a bot that trades on prediction markets. That took me down a rabbit hole into the Kelly criterion, and since I'm comfortable with the mathematics even though I've never studied philosophy formally, I worked through the standard derivation myself: the bet fraction that maximizes the expected value of the logarithm of your bankroll after each wager. Every serious source I've found describes this fraction as the mathematically optimal stake, which matches what I derived, so I felt reasonably confident I understood the mechanics.

What I can't reconcile is that almost everyone I trust on this topic — quant forums, a couple of papers that kept invoking something called "geometric rationality" (a term that went over my head), and a friend who trades systematically for a living — recommends staking only half or a quarter of the full Kelly fraction rather than the full amount. If full Kelly truly is the optimal bet size, doesn't wagering less than that just mean settling for a lower expected return because you don't quite trust the math? My friend tried to explain the practice by talking about "ergodicity" and some distinction between averaging across many parallel copies of yourself and averaging over time for the single copy that actually exists, but that struck me as a roundabout way of saying "be more risk-averse than the formula recommends," dressed up in fancier vocabulary.

I'd rather understand what's actually going on than just adopt the half-Kelly convention because it's popular. Is there a genuine mathematical or conceptual justification for fractional Kelly that I'm missing, and is "geometric rationality" doing real conceptual work here or is it essentially a rebrand of ordinary risk-aversion?
