In my philosophy elective, we spent a week on the regress problem in epistemology: foundationalists hold that a chain of justification must terminate in a belief that needs no further support, while infinitists hold that an endless, non-repeating chain of reasons can justify a belief even though it never terminates. Reading further on my own, I found that contemporary metaphysicians face a structural cousin of this same problem, but for grounding rather than justification: must every chain of the form "this fact obtains in virtue of that fact, which obtains in virtue of some further fact, and so on" eventually bottom out in something that is not itself grounded in anything further, or could such a chain descend forever with no fundamental level at all? I would like to see this less familiar version of the dispute argued out properly. Write a dialogue between a metaphysical foundationalist, who holds that grounding must be well-founded on pain of nothing ever really being explained, and a metaphysical infinitist, who denies this. The infinitist should not simply insist that infinite descent is "possible in principle"; they need to directly answer the foundationalist's worry that a chain with no terminus explains nothing, and the foundationalist should, in turn, engage with whatever positive account of explanation the infinitist offers rather than just repeating that a base is required. Please make each turn respond to the specific point made immediately before it, so that neither side ends up talking past the other.
