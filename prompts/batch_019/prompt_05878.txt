This semester I'm taking a research seminar built entirely around Hume's first Enquiry, and we've just spent two weeks on Section X, "Of Miracles." I've been reading further into the secondary literature on the essay's structure: the "proof against proof" argument in Part 1, which is meant to show a priori that testimony could never be sufficient to establish a miracle, and the four independent grounds Hume gives in Part 2 for doubting that any actual miracle report has met that standard, including the well-known "contrary miracles" argument about rival religious traditions undercutting each other's evidence. What I keep getting stuck on is how much of this survives John Earman's charge, in Hume's Abject Failure, that the whole argument quietly begs the question by treating "uniform experience" against miracles as already settled rather than as itself the thing in dispute.

A few of us run a small philosophy podcast out of the department, and next month's guest is a philosopher of religion who has just finished a book defending a broadly Bayesian reconstruction of Hume's argument against exactly that objection: her position is that once "proofs" are cashed out as priors and likelihoods, the question-begging worry disappears. I mentioned the invitation to my professor, who was skeptical that translating the argument into probabilistic language changes anything of substance, so I'd like the interview to actually test that claim rather than let her simply restate the reconstruction from her book.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation? I'd like at least one question on each of the following, and for every question a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give: (a) whether cashing out "uniform experience" as a prior probability just relocates the question-begging worry into the choice of prior rather than resolving it; (b) whether a Bayesian version of the Part 2 "contrary miracles" argument still delivers Hume's strong conclusion that testimony could never suffice to establish a miracle, or only the much weaker conclusion that we should be antecedently skeptical; (c) the passage on the Indian prince who refuses to believe in ice, and whether the fact that he was justified in his disbelief and yet turned out to be wrong shows that "proportioning belief to experience" is a poor guide to truth in exactly the cases where the argument most needs it to be reliable; and (d) whether her reconstruction offers any principled way of distinguishing testimony for a miracle from testimony for a well-attested but wildly unprecedented natural event, given that both are initially discontinuous with past experience in the same formal sense.
