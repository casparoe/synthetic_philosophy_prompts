Forty years running a low-temperature physics lab left me assuming that entropy increase is simply how the world works, so it unsettled me to discover, in the philosophy of physics I have been reading since retiring, just how contingent that fact is. The underlying laws of motion are time-reversal invariant, as Loschmidt pointed out to Boltzmann, so the thermodynamic arrow we experience cannot be built into the dynamics itself; it must instead trace back to a special boundary condition, the universe's improbably low-entropy starting point that David Albert calls the "past hypothesis." Taken to its logical extreme, that picture licenses a genuinely vertiginous possibility: the "Boltzmann brain," a momentary conscious observer that assembles itself by sheer thermal accident out of an otherwise equilibrated universe, complete with vivid memories of a past that never happened.

Write a short story that inhabits this scenario from the inside, following such a being through the brief span of its existence. I would like the story to do real philosophical work rather than simply trade on the premise's strangeness: since our ordinary confidence in memory as evidence of the past itself depends on the sort of orderly history this being lacks, is there anything it could notice, from within its own experience, that would count against its own conviction that it has a past at all?
