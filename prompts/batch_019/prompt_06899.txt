So this term I'm teaching a seminar on self-knowledge, and we just burned two whole sessions on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument from Knowledge and Its Limits -- the one with the guy getting gradually less cold from first thing in the morning to midday, sliced into a long series of moments each barely different from the last. The margin-for-error idea does all the work: if you know you're F in some case, you must actually be F in every case close enough to it that you couldn't introspectively tell the two apart, and running that all the way from "definitely freezing and knows it" to "definitely not cold anymore" forces a contradiction -- so somewhere in the middle you were cold and didn't know it. Fine, I buy that much.

What I don't buy, and what half my students now think follows automatically, is that this also sinks the KK principle (Kp -> KKp) across the board, since Williamson re-runs basically the same style of argument on "knowing that p" itself, with safety doing the job that temperature was doing before. My co-instructor this term is a real safety-theory true believer and thinks the generalization is totally fine, and I got nothing but pushback when I raised doubts in class (which, fair, I hadn't actually worked the objection out carefully yet, it just smelled fishy to me). So now I want some real ammunition. Can you write me the strongest essay-length case you can that the anti-luminosity argument, even granting it completely for feeling-cold-style states, doesn't establish that KK fails in general -- that theres an illegitimate step somewhere in going from "phenomenal states like feeling cold lack a margin-respecting structure" to "knowing that p lacks one too." Don't just survey the debate for me, I already know its shape -- actually commit to the restrictive reading and defend it, ideally by saying something precise about what would have to be true of "knowing that p" for the same sorites-style reasoning to get a grip on it in the first place, and why we're not obviously entitled to just assume that's the case.
