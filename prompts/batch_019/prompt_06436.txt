Q3 (20 pts). Consider two people who both fail to water a houseplant that consequently dies of dehydration: (a) Priya, who explicitly agreed to housesit and water the plant for two weeks while its owner was away, and (b) a stranger on the other side of the country who has never heard of the plant and also did not water it. For both Priya and the stranger it's true that "if they had watered the plant, it would not have died." (i) Explain why cases like this generate a puzzle for treating omissions as causes — this is sometimes called the problem of profligate (or promiscuous) omissions. (ii) Explain how the puzzle bears specifically on Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation. (iii) Drawing on at least one of Beebee, McGrath, Moore, or Sartorio, assess whether an adequate response to the puzzle can avoid smuggling in normative or context-dependent considerations, and defend your own view. (300–500 words.)

Student answer:

"The puzzle is that ordinary counterfactual dependence is far too generous about what counts as a cause of the plant's death. Both Priya and the total stranger satisfy the counterfactual test — in each case, if they had watered the plant, it wouldn't have died — so a simple counterfactual theory seems forced to say that both of them are causes, along with every other person on Earth who also didn't water it and also could have. This is the problem of profligate omissions: it makes causation absurdly cheap, since almost anyone's inaction 'causes' almost anything, once you allow that not-doing-something can be a cause at all.

This is a direct problem for Lewis, because his theory of causation is explicitly built on counterfactual dependence: c causes e iff e counterfactually depends on c (roughly, iff e wouldn't have occurred without c). Since Priya's not-watering and the stranger's not-watering both satisfy this dependence relation equally well, Lewis's own theory can't tell them apart — by his lights they come out as exactly the same kind of cause, which clashes badly with the intuition that only Priya is really responsible.

Beebee's response to this is to bite the bullet: she agrees that, strictly, both omissions are causes in the metaphysically thin sense, but says we only ever assert the Priya one in conversation because it's the salient, contextually relevant one — she calls this second, restricted notion 'quasi-causation.' I think this is basically right, but McGrath pushes further and shows the real cost of it: the only way to explain why Priya's omission is the salient/relevant one is to point to the fact that she had a duty to water the plant and the stranger didn't. But a duty is a normative fact, not a physical one. Since norms aren't part of the mind-independent causal structure of the world, it follows that 'causation' by omission was never really causation at all, just moral responsibility wearing a causal disguise. So I'd actually go further than McGrath and say there's no such thing as genuine causation by omission — only genuine causation by doing, plus a separate practice of blaming people who fail to meet expectations."
