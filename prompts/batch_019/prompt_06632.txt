Last night a friend of mine, who is finishing a doctorate in decision theory, and I got into a long argument that neither of us managed to win. It started from Aumann's agreement theorem: two Bayesian agents who share a common prior and have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for some event must, as a matter of logic, hold identical posteriors. I pointed out that people who have shared all their relevant evidence still disagree constantly, which seems to show that one of the theorem's premises is simply false of real disagreements. My friend's response was that this is exactly what you would expect if the two parties never had a common prior to begin with, and that positing different priors amounts to admitting that at least one side isn't fully rational. Her view, following what she called "the Harsanyi doctrine," is that any two ideally rational agents who have received no information at all are in a symmetric epistemic position, and so should be modeled as sharing a single prior, with all subsequent disagreement traceable to differences in evidence rather than to some primitive difference in starting belief. I objected that this "common prior assumption" is carrying an enormous amount of weight for something that is almost never argued for directly, that priors have to come from somewhere, and that there is nothing obviously irrational about two agents' fundamental priors differing even absent any asymmetry in what they have observed. We went back and forth for a long time about whether the assumption is a genuine requirement of rationality, an assumption that can always be rescued from any apparent counterexample by redescribing "different priors" as "different information," or just a convenient fiction that happens to make equilibrium concepts in games of incomplete information tractable — and got nowhere.

Since neither of us made real progress in the moment, I would like you to write a dialogue that picks up roughly where we left off, between a defender of the common prior assumption as a substantive rationality requirement and an opponent who holds that rational agents may simply start out with heterogeneous priors. Please have them actually engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another, and make sure the exchange addresses at least the following: whether any apparent case of "different priors" can always be redescribed as a case of "different information" (and, if so, whether that redescription does real philosophical work or is just relabeling); what becomes of Aumann's theorem and the related no-trade results once the common prior assumption is dropped; and whether there is even in principle some observable difference between a world of Bayesians with heterogeneous priors and a world of Bayesians with a common prior but asymmetric information. I would rather you not let either side simply win — give each of them their best replies to the other's objections, including the standard objections to their own position, so that it is genuinely unclear by the end which of us, if either, was right.
