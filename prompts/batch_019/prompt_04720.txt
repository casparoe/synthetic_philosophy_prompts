For my high school's Jewish philosophy elective, I've been comparing two classic answers to why the Torah commands animal sacrifice. In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32), Maimonides argues that God never actually desired sacrifice for its own sake: since the ancient Israelites had grown up in a world saturated with pagan sacrificial worship, the practice was retained and redirected toward the one true God as a concession to human nature, a way of weaning people off idolatry gradually rather than all at once, and he backs this up with prophetic passages that seem to rank obedience above sacrifice. Nahmanides, commenting on Leviticus 1:9, rejects this account: he points out that Abel's offering was accepted and Noah's sacrifice is explicitly called a "pleasing odor" to God, both well before any idolatrous practice existed for the Torah to be reacting against, and he instead reads the details of the sacrificial rite as symbolically standing in for what the sinner himself deserves. Could you write a dialogue between the two of them in which each raises his strongest objection to the other's account and genuinely responds to it, rather than simply restating his own position? I'm especially curious how Maimonides would answer the objection from Abel and Noah.
