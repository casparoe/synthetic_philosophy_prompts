I'm a backend engineer, and a few weeks ago a few of us on my team got into a surprisingly heated debate about whether "microservices" and "a monolith with clean internal module boundaries" are genuinely different architectural approaches, or whether people arguing about it are really just attaching different meanings to words like "service" and then talking past one another. Someone remarked that this was probably a "merely verbal dispute," which sent me down a rabbit hole into the metaontology literature, since it turns out philosophers have tried to give a rigorous account of exactly this distinction between disputes that are merely verbal and ones that are substantive.

From what I've been able to piece together, David Chalmers has proposed something like a "method of elimination" for testing whether a dispute over a claim containing some contested term is verbal: you check whether the disagreement survives once each side is allowed to disambiguate what it means by the term, for instance by attaching a subscript to it for each candidate meaning in play. If, once this is done, each side is happy to accept the other's claim under the other's reading, and no first-order disagreement about the non-linguistic facts remains, that's supposed to be evidence that the original dispute was verbal. Apparently this is meant to bear on genuinely classic metaphysical disputes too, such as whether arbitrary collections of objects (say, my left shoe fused with the Eiffel Tower) compose some further object; this connects to Eli Hirsch's idea that there may be several equally legitimate candidate meanings for the existential quantifier itself, so that mereological universalists and nihilists could simply be speaking different, equally "correct" existence-dialects rather than disagreeing about the world.

What I haven't been able to fully wrap my head around is Ted Sider's response to this, which as far as I can tell holds that even after this kind of disambiguation there can be a further, substantive question left over: which of the candidate meanings is the one that actually "carves nature at its joints," i.e., which concept of existence we ought to use if we want our language to track objective structure rather than just picking an arbitrary but internally consistent convention. That sounds like it might reopen precisely the disagreement the elimination test was supposed to have dissolved. Could you explain, in more depth than I'd get from a general encyclopedia summary, how this tension between the elimination test and Sider's "joint-carving" move is supposed to work, whether it genuinely threatens the verdict that disputes like the composition debate are verbal, and how a committed Hirsch-style deflationist about ontology might try to respond?
