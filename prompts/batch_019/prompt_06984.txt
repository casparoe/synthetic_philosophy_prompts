The pop treatment of the grandfather paradox usually ends in one of two moves: declaring backward time travel metaphysically impossible, or dissolving the paradox via branching timelines (you kill a version of your grandfather in some other branch) or a Novikov-style self-consistency principle (reality always conspires to stop you). David Lewis's classic treatment takes a more deflationary line: he argues the paradox trades on an equivocation in the word "can." There is a sense in which the time-traveling Tim can kill his younger grandfather — relative to his marksmanship, the loaded gun, the clear shot, nobody around to stop him — and a sense in which he cannot, relative to the complete, fixed history of the actual world (which includes Tim's own later birth). Neither claim is false; they are simply relativized to different sets of background facts held fixed, and the apparent contradiction dissolves once that shift is noticed. This has always struck me as close kin to the compatibilist move that shows up constantly in free-will discussions on rationalist blogs, where "could have done otherwise" likewise gets cashed out relative to which facts you hold fixed.

Give an example of a time-travel scenario, distinct from the standard kill-your-own-grandfather case, that brings out this same equivocation on "can" as sharply as possible — ideally one where the two relativized senses pull hard against each other and against ordinary intuition. Walk through how each sense of "can" applies in your example, and say a little about why Lewis's semantic diagnosis is doing different work than an appeal to branching worlds or a self-consistency principle alone.
