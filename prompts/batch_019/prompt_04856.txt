I've been re-reading *Marriage and Morals* for a seminar on the history of ethics, and I want to press on what looks like an internal tension in Russell's argument. He wants the moral status of extramarital relationships to depend almost entirely on their consequences for children and for the stability of the household, rather than on any intrinsic duty of fidelity. Yet he also treats jealousy as one of the most powerful and destructive of human passions in practice, even as he insists it is largely a product of proprietary social convention rather than biological necessity, and could be much diminished by a different upbringing. Please argue that, applied honestly to people actually raised under existing conventions — rather than to some future generation reformed along the lines Russell recommends — his own premises yield a considerably more restrictive verdict on extramarital relationships than the permissive one he draws for his contemporaries.
