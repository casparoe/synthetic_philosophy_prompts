A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about mental causation, and kept running into the same snag over Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. I was defending it: if the physical domain is causally closed, so that every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, then a mental property that is merely realized by (rather than identical to) that cause starts to look causally superfluous -- which suggests non-reductive physicalism can keep its anti-reductionism only by paying for it with epiphenomenalism. My friend's objection was that this reasoning, if it works, would equally exclude chemistry, biology, or any other higher-level science from doing genuine causal work whenever it is realized by lower-level physical goings-on, which seems too strong a conclusion for anyone to accept -- so something must be wrong with singling out the mental case. I recalled that Kim has some way of trying to block this generalization, but neither of us could reconstruct it well enough to settle things. Could you explain how Kim attempts to contain the exclusion problem to mental (or at least to phenomenal) properties specifically, rather than letting it generalize into wholesale skepticism about higher-level causation, and then give your own assessment of whether that containment strategy is principled or just an ad hoc way of dodging an unwelcome result?
