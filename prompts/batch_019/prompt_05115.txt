This semester I'm taking a seminar on the epistemology of testimony, and we've just spent a few weeks on moral testimony -- cases where someone forms a moral belief and acts on it purely because a trusted person told them to, without grasping the reasons themselves. Two people in my reading group had a running argument about this that never really got resolved, and I'd like help sorting out who was right, and where.

The case: Mara has never thought much about the ethics of buying from fast-fashion brands that rely on sweatshop labor. Her roommate, whom she regards as unusually thoughtful about such things, tells her it's wrong to keep buying from them, and Mara stops immediately, purely on the roommate's say-so, with no independent grasp of why.

(1) Owen said this is unobjectionable: if the roommate is reliable, Mara now has a true, justified belief and is doing the right thing -- demanding independent insight before she may act on it is just moral exceptionalism, since we don't ask that of someone who takes their doctor's word about a diagnosis.

(2) Sana replied that there's a real asymmetry: taking the doctor's word leaves nothing missing in Mara as a patient, but giving up fast fashion purely because she was told to doesn't display anything about her as a moral agent -- it lacks the worth an action has when performed from understanding, even though the outward act is identical.

(3) Owen answered that this only shows understanding is more admirable, not that pure deference is impermissible; given ordinary limits on time and expertise, deferring to someone wiser is simply the responsible way to act well, just as with a financial advisor.

(4) Sana came back with: moral judgments are supposed to be genuinely one's own, direct expressions of one's will, in a way a belief about a diagnosis isn't -- someone who only ever executes other people's moral verdicts hasn't taken a moral stand at all, they've just complied.

Could you go through moves (1)-(4) and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like your take on a complication neither of them raised: none of us derived our own basic moral outlook from scratch either, so how sharp can Sana's distinction between legitimate moral upbringing and objectionable deference really be?
