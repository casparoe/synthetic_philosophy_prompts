So my philosophy of science seminar spent a week on Bayesian confirmation theory, and two people in the group, Marcus and Elena, ended up arguing about "the problem of old evidence" for like three days straight in our class chat afterward. Quick context in case it's useful: the worry is that if you already know some fact E for certain before a hypothesis H even gets proposed, standard Bayesian updating says E can't raise your credence in H once H shows up, since P(E) is already 1 and conditioning on something you're already certain of does literally nothing. The classic example is Mercury: astronomers had known for decades that Mercury's perihelion precesses a bit faster than Newtonian gravity predicts, and nobody could explain the gap. When Einstein showed general relativity entails exactly that extra precession, everyone treated it as some of the strongest evidence ever offered for a physical theory, even though the "evidence" was old news to literally everybody involved. So on paper Bayesianism seems to say the most famous confirmation story in physics wasn't really confirmation, which seems bad.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Marcus opened by saying this just refutes Bayesian confirmation theory, full stop -- a theory of evidential support that gets its field's favorite textbook example backwards isn't facing a puzzle, its facing a disproof. (2) Elena said the fix is that the old fact E isn't what's doing the confirming, its the newly discovered logical fact that H entails E -- nobody, not even Einstein, automatically knows every consequence of their own equations the second they write them down, you have to grind through the derivation. So the real new evidence is "GR implies this exact precession," which he only became certain of after doing the math, and that's what actually moves the credence. (3) Marcus replied that this rescue breaks the very framework it's trying to save, since ordinary probability theory already assumes you're certain of every logical consequence of everything you believe -- so "uncertain about an entailment" isn't a state that theory has room for without a rebuild. And even granting the rebuild, the size of the boost depends on how confident Einstein supposedly was, in advance, that the entailment would hold, which is a number nobody can pin down non-arbitrarily. He said a cleaner fix is to just ask what Einstein's credence in general relativity would have been had he not already known about the precession, and check whether learning it would have raised that -- no weird partial-logical-ignorance machinery required. (4) Elena said the logical-omniscience cost isn't invented just to save this case, since Bayesians already owe some story about why people get surprised by proofs and calculations generally -- and anyway, Marcus's counterfactual fix isn't actually cleaner, because there's no unique fact about what Einstein would have believed if you subtracted one piece of evidence out of a web of beliefs he'd partly built on top of it. Pick a different way of "removing" E and you get a different verdict, so he's just relocated the arbitrariness, not removed it.

Neither of them budged, and honestly they both seemed to be onto something real.
