In my school's decision theory club, we recently covered I. J. Good's well-known theorem that a Bayesian agent's expected utility can never be lowered in expectation by looking at free evidence before acting. Several clubmates took this as settling the question of whether a rational person should always want more information, no matter what. I think that inference moves too fast. Please argue that Good's theorem does not support this general slogan. As your central case, use the phenomenon the economist Jack Hirshleifer identified: the mere existence of sufficiently accurate information can destroy risk-sharing arrangements, like insurance, that would otherwise make everyone better off ex ante, so that a fully rational person can end up worse off simply because such information exists, even if nobody is ever compelled to look at it. Be precise about why this is not a counterexample to Good's proof itself, but rather a case that falls outside what the proof assumes.
