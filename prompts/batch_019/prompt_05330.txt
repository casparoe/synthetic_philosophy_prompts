"(a) Incommensurability means two paradigms can't be measured against a shared standard, kind of like how you'd need a common ruler to compare two lengths. It's a stronger claim than mere disagreement, because disagreement assumes both sides are talking about the same problem using words that mean the same thing, so evidence could in principle settle who's right. With incommensurable paradigms the terms themselves shift meaning, and problems that used to be central can stop counting as legitimate science at all. A good example is the move from phlogiston chemistry to Lavoisier's oxygen theory: the two sides weren't just giving competing answers to the same question, they were carving up substances and reactions differently, so a phlogiston chemist and an oxygen chemist are basically speaking two different, mutually untranslatable languages and can never really grasp what the other one means.

(b) Lakatos's jab was that if there's no neutral, paradigm-independent standard for comparing theories, paradigm change is just persuasion and crowd behavior, not reasoned judgment. Kuhn's response was that scientists still share general values across paradigm changes -- accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness -- even though there's no fixed algorithm for weighing these against each other, which is exactly why two rational scientists can look at the same shift and reasonably disagree for a while. He also argued this isn't arbitrary, because each new paradigm really does get us closer to how nature actually is, so later theories count as progressive in the sense of being objectively truer than what they replaced, which is what turns paradigm change into progress instead of just change. I think that mostly answers the 'mob psychology' charge, since shared values are still reasons and not just crowd behavior, but I'm less sure it gets Kuhn fully off the hook for relativism."
