I'm writing my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy on Wittgenstein's private language argument, and I keep getting stuck on section 258 of the Philosophical Investigations. There, Wittgenstein imagines someone who wants to keep a diary of a recurring sensation: she assigns it the sign "S" and writes that sign in a calendar every day the sensation occurs. He argues that she cannot really give herself an ordinary definition of "S" — at most she can perform a private ceremony of concentrating her attention on the sensation as she writes the sign down. The part I cannot get past is his conclusion that, in this situation, nothing distinguishes really remembering the connection between "S" and the sensation from merely seeming to remember it, so that, in his words, "whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right.'"

What I can't figure out is why this doesn't equally threaten ordinary public words. When I use the word "blue" correctly today, isn't that also just a matter of my memory telling me it matches how I've used the word before? Could you walk me through what Wittgenstein thinks is actually different about the private case, and whether commentators agree on why memory suffices to fix the meaning of public words but allegedly cannot do the same for a private sign like "S"? I'd like something that engages with this specific passage and its interpretive difficulties, rather than a general overview of "the private language argument."
