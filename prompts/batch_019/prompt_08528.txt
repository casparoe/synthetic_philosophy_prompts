I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD, mostly working on moral psych / experimental philosophy, and I'm still chewing on an arguement two of my labmates had after our reading group last week (we'd just gone through a stack of Knobe-effect papers, so everyone was primed for it). One of them, Naomi, is piloting a vignette for her dissertation that's basically a reskinned version of the classic chairman/environment case: a hospital administrator is told that switching scheduling software will save money, and in one version it'll also increase nurse burnout as a side effect, in the other version it'll decrease burnout as a side effect, and either way she says something like "I don't care about that, I just want to cut costs" and goes ahead. Like clockwork, way more of her pilot subjects said she "intentionally increased burnout" in the harm version than said she "intentionally decreased burnout" in the help version.

The argument was about what to make of that asymmetry, and it went back and forth enough that I wrote it down afterward because I knew I'd want to chew on it more. (1) Theo said this just shows moral valence is constitutively part of the ordinary concept of intentional action -- we're not erring, we're correctly applying a concept that's always had a normative component built in, same as "reckless" does. (2) Naomi said if that were true you shouldn't need morality specifically to get the effect, and that the pattern looks a lot more like Alicke's culpable-control story: people fix a global blame verdict first and then reverse-engineer the supporting judgments (foresight, causation, intent) to match it, which makes the asymmetry a bias, not a competence. (3) Theo came back with the fact that this is apparently one of the most robust results in the whole x-phi replication literature -- I want to say it held up across nearly every site in that big Cova et al. cross-cultural project -- and argued a sloppy, ad hoc, motivated-reasoning bias has no business being that stable across cultures and contexts. (4) Naomi said robustness has nothing to do with correctness -- plenty of very robust, automatic biases exist too -- so at most he'd shown people can't help doing it, not that they're right to. (5) Theo then brought up a finding I'm pretty sure is Uttich and Lombrozo's, that you get basically the same intentional/unintentional split even for purely non-moral, conventional or statistical norm violations, and said this generality is exactly what real conceptual competence would predict, whereas a blame-justifying bias has no obvious reason to show up when there's nobody around to blame. (6) Naomi's comeback was that abnormal outcomes are just independently more salient and attention-grabbing regardless of morality, which would inflate perceived foresight and hence perceived intentionality through completely mundane attention mechanisms, no norm-tracking required -- so his cross-domain finding doesn't actually favor his side over hers.

Neither of them budged and then we all had to go grade sections, so its been rattling around in my head since. Could you go through it move by move -- (1) through (6) -- and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? And then, since I know that's already a lot, close with your own overall take on whether the "competence" side or the "bias" side is closer to right about the Knobe effect, and why.
