I've been working through Karl Popper's discussion of "verisimilitude" in Conjectures and Refutations for a philosophy of science seminar, and one episode in its history keeps tripping me up. Popper wanted a rigorous sense in which a later, still-false scientific theory could count as objectively closer to the truth than the false theory it replaced -- without some such notion, it is hard to see what "scientific progress" could even mean, given that our best theories keep turning out to be false in the end.

His proposed formal definition compared theories via their "truth content" and "falsity content" (roughly, the sets of true and false statements that follow from each theory): theory A has more verisimilitude than theory B just when A's truth content includes B's, A's falsity content is included in B's, and at least one of those two inclusions is strict. What I only half-understand is why this definition is now treated as essentially obsolete. My impression is that Tichý and Miller, working independently in the mid-1970s, didn't just find an isolated counterexample, but proved something closer to a structural collapse: that on Popper's own definition, a false theory can essentially never come out as more verisimilar than another false theory -- which undermines the whole project, since almost every interesting comparison in the actual history of science is a comparison between two theories that are both false.

Could you walk me through, at an intuitive rather than fully formal level, what is actually doing the work in that argument -- what is it about comparing how much true content and how much false content a theory has that forces this collapse? I would also be curious whether you think this was a fixable defect specific to Popper's particular formalization, or whether it points to something deeper about why truthlikeness resists this kind of purely logical, consequence-based treatment, in contrast to the similarity-based approaches to verisimilitude developed afterward.
