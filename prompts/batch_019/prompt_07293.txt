I'm working on a piece about how well classic philosophical thought experiments hold up once real physics is brought to bear on them, and I keep getting stuck on one particular pairing. Max Black's 1952 dialogue asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two spheres, exactly alike in every intrinsic property and, because there is nothing else in that universe for either one to relate to besides the other, seemingly alike even in their relations. Black's conclusion is that despite this total qualitative match, there are still two spheres and not one, which he takes as a refutation of Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles: the claim that no two distinct things can share every single property.

What I can't quite work out is how directly this maps onto something physicists treat as entirely ordinary: electrons or photons that quantum mechanics describes as not merely similar but strictly indistinguishable, a fact built into Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac statistics rather than stipulated for the sake of an argument. Is that really the same puzzle as Black's spheres, just realized by nature instead of by a philosopher, or does it diverge from it in some way that matters?

Here's the specific complication I'd like help with. I've read that Black's spheres are not, strictly speaking, wholly indiscernible after all, since each stands in an irreflexive relation to the other (some distance apart) that nothing bears to itself, and that this "weak discernibility," as it's sometimes called, can be used to rescue a version of Leibniz's principle even here. I want to know whether that same rescue is available for genuinely identical quantum particles, and whether it works equally well for fermions, which the Pauli exclusion principle keeps from ever occupying quite the same state, as it does for bosons, which face no such restriction. If weak discernibility saves the principle for fermions but fails for bosons crowded into an identical state, that would suggest quantum mechanics is a more serious threat to Leibniz's principle than Black's own thought experiment ever was. Could you walk through both cases side by side and tell me whether that comparison actually holds, rather than treating the philosophical and physical examples as two illustrations of the same point?
