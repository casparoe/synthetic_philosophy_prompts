I'm a software engineer who has spent a fair amount of spare time this past year going down the rationalist / AI-safety blogosphere rabbit hole — LessWrong, the EA Forum, that whole cluster of sites. One idea from that world I can't quite shake is the notion of "acausal" interaction between sufficiently capable agents: the claim that two minds with no causal link to each other whatsoever, no shared past and no way to ever physically meet, could still threaten, blackmail, or bargain with one another simply by each simulating the other's reasoning closely enough to know how it will respond. I gather this is basically the motivation behind decision theories like functional or updateless decision theory, which treat an agent as its decision-making algorithm rather than as any one physical instantiation of it, so that the same procedure running elsewhere can, in effect, be acted on from a distance. The standard illustration of all this is Roko's basilisk, which I already know well enough that I'd rather not just receive a dressed-up retelling of it.

What I actually find hard to hold onto is the proposed defense against acausal extortion known as a "surrogate goal": an agent worried about being threatened deliberately adopts some secondary objective it doesn't really value, and commits in advance to protecting that decoy exactly as fiercely as it would protect something real, so that a rational extortionist gets steered into attacking the decoy instead of whatever the agent actually cares about. I can follow each step of that argument in isolation, but the moment I picture the exchange from the extortionist's side, my grip on it slips: if the threatener is sophisticated enough to be worth worrying about in the first place, wouldn't it simply see through the decoy and either ignore it or go straight for the real target? I'd like to understand exactly where that objection is supposed to fail, if it does.

Could you write me a short story that works through this, in the spirit of the more idea-driven end of hard science fiction — something closer to how The Three-Body Problem handles game theory between civilizations that can never directly communicate than to a typical AI-doom parable? I don't mind what setting you choose (a pair of AIs, an uploaded mind and a distant predictor, a whole civilization sizing up a stranger's incentives), as long as the plot's actual turning points track the underlying argument rather than treating "surrogate goal" as unexplained technobabble. I'd like to come away from it with a sharper gut sense of what the strategy is really doing, and where it might quietly fail.
