I've been down a rabbit hole this week on higher-order theories of consciousness for a seminar paper, and I keep getting stuck comparing two versions of the higher-order thought (HOT) view that I don't think the intro readings distinguish carefully enough. Rosenthal's original version says a mental state is conscious just when it's actually accompanied by a distinct, roughly simultaneous higher-order thought to the effect that you're in that state — so at any given conscious moment there's supposed to be a real, occurent HOT doing the work. Carruthers thought this was psychologically nuts (something like: it would require a constant torrent of actual higher-order thoughts about basically every fine-grained detail of your visual field, your bodily sensations, etc, all the time), so he went dispositionalist instead — a state is conscious as long as it's merely available or poised to produce a HOT via some higher-order-thought-generating/mindreading system, whether or not one ever actually fires.

Can you compare these two versions properly for me? What I really want to know is whether Carruthers' move actually buys him anything, or whether it's a bit of a trick — like, if no actual higher-order representation needs to occur, what's left to explain why the state has any subjective feel at all, as opposed to it just being a state that's disposed to seem a certain way under certain conditions? Does Rosenthal (or anyone else) have a good version of this objection against Carruthers, and does Carruthers have a decent comeback? And overall, which of the two do you think is in better shape as an actual theory of phenomenal consciousness, once you weigh the "too much machinery" problem against the "explaining away the phenomenon" worry? Happy to be told I've got the two positions slightly wrong, by the way — most of what I know here is from lecture slides rather than the primary texts.
