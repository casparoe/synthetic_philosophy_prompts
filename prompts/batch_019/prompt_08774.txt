A few friends from my old philosophy-minor cohort still get dinner every couple months, and we always end up relitigating some old thought experiement once enough wine is involved. Last time it was Kavka's toxin puzzle -- the one where an eccentric billionaire puts a vial in front of you that'll make you violently sick for a day (nothing lethal, nothing lasting) and says: if you form a genuine intention tonight to drink it tomorrow at noon, he'll wire you a million dollars first thing in the morning, well before noon even arrives. He's got some foolproof way of telling real intentions apart from bluffs. The catch that makes it a puzzle: once the money's in your account, actually drinking the stuff gets you nothing extra -- it's just a day of misery for zero further reward, since you're getting paid either way.

Two people at the table, Nate and Elena, got into it for a solid twenty minutes and never really landed anywhere. Trying to reconstruct it as fairly as I can:

(1) Nate said people resolve to do things against their own future interest constantly -- keeping promises, sticking to diets -- so there's nothing paradoxical here, you just decide tonight and follow through at noon like with any other resolution.

(2) Elena said that's not actually the hard part. The hard part is whether tonight-you even counts as genuinely intending it, given that tonight-you already knows noon-you will have zero reason to go through with it once the money's cleared. An intention isn't just words, it comes bundled with an expectation that you'll act on it -- so if you can predict right now, with total confidence, that your own future reasoning will correctly tell you not to bother, in what sense have you formed a real intention instead of just performing one?

(3) Nate said resolve has worth on its own terms. If he decides now to be someone who follows through on commitments once they're made, that's not nothing -- being reliable, even to yourself, even unobserved, is worth something.

(4) Elena said "worth something" has to cash out as an actual reason to drink it at noon, or it doesn't. If it does, fine, but then there's no puzzle, he's just got a reason and acts on it. If it doesn't cash out as a reason at noon, he's just insisting reliability matters even where it provably buys nothing. Can't have both.

(5) Nate said she's grading this move by move when she should grade the whole plan at once. A policy of following through on resolutions like this one nets a million dollars in this exact case, so the policy is rational even if the last step looks pointless in isolation -- same way one brick doesn't hold up a bridge by itself, and that doesn't make bridges irrational.

(6) Elena said that kind of defense normally works because the policy pays off across all the other times you'll face a similar choice -- that's the actual reason we don't relitigate rules of thumb every single time. But Kavka's case is built to be one-shot on purpose: no repeat billionaires, nobody watching, no reputation at stake. So "the policy is rational" is quietly borrowing a repeated-game justification for a case specifically designed not to have one. It's not even like a one-off deathbed promise, where at least somebody else was owed something -- here, the second the money lands, nobody and nothing is owed anything by him actually drinking that vial.

They moved on to dessert without settling it, which has bugged me since. Could you go through the exchange point by point?
