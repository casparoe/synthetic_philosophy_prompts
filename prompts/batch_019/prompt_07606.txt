Here's the answer (real student, anonymized, ~230 words):

"Parfit's Reductionist View holds that a person's identity over time is not some further, deep fact about a separately existing self, but just consists in relations of physical and psychological continuity holding between earlier and later person-stages. He points out that this lines up with the Buddha's teaching of anatta, or non-self. Nagasena's chariot simile makes the same structural point: when King Milinda asks him to point to 'the chariot' apart from the wheels, axle, and frame, Nagasena shows that 'chariot' doesn't name some extra thing over and above the parts arranged and functioning together, it's just a convenient label for them. In the same way, 'self' is a convenient label for the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness), not the name of some further owner behind them. Since there's no such owner, Parfit and the Buddhist tradition end up as eliminativists about persons: once you subtract the deeper self, there's nothing left for 'person' to pick out, so persons are empty of any real referent even at the everyday level, exactly as the deeper self is.

Buddhist metaphysics goes further in Madhyamaka, where Nagarjuna argues that even the aggregates themselves lack inherent existence (svabhava) — they too are only dependently originated and conventionally designated, so the reduction doesn't bottom out in some set of ultimately real parts the way Parfit's continuity relations seem to. On this point Nagarjuna's critique is more radical than Parfit's, since Parfit is only a reductionist about persons, not about the physical and mental events he reduces them to."
