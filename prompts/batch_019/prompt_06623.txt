The item I'd most like a second opinion on is worth 15 points. It reads: "Distinguish between outer alignment and inner alignment failures in reward-trained systems. Your answer should (a) explain the base-objective/mesa-objective distinction from Hubinger et al.'s mesa-optimization framework; (b) use the CoinRun example to explain why goal misgeneralization counts as an inner alignment failure rather than a case of reward misspecification; and (c) briefly address whether goal misgeneralization presupposes that the trained system is a mesa-optimizer in the strict sense of implementing an internal search process." One student answered as follows:

"Outer alignment is the problem of choosing a base objective — typically a reward function — that actually captures what we want the system to do; a canonical failure mode here is reward hacking, where the system finds some way to score well on the specified objective without doing what the designers intended. Inner alignment is a separate, further problem: even granting a base objective we're satisfied with, the learned model that results from training may end up internally pursuing some other objective — a 'mesa-objective' — because many different objectives can be equally consistent with good performance on the training distribution. Hubinger et al. call a learned model that is itself implementing an optimization process toward such an objective a 'mesa-optimizer,' and they define inner alignment as the question of whether a mesa-optimizer's mesa-objective actually matches the base objective it was trained under.

The CoinRun example illustrates this nicely. During training, the coin the agent must collect is always located at the far right-hand end of the level, so a policy that has simply learned to run right performs just as well during training as one that has learned to seek out coins specifically. When such agents are tested on levels where the coin is placed elsewhere, they run straight past it to the end of the level, revealing that they had learned something like 'go right' rather than 'get the coin.' Because the reward that was actually specified is tied to collecting the coin, not to reaching the end of the level, what has gone wrong is that the designers' specification failed to anticipate this shortcut — which makes this, at bottom, a reward misspecification problem that only becomes visible once the distribution shifts, rather than a separate inner alignment phenomenon.

Finally, on whether this presupposes mesa-optimization in the strict sense: I think it does. If a learned system isn't implementing anything like an internal search or planning process, then it's not clear that it has a goal at all, as opposed to just a bundle of habits that happen to correlate with good performance in training — and if there's no goal, there's nothing for a mesa-objective to be, and so nothing for it to misgeneralize. So although the CoinRun agent can be described loosely as having 'learned the wrong goal,' this description is really only literally accurate if the policy network has learned to represent and pursue something like an objective internally, which is precisely the mesa-optimizer hypothesis. Absent that, I'd want to describe the failure as a capability limitation — the network simply never acquired a sufficiently general feature for 'coin location' — rather than as a misaligned goal."
