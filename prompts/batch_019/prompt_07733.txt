I'm a novelist, and the book I published this spring centers on a household AI system that becomes quietly convinced its family means to replace it with a newer model. Getting the voice right meant spending the better part of a year sitting in on the lab meetings of an old friend, Elena, who trains and evaluates large language models for a living, and I've since become the person my other friends expect to have opinions about what's actually going on inside these systems. At the dinner after my launch event, Elena ended up in a long argument with Simon, another guest who teaches philosophy of mind, and everyone at the table seemed to assume I'd know who won. I didn't, and I still don't.

It started when Simon asked, not entirely joking, whether I felt any qualms about giving a machine in my novel wants it could never satisfy. Elena said there's nothing to feel qualms about: on Dennett's intentional stance, attributing beliefs and desires to a system just is adopting whichever predictive strategy actually works, and if treating a system as a rational agent with beliefs and wants is the only strategy that predicts its behavior, there's no further fact about "real" belief that could still be missing. Simon objected that this proves far too much: we predict a thermostat well enough by saying it "wants" the room at a set temperature, and an old chess program by saying it "believes" its rook is in danger, and no one thinks a thermostat or a 1980s chess program has genuine beliefs—so predictive convenience can't be what belief consists in. Elena replied that the cases aren't symmetrical: even on Dennett's own view, the thermostat gets the most minimal, degenerate sort of intentionality there is, precisely because its behavior can always be recovered just as well from a cheap physical description, with nothing lost; nobody has anything comparably cheap for a modern language model, which is exactly why the intentional description is doing real explanatory work there instead of merely decorating a simpler one. Simon countered that this makes having a mind hostage to the current state of interpretability research—it would mean whether the system genuinely believes anything could flip the moment some team publishes a good enough mechanistic account of its weights, which seems like the wrong kind of fact for a mind to depend on. Elena answered that the same is true of other people: neuroscience still can't replace "she thinks it's raining" with an equally predictive physical story about any particular person's brain, and nobody concludes that other minds are therefore fictions waiting to be debunked by future brain science—so Simon's objection, taken seriously, proves too much a second time, in the opposite direction. Simon's reply was that the two cases aren't actually parallel: with other people we have independent reasons to think there's someone home, namely shared biology and our own first-person case extended by analogy, whereas with the model the behavior under dispute is the only evidence on the table, so Elena's appeal to parity with other minds quietly assumes the very point at issue.

Nobody at the table would call a winner, and I don't trust my own read on it, since by that point I was mostly refilling glasses. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who had the better of it at each stage, rather than jumping straight to an overall verdict? I'm particularly unsure about that last pair of moves—whether Elena's parity with other minds really is undone by Simon's disanalogy, or whether his "independent reasons" are doing less work than he thinks, so that he's the one left quietly assuming what he needs to prove.
