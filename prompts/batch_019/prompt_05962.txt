I spent my working life doing fieldwork in animal behaviour, and only since retiring have I had time to read through the more formal, game-theoretic literature that grew up around ideas I had previously just taken on faith. Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle is a case in point. Its popular formulation, repeated in most textbooks and in Zahavi's own popular writing, holds that a trait can only be an honest signal of quality if it is costly to produce, since only genuinely high-quality individuals can afford the cost. Yet when I worked through Alan Grafen's 1990 game-theoretic vindication of the principle, the condition doing the real work did not seem to be that the signal be costly in any absolute sense; it was that the marginal cost, or the marginal cost-to-benefit ratio, of increasing the signal declines as quality rises, so that a stable, separating equilibrium exists in which signal level tracks quality. On that reading, a sufficiently high-quality signaller could in principle bear very little net cost at equilibrium, since the cost that actually enforces honesty is largely counterfactual: the price a lower-quality cheater would have had to pay to mimic the signal, rather than one that anybody pays along the equilibrium path. Please argue against the popular generalization that honestly informative signals must be costly, in absolute or net terms, to the individuals who send them, and defend the more deflationary, marginal-cost reading of the handicap principle in its place, taking seriously both the strongest theoretical objections a defender of the traditional reading might raise and the apparent empirical costliness of showcase signals such as the peacock's train or the stotting of gazelles.
