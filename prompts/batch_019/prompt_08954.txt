So I'm TAing an early modern seminar this term and got sucked into a bit of a Hobbes rabbit hole that's honestly more interesting than the papers I'm supposed to be grading right now. Here's the thing I can't quite pin down: Hobbes thinks geometry is basically the only fully certain science available to us, and the reason (as I understand it) is that geometric objects are things we ourselves construct via definitions -- we draw the circle, we stipulate what counts as a "line" -- so we can know their causes with total certainty in a way we never can with natural bodies, since we didn't make those and can only ever guess at how God/nature put them together. Fine so far. But then in Leviathan (and De Corpore) he clearly wants civil philosophy to be just as certain as geometry, built up demonstratively from definitions in exactly the same style. What I can't figure out is whether his move there is actually the SAME argument -- i.e., commonwealths, unlike rocks, really are artifacts that we construct via covenant, so in this one specific respect politics is more like geometry than physics is -- or whether he's just borrowing the rhetorical prestige of geometric certainty without earning the same epistemic warrant for it.

If you can work it in, I'd also love your take on something that's been nagging me: Hobbes spent something like the last two decades of his life insisting, against Wallis, over and over and in print, that he'd squared the circle, and never really budged even after being pretty thoroughly humiliated for it. Does that whole saga actually tell us anything about whether his theory of why geometry is certain was any good to begin with, or is it seperable from that -- like, could a guy have a perfectly coherent theory of geometric certainty and still just be bad at doing actual geometry?
