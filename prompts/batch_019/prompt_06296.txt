In my graduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, two students got into a disagreement about Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue ("白馬非馬" — "a white horse is not a horse") that neither has been willing to drop. (1) Lena argued that the whole thing is sophistry: both of Gongsun Long's moves — that "horse" names shape while "white" names color, so naming a color is not naming a shape; and that a yellow or black horse will satisfy "bring me a horse" but not "bring me a white horse" — work only by sliding between "a white horse is not identical to a horse" and "a white horse is not a kind of horse," and nobody who isn't playing a word game actually thinks a white horse fails to be a horse. (2) Marcus objected that this charge of equivocation just assumes our count-noun grammar is the right lens: on Chad Hansen's "mass noun hypothesis," classical Chinese terms like 馬 behave more like "water" than like "a horse," so "white-horse-stuff" is genuinely a different portion of stuff from "horse-stuff" in general — the argument only looks like a trick if you insist on smuggling in a sortal individuation the language may not even mark. (3) Lena replied that this doesn't rescue Gongsun Long, since the claim only startles anyone because the audience is simultaneously hearing 馬 the count-noun way, as "a horse" one could ride away — so the mass-noun story, even if correct as semantics, relocates the equivocation rather than dissolving it. (4) Marcus answered that the "seeking a horse" argument doesn't need mass nouns at all: it is a straightforwardly extensional point that "a horse" and "a white horse" pick out overlapping but distinct sets of individuals, and dismissing that as a triviality Gongsun Long merely lacked the vocabulary to state plainly is itself anachronistic.
