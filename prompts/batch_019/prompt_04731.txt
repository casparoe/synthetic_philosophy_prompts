"Williamson (Knowledge and Its Limits, ch. 4) argues against the thesis that mental states are 'luminous.' Using the example of gradually feeling cold over the course of a night, reconstruct his argument against luminosity, and explain why he thinks the same reasoning tells against the KK principle (the claim that knowing that p puts one in a position to know that one knows that p)."

Here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted (minus their name):

"A condition is luminous if, whenever a subject is in that condition, she knows that she's in it — so introspectible mental states like feeling cold are supposed to be paradigm cases of luminous conditions, in contrast to non-mental facts (like the number of hairs on your head) that you could be in without knowing it.

Williamson's argument uses a case where you fall asleep feeling freezing cold at 11pm and wake up feeling perfectly comfortable at 7am, with the temperature dropping and then rising so gradually that you can't feel any difference between your state from one minute to the next. Divide the night into a long series of moments t0, t1, ..., tn, where t0 is when you're clearly cold and tn is when you're clearly not, and where you can't discriminate ti from ti+1. Because 'feeling cold' is a vague predicate, there's no sharp cutoff between the cold moments and the non-cold moments, so for knowledge of your own state to be possible at all near the boundary, there has to be a margin for error: if you know at ti that you're cold, that knowledge must be safe, and since ti+1 is indistinguishable from ti, you must still be cold at ti+1 too, or else the very same belief could easily have been false there. Given that you're cold and know it at t0 (by luminosity), the margin for error principle lets you run this step over and over, so you'd have to be cold at every single ti, including tn — but by hypothesis you're not cold at tn. Contradiction. So luminosity fails: you can feel cold without knowing that you feel cold.

Since knowing something is itself a mental state, and mental states aren't luminous, it follows that whenever you know p, you're not automatically able to know that you know p — there will be borderline cases close to the edge of your evidence where you know p but fail to know that you know it. So the KK principle, taken as a general law, is false: introspection just isn't reliable enough to always catch its own successes, which means we can never really be sure we know anything at all."
