So about three weeks ago I left one annoyed comment on our building's facebook group about a parking dispute, someone screenshotted it, and it ended up on one of those "you won't believe this HOA drama" accounts on twitter — for about four days I was "unhinged parking guy" to a few hundred thousand total strangers, quote-tweeted by accounts with 12 followers and accounts with half a million, none of whom have ever met me or could pick me out of a lineup. My friend Priya, who's doing a philosophy phd, told me half as a joke that I should read Kierkegaard's stuff on the press and "the public," since apparently something almost identical happened to him back in 1846 — a Copenhagen gossip paper called The Corsair spent literal years mocking his appearance and his broken engagement after he'd more or less dared them to in print. So I actually read the short book that gets excerpted as The Present Age, and it kind of wrecked me a bit — this idea that "the public" isn't a group of people at all, its a phantom that lets thousands of people feel like they said something without any single one of them having said anything they'd put their name to.

Here's the part that really got me though: when I mentioned all this to my aunt Marilyn, who's been a reporter and then an editor at our city paper for 41 years, she laughed and said that's basically been her whole office philosophy since journalism school — apparently she's been telling interns for decades that "the public" they imagine they're writing for is a phantom, and the only thing that's ever real is the one actual reader in front of you. Which is a lot to find out about your aunt three weeks after getting shredded by exactly that phantom yourself. Marilyn's retiring now (her paper went online-only last year and laid off half the newsroom), and because I'm apparently "the writer" in the family I got asked to give the toast at her goodbye dinner this saturday, which feels like a cruel joke given I currently can't finish a sentence without picturing someone screenshotting it. Half the room is going to be old newsroom people who've heard her do this bit themselves over the years and will clock it instantly; the other half is family who have never read a word of philosophy in their life and just want to hear something warm about Marilyn.

Could you actually write me that toast? Five minutes, tops. I don't want it to turn into a philosophy seminar and I definitely don't want it to be some doom-and-gloom eulogy for journalism, but I also don't want to pretend the last three weeks didn't happen to me. I think there's something real in there about the difference between what Marilyn actually did for 41 years and what almost got done to me, and i'd love it if the toast could hold onto that without ever hitting anyone over the head with it.
