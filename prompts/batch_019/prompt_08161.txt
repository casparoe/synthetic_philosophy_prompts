Ok so this is a bit of a rabbit hole but bear with me. I was reading through some old LessWrong/ACX-adjacent threads on voting theory (the kind of thing that eventually happens to everyone who hangs around this corner of the internet long enough) and got nerd-sniped into re-reading the chapter of Mill's Considerations on Representative Government where he goes on at length about Thomas Hare's scheme for proportional representation -- the single-transferable-vote thing -- as basically the cure for what he calls the "tyranny of the majority" in ordinary elections. He's really into this, frames it as the difference between true and false democracy, not just a minor technical tweak.

Problem is, I also know just enough social choice theory to know that Hare's STV is exactly the kind of system Arrow's impossibility theorem has beef with, decades after Mill is safely dead and can't defend himself. It fails independence of irrelevant alternatives (eliminating a losing candidate partway through the count can flip who wins between two other candidates who were never eliminated), and it isn't even monotonic -- there are legitimate cases where getting more first-preference votes makes a candidate lose. So Mill's flagship fix for fair aggregation of preferences turns out to be a textbook illustration of Arrow's basic point: no non-dictatorial rule can satisfy every reasonable-sounding fairness condition at once, and STV just happens to be a concrete case of that.

What I can't work out is what Mills best comeback would actually be if someone sat him down and walked him through the proof. Does he just shrug -- "no system is perfect, STV is still the least-bad option, that was my argument all along" -- or does he have a sharper move available given the rest of his commitments? His utilitarianism seems to assume you can weigh and compare utility across people, and later economists (Sen and others) pointed out that once you allow that kind of cardinal, interpersonally-comparable information into the picture, Arrow's impossibility result stops applying. So could Mill plausibly reject the whole ordinal-preferences-only framework the theorem is stated in, rather than just eating the bad news about STV? I'm less interested in a quick guess than in you actually tracing through which response fits best with everything else he wrote -- On Liberty, the utilitarianism, his arguments for representative government -- and being upfront about where that response would leave him committed to something he'd probably have hated.
