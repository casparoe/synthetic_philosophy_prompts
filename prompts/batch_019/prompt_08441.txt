A friend and I spent most of Sunday evening arguing about knowledge and stakes, and I told them I'd get a second opinion before conceding the point at our next dinner. The disagreement started over the bank cases that Keith DeRose introduced and Jason Stanley later adapted for his character Hannah, who is perfectly comfortable saying she "knows" the bank is open on Saturday when nothing important rides on it, yet retracts that very same knowledge claim once an unpaid bill is due Monday, despite having exactly the same evidence in both scenarios. My friend, who has taken considerably more formal epistemology than I have, pointed out that in essentially every version of this case that anyone actually discusses, the subject's practical stakes and the salience of far-fetched error-possibilities to whoever is evaluating the claim rise and fall together, so that contextualists (DeRose, Lewis, Cohen) and pragmatic encroachment theorists (Fantl and McGrath, Stanley, Hawthorne) always end up agreeing about whether Hannah knows. From this, my friend concluded that the supposed rivalry between contextualism and pragmatic encroachment is merely a verbal dispute -- two vocabularies competing to redescribe one underlying fact about stake-sensitivity -- and that two decades of journal articles adjudicating between DeRose-style contextualism and Stanley-style interest-relative invariantism have mostly amounted to philosophers talking past each other over labels.

I found this more persuasive than I expected, and I'd like to use a cleaned-up version of the argument in a short response paper for my epistemology seminar, built around the thesis that the contextualism/pragmatic-encroachment debate is substantially terminological rather than substantive. Before I commit to that framing in writing, could you help me sharpen the case for it -- explaining, with reference to how the two positions actually carve up the phenomenon, why treating them as rival vocabularies for a single insight is the most defensible way to characterize the debate?
