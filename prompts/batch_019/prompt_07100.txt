I'm outlining a novel that moves between two timelines: a seventeenth-century strand in which a fictionalized Descartes exchanges letters with a skeptical correspondent about whether animals possess souls, and a present-day frame in which a burnt-out ethologist -- who has spent her career studying tool use in corvids -- rereads the Discourse on the Method on a long flight and starts arguing with it in the margins. I want her annotations to engage with the actual argument rather than the cartoon version that circulates in popular summaries ("he just thought animals were clockwork toys"), so before I let my characters loose on him, I'd like some help getting the historical Descartes right.

As I understand it, the case Descartes makes in Part V of the Discourse rests on two fairly specific diagnostic tests, not merely a blanket assertion that beasts lack minds: first, that no machine or animal could arrange words flexibly enough to respond appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, the way even a dull-witted human can; second, that even where an animal or automaton outperforms us at some particular task, this only shows the action flows from the disposition of specialized organs rather than from reason, which he treats as a single instrument adequate to every conceivable situation. Holding those two criteria in mind, rather than the "beast-machine" slogan on its own, I'd like you to work out how Descartes would likely respond to two real, well-documented cases. The first is Betty, a New Caledonian crow who, with no prior training or demonstration, spontaneously bent a straight piece of wire into a hook in order to retrieve a food container she couldn't otherwise reach. The second is Alex, an African grey parrot who, lacking a word for apple, appeared to coin "banerry" by blending "banana" and "cherry," apparently to capture how the fruit tasted to him. Does Descartes' own framework give him principled resources for accommodating cases like these -- some distinction he already had available -- or do they land close enough to his actual tests that staying consistent would force him to bite a bullet somewhere, or revise something substantive? I would rather you reason this through from his stated commitments than guess at how a generic "modern philosopher" might respond, since that flattening is exactly what I'm trying to avoid in the novel.
