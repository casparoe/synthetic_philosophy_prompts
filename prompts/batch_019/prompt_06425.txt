I spent most of my working life as a condensed matter physicist, with a particular fondness for critical phenomena and phase transitions, so the renormalization group has always felt to me like a piece of everyday equipment rather than a philosophical puzzle. Since retiring I've enrolled in a philosophy of science course at my local university, mostly out of curiosity about the conceptual scaffolding behind ideas I used for decades without ever examining too closely, and this week's module on intertheoretic reduction has left me with a puzzle I can't quite resolve on my own.

On one side there is Ernest Nagel's classical account of reduction: a theory reduces to another when the terms of the reduced theory can be linked to those of the reducing theory by suitable bridge laws, and its laws can then be derived from the reducing theory together with those bridge laws and the relevant boundary conditions. This is, more or less, the story I was implicitly taught about how thermodynamics reduces to statistical mechanics. On the other side is an argument I've encountered in the work of the philosopher of physics Robert Batterman, who focuses on how we actually explain universality: the fact that a fluid near its liquid-vapor critical point and a magnet near its Curie point share exactly the same critical exponents, despite being built out of entirely different microphysics. Batterman argues that this explanation does not proceed by deriving the macroscopic behaviour from the microscopic details; it proceeds by coarse-graining most of those details away, because the relevant limit, the thermodynamic limit of infinitely many particles, is a singular one, in which the limiting behaviour is qualitatively unlike the behaviour at any large but finite size. On his view, this counts as a genuine, if modest, form of emergence that the Nagelian picture was never built to accommodate.

I would like your help thinking this through properly. Could you first lay out both positions with enough precision that their disagreement is clear, and then tell me whether they are really as opposed as they seem, or whether the renormalization-group story could instead be read as an unusually sophisticated instance of Nagelian reduction rather than a genuine counterexample to it? And if you had to come down on one side, which framework do you think better captures what physicists are actually doing when they explain universality in critical phenomena: reduction, emergence, or does the very distinction start to break down under this kind of pressure?
