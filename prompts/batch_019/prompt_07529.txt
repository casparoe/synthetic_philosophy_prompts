I'm a novelist assembling a linked collection of short stories about diagnosis and self-narrative for my next book, and the piece I'm stuck on is meant to dramatize Ian Hacking's idea of the "looping effect" -- his claim that classifying a person as a certain kind of patient doesn't leave the kind alone the way physics leaves quarks alone. The newly classified person can learn the label, reorganize memory and symptoms around it, and so change what the next instance of the category looks like, which is why Hacking thought clinicians were forever chasing what he called a "moving target" rather than describing a fixed condition, and why he suspected that naming a new kind of patient can amount to making up a new kind of person. I keep circling his two canonical cases -- the multiple-personality wave he anatomizes in Rewriting the Soul, and the nineteenth-century fugueurs, Albert Dadas above all, in Mad Travelers -- and a psychiatrist friend who read an early outline over dinner last week insists I'm still just retelling one of them with the names filed off.

So here is the assignment I want to set myself, and I'd like to see your attempt at it first: a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, narrated from close inside the mind of the clinician who first names and describes a new diagnostic category -- not the patient -- set in whichever period or institution you think best serves the mechanism (a mid-century state hospital, a Cold War veterans' clinic, a present-day teletherapy platform, your call). The loop itself has to become visible as plot rather than commentary: early cases should be messy and idiosyncratic, later patients should arrive having already heard about the syndrome and begin performing it more precisely than the index cases did, the clinician should revise the diagnostic criteria to keep pace with them, and by the end the clinician can no longer honestly say whether they discovered a kind of person or helped manufacture one. I want Hacking's distinction between "indifferent kinds," which don't care how we sort them, and "interactive kinds," which do, to be doing real work in what actually happens to the patients and the paperwork, not announced as a speech by anyone in the story, the clinician included.

One constraint on the ending: no scene where the clinician confesses to a colleague that they've been playing God, and no final verdict on whether the category was real all along or brought into being by the sorting. Hacking never settles that question for multiple personality, and I'd like the story to close on the same kind of productive uncertainty rather than resolving it either way.
