I'm a political theory PhD student working on constitutional precommitment, and I'd like to pressure-test the Ulysses analogy on the hardest case I know of, rather than the usual bill-of-rights or supermajority-threshold examples: unamendable "eternity clauses," such as Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law, which permanently place human dignity and the federal, democratic, and social-state structure beyond the reach of any future amendment, however large the majority favoring change.

Please write a dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors. The Precommitment Defender holds that eternity clauses are the purest realization of a Ulysses contract: just as Ulysses, while still clear-headed, orders his crew to keep him bound no matter what he says once the Sirens start singing, a founding generation that has just watched an elected government dismantle democracy through nominally legal means is entitled to permanently disable the amendment procedure at exactly the point where some future majority might try to do the same. The Skeptic denies that the analogy survives contact with two disanalogies, and should press both relentlessly: first, Ulysses's binding is self-liquidating, since the ship sails past the island within a known, finite stretch of time, after which his own judgment automatically governs again, whereas an eternity clause has no comparable terminus and binds every future generation without limit; second, Ulysses, in one lucid moment, knows exactly what the danger is and why his own later pleas should be discounted, whereas no founding generation can know in advance which future majorities will be Sirens and which will be legitimate constitutional learning that the clause wrongly forecloses.

The Precommitment Defender needs a real answer to both points, not a concession or a change of subject, and the Skeptic should have to say whether their own position collapses into Jefferson's more radical claim, made in his 1789 letter to Madison, that no constitution may rightfully bind beyond the lifetime of the generation that made it, or whether there is a principled stopping point short of that. Let the exchange run long enough for each side to be genuinely tested, and don't let either side concede more than the arguments actually force.
