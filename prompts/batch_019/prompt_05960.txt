For the past year or so I have been teaching myself moral philosophy in my spare time — mostly by falling down Wikipedia and Stanford Encyclopedia rabbit holes, and by listening to old philosophy podcasts on my commute, since I never studied any of this formally. One view that immediately appealed to me was emotivism, or expressivism more generally: the idea that when someone says "stealing is wrong," they are not describing some fact about the world but doing something closer to booing or cheering, so that the sentence expresses disapproval rather than stating a truth. Whenever this view comes up, though, people mention something called the "Frege–Geach problem" as though it were the single biggest reason the view fails, and I have now read several summaries of it without ever quite feeling like it clicked. I even asked a friend who took a couple of philosophy courses in college to walk me through it, but I do not think he remembered the details well enough for it to stick either.

Here is as far as I have gotten on my own: I understand that a sentence like "lying is wrong" can appear unasserted, as the "if" part of a conditional — for instance, "if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" — and that arguments built out of such conditionals seem to be perfectly valid. What I do not understand is why this is supposed to be a special problem for expressivists specifically, rather than just an interesting fact about logic in general. My instinct is that an expressivist could simply say the whole conditional expresses some more complicated attitude too, and I cannot see why that response would not just settle the matter. Could you walk me through, in plain terms and without presupposing any background in formal logic, exactly where the real difficulty is supposed to lie, and why so many philosophers apparently think that the "obvious" response I just described does not actually work?
