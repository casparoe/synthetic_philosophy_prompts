So I spent almost forty years as a field biologist doing biodiversity surveys, and I always figured 'species' was basically just a plain biological fact, sort of like an atom or a cell. Now that I'm retired and reading philosophy of biology, I ran into Ghiselin and Hull's old claim that species aren't natural kinds at all -- that a species is actually an individual, a spatiotemporally bounded historical entity the same way an organism is, and the organisms in it are parts of that individual rather then instances of a type (the way two electrons are instances of 'electron'). It struck me as a category error at first, but it's grown on me. Could you actually make the case FOR that individuality thesis, instead of just explaining it to me -- and really engage with the two objections that seem toughest to me as a biologist: that species still seem to support generalizations (about development, physiology, whatever) that feel more law-like than just accidental facts about one historical particular, and that the 'glue' Hull leaned on, interbreeding and gene flow, isn't really there for asexual lineages or bacteria, so the thesis might not generalize across the tree of life at all. Don't just survey the debate, pick a side and argue it.
