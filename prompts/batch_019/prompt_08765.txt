In a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, we recently worked through the shift in the literature from David Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation to James Woodward's interventionist account, and I've come away doubting that the latter is really a distinct theory at all. Lewis analyzes causation in terms of counterfactual dependence between distinct events, with the relevant counterfactuals evaluated via overall similarity among possible worlds, while Woodward analyzes it instead in terms of what would happen to an effect variable if a hypothetical intervention were performed on a cause variable. But an intervention just specifies a particular kind of counterfactual antecedent - one in which the cause variable is changed "from outside," so to speak, rather than through its normal causes - so it looks to me like Woodward has merely carved out a privileged subset of the counterfactuals Lewis already had in play, rather than proposing a genuinely different account of what causation consists in. After all, Woodward's own framework singles out interventionist counterfactuals as a special, restricted class of counterfactuals in the first place, which seems to concede exactly this point.

When I raised this in discussion, a classmate objected that the two theories handle cases of late preemption quite differently, and that this difference reflects something theoretically deep rather than a mere narrowing of scope, but I wasn't fully convinced this couldn't just be put down to Woodward having a tidier formal apparatus for the same underlying idea, rather than the two approaches doing fundamentally different philosophical work. Is there a principled sense in which interventionism deserves to be treated as a separate research program from the counterfactual tradition out of which it grew, or is the sharp contrast drawn between "counterfactual" and "interventionist" theories in survey articles mostly a matter of emphasis and formal bookkeeping rather than genuine theoretical disagreement?
