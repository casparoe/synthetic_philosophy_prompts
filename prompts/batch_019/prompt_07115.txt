I'm several drafts into a novel that follows three generations of a family in Kumasi, and the emotional center of the book has shifted, somewhat to my own surprise, onto the grandmother figure's advancing dementia and the way her extended family and neighbors have begun, almost imperceptibly, to withdraw the small daily acts of consultation and deference they once extended to her — as though her standing as a full person in the household were ebbing along with her memory. One of my point-of-view characters is her grandson, a philosophy graduate who left for Legon and then London, and I want him to be able to articulate what unsettles him about this in terms sharper than sentimental protest. In researching the background for his voice, I've come across a real and quite pointed disagreement within African philosophy that bears directly on this kind of case. It runs between Ifeanyi Menkiti's processual, communally-conferred account of personhood — on which one becomes more or less a person through moral and social incorporation into community over a lifetime, rather than simply by virtue of being born of human parents — and Kwame Gyekye's "moderate communitarian" reply, which grants that persons are substantially constituted by their relations but insists on some innate core of personhood and entitlement that the community cannot simply confer or withdraw. (Menkiti writes largely out of Igbo material, Gyekye out of Akan, which may or may not matter to how far the disagreement generalizes.)

What I would like from you is a careful comparison of these two positions, treated as two competing ways of cashing out the broader, Ubuntu-style intuition that a person is a person through other persons. Where, precisely, do Menkiti and Gyekye disagree, and where might the disagreement be more terminological than substantive? I am especially interested in whether Gyekye's moderate view actually resolves the worry that seems to motivate it — namely, that a fully relational, achievement-based conception of personhood leaves the standing of the diminished, the marginal, or the non-paradigmatic person uncomfortably exposed — or whether it merely relocates that worry, for instance into disputes about which capacities count as the innate, non-negotiable "core" of a person. If you had to advise a novelist on which view better honors a case like my grandmother character's, which would you choose, and why? I would rather have your own considered philosophical judgment, argued for, than a diplomatic summary that splits the difference.
