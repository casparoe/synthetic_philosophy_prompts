A friend of mine — a physicist by training — and I recently spent an entire dinner arguing about the persistence of objects through time, and neither of us budged an inch. He defends perdurantism: ordinary objects are four-dimensional "space-time worms" that persist by having a distinct temporal part at every moment at which they exist, and he likes that this maps neatly onto the relativistic block universe, where there is no observer-independent fact about what counts as "wholly present" right now. I held out for endurantism: things are wholly present whenever they exist, and persistence is a primitive relation that doesn't reduce to the sharing of temporal parts.

The trouble is that we kept reaching for the same handful of examples — the Ship of Theseus, Tibbles the cat losing his tail, the lump of clay and the statue, the problem of temporary intrinsics (a person who is bent while sitting and straight while standing) — and we both just recited the standard rejoinders we'd read a hundred times before (the perdurantist's usual move of saying two coincident-but-distinct space-time worms were there all along, the endurantist's usual move of going adverbialist or indexicalist about shape) without either of us actually being moved. It left me wanting a genuinely fresh test case rather than another pass through the classics.

So here is what I would like from you: construct a single, original scenario about persistence through time that does not simply repackage one of the cases above. Ideally it should turn on something structurally harder than plain qualitative change — for instance, fission or fusion of an object, gappy or intermittent existence (the object ceases to exist for a stretch and is later reassembled), ineliminably vague temporal boundaries, or a causal-loop/time-travel setup in which an object's later temporal parts causally affect its earlier ones. Describe the case in enough concrete detail that endurantists and perdurantists are actually forced apart, meaning a sympathetic defender of each view should be able to give a determinate, independently motivated verdict about identity, and the two verdicts should genuinely conflict rather than the disagreement dissolving into a merely verbal dispute once each side restates its general theory. Then briefly explain, for each side, what it is committed to saying about your case and what that commitment costs it.
