so I had this whole argument with my friend Marcus last night about Kant's universalizability test and couldn't sleep after, so I typed up my side of it. would love a sanity check from someone less annoyed with me than he currently is.

We were trying to run the categorical imperative on a maxim that isn't one of Kant's original four examples: "I will slightly exaggerate my qualifications on a job application whenever the claim is specific enough to sound credible but small/vague enough that it's very unlikely anyone checks." (rounding "used Python a bit" up to "proficient," turning a 3-month internship into "6 months, part-time," that kind of thing.)

Here's what I wrote up:

Contradiction-in-conception test: universalize it — everyone does this whenever they think they can get away with it. Is that even conceivable, the way "everyone breaks promises whenever it's convenient" allegedly isn't? I don't think so. Resumes would still function, employers would just discount everything a bit and get better at reading between the lines. Nothing about the practice seems to logically collapse the way promising supposedly does once lying-promises are universal. So this maxim survives CC.

Contradiction-in-will test: but I don't think you can rationally will the universalized version, because the whole point of padding your resume is to get a relative edge off an information asymmetry — and if everyone's doing it, employers just adjust their discount rate to match, so you end up exactly where you started except now nobody's resume means much. Willing it defeats the very purpose you had for adopting the maxim. Structurally that felt to me like the same move Kant makes for why you can't will universal non-beneficence.

Where me and Marcus actually split: I said this means the maxim fails CW but not CC — more like an "imperfect duty" violation, shady but not strictly forbidden. He said that's a distinction without a difference: once you notice you can rescue basically any maxim from CC failure by just saying "well the practice would survive in some degraded form," you can do that to everything, which means CC isnt doing any real work — and he thinks that INCLUDES the original false-promising case, which he says is secretly also a will-contradiction dressed up as a conceivability problem. I half-remember from an ethics seminar that this is basically the move Korsgaard makes in "Kant's Formula of Universal Law" — folding the logical-contradiction and teleological-contradiction readings into one "practical contradiction" story where every failure, CC or CW, ultimately comes down to the maxim defeating its own purpose — but it's entirely possible I'm garbling the title or the argument.

I pushed back that there's still a real difference between "this practice cannot even be conceived to exist once universal" and "the practice limps on but stops serving your purpose," and that if most of Kant's own cases turn out to be the second thing, that's a problem for how clean the CC/CW line is advertised as being, not proof the line is fake. We never resolved it.

Can you actually dig into this: (1) am I even running CC vs CW correctly, (2) does my resume case actually land where I think it does, (3) is "the promising case is secretly a CW argument" a real, known criticism of Kant or something I've half-invented, and (4) between me and Marcus, who's closer to right about whether the CC/CW distinction is doing genuine philosophical work?
