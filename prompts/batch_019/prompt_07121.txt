I'm a PhD student in biostatistics, and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group this term largely because the reference class problem keeps resurfacing, in a more practical guise, in my own dissertation work on individualized risk prediction: the perennial question of whether a given fifty-year-old smoker's ten-year cardiovascular risk should be read off the statistics for "smokers," "male smokers," "male smokers with this LDL and this family history," or some narrower class still. I can motivate Reichenbach's original version of the puzzle for a statistically literate audience without much trouble: a singular event falls under indefinitely many reference classes, the frequencies across those classes can diverge sharply, and his own proposed fix — take the narrowest class for which reliable statistics can still be compiled — just relocates the arbitrariness instead of removing it, since "reliable" and "narrowest" pull in opposite directions.

When I raised the next part at last week's meeting, the professor running the group just said it was "all in Hájek" and moved on, which was not much comfort on my third pass through the paper. What I haven't managed to reconstruct to my own satisfaction is the move philosophers like Hájek make when they argue that this is not merely a frequentist embarrassment, but that the very same structural problem reappears for the two views people usually reach for specifically in order to escape it: single-case propensity theories and unconstrained subjective (Bayesian) credence. On the propensity side, I gather the trouble has to do with individuating the "chance setup," or repeatable generating conditions, that a given trial counts as an instance of — which sounds suspiciously like reference-class selection wearing a different hat. On the Bayesian side, I gather it has to do with how one's "total evidence" gets partitioned, or with which of several candidate known frequencies a credence is supposed to defer to once something like Lewis's Principal Principle enters the picture. Whenever I try to state the argument in my own words, though, it comes out sounding more like an equivocation on "reference class" than a genuine re-derivation of the same difficulty on new terrain. Could you walk me through, in fairly plain and concrete terms (ideally with one worked example for each escape route), why a propensity theorist and a Bayesian are not entitled to their sense of safety, and tell me honestly if there's any point where you think the parallel to Reichenbach's original problem is being stretched thinner than its proponents let on?
