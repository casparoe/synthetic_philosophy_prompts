I spent my working life as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since taking retirement three years ago I have been filling the gap left by the laboratory with a rather less disciplined tour through philosophy of science. Lately I have been stuck on Popper's notion of verisimilitude, which appeals to something I always took for granted as a working scientist: that a theory can be strictly false and yet still meaningfully "closer to the truth" than the theory it replaced — Newtonian mechanics with respect to Aristotelian physics, say, or the kinetic theory of heat with respect to the caloric theory. I read Popper's original content-based definition in Conjectures and Refutations, and then, rather deflatingly, learned from a friend who did his doctorate in logic that Tichý and Miller independently proved in 1974 that the definition trivializes for false theories: in effect, no false theory can come out as more verisimilar than another false theory on Popper's own account, which is exactly the comparison his apparatus was designed to capture. That sent me down a rabbit hole of the repair proposals that followed, and I would like some help weighing them against one another.

Below are six positions one might take on how, if at all, to rescue a workable notion of verisimilitude after the Tichý–Miller result. I would like you to sort them into tiers S/A/B/C/D, where S means "comes closest to giving a rigorous, language-invariant notion that still licenses the ordinary judgments scientists make about theory succession" and D means "does not really solve the original problem, or gives up on it." Please judge each position specifically on (i) whether it escapes the Tichý–Miller triviality result, (ii) whether it is reasonably invariant under redescription in a different but empirically equivalent vocabulary — the "language-dependence" problem that dogs several of these proposals — and (iii) whether it delivers verdicts that match actual historical cases of theory succession, rather than only working for toy examples built out of a handful of atomic sentences.

The six positions:
1. Popper's original definition, which compares theories via the set-theoretic inclusion of their true and false logical consequences ("truth content" and "falsity content").
2. The "likeness" approach developed independently by Tichý, Hilpinen, and Oddie, which replaces content-inclusion with a measure of the average or minimal distance between a theory's models and the actual world, computed over some space of possible state-descriptions.
3. Niiniluoto's "min-sum" measure, which combines a similarity-to-truth component with a content/specificity component into a single index.
4. Schurz and Weingartner's attempt to repair Popper's original approach on its own terms, by restricting attention to the "relevant" consequence-elements of a theory rather than its full deductive closure.
5. The more recent extension of truthlikeness theory (associated, among others, with Cevolani and Festa) to theories that yield quantitative, numerical predictions, rather than just sets of qualitative true/false sentences.
6. Laudan's skeptical response: that the historical record of empirically successful-but-false theories undermines the whole project of a formal, realism-vindicating notion of verisimilitude, so that the project is better abandoned than repaired.
