I'm a novelist currently at work on a book in which one of my central characters — a man who trained as a Sinologist decades ago before drifting out of academic life — is unexpectedly asked to speak at a memorial service, and finds himself reaching for a passage from the Zhuangzi rather than anything more conventional. Before I let him use it on the page (or, more plausibly, half-remember and slightly mangle it, which struck me as truer to how people actually deploy old texts at funerals), I wanted to make sure I understood it myself, and I've gotten stuck. The passage is the short episode in the "Nourishing the Lord of Life" chapter about the death of Lao Dan, which I have in James Legge's 1891 translation:

"When Lao Dan died, Qin Shi went to condole (with his son), but after crying out three times, he came out. The disciples said to him, 'Were you not a friend of the Master?' 'I was,' he replied, and they said, 'Is it proper then to offer your condolences merely as you have done?' He said, 'It is. At first I thought he was the man of men, and now I do not think so. When I entered a little ago and expressed my condolences, there were the old men wailing as if they had lost a son, and the young men wailing as if they had lost their mother. In his attracting and uniting them to himself in such a way there must have been that which made them involuntarily express their words (of condolence), and involuntarily wail, as they were doing. And this was a hiding from himself of his Heaven (-nature), and an excessive indulgence of his (human) feelings; a forgetting of what he had received (in being born); what the ancients called the punishment due to neglecting the Heaven (-nature). When the Master came, it was at the proper time; when he went away, it was the simple sequence (of his coming). Quiet acquiescence in what happens at its proper time, and quietly submitting (to its ceasing) afford no occasion for grief or for joy. The ancients described (death) as the loosening of the cord on which God suspended (the life). What we can point to are the faggots that have been consumed; but the fire is transmitted (elsewhere), and we know not that it is over and ended."

A few things here have me going in circles. First, Qin Shi's whole speech seems to be a rebuke — of the mourners, and by implication of Lao Dan himself — for some kind of excessive attachment, summed up in that odd phrase "a hiding from himself of his Heaven(-nature)." But Lao Dan is usually taken to be a stand-in for Laozi, about as close to a sage as this text has to offer, so I can't work out what it means for the story to convict him, retroactively, of having failed at the very thing he supposedly embodies. Second, I don't understand the relationship between "the loosening of the cord on which God suspended the life" and the closing image of the faggots and the fire — whether these are two illustrations of a single point, or two different, perhaps competing, pictures of what death is. And third, that last line about the fire being "transmitted elsewhere" while "we know not that it is over and ended" is the crux I most need help with: I've seen it invoked both as an early gesture toward something surviving bodily death and as a strictly anti-survivalist image, in which an impersonal process simply continues on new fuel while nothing that could be called a self persists at all. Could you go through the passage more or less clause by clause, tell me which of these readings the internal logic of the passage actually favors, and flag anywhere Legge's distinctly nineteenth-century phrasing (especially "God") might be quietly tilting the interpretation in a direction that a more modern, less theologically loaded translation would not?
