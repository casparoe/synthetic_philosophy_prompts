I'm about halfway into a novel where one chapter is a long dream sequence — my protagonist, a burnt out philosophy adjunct, ends up at a dinner party with Leibniz of all people. My writing group thinks the whole chapter is pretentious but I'm keeping it anyway. Its a device for a bunch of stuff about coincidence and correspondence in her life (pre-established harmony is doing a lot of thematic lifting, obviously), and I want one stretch of the dinner scene to be her walking him through Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against mental causation and watching how he reacts. Honestly I don't know what he'd say beyond a vague sense that he'd be smug about it.

For context in case it's useful: Kim's argument says physical effects always have a sufficient physical cause (causal closure of the physical), and if mental properties are genuinely distinct from the physical properties they supervene on — not identical to them, which is what most non-reductive physicalists want to say — then any physical effect a mental state seems to bring about already has a physical cause standing right there doing the job. Since we don't want every such effect to be systematically overdetermined by two separate sufficient causes, something has to give, and its the mental cause that loses out, so mental properties turn out epiphenomenal unless they just reduce to the physical after all.

What I'd love from you is an actual reconstruction of how Leibniz would respond to this if someone explained it to him cold, in the reasoning style of his letters to Arnauld or de Volder rather than a modern gloss. Would he treat Kim's puzzle as basically the old interaction problem he thought harmony had already dissolved, just re-dressed in twentieth century physicalist language? Or would he say the whole argument smuggles in a premise — that bodies exert genuine causal influence on each other in the first place — that he'd already rejected on independent grounds, so the puzzle never even gets started for him? I want enough actual substance that I can steal phrasing and moves for the dialogue, not just a one line verdict.
