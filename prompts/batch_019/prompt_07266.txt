I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on the semantics and pragmatics of conditionals, and we've just spent two sessions working through David Lewis's papers on the triviality results for the indicative conditional. Once students see the proof that a truth-conditional semantics for "if," paired with an unrestricted Adams' Thesis, collapses into absurdity, nearly all of them converge immediately on the No Truth Value (NTV) view as the obvious moral, treating Lewis's own refusal to draw that conclusion as a failure of nerve rather than as a considered position with resources of its own. Next week we read Edgington's case for NTV, and before that discussion I want to hand the class the strongest version of the alternative Lewis was gesturing at, so that NTV has to earn its place rather than being adopted by default.

Please write that argument, addressed to a reader who already has the triviality proof and the standard menu of responses in view, so there is no need to re-derive anything. Its thesis should be that the real lesson of the triviality results is not that indicative conditionals lack truth conditions, but that Adams' Thesis needs to be reinterpreted as a constraint on assertability rather than on truth-conditional content — leaving room for a broadly truth-conditional semantics for "if," of the kind Jackson later worked out via the material conditional plus a robustness condition on assertion.

There are two moves the argument cannot just wave at. First, it should explain why the fallback of restricting Adams' Thesis to an agent's own actual credence function, rather than to the whole class of credence functions closed under conditionalization, does not by itself dissolve the problem, given how little closure is actually needed to regenerate a trivializing result. Second, it should meet head-on the consideration that does the real work for the NTV theorist: that on a material-conditional semantics, "if A, C" comes out true whenever A is false, no matter how irrelevant or absurd C is, and this looks like it gets plain cases of conditional falsity wrong rather than merely mispredicting facts about assertability.
