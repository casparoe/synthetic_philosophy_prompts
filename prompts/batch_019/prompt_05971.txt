I'm a retired physicist (spent about 35 years doing statistical mechanics and turbulence) and I've been having alot of fun lately reading moral philosophy for the first time. Last week I fell down a rabbit hole on the "cluelessness" objection to consequentialism and scribbled some notes trying to make sense of it with tools from my own field. Would you mind reading through the little argument below and telling me honestly where it's weak or where I've gone wrong? I don't need it to be nice, just accurate.

Here's the setup as I understand it (mostly from Lenman, and then Greaves): consequentialism says an act is right in virtue of its total effects, but almost every act - lending someone a book, missing a bus, whatever - sets off causal ripples that fan out into the far future in ways we can't possibly trace: who meets who, who gets born, who doesn't. Greaves apparently splits this into "simple" cluelessness, where the untraceable effects seem to cancel out symmetrically so we're licensed to just ignore them, and "complex" cluelessness, where we have some reason to think the effects are skewed one way but no principled way to say by how much. Its the complex kind that's supposed to be the real threat, since you can't just average it away.

My half-baked idea: this looks a lot like molecular chaos in a gas. We can't track individual molecule trajectories - tiny perturbations blow up exponentially - and yet thermodynamics still gives rock-solid predictions about pressure and entropy, because those are ensemble properties, not facts about any one trajectory. So maybe cluelessness dissolves once you stop asking "what will actually happen" and start asking "what's the expected value over the ensemble of ways things could go" - the same move that rescues prediction in statistical mechanics.

Here's what bugs me though: in physics, ensemble averaging is licensed by real symmetries (or the ergodic hypothesis), not just by our ignorance. In the moral case I can't point to anything playing that role - my "ensemble" of possible futures might just be a made-up probability distribution dressed up to look objective, which seems like exactly what complex cluelessness says you're not entitled to. So maybe my analogy just restates the problem instead of solving it.

Does this hold together at all, or is importing ensemble reasoning into ethics a straightforward category error? And is there something in the literature that already makes, or already demolishes, this exact move?
