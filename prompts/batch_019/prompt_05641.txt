Question (20 points): Explain the Nyāya school's account of the pramāṇas, focusing especially on anumāna. Your answer should (a) list the pramāṇas Nyāya recognizes, (b) reconstruct the classic inference of fire from smoke using the five members (avayava) of the Nyāya syllogism, (c) explain the role of vyāpti in licensing the inference, and (d) contrast the Nyāya position with at least one rival school's treatment of a pramāṇa.

Student's answer: Nyāya is a realist school that recognizes four pramāṇas, or valid means of knowledge: pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison), and śabda (testimony). This puts it in the middle of the spectrum of classical epistemologies: the Cārvākas accepted only perception, while the Mīmāṃsakas and Advaita Vedāntins went as far as six.

Take the stock example of inferring fire on a distant mountain from the smoke above it. Nyāya breaks the inference into five members: the pratijñā or thesis ("the mountain has fire"), the hetu or reason ("because it has smoke"), the udāharaṇa or example, drawn from a case where the connection is already familiar ("wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen"), the upanaya, applying the rule to the case at hand ("the mountain has smoke of that kind"), and the nigamana, which restates the conclusion ("therefore the mountain has fire").

What entitles us to move from smoke to fire is vyāpti, the invariable concomitance between the hetu and the sādhya: every case of smoke is a case of fire, without exception. Nyāya thinks the Cārvākas were wrong to reject this apparatus, since their skepticism about inference really amounted to a broader skepticism about whether our experience corresponds to any external world beyond the immediate deliverances of the senses, which is too strong a doubt to build an epistemology on.

Nyāya's other non-perceptual pramāṇa, upamāna, works on a similar analogical footing: from an observed resemblance between two things, we reason to further, unobserved facts about them. The classic case is the gavaya, a wild ox: told that a gavaya resembles a cow, a traveler who later meets one in the forest can infer, by that resemblance, further facts about the animal's temperament and habits, in just the way one might infer a new drug's side effects from its resemblance to a known one.

Since vyāpti-knowledge is itself just the residue of repeated perception, and upamāna is a form of analogical inference, the four pramāṇas are best understood as different routes that all terminate in pratyakṣa: anumāna, upamāna, and śabda are elaborations of perception rather than independent sources of knowledge in their own right.
