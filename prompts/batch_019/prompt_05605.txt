So I've been on a bit of a Timothy Williamson kick lately (it started because someone linked "Knowledge and Its Limits" in an old LW thread about the KK principle, and I fell down the rabbit hole for like two weeks). I wrote up a short explainer of his anti-luminosity argument for my blog, plus an extension of my own that I slapped an "epistemic status: shaky" tag on, and I wanted to run the whole thing by someone before I post it since I don't really have anyone IRL to sanity check my epistemics against.

Here's the gist of the explainer part: Williamson calls a condition "luminous" if, whenever it obtains, you're automatically in a position to know that it obtains -- basically the thesis that your own mind is transparent to you. He knocks this down with a cold-mornings case: picture a sequence of a thousand mornings where the temperature creeps up by an amount too small for you to ever notice, so morning 0 you're freezing and by morning 1000 your not cold at all, but any two mornings next to each other feel completely identical to you. Assume for reductio that feeling cold is luminous. On morning 0 you feel cold, so (by luminosity) you know you feel cold. But knowledge needs a margin for error: a belief only counts as knowledge if it stays true across every case you can't discriminate from the actual one, otherwise it wasnt safe enough to be knowledge to begin with. Morning 1 is indistinguishable from morning 0, so "knowing you're cold on morning 0" forces you to actually still be cold on morning 1. And since you're cold on morning 1, luminosity says you know that too, which by the same margin step forces you to be cold on morning 2, and so on. Chain this all the way up and you get the absurd result that you're still cold on morning 1000, which contradicts the setup, so luminosity must be false somewhere along the chain -- there's some morning you're cold without knowing it. And because "knowing that p" is itself just a condition like any other, running the identical argument on it refutes the KK principle directly: there are cases where you know p without knowing that you know p.

The extension I tried adding: I think this explains something that comes up in rationalist calibration practice, namely why basically nobody's stated credence is ever "71.4%" instead of a round "70%" or "75%". My hunch was that this isn't just a reporting convention but a genuine luminosity failure -- your credence can drift smoothly over a long run of updates in steps too small to introspect individually, so the same margin-for-error chain should mean you're never actually in a position to know your exact credence, only some fuzzy band around it.
