"Aquinas takes over from Aristotle the idea that what separates a true king from a tyrant is who benefits from the rule: a king governs for the common good of his subjects, a tyrant for his own private advantage. On top of this Aquinas distinguishes two ways a ruler can be a tyrant -- by defect of title, where he has seized power he was never entitled to, and by his manner of exercising rule, where he governs oppresively even though his claim to the office is sound. The first is really the central case for Aquinas: strictly speaking a 'tyrant' is someone with no rightful claim to rule at all, and oppressive government by a king whose title is otherwise good is more of a secondary or borderline instance of the same idea.

Because a usurper never had a rightful claim to begin with, Aquinas holds that, where there is no superior able to judge the matter, a private citizen who removes such a man by force does no wrong -- the usurper has, in taking power without right, made himself into something like a beast preying on the community, and killing him isn't homicide in the way killing a lawful ruler would be. This is basically the old Ciceronian idea, later picked up by John of Salisbury, and Aquinas is drawing directly on that tradition here.

Where the title is sound but the rule has turned oppressive, Aquinas is much more careful. If the tyranny is bearable, subjects should put up with it, since deposing a king is itself risky and could easily produce something worse. If it becomes unbearable, though, the fix has to come from public authority, not private initiative: the community, if it originally had the right to set up the king, can restrict or depose him; if he was imposed on them by some higher power, they should appeal to that power instead; and if no human remedy is available, Aquinas's advice is prayer. Aquinas's caution here anticipates, while being much more restrictive than, later tyrannicide doctrines like Juan de Mariana's, which was willing to justify killing even a fully legitimate king."
