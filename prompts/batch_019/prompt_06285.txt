I've been working through Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory with a small reading group, and every presentation of it we've produced in discussion motivates the theory the same way: expected utility theory can only accommodate Allais-paradox preferences by attributing an implausible utility function to the agent, whereas REU accommodates the same preferences cleanly by letting a risk function, applied to decumulative probabilities, do that work instead. That illustration has become completely routine for us, so I want to look at a less-discussed consequence of the theory: what happens to REU maximizers when a gamble unfolds over time rather than resolving all at once.

As I understand it, any theory that weakens independence to accommodate Allais-type preferences runs into a three-way tension. It cannot, in general, simultaneously (i) treat a multi-stage lottery as equivalent, for evaluative purposes, to the single-stage lottery obtained by multiplying out its stage probabilities, (ii) guarantee that the plan an agent adopts for a multi-stage decision is one she will still want to carry out once she actually reaches the later stage, and (iii) retain full independence -- and independence is already off the table, since giving it up is exactly what lets REU handle Allais-type cases in the first place.

I'd like this made concrete for REU specifically, not left as a general remark about non-expected-utility theories. Please construct an explicit two-stage lottery -- concrete outcomes, concrete probabilities, and a specific risk function r of your choosing that is strictly convex or strictly concave rather than linear -- for which a risk-weighted expected utility maximizer's valuation of the compound lottery, computed by first reducing it to its single-stage equivalent, disagrees with her valuation of the same lottery computed recursively: finding the certainty equivalent of the second-stage sub-lottery first, then folding that back into the first stage. Show both calculations in enough detail that the disagreement is actually demonstrated rather than asserted, say which of the two remaining commitments -- reduction or dynamic consistency -- you think a defender of REU is best advised to give up, and comment on whether treating the whole decision tree, rather than each node, as the proper object of REU evaluation genuinely resolves the tension or just declines to choose between them.
