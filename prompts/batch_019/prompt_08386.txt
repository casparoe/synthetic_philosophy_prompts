So I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week that started from an old Overcoming Bias/LW-style argument about calibration and "beliefs as probabilities, not binary states," and it looped back around to Moore's paradox -- the "it's raining, but I don't believe it" thing. Everyone who's taken an intro epistemology class has the standard story memorized (it's not a contradiction, it's absurd to assert or believe anyway, cue something about assertion norms or Wittgenstein finding this deeply important for some reason). But it occured to me that there's a neighboring puzzle, the preface paradox -- the one where an author sincerely believes every individual claim in her book but also sincerely believes, in the preface, that a book that long almost certainly contains at least one error somewhere -- which has basically identical surface structure (a bunch of attitudes that look fine one by one but weird taken together), and yet philosophers seem to treat the two completely differently.

Here's the bit that's actually bugging me: the standard Bayesian move on the preface paradox (stop talking about flat binary "belief" and just track calibrated credences instead) seems to make that whole paradox evaporate -- of course you can have high credence in each of a thousand individual claims and still expect at least one of them to be false, that's just a union bound, nothing remotely irrational going on. But when I try running the same move on the Moore sentence -- like "it's raining, but my credence that it's raining is 0.05" -- it still feels off to me in a way the preface case doesn't, even though nothing about probabilism says a well-calibrated person can't have low credence in something that happens to be true. I can't quite pin down why one paradox fully dissolves under the credence reframe and the other apparently doesn't (or maybe I'm wrong that it doesn't!).

Could you write me an essay that actually compares Moore's paradox and the preface paradox properly -- not just "here are two paradoxes involving belief," but specifically: do they share the same underlying structure or not, does recasting both in terms of credence rather than binary belief dissolve them to the same degree, and if not, what exactly is left over in the Moore case that isn't left over in the preface case (and why)? Please don't just retreat to "it's about assertion norms" as though that settles it -- I've already read that move, I want you to actually dig into the credence angle. And end with an actual verdict on which of the two is the more fundamentally puzzling one, i.e. more resistant to being dissolved by standard formal-epistemology tools, rather than a wishy-washy "both are interesting in their own way."
