I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language finishing a dissertation chapter on Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and it's been bothering me that almost every popular retelling of the gavagai case from *Word and Object* reduces it to a cute anecdote about a linguist mistranslating "rabbit." That framing loses the actual force of the argument: Quine's claim isn't that translation is merely difficult, but that a linguist who possessed complete knowledge of every disposition to assent or dissent that a speech community could ever display would still be unable to determine whether a given term refers to rabbits, to rabbit-stages, to undetached rabbit-parts, or to rabbithood as such, because all of these hypotheses are compatible with exactly the same stimulus meanings. The gap can only be closed by "analytical hypotheses" that the translator supplies from her own background scheme rather than reads off the evidence, and — this is the part popular treatments tend to skip — Quine insists this is a claim about there being no further fact of the matter, not merely a limit on what any actual linguist could discover.

My department is running an informal "philosophy and fiction" evening next month where students read a short piece connected to their research, and I'd like to submit a story that dramatizes this claim properly rather than just gesturing at it with a generic "the aliens don't understand us" premise. Here's a seed: a field linguist performing what Quine calls radical translation — working on a language with no known relatives and no prior history of contact — constructs two complete, rival translation manuals that agree on every prediction about behavior and every sentence's truth conditions, yet diverge irreconcilably on what the sub-sentential terms refer to. I'd like the story to build toward a moment where she, or a colleague reviewing her notes, realizes that no further fieldwork, however exhaustive, could ever adjudicate between the manuals — not because the right answer is hidden somewhere, but because there isn't one to find.

Could you write this as a self-contained short story of roughly 1,000-1,500 words? Feel free to choose whatever setting and narrative voice serves the idea best — a grounded ethnographic drama, a first-contact scenario, or anything else that makes the underdetermination vivid. I would genuinely prefer an ending that leaves the indeterminacy unresolved rather than one that quietly smuggles in a determinate answer for the sake of narrative closure; a tidy resolution would actually be less faithful to the philosophy than an honestly open one.
