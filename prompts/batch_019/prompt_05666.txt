So I'm working on a piece about free will and fatalism, and I keep getting stuck on Aristotle's old sea battle problem -- the puzzle about whether "there will be a naval battle tomorrow" is already true or false today, and what that's supposed to do to the openness of the future. I get the basic setup fine, it's the modern fixes that trip me up. Every explainer I find just lists them off -- Ockhamism, supervaluationism, the "thin red line," MacFarlane's relativist truth stuff -- like a menu, without every making two of them actually fight, so I can never tell which move is doing real work and which is just relabeling the mystery. (A philosopher friend of mine insists the thin red line is basically a scam, but I want to hear it argued properly before I believe her.)

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of time who are, for fun, literally standing on a cliff at dawn watching two fleets close in on each other, arguing over whether it's already true, right now, that there's going to be a battle? Make one an Ockhamist who thinks the sentence has a determinate truth value already and who reaches for something like a privileged actual future (the thin-red-line move) to explain what's fixing it -- give her a real version of the accidental-necessity reply, i.e. truth now doesn't mean the present causally settles or necessitates the outcome. Make the other a supervaluationist who denies bivalence here, who explains her own apparatus (true only once every possible continuation agrees, otherwise a gap), and who presses the Ockhamist with the obvious comeback: what actually distinguishes "the" privileged branch from the others, if nothing about the present does? Then have the Ockhamist hit back with the akward retrospective problem -- once the battle happens, don't we want to say it WAS already true the day before, and can the gap view really honor that? I want them evenly matched, actually responding to each other's specific point instead of just restating their own view, and I want it to end right as the fleets are about to collide, with neither side having conceded a thing.
