I'm taking a jurisprudence seminar and we just spent two weeks on the Hart-Devlin exchange from the late 50s/60s -- the one kicked off by the Wolfenden Report, where Devlins basic move is the "disintegration thesis": a society just IS its shared morality, so it has the same right to legislate against private immorality as it does against treason, and the test for whether a moral feeling is genuine and widespread enough to legislate on is basically what the reasonable man (the "man on the Clapham omnibus") would feel -- disgust, intolerance, indignation. Hart's reply in Law, Liberty, and Morality is the one everybody quotes -- harm principle, don't criminalize stuff just because people are grossed out -- but I only ever see paraphrases of the two of them side by side in textbooks, never an actual back and forth.

So can you write a dialogue between Hart and Devlin, like they've been brought back for one more round, arguing about something neither of them actually wrote about -- say, whether the law should be allowed to ban a fully consensual, private practice with no unwilling victim (pick something like an underground extreme body-modification club, or a members-only "duel to first blood" society) purely because most people find it viscerally disgusting. Devlin has to actually use his own machinery -- the disintegration thesis, the reasonable-man test, the treason analogy -- and deal with the obvious comeback that his test can't tell a genuine moral conviction apart from raw prejudice. Hart has to lean on the harm principle but also reckon with the fact that his own "minimum content of natural law" idea already admits that law is partly grounded in shared facts about human nature -- so he's got to explain why that isn't just a smaller-scale version of what Devlin is saying. Neither one should be a pushover -- they were both genuinely sharp people, so I want real engagement with each other's strongest points, not a strawman fight.
