I'm taking a seminar this term on Gödel's philosophical writings, and we've just moved past the incompleteness theorems into his more idiosyncratic metaphysical and theological commitments — in particular, the case for an afterlife that surfaces in his letters to his mother, Marianne, and in the philosophical remarks that Hao Wang recorded from their conversations. Our professor mentioned this almost in passing, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since. As I understand it, Gödel's argument wasn't a conventional religious one so much as an expression of rationalist optimism about the structure of the world: a genuinely rational universe, he thought, would not allow a person's intellectual and moral development simply to be extinguished at death without further point, any more than it would make sense to construct an elaborate apparatus and then scrap it just as it became ready for use. This seems to connect to his broader anti-mechanism about the mind — the disjunctive conclusion of the 1951 Gibbs lecture, where he argues that either the human mind outstrips every finite, Turing-style machine, or there exist number-theoretic truths that are absolutely undecidable for us — and more generally to his considerable sympathy for Leibniz's rationalism and monadology.

For a term paper, I'd like to push this reconstruction toward a question Gödel plainly never had occasion to consider: the transhumanist proposal of "mind uploading," on which scanning a brain's fine-grained structure and then running the resulting computational description on different hardware is supposed to let a person survive, or even be revived after, bodily death. Given what we know about why Gödel expected an afterlife in the first place, and given his independent doubts that a mind simply is a computational or mechanical process, how do you think he would have responded to uploading as a proposed route to personal survival? Would his rationalist argument for an afterlife make him at all sympathetic to the project, or would his anti-mechanism actually leave him more skeptical of uploading than a convinced materialist would be? I'd like you to work through the real tension between these two strands of his thought, rather than settling for something like "Gödel was religious, so he'd have rejected it" or "Gödel loved formal systems, so he'd have welcomed it."
