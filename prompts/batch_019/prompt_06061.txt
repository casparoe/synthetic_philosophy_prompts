I've been reporting a longread on the rationalist/effective altruism crowd and their love affair with Bayesian reasoning, and one thing that keeps coming up in interviews is "Good's theorem." A couple of people have explained it to me as basically a mathematical proof — from I.J. Good, apparently the same guy who was a codebreaker at Bletchley Park with Turing before he became a statistician — that the expected value of information can never be negative. The way it was put to me: a rational agent should always want more free information before deciding anything, so treating ignorance as a legitimate preference rather than a correctable bias doesn't really hold up. One source's exact line was "there's no such thing as a good reason not to want to know something, if its free to find out."

Except for a completely different piece last month I interviewed a genetic counselor, and she told me a large share of people at risk for Huntington's disease deliberately choose not to get tested, and that this is treated as a totally normal, even textbook-standard, choice in her field rather than some mistake to be talked out of. That feels like a flat contradiction to me — either decision theorists have this wrong and ignorance really can be the smarter move, or genetic counselors are quietly encouraging irrational behavior in their patients. Can you help me sort out which side is actually mistaken here, or is there some wrinkle in what the theorem is really claiming that would make both of these things true at once? I'd rather get an actual answer than a "well, both have a point" shrug.
