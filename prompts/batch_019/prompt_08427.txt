I'm taking a graduate seminar this term on the epistemology of peer disagreement. Most of our assigned readings are squarely informal -- Elga's "Reflection and Disagreement," Christensen's "Epistemology of Disagreement: The Good News," a couple of papers by Kelly -- but the syllabus also sets aside a week for the formal, decision-theoretic backdrop that occasionally gets invoked in these debates: Aumann's agreement theorem. Our professor gave the standard statement (two Bayesians who share a prior cannot have posteriors on some event that are common knowledge and yet unequal) and sketched the usual proof using information partitions and "self-evident" events. Office hours afterward didn't fully settle something that's been bothering me since.

What I can't get an intuitive handle on is why the theorem needs the posteriors to be common knowledge, full stop, rather than something weaker -- say, each of us simply and correctly knowing the other's number, or knowing it up through some large but finite level of "I know that you know that I know...". If you sincerely tell me your posterior and I tell you mine, it feels like we already both just know both numbers; so where, concretely, does the argument for equality actually break down if that mutual knowledge is real but only iterated to some finite depth, rather than all the way up to genuine common knowledge? I would like the point of failure located within the partition/self-evident-event machinery itself, rather than an answer that just re-invokes the definition of common knowledge and declares that finite levels don't count.

This is tangled up with a second thing I haven't been able to shake. Our professor also mentioned, more or less in passing, the Geanakoplos-Polemarchakis result that if two people with a common prior simply take turns publicly announcing their current posterior on some event -- never disclosing the private evidence behind it, only the bottom-line number -- they are guaranteed to converge to exact agreement within finitely many rounds. That seems to manufacture, through sheer repetition, precisely the common knowledge that a single exchange of numbers apparently fails to produce. Could you explain, again at the level of intuition rather than by grinding through the induction, what repeated announcement is actually doing here -- how mutual knowledge gets bootstrapped into common knowledge over successive rounds, given that no one ever learns anything new about the underlying event itself, only about what the other person currently believes about it?
