I'd like your help extending a piece of classical Indian epistemology to a case its author could not have anticipated. Gaṅgeśa Upādhyāya's Tattvacintāmaṇi (c. 1325) gives Navya-Nyāya's canonical account of the four pramāṇas. Its Śabda-khaṇḍa is unusually precise about what makes testimony a genuine source of knowledge: a hearer acquires śābdabodha (verbal cognition) only from the words of an āpta — a speaker whose utterance issues from real knowledge (jñāna) and the intention to convey it as it is (tātparya) — and only when the sentence itself satisfies ākāṅkṣā (syntactic expectancy), yogyatā (semantic fitness), and āsatti (contiguity of the words heard). Nyāya was, moreover, already willing to deny independent status to pramāṇas that rival schools accepted, reducing Mīmāṃsā's arthāpatti (postulation) to a species of anumāna (inference) whenever its own criteria for a distinct pramāṇa weren't met.

Suppose Gaṅgeśa were confronted with this case: a person forms a true, well-founded belief — about a medical prognosis, say, or tomorrow's weather — purely on the strength of a fluent assertion produced by a large language model, where no individual human knower stands behind that particular sentence and nothing answers to a speaker's tātparya in generating it. Arguing strictly from Gaṅgeśa's own commitments rather than present-day intuitions about reliability, how would he most plausibly classify the resulting cognition: as a genuine, if attenuated, case of śabda pramāṇa; as reducible to anumāna, with the machine's output serving merely as a sign from which the hearer infers the actual state of affairs; or as falling short of pramā altogether? Ground your answer in the specific roles that āptatva and tātparya play in constituting śābdabodha, and in Nyāya's parataḥ-prāmāṇya doctrine that a cognition's validity, when established, is established extrinsically.
