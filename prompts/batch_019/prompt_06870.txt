Since retiring from a career in experimental physics — mostly optical spectroscopy — and taking up philosophy in earnest, I have been working through a small reading group's syllabus that paired Nelson Goodman's Fact, Fiction, and Forecast with Kripke's short book on rule-following, Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, and I read the two back to back. Goodman has us consider the predicate "grue," true of a thing just in case it is examined before some future date and green, or not examined before that date and blue; every emerald anyone has ever looked at confirms "all emeralds are grue" exactly as well as it confirms "all emeralds are green," even though the two claims part ways for any emerald first examined after that date.

Kripke's Wittgenstein, meanwhile, has us consider "quus," a variant addition rule that agrees with ordinary addition on every sum I have actually performed but diverges once the numbers involved are larger than any I have previously added, and argues that nothing about my past behavior or mental history can settle whether I have always meant addition rather than this deviant "quaddition." The more I sat with the two books, the more it seemed to me that they turn on exactly the same trick: a deviant function that is indistinguishable from the intended one on all the finite evidence available so far, yet licenses wildly different verdicts from that point on. If that is right, then Goodman had, in effect, already solved Kripke's puzzle nearly thirty years early, since his own proposed answer — prefer "green" to "grue" because "green" is the entrenched predicate, the one with an actual track record of successful past projection — should carry over directly: "plus" is simply the entrenched rule, the one with the track record of use, as against the upstart "quus."

Yet Kripke's book, at least in the parts I have worked through, never engages with Goodman's problem at all, which strikes me as a strange silence if the two really are the same discovery, made independently in two subfields that don't talk to one another. I would be grateful if you could tell me whether this identification holds up and, if it does not, exactly where it breaks down. I am particularly keen to know whether the parallel I have drawn between "entrenched" and "the rule I am dispositionally set up to follow" survives scrutiny, since on the face of it those look to me like the same move dressed in different vocabulary.
