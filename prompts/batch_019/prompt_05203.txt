I volunteer on the technical review panel of a small AI-safety grantmaking fund. We have received an application from a team proposing to spend a two-year grant developing 'consistency-based' ELK reporters: following a strategy floated in ARC's original Eliciting Latent Knowledge report, they would train a reporter head to answer many logically and causally related questions about a scenario -- rephrasings, counterfactual variants, questions about intermediate steps -- and penalize any contradictions among its answers, on the theory that a reporter lying about the one fact that matters will eventually trip over itself under enough cross-examination. Two panelists want to reject the application outright, on the grounds that the report's own discussion already shows why this fails in the worst case. A 'human simulator' reporter -- one that answers as a human would given only the (possibly tampered) sensor feed, e.g., insisting a vault's diamond is safe because the camera still shows it, rather than reporting the predictor's true internal state -- can be made just as self-consistent as an honest 'direct translator,' since it is not opportunistically lying but systematically reporting a coherent, if false, picture, and since exhaustively checking consistency across the combinatorially many related questions is not practically feasible in any case. The other three panelists want to fund the proposal regardless: a known worst-case failure mode does not make an approach worthless if it raises the empirical cost of building a deceptive reporter with present-day models, and they do not want the panel to fund only techniques that already come with a full theoretical solution attached. Please draft the panel's decision memo -- fund, decline, or fund with conditions -- with reasons the applicants could actually act on, and engaging the intractability objection on its merits rather than dismissing it as merely theoretical.
