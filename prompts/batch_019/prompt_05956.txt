I'm working on a feature about the small but fast-growing "grief tech" industry: startups (and assorted hobbyists) that use language models to let the bereaved keep texting or talking with a chatbot trained on a dead relative's messages, voicemails, and social media history, tuned to answer in their voice. Journalists and a few bioethicists have started calling these things "griefbots" or "deadbots," and most of the coverage stalls out at "is this comforting or is this creepy," without really working through whether building or using one is a species of lying, and if so, whose lie it is and how serious a one.

That question sent me back to Augustine, who I think is the hardest-line theorist of lying the Western tradition has produced. In De Mendacio, and even more unforgivingly in Contra Mendacium, he insists that what makes an utterance a lie is entirely the speaker's intention to assert as true something they themselves believe false, regardless of whether the statement itself happens to be true, and regardless of any good that comes of it; this is why he treats lying as corrupting the liar's own soul rather than as merely a harm done to whoever is deceived. He pushes this further than almost anyone before or since: even discussing the Hebrew midwives who lied to Pharaoh to save the infants marked for death, he credits their mercy but still refuses to call the lie itself anything other than sin, and he is generally read as denying that any end, however good, can license an assertion one believes false.

Working from that apparatus rather than from general impressions of Augustine, how do you think he would actually rule on deadbots? I'm especially interested in three things. First, whether he would even count a chatbot's output as a lie in his sense at all, given that lying for him requires a will to deceive and software has none, so that any culpable deception would have to belong instead to the company simulating a voice, or to the user. Second, whether it would matter to him that most users know perfectly well they are talking to software, and may be treating the exchange more like a keepsake or a private ritual than like a claim about who is alive. And third, whether his refusal to permit even life-saving lies means he would place grief-comfort exactly alongside the sickbed and rescue cases he already ruled on: pitiable in motive, but not one bit more permissible.
