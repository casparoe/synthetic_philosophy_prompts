And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"On the buck-passing view (associated with Scanlon), to call something good isn't to attribute some further reason-giving property, 'goodness,' to it -- it's to say the thing has other, more basic properties that give us reason to adopt pro-attitudes toward it: to desire it, promote it, admire it, and so on. The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is a counterexample to this: imagine a demon who will torture you unless you come to admire him. You now have a reason to admire the demon, but the demon obviously hasn't thereby become admirable, so the buck-passing analysis, taken at face value, gets the wrong result -- it has to say the demon is good, since there's a reason to admire it. This is basically a restatement of Moore's open-question argument: Moore already showed that defining 'good' via some other property always invites the further question of whether that property is really good-making, and the demon case is just a vivid, reasons-based version of the same worry. The standard fix is to distinguish reasons that bear on whether the object merits the attitude (object-given reasons) from reasons that bear only on how good or bad it would be to be in the attitude-state itself (state-given reasons, like the demon's threat), and to stipulate that only object-given reasons count for buck-passing purposes. Once that restriction is built in, the demon case stops being a problem, since the demon only supplies a state-given reason, and those don't count -- so the fix fully vindicates buck-passing against this objection."
