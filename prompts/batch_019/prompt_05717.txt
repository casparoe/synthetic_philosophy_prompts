I'm a physics PhD student—my own research is in condensed matter, so this is well outside my usual territory—but I've been sitting in on an informal foundations-of-physics reading group that just spent two sessions on Earman and Norton's 1987 paper on the hole argument, and it hasn't left me alone since. The technical core is clear enough: because Einstein's field equations are invariant under arbitrary smooth coordinate transformations, you can take any solution, pick a region containing no matter, and actively drag the metric field around inside that region while leaving everything outside it untouched. What you get is a distinct mathematical solution, identical to the original everywhere outside the hole but disagreeing inside it. If spacetime points are genuine individuals with identities of their own, independent of whatever fields happen to be defined on them, then nothing in the physics picks out which of these solutions is the real future of the region, and general relativity turns out to be radically indeterministic. What hooked me is that Earman and Norton present this explicitly as a technical descendant of Leibniz's objections to Newton and Clarke about absolute space, which sent me back to the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence for the first time since an undergraduate survey course—and which, I've since learned, is close kin to the worry that made Einstein himself briefly reject generally covariant field equations around 1913, before he talked himself out of it two years later with the point-coincidence argument.

The more I sit with both arguments side by side, though, the less sure I am that the lineage is as clean as it gets presented in the reading group. Leibniz's own shift argument compares two entire possible worlds—ours, and one just like it but shifted uniformly through space, or begun a few years later—and rules out the extra one by way of the identity of indiscernibles (PII) together with the principle of sufficient reason (PSR): God could have had no reason to prefer one over the other, and since no internal relation would differ either way, the two "worlds" were never really distinct to begin with. The hole argument instead generates its two disagreeing models out of a single history, by a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside the hole and arbitrary inside it, and whether that pair amounts to two genuine possibilities or one possibility redescribed twice (what people now call Leibniz equivalence) is exactly what's in dispute between substantivalists and relationalists, rather than something Leibniz's own machinery obviously settles in advance the way it does for his own example.

So here is what I would like help thinking through: if you could fully brief Leibniz on general covariance and the hole construction, do you think he would recognize it as simply his shift argument dressed in the language of differential geometry, and treat the resulting indeterminism as fresh vindication of relationalism—or would something in his own commitments make him hesitate, given that PII and PSR were built to compare static possible worlds rather than to diagnose a theory's failure to fix its own future? And what do you suspect he would say to a "sophisticated substantivalist" who grants that spacetime points are real, mind-independent individuals but denies that the diffeomorphic models represent distinct possibilities at all—would that concession have struck him as a genuine rescue of substantivalism, or as relationalism readmitted under a new name?
