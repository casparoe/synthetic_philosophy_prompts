I'm taking a seminar this term on early modern natural philosophy, and this week's session covered the General Scholium that Newton added to the Principia, his correspondence with Richard Bentley, and the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence (in which Samuel Clarke was effectively arguing Newton's side). What struck me is that Newton actually runs together at least two rather different theological arguments from physics. One, developed partly with Bentley and partly in the Opticks queries, is a "sustainer" argument: since he thought the initial, highly ordered arrangement of the planets could not be a product of blind mechanical causes alone, and since he worried that the mutual gravitational tugging among planets and comets would gradually accumulate into irregularities, he suggested that God might need to step in occasionally and give the system, in his words, "a reformation." The other argument is more purely metaphysical: in those same queries, and in an unpublished manuscript now known as De Gravitatione, he suggests that infinite space itself is bound up with God's own omnipresence (the famous "sensorium" language), so that space and time are not properties of bodies but are somehow constituted by God's own boundless, eternal existence. Leibniz attacked both lines during Newton's lifetime, memorably comparing Newton's God to a watchmaker whose watch is so badly made that he must keep fixing it, and defending a purely relational view of space and time instead. A couple of us in the seminar got into an argument about whether the "design" line and the "maintenance" line are really as separable as I've just made them sound, which is partly why I want a fresh take.

My real question is about what happened once Newton was no longer around to answer back. Within about sixty years of his death, Lagrange and Laplace showed, using perturbation theory, that the irregularities Newton worried about are largely self-correcting oscillations rather than a runaway accumulation, at least to the order of approximation they could check (we now know from later chaos-theoretic work that the full long-run picture is messier, but that's a separate complication). Then, roughly two centuries later, general relativity abolished any privileged absolute space or time of the sort Newton's theology needed, replacing it with a dynamical geometry that leaves no obvious room for anything like a divine sensorium. Taking Newton's actual, stated commitments seriously, rather than just assuming he'd smoothly update into a modern theist, how do you think he would have responded to each of these two developments? Would one have been substantially more damaging to his overall theological project than the other, and is there some more defensible core of his view — that the order of nature manifests and depends on divine governance — that could survive both, or were the specific arguments he actually gave doing too much of the load-bearing work for that core to be separable from them? I'd rather have an answer that reconstructs the actual structure of his arguments than one that just gestures at "he was devout, so he'd find a way."
