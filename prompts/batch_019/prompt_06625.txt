Bit of context: I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology, but I sit in on a CS/AI-safety reading group most Fridays because half my committee is in that world too. Two regulars, Marcus (ML theory) and Elena (also philosophy, but does phil of mind), got into a real argument after we finished ARC's "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" report — specifically the part about the human simulator vs. direct translator problem for the reporter. It kept going on Slack for like two days and I ended up saving the whole thread because I honestly cant tell who's winning.

(1) Marcus's opening move: penalize the reporter for extra computation (a speed prior, basically) and you should get the direct translator, since direct translation is just reading off the predictor's own internal state — cheap by construction — while faking what a fooled human would conclude from tampered sensor data means running a whole seperate simulation of human judgement, which costs more. (2) Elena said that assumes translating is cheap, but the entire reason ELK is hard in the first place is that we have no guarantee the predictor's internal ontology lines up with human concepts at all — that translation step could easily be the expensive part, in which case the same prior favors the human simulator, since it was built to output human-shaped answers from the start. (3) Marcus came back with: fine, but in the tampering cases we actually care about, the predictor has to already represent what will really happen to the diamond just to predict the video accurately, so that fact is sitting latent in its state for free, whereas the human simulator only has the tampered sensor data to go on and has to solve a strictly harder inverse problem from worse information — that should cost more, not less. (4) Elena's reply: "harder inference problem" doesn't imply "more compute actually spent by whatever solution gradient descent happens to find," and if his story were right you'd expect at least some drift toward direct translation even in easy, non-tampering cases, where translation is cheap by his own logic too — but we don't see that either, so maybe cost isn't the variable doing the work. (5) Marcus said the lack of drift in easy cases is exactly what his view predicts, not a problem for it: both strategies get identical reward and comparable cost there, so there's no gradient pressure toward either one, and the divergence should only show up once genuinely hard tampering is actually present in training. (6) Elena's last word was that this just relocates the problem — the whole point of ELK is that we can't safely put well-labeled, genuinely hard tampering cases in the training set, so the divergence he's counting on can only appear exactly where we're unable to supervise it, meaning we could never confirm from training behavior that the reporter drifted the right way. Which, she said, defeats the purpose of proposing this as a training-time fix at all.

Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just an overall winner? And is there a real, defensible position somewhere in this neighborhood that neither of them quite lands on? I keep feeling like they're both right about something and wrong about something else but I honestly can't figure out what.
