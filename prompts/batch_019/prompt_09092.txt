I've spent the past few months reporting on the informal forecasting pools that have sprung up among researchers at a couple of the institutes I cover—casual arrangements where people post their own odds on things like grant outcomes, election results, or whether a given study will replicate, and colleagues can bet against them for small stakes. To keep people honest, the unwritten rule is that whoever posts a number has to be willing to take either side of a bet at that price. A recent episode in one of these pools has stuck with me, and I'd like a second opinion before I write about it.

A public-health researcher in the group posted odds on an upcoming committee vote that was certain to go exactly one of three ways: the proposal would be rejected outright, approved with amendments, or approved as written. She priced rejection at 55 percent, amendment at 40 percent, and approval as written at 30 percent—each of which, taken by itself, struck everyone as a perfectly defensible guess. A colleague noticed that the three numbers added up to 125 percent and, invoking that rule, quietly worked out stakes across all three bets at once so that whichever way the vote went, what he'd collected from her up front would exceed what he owed her back. He duly ended up ahead when the vote came in, and would have ended up ahead no matter what the committee had decided. When this was pointed out to her, she wasn't particularly troubled: each of her three numbers, she said, had been her honest best guess at the moment she posted it, and getting caught out by someone clever enough to notice a pattern across three separate quotes reflects on her arithmetic, not on whether her actual beliefs about the vote were reasonable.

I can't decide who has the better of this. "Guaranteed to come out behind no matter what happens" sounds like exactly the sort of thing that should worry someone about their own beliefs, and not just their bookkeeping—yet it feels strange to say her thinking about the vote was somehow defective when the flaw only became visible once someone else stitched three separate numbers together. It also makes me wonder whether it would matter if she'd given those same three numbers in three unrelated conversations, weeks apart, to people who never compared notes, so that nobody could ever actually have assembled the profitable combination against her: would her beliefs have been any less flawed simply because no one was ever positioned to exploit them? I'd be grateful for your take on who has the better argument, on whether there's a rigorous case that this kind of vulnerability really does expose something wrong with a person's beliefs themselves rather than merely their pricing, and on whether that case survives the fact that people are almost never actually forced into a bet against a cleverly assembled combination in ordinary life.
