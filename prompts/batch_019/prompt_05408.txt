I'm working on a magazine feature about how biologists and philosophers manage to talk about a trait's "function" without smuggling in old-fashioned teleology, and after a dozen interviews I've noticed that almost every source — and, embarrassingly, every draft of my own explainer — leans on the same two illustrations to introduce the split between etiological and causal-role theories of function. The etiological camp, running from Larry Wright's original proposal through Karen Neander's and Ruth Millikan's refinements, holds that a trait's function is whatever effect it was selected for across past generations; the causal-role camp, following Robert Cummins, holds that a trait's function is simply whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of a containing system, with no essential appeal to history at all.

Everyone illustrates the gap with the heart (its function is to pump blood, not to make a thumping noise, even though causal-role theorists have their own way of trying to rule the noise out) or with Davidson's Swampman, the freak molecular duplicate of a person with no evolutionary history, whose heart, according to etiological theories, has no function at all, even though it pumps blood perfectly well. One philosopher I interviewed was blunt about it: he suspects that any "new" example anyone proposes will, on inspection, turn out to be the heart-and-noise case wearing a costume.

I'd like to test that claim. Could you invent an original example — a specific real or biologically realistic organism, organ, trait, or behavior, excluding the heart, camouflage or coloration, and any Swampman-style duplicate — in which an etiological theorist and a causal-role theorist would deliver genuinely different verdicts about what function the trait has, or whether it has one at all? Walk through what each side would say about your case and pinpoint exactly where and why their verdicts diverge, then tell me, in your own editorial judgment, whether your example is doing real philosophical work or whether my source is right that it secretly collapses back into the same old dispute over pumps and thumps.
