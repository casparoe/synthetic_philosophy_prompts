So a buddy of mine who just started law school got me arguing over the weekend about "textualism" vs "purposivism," which are apparently the two big camps on how judges are supposed to read statutes, and it's been stuck in my head ever since. Being a programmer, I keep mapping it onto something more familiar: one camp is basically "the compiler doesn't care what you meant, it only cares what you wrote, so parse the literal syntax and that's the contract," and the other camp is more like a code reviewer going "sure, but what was this function actually *for*, what bug was the original dev trying to fix, and we should honor that even when the literal code technically says something dumber." My friend sent me a paragraph from an old Supreme Court case that's apparently the classic battleground for exactly this fight — Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, from 1892 — and I've reread it like six times and only half get it:

"It is a familiar rule, that a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers. This has been often asserted, and the reports are full of cases illustrating its application. This is not the substitution of the will of the judge for that of the legislator; for frequently words of general meaning are used in a statute, words broad enough to include an act in question, and yet a consideration of the whole legislation, or of the circumstances surrounding its enactment, or of the absurd results which follow from giving such broad meaning to the words, makes it unmistakable that the legislature intended to include the particular act."

Two things I could use help with. First, just as a matter of plain logic — how can something be "within the letter" of a statute but somehow not "within the statute"? Isnt the letter just what the statute says, literally? It reads like its smuggling in the conclusion through fancy wording, but maybe theres a term-of-art thing going on with "the statute" that I'm not getting. Second, and this is the part I can't work out on my own: I know modern textualists (Scalia apparently built half his career ranting about this exact case) treat the whole opinion as judges just making up whatever result they wanted. But the passage itself only claims to invoke a pretty narrow "absurd results" exception, which I thought even hardcore textualists are sometimes ok with, kind of like the "scrivener's error" thing? So is the real objection to this case that the court used "absurdity" as cover to go way further into open-ended purpose-hunting than that doctrine actually allows, or would a committed textualist reject even this narrow letter-vs-spirit move on principle, no matter how modestly it's applied? I already know the "textualism = words, purposivism = intent" elevator pitch, so I'm hoping for the actual mechanics of where people who think hard about this draw the line, not the one-liner version.
