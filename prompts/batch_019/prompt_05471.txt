I've spent a fair amount of time on rationalist blogs and forums arguing about when apparent consensus is actually informative — the kind of argument that flares up whenever someone brings up expert forecasts, prediction markets, or Aumann's agreement theorem — and one piece of formal machinery keeps coming up in that context: the informational-cascade models due to Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch, and the related herd-behavior model from Banerjee. The result that still strikes me as almost unsettling, even after working through the proofs more than once, is this: if a sequence of people each observe only the actions (not the underlying reasons) of everyone who decided before them, then after only a handful of decisions it becomes individually rational for every subsequent person to set aside their own private information entirely and simply copy the accumulated majority — even in cases where, had everyone's private signals been honestly pooled, the "wrong" option would have been exposed as wrong. What is hard to convey to people outside this literature is that this isn't a story about stupidity or conformity in the ordinary sense: every actor in the chain is doing exactly what a rational Bayesian updater should do, given how little of the situation they can actually see. The Emperor's New Clothes gets at a folk version of the same idea, but without any of the bookkeeping; most fictional treatments I've come across otherwise (bank runs, market manias) do little better, gesturing at "crowd psychology" instead of dramatizing the actual, step-by-step rationality behind each individual's capitulation.

I would like you to write a short story (somewhere in the 800–1500 word range) that gets the mechanics right without ever stating them outright. Set it, if you would, in a small fishing village where, over a run of mornings, boat captains decide one after another whether to sail out past a headland while a storm system loiters somewhere offshore. Each captain has some private, imperfect evidence to go on — a barometer reading, an old injury that aches before bad weather, something read in the behavior of the gulls — but each also watches how many boats have already left that morning and how many of yesterday's boats returned safely, and weighs that alongside their own evidence before deciding. Without any character pausing to explain the theory out loud, the story should make it legible that the earliest departures are driven mostly by private judgment; that a few boats in, the accumulated weight of observed decisions makes it individually sensible for a captain to override private doubt; and that the resulting run on the harbor is fragile in a specific sense, since it is backed by far less real evidence than it appears to be, such that a single small new fact, or a single visible holdout, would be enough to put the whole pattern in question. I would rather you not resolve that tension too neatly; I am especially interested in whether you can make such a moment of doubt or reversal feel just as locally rational, from inside a character's head, as the cascade that produced it in the first place.
