I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I recently spent some time reading Plato's Laws and was surprised by how different it is from the popular image of Plato as a stern opponent of bodily pleasure. In Books 1 and 2, the Athenian Stranger argues, to the visible alarm of his Spartan and Cretan companions, that a well-run city should actually institute regulated drinking parties: held under a sober leader (what the Greeks called a symposiarch) and governed by fixed rules, these parties serve as a kind of harmless test that reveals a person's true capacity for self-control once ordinary inhibitions are loosened, much as controlled exposure to fear is used elsewhere to test and train courage. He even proposes age-graduated laws under which mature and older men are given considerably more license to drink, up to real intoxication at festivals of Dionysus, on the grounds that wine loosens the rigidity that comes with age and lets them join in choral song and worship without the shame they would feel sober. Megillus and Cleinias, by contrast, start from the conviction, common to Sparta and Crete, that such gatherings should simply be forbidden or tightly curtailed, since in their experience wine breeds disorder rather than virtue.

Please write a dialogue, set at a present-day work conference dinner, between two attendees who end up defending these two positions: one revives something close to the Athenian's case that supervised, rule-governed drinking is a genuine tool of moral education, and the other revives something close to the Spartan-Cretan case that it should be discouraged or banned outright. Keep both sides equally sharp, have each press the other on their strongest point, including whether there is a real disanalogy between training courage through controlled exposure to fear and "training" temperance through actual intoxication, and don't let either side simply concede by the end.
