Over the past couple of years I have taught myself some formal logic, without any academic background in philosophy, and I keep returning to dialetheism: the view that some contradictions are actually true, together with the paraconsistent logics designed to stop a single contradiction from entailing absolutely anything, a pattern of inference usually called the principle of explosion. What keeps me from being persuaded is the strengthened Liar, the sentence that says of itself, "This sentence is not true." If the dialetheist's own negation can hold together with truth, so that a sentence can be both true and not-true at once, then it seems they can never manage to say that any claim is true and only true, since the very trick that generated the original paradox can be repeated on that very claim. But if they instead reach for a stronger "not," one that genuinely excludes truth, that looks like smuggling classical logic back in through the side door. I would like to see two equally capable people argue this out rather than read another summary of the position. Could you write a dialogue between a committed dialetheist and a logician who holds that no contradiction can ever be true, in which the logician presses exactly this difficulty, the dialetheist answers with more than a repetition that some contradictions are true, and each side responds to the specific points the other actually makes?
