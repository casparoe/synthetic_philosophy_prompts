Over the past year I have worked my way through a few different translations of the Analects and the Mencius, and one interpretive claim keeps coming up that I have not been able to settle in my own mind. Several scholars, especially Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, argue that Confucian ethics should not be read as a variety of virtue ethics at all, but as something they call "role ethics": on this view, a person just is the sum of their concrete relationships (parent and child, ruler and subject, elder and younger, friend and friend), rather than an individual who first has a character and only then happens to stand in those relationships. I find the idea attractive, but I suspect it might really be a difference of vocabulary and emphasis dressed up as a difference in kind. Please compare Confucian role ethics with Aristotelian virtue ethics directly on this point: is there something the relational, role-based picture of the person can explain or capture that a sufficiently social and other-regarding virtue ethics cannot, or does role ethics collapse, on close inspection, into a regional variant of virtue ethics with unfamiliar terminology? Please commit to one answer and defend it, rather than simply listing considerations on both sides.
