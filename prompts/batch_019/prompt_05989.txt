Six weeks ago my audiologist confirmed that my hearing has dropped low enough to qualify me for a cochlear implant, and I now have to decide within the next couple of months, before what remains of my natural hearing degrades further. I grew up hard-of-hearing, most of my closest friends are Deaf, and the decision has led me into a good deal of reading, including L. A. Paul's work on transformative experience, which is the first framework I've found that actually names what's unsettling me. The surgery appears to be both epistemically transformative (no one can tell me in advance what electronically mediated hearing will actually sound or feel like) and personally transformative (implantees often describe their sense of what matters, even their relationship to music and language, shifting in ways their pre-implant selves would not have endorsed). Ordinary cost-benefit reasoning quietly assumes a stable person doing the weighing, and that assumption is exactly what is in question here. Please write me a short story, in the vein of philosophically serious speculative fiction, that dramatizes this precise bind: a protagonist facing a choice that is both epistemically and personally transformative. The setting need not involve hearing, deafness, or implants at all — I would rather see the underlying problem given an unfamiliar shape than watch my own situation reflected back at me — but the story should resist the two easy exits: the post-transformation self simply reporting back that it was all worthwhile, or the character declining and letting the status quo win by default.
