Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading my way into political philosophy, and I have repeatedly encountered the Condorcet Jury Theorem as the centerpiece of "epistemic" arguments for democracy; Christian List and Robert Goodin's generalization of the theorem, together with David Estlund's more skeptical treatment of such arguments, have been my principal guides. Having spent decades concerned with the difference between correlated and independent error in instruments, I distrust the theorem's independence assumption: real voters share newspapers, social networks, and priors, and even mild correlation is known to badly undercut its asymptotic guarantee. I also doubt that "competence" (a better-than-even chance of being right) is a coherent notion once we leave determinate factual questions for contested value questions, and the theorem is in any case an asymptotic, large-number result that may say little about juries or committees of realistic size. I find it more than a little ironic, besides, that the very essay in which Condorcet gives us this optimistic aggregation result also gives us the paradox of cyclical majorities.

Could you write an essay weighing whether the theorem, honestly qualified, still supports the epistemic superiority of inclusive majority rule -- taking into account the strongest replies a defender of the theorem could make to the worries above -- or whether it actually points elsewhere, toward something like epistocracy, or toward no determinate conclusion at all? Please engage directly with the independence and competence assumptions rather than resting on the textbook statement of the theorem, and close with an actual verdict rather than a mere survey of considerations: something of the shape "the theorem supports X only under conditions Y, which are (or are not) realistic for modern electorates," defended against the strongest objections you can muster against your own verdict.
