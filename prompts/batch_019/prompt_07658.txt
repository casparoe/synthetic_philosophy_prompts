During my career I worked mainly in statistical mechanics — entropy, fluctuation theorems, and the like — so when I began reading popular and semi-technical cosmology after retiring, the notion of a "Boltzmann brain" caught my attention immediately. As I understand it, if the universe settles into an eternal de Sitter phase with a stubbornly nonzero vacuum energy, spontaneous thermal fluctuations will, given enough time, occasionally assemble a fully self-aware brain complete with false memories of an entire life and history, and such freak brains would vastly outnumber "ordinary" observers like ourselves who arrived via the long, low-entropy route of stellar and biological evolution. Cosmologists generally treat the conclusion that we are probably Boltzmann brains as an absurdity, and use it to disqualify or constrain models of the universe's long-term fate that predict it.

I recently raised this in a small philosophy discussion group I joined after retiring, where we had just finished working through Descartes' First Meditation and some of the more recent brain-in-a-vat literature. One member insisted that the Boltzmann brain worry is nothing new — just Descartes' evil demon dressed up in a lab coat. I am not convinced this is right. It seems to me that the Boltzmann brain scenario has a rather different logical shape from the classical skeptical hypotheses: it is not an arbitrary, logically possible alternative introduced from outside to embarrass our claims to knowledge, but a conclusion that appears to fall out of taking certain of our own best physical theories seriously and reasoning about which observers, among all those with our exact memories, are "typical." I also recall Sean Carroll arguing, somewhere in his writing on this topic, that theories predicting Boltzmann brain domination are in some sense self-undermining, or "cognitively unstable": the very reasoning and memories that led you to accept such a theory would themselves be no more trustworthy than the random neural noise of a Boltzmann brain, if the theory were true.

I would be grateful for your help thinking this through more carefully. Could you compare the epistemic structure of the Boltzmann brain problem with that of classical Cartesian skepticism (the evil demon and its brain-in-a-vat descendants), addressing specifically whether the two amount to the same kind of threat to knowledge or to genuinely different ones? I would also like you to weigh in on whether standard anti-skeptical strategies aimed at the Cartesian scenario — Descartes' own cogito, say, or Putnam's semantic-externalist argument that an envatted brain's words could not even refer to real vats — have any purchase against the Boltzmann brain worry, or whether its self-undermining, "internally generated" character makes it immune, or perhaps even more resistant, to that style of response.
