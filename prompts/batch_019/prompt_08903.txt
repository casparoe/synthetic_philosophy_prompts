I spent something like thirty years doing fieldwork on ungulate population dynamics -- elk mostly, some mule deer -- before I retired, and I've somehow tumbled into reading moral philosophy since then, which none of my old colleagues can quite believe. Lately I've been stuck on the "wild animal suffering" literature (Horta, Tomasik, and that McMahan piece in the Times, "The Meat Eaters," or whatever it was called, that everybody in this corner of philosophy still seems to be arguing about). This lands a bit differently for me than it probably does for most readers, since I spent literal decades tallying overwinter fawn mortality, and the thought that all that starvation and predation might amount to a moral emergency on the scale these people describe is honestly hard to shake off.

Anyway, I help run a little philosophy-and-nature discussion night at our library, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends the strong version of the view: that the sheer scale of suffering in wild populations generates a real, if distant, obligation to eventually intervene in ecosystems -- contraception, vaccination, habitat engineering, maybe eventually predator control -- rather than just leaving nature "be." I don't want to let him just restate the thesis for an hour, so could you draft me 8 to 10 interview questions, each with a quick follow-up keyed to whatever answer he's most likely give? I want at least one question pressing on the epistemic-humility objection (that we're nowhere near competent enough to re-engineer food webs without triggering worse cascades), one on how the view squares with mainstream conservation ethics, which tends to value ecosystems and "wildness" holistically rather than tallying up welfare, one on whether his logic commits him to worrying about insects and zooplankton too once you take the numbers seriously, and one that makes him either name an actual real-world intervention already underway that fits his view or admit that none really exist yet at the scale he needs. If you can work in a question or two that catches him treating "prevent suffering" and "let happy lives exist" asymmetrically without quite noticing it, even better -- that inconsistency bugs me every time I read this stuff.
