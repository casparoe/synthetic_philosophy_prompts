I'm a junior at my high school and one of two student representatives on our Academic Technology Committee, the joint teacher-student body that decides which software the school adopts. This term we're voting on whether to license an AI writing-integrity platform I'll call ScribeCheck, which a few neighboring districts already use: a teacher uploads an essay, and the tool returns a flag ("likely AI-generated," "likely original," or "inconclusive") along with a short written rationale, something like "flagged due to unusually low sentence-length variance in paragraphs two and four, inconsistent with this student's earlier submissions." After our AI Ethics Club read some material on why a sophisticated system's stated reasons for a judgment don't have to correspond to whatever inside it actually produced that judgment, I raised this as a concern at committee, and our faculty chair has now asked me to turn it into a short memo before Thursday's vote, since several teachers already talk about citing the rationale text directly in an Honor Council hearing.

The committee is genuinely divided, and I want the memo to represent that fairly rather than caricature the other side. Several teachers argue that a tool which explains itself in specific, checkable-sounding terms is obviously more trustworthy than a bare score, and that refusing to let anyone use the rationale would waste ScribeCheck's main advantage over the similarity-checking software we already own. Our computer science teacher and I are worried about something more specific: the rationale generator was presumably built, or at least selected by the vendor, to produce explanations that sound convincing and line up with the flag, not necessarily to report whatever computation inside the model actually generated that flag -- and the two could come apart precisely in the hardest cases, like an essay with unusually uniform sentence structure because the student has spent years in competitive debate, or one that was heavily line-edited by a human writing tutor, which are also the cases where a teacher has no independent way of telling whether the stated explanation is accurate.

Could you write the memo I'll circulate to the committee ahead of the vote? It should give one clear recommendation -- adopting the tool as-is, adopting it with specific restrictions on how the rationale may be used, running a time-limited pilot with defined success criteria, or declining to adopt it for now -- along with a realistic timeline, and it should engage seriously with the strongest form of the view that an explained flag is always better than an unexplained one, rather than dismissing that view out of hand.
