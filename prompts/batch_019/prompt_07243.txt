I am putting together a short taxonomy for a graduate seminar on self-reference in formal epistemology, organized around the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. Löb's theorem tells us that if a consistent theory extending Peano arithmetic proves "if this is provable, then it is true" for some sentence, it must already prove that sentence outright; instantiated at the sentence "false," this just is Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. So a proof-based agent can never adopt, as a general policy, the thought that whatever it — or a like-minded successor — manages to prove is thereby true, except where it already had the thing proved; treated as a genuine policy for arbitrary claims, the assumption collapses the system into inconsistency.

A number of quite different strategies have been proposed in response. Please rank the following eight by how much genuine philosophical work each does toward actually meeting the obstacle, as opposed to relocating it, relabeling it, or simply learning to live with it:

- Tiling agents built on parametric polymorphism (Yudkowsky and Herreshoff)
- Reflective oracles
- Logical induction
- Justification logic's use of explicit proof terms in place of a blanket provability predicate
- Transfinite progressions of increasingly strong theories, in the spirit of Turing's and Feferman's work on ordinal logics
- Substructural restrictions on contraction, imported from the literature on Curry's paradox
- Deflationary appeals to bounded, track-record-based trust in place of proof-theoretic certainty
- Outsourcing verification to external overseers rather than securing it internally
