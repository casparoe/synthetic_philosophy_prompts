I've been slowly working through Judea Pearl's *The Book of Why* alongside the SEP entries on causation, counterfactuals, and causation-and-manipulability, partly because a few old LessWrong threads on do-calculus and causal decision theory kept assuming a familiarity with this material that I didn't quite have yet. One thing that's been nagging at me is that popular summaries tend to compress the history into a single line: Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation ran into trouble with preemption, and Woodward's interventionist, structural-equations approach (the one worked out in *Making Things Happen*) fixed it. When I actually read into the preemption literature, that seemed too quick, and I raised as much at my local rationalist meetup, though it didn't get much traction there either. Take the standard case, sometimes called the Suzy-and-Billy case: Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle; either throw, on its own, would shatter it; but Suzy's rock happens to arrive first, so the bottle is already in pieces by the time Billy's would have gotten there. Intuitively, Suzy's throw caused the shattering and Billy's didn't, even though it isn't true that the bottle wouldn't have shattered if Suzy hadn't thrown (it would have, via Billy). Lewis's answer, going back to the 1973 "Causation" paper and refined in "Causation as Influence," is to require an unbroken chain of stepwise counterfactual dependence running through fine-grained intermediate events, a chain that exists on Suzy's side and not on Billy's. But the most natural interventionist test — would surgically intervening on Suzy's throw, holding Billy's throw fixed at its actual value, change whether the bottle shatters — seems to give exactly the wrong verdict, for exactly the same reason: with Billy's throw held fixed, the bottle shatters no matter what Suzy does. So interventionists need a repair of their own (of the kind Halpern and Pearl build into their formal definition of actual causation, which lets some variables be evaluated away from their actual values instead of simply being held fixed), and it isn't obvious to me that this is any less of a patch than Lewis's chains.

Rather than just asserting that the two theories are in the same boat, I'd like to see the matter actually fought out. Could you write a dialogue between a committed Lewisian counterfactual theorist and a committed Woodwardian interventionist who watch the Suzy-and-Billy throw unfold in front of them, each trying to convince the other that their own account handles the case more cleanly? I want real engagement rather than a rout: have the Lewisian press the interventionist on whether the repair needed to fix the naive intervention test is any more principled than chains of stepwise dependence, and give the interventionist a genuine comeback — about what independently motivates the repair, say, or about cases where the two patches come apart — rather than a quiet concession. Feel free to let them get properly technical (structural equations, backtracking counterfactuals, the individuation of events, and so on); I would much rather have that than a version simplified for newcomers.
