I've been on a real Integrated Information Theory kick lately (my one friend who does computational neuroscience got me hooked, and now I can't stop), and the part I keep getting stuck on is the "exclusion postulate" -- the idea that out of every possible way you could draw a boundary around some interacting parts (a neuron, a brain, a brain plus the chair its sitting in, whatever), only the one grouping with the actual maximum of integrated information, phi, gets to be a subject of experience at that moment. Everything else that overlaps with it, even stuff with plenty of phi of its own, just doesn't count, isn't "there" experientially -- which is apparently why your individual neurons aren't each having their own tiny experience on the side even though something is clearly integrating information at that level too. Once you sit with it a while it's a genuinely strange peice of machinery, because it turns consciousness into a kind of invisible competition rather than just a matter of how much integration happens to be going on.

So here's the story I want and can't quite write myself. Near-future palliative care ward. Hospitals have started using an experimental "bridging" device that links a dying patient's brain activity with a family member's, sold to families as a way to share the patient's last stretch of experience with them, to feel what they're (maybe) still feeling in their final hours. Give me a short story about one family using it on what turns out to be the last night, where a character who actually understands the theory -- a hospice neurologist, a former phi-lab tech turned hospice volunteer, doesn't matter who -- has to sit with, and maybe say out loud to people who don't want to hear it, the possibility that linking the two brains doesn't share anything at all. It just changes who wins the competition, and the dying relative's own thin, flickering claim to being "there" might get crowded out completely the second the machine switches on, well before the heart or brainstem ever gives out.

I mostly write literary fiction rather than hard sci-fi, so it doesn't need to explain the theory properly -- if anything I'd rather the theory stay half-submerged, load-bearing but not lectured about. I'm picturing something like 1500-3000 words, though flash length is fine too if that's where it wants to go. Mainly I don't want it to resolve too neatly -- I don't need the neurologist to be proven right or wrong by the end, I just want the family (and the reader) left genuinely holding the question of whether they just watched someone die twice, or once, or not yet.
