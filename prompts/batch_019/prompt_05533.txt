I am a retired experimental physicist who only took up Pascal in earnest after retiring, prompted by a nagging feeling that I had spent forty years relying on his triangle and on expected-value reasoning without ever having actually read the Pensées themselves. The Wager fragment gave me no trouble, but I have since spent several evenings stuck on the material usually gathered under headings like "Justice, force" and "Reason of effects," and I find I understand the famous one-liner -- that justice without force is powerless, force without justice tyrannical, and that since we could not make what is just strong we have instead made what is strong just -- rather better than I understand the argument that is supposed to stand behind it.

What I would like help with is the three-tier structure he builds around custom and "opinion": ordinary people who obey a law, or defer to rank, because they sincerely believe it just; the "demi-habiles," the half-clever, who see through this belief and conclude the whole business is arbitrary and beneath them; and then some further, more fully wise position that, as far as I can tell, ends up recommending exactly the same outward deference to custom as the first group, only for a different reason. I do not yet see how that third position is supposed to be reached, or what really distinguishes it from simple naivety if the two land in the same place. A friend from my old laboratory, who studied some philosophy decades ago, waved the whole passage off as "just Hobbes with better prose," but that verdict feels too quick to me. So: is this three-tier apparatus doing genuine explanatory work in showing why force and justice end up glued together in practice, or is my friend right that it is mostly decoration on top of a blunter, essentially Hobbesian point?

I would also value your view on whether Pascal's separate remarks on the three "orders" of greatness -- body, mind, and charity -- and his definition of tyranny as wanting, through force or through cleverness, what is properly owed only within a different order, are meant to bear on the justice-and-force fragment at all. He died, after all, before arranging any of this into a finished argument, so it is possible I am imposing more system on a set of loose notebook fragments than is really there. I would rather have the actual reasoning spelled out, with its moving parts distinguished from one another, than be handed back the single sentence I already had memorized before I started this.
