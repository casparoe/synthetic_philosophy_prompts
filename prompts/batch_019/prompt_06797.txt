I'm writing a novel in which one of my characters, a young philosopher at Cambridge in the early 1920s, is working through G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica in her private notebooks, and I want her reasoning to be philosophically serious rather than a caricature. The passage that most preoccupies her comes from the chapter Moore calls "The Ideal," where he asks us to compare two unwitnessed worlds:

"Let us imagine one world exceedingly beautiful. Imagine it as beautiful as you can; put into it whatever on this earth you most admire — mountains, rivers, the sea; trees, and sunsets, stars and moon. Imagine these all combined in the most exquisite proportions, so that no one thing jars against another, but each contributes to increase the beauty of the whole. And then imagine the ugliest world you can possibly conceive. Imagine it simply one heap of filth, containing everything that is most disgusting to us, for whatever reason, and the whole, as far as may be, without one redeeming feature... The only thing we are not entitled to imagine is that any human being ever has or ever, by any possibility, can live in either, can ever see and enjoy the beauty of the one or hate the foulness of the other. Well, even so, is it irrational to hold that it is better that the beautiful world should exist than the ugly one?"

Moore takes this to show that beauty retains some real, if modest, intrinsic value even when nobody ever beholds it. Yet elsewhere in the same chapter he argues that the finest things in life are what he calls "organic unities," whose value as wholes — the cognition of a genuinely admirable object bound up with the fitting feeling toward it — far exceeds the sum of their parts, and he relies on precisely this structure to explain why mutual personal affection between people who truly appreciate one another's real qualities ranks among the most precious things we know. For my character's sake, could you help me work out whether these two claims can genuinely be held together, or whether the thought experiment about the unwitnessed worlds sits in tension with the organic-unity argument — and, if so, how a careful reader of Moore might go about resolving it?
