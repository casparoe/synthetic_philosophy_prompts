Since retiring after thirty-odd years as a field entomologist, I have kept up one particular habit: on most mild, dark nights between April and October I run an actinic light trap in the garden and log every moth that turns up before releasing it the next morning. I have also, more recently, joined a small philosophy discussion group at the local library, largely out of curiosity, and a few weeks ago we spent an evening on Hempel's raven paradox. I found it amusing at first, in the way a good logical trick usually is, but the more I turn it over the more it unsettles me about habits of inference I relied on for my entire working life without ever examining them closely.

As I now understand the argument, the trouble follows from two seemingly innocuous principles: Nicod's criterion, that a generalization of the form "all A are B" is confirmed by any observed object that is both A and B, and is simply neutral with respect to anything that isn't an A at all; and the equivalence condition, that whatever confirms a statement must equally confirm any statement logically equivalent to it. Since "all ravens are black" is equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens," Nicod's criterion applied to that second formulation says that a white shoe, being neither black nor a raven, confirms it — and the equivalence condition then forces us to say the shoe confirms the original claim about ravens as well. Hempel himself accepted this conclusion rather than rejecting either principle, treating our discomfort as a merely psychological artifact of background knowledge (we already know there are vastly more non-black things than ravens, so the shoe teaches us almost nothing) rather than as a sign that the logic itself has gone wrong. I have since read that I. J. Good pushed back with a scenario, built from explicit background probabilities, in which finding an actual black raven could count against "all ravens are black" rather than for it — which suggests that whether, and how much, a piece of evidence confirms a hypothesis cannot be read off its logical form alone.

What unsettles me is that field taxonomy runs on generalizations of exactly this shape ("every specimen of this species shows an unbroken pale line near the hindwing margin," and so on), and I now recall that my colleagues and I treated the sheer bulk of unrelated, unremarkable specimens we handled over the decades — almost none of which were actually members of the species in question — as a kind of quiet background reassurance for such claims, without ever asking whether that reassurance was doing any logical work at all. I would value your assessment of the most important implications of the raven paradox, and of the Hempel–Good exchange over it, for how much genuine evidential weight this sort of "no exception among all the irrelevant specimens I've seen" reasoning really deserves — both in hindsight, for the taxonomic generalizations my career was quietly built on, and in practice, the next time a fellow moth-trap enthusiast in our group tells me she has logged eleven thousand individuals over three seasons without once finding an exception to some cherished rule of thumb.
