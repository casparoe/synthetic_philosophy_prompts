My colleague of nearly thirty years, who spent her career defending Strawson's account of moral responsibility in "Freedom and Resentment," died last month. I have been asked to deliver her eulogy at a memorial where half the room are philosophers who work on moral responsibility for a living and will catch every reference, while the other half is family who won't recognize any of it. She was notorious, at every funeral she ever sat through, for objecting when eulogists quietly turned an ordinary excuse ("she didn't mean it") into something that treats a whole character as a mechanism to be explained rather than a person to be resented, forgiven, or thanked, and equally when eulogists talked as though grief or gratitude needed some guarantee that she could have done otherwise to be legitimate. Her own settled view was that resentment, gratitude, and forgiveness are fully warranted participant reactive attitudes whatever turns out to be true about determinism, and that it is this way of treating people, not any metaphysical discovery, that our relationships with them actually rest on. Write the eulogy on those terms: it should enact grief and gratitude rather than explain them, include one specific, real flaw of hers worked through toward something like forgiveness rather than excused away, and nowhere lapse into the objective attitude or lean on could-have-done-otherwise to make the gratitude feel earned.
