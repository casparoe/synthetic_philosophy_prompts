I'm a software engineer (backend, mostly) with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been working through Hume's *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* at night, mostly out of curiosity. When I got to Section X, "Of Miracles," I expected the argument to collapse into the one-line version everyone already knows secondhand ("extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence"), but it turned out to be more layered than that. Hume seems to be running two distinct arguments: an "in principle" one, where the uniform experience underwriting a law of nature is weighed directly against any possible testimony to a violation of it, and a separate "in practice" argument about why testimony for miracles specifically tends to be weak (too few witnesses of good sense and integrity, a natural human love of the marvelous, and the fact that rival religious traditions' miracle stories tend to undercut one another).

Digging a bit further, I came across two things that caught my interest. First, that Richard Price, the clergyman who edited and posthumously published Thomas Bayes' essay on probability, also wrote one of the earliest direct rebuttals to Hume's argument on miracles. Second, that this debate has been revisited by philosophers of science using explicit Bayesian machinery (I've seen John Earman's name attached to a whole book on the subject), recasting "uniform past experience" as an extremely low prior probability for a miracle, and testimony as evidence that is supposed to update that prior. Since I use Bayesian reasoning all the time in a much less philosophically fraught setting (interpreting A/B test results, tuning anomaly detectors), I'd like to understand whether translating Hume into that framework actually strengthens his case or quietly changes the terms of the debate.

Could you compare Hume's original, informal argument against trusting miracle reports with this kind of Bayesian reconstruction of it? In particular, when Hume's reasoning is made explicit in terms of priors and likelihood ratios, does that vindicate his conclusion, or does it expose a problem that his rhetorical presentation obscures — for instance, the worry that setting the prior low enough on "uniform experience" grounds guarantees in advance that no accumulation of testimony could ever be sufficient? Please reach and defend an actual verdict on whether formalizing Hume's argument this way helps or undermines it, rather than just presenting both readings as equally defensible.
