A friend of mine—she's finishing a PhD in philosophy of physics—and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about whether Bell's theorem actually forces us to abandon locality. She kept pressing the case for superdeterminism: if the hidden variables describing an entangled pair are correlated with the experimenters' later choice of measurement settings, because both ultimately trace back to a shared cause in their common past, then the theory can stay perfectly local and still reproduce the statistics that violate the Bell inequalities. I objected that this looked less like a solution than a way of buying locality on credit, but I couldn't pin down exactly why denying "measurement independence" should strike us as more suspicious than denying locality itself, and she had a good answer for everything I threw at her.

I run a small podcast on philosophy of physics, and I've booked a guest for next month who has built a research program around exactly this position. He's fond of deflecting the standard "conspiracy" objection with some version of the line that any deterministic theory correlates everything with everything eventually, so there's nothing especially sinister about correlating hidden variables with detector settings — "that's not conspiracy, that's just physics." I don't want the episode to consist of him restating that one-liner eight different ways, so could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press past it? I'd like at least one question on why giving up measurement independence is supposed to be a cheaper price than giving up locality; one on how the position holds up as "freedom of choice" experiments push the required common cause further and further back in time, toward sources like distant quasars — and whether there's any principled place for a superdeterminist to stop retreating short of the Big Bang; and one comparing his common-cause story to retrocausal accounts that deny the same independence assumption through backward-in-time influence instead of a shared past. For each of those three, please add a pointed follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. Close the sequence with a question asking him to state, concretely, what observation would ever count as evidence against the view.
