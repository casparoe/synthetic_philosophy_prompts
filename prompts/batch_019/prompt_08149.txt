Over dinner last week, a friend who works in decision theory talked me out of thinking there is anything deeply puzzling about weakness of will. Her argument ran roughly as follows: the ancient problem, as Aristotle poses it and Davidson later sharpens it — how someone can judge, all things considered, that A is better than B, and yet freely and knowingly choose B anyway — is really just an artifact of assuming that agents discount future rewards exponentially. Once you allow for a hyperbolic discount function instead, the value curves for a smaller-sooner reward and a larger-later reward are guaranteed to cross as the smaller-sooner option draws nearer in time, so the agent's ranking of the two options flips automatically as a function of temporal distance. On her view, this means Ainslie's work on hyperbolic discounting and picoeconomics has, in effect, absorbed the old philosophical puzzle into rational choice theory: there is no further mystery about "knowledge not being used," as Aristotle would have it, or about a mismatch between a prima facie and an all-out judgment, as Davidson would have it — akrasia just is what a time-inconsistent discount function looks like from the inside.

I found this fairly persuasive at the time, and I have not been able to come up with a good rebuttal since. Assuming my friend has this basically right, should philosophers who still treat weakness of will as a live, open puzzle about the relation between judgment and action, rather than as a solved problem in intertemporal choice, be regarded as simply behind the curve on the relevant decision-theoretic literature? I would like to understand exactly how far the discounting story is actually supposed to reach.
