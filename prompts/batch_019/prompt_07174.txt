After thirty-five years running an experimental condensed-matter physics lab, I retired two years ago and have finally had the time to read the philosophy of mathematics I always meant to get to. Like many physicists of my generation, I first encountered mathematical logic sideways, through Hofstadter and later Penrose, and came away with a clear picture of what it means for a formal system to be incomplete: a consistent system strong enough to encode arithmetic will always contain sentences that are true of the natural numbers but that the system's own axioms are too weak to prove. This past term I have been auditing a philosophy of mathematics seminar at a local university, where we spent two sessions on Gödel's constructible universe and Cohen's method of forcing, and on their joint upshot that the continuum hypothesis is independent of ZFC. I confess I filed this away under the same heading without much reflection: CH, I assumed, is simply the Gödel phenomenon transplanted into set theory, a fact about the size of the continuum that is determinately true or false of the actual universe of sets (what set theorists call V), with independence telling us only that ZFC's axioms, like Peano arithmetic's, are too weak to reach it. I found this rather satisfying, since it seemed to hand mathematical Platonism a clean victory: there is a fact of the matter sitting out there waiting for someone to find the right axioms, exactly as there are unprovable arithmetic truths waiting for a stronger system to prove them.

What has puzzled me since is that neither the seminar nor my subsequent reading treats CH this way. Instead, whether CH even has a determinate truth value seems to be an open question in its own right, with serious set theorists and philosophers arguing that there may be no fact of the matter for any new axiom ever to reveal, rather than simply an arithmetic-style truth we have not yet reached. If the independence of CH from ZFC really is the same kind of result as Gödel's incompleteness theorems, shouldn't that dispute already be settled in favor of the Platonist reading I arrived at? And if it is not the same kind of result, I would like to understand exactly where the analogy breaks down: what does the proof of Gödel's theorem give us about the natural numbers that the Gödel-Cohen independence proof does not give us about the universe of sets?
