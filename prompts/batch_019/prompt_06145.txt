I'm a biostatistics PhD student, and this week's causal inference seminar sent me down a bit of a rabbit hole about what exactly Simpson's paradox is a paradox of. The instructor's story was the standard one: an association reverses between the aggregated data and the data stratified by some third variable, and the "true" effect is whichever one survives once you've correctly identified the confounder(s) and adjusted for them — draw the right causal DAG and the paradox dissolves. Afterward, a few of us got stuck on cases involving odds ratios (and, in survival analysis, hazard ratios), where the crude and stratum-specific estimates can apparently diverge even when the stratifying variable is demonstrably not a confounder — say, because it's balanced by randomization — purely because those particular effect measures are mathematically non-collapsible. That seems like a genuinely different phenomenon from confounding, even though it produces the same reversal signature, and I don't think our instructor's causal-graph-first story leaves any room for it.

Could you write me a dialogue between two sharp, equally matched causal inference researchers hashing this out over coffee after the seminar? One should defend something like the causal-graph-first view: once you fix the estimand and draw the correct DAG — settling whether the third variable is a confounder, a mediator, or a collider — every instance of Simpson's paradox is fully diagnosed, and talk of "non-collapsibility" is at best a restatement of that story in different vocabulary and at worst a distraction from it. The other should defend the view that non-collapsibility is a genuinely distinct source of divergence between marginal and conditional estimates, one that can show up even in a randomized experiment with no confounding, mediation, or collider bias anywhere in sight, and that flattening it into "just another causal-graph problem" obscures something real about why adjusted and unadjusted odds ratios computed from the very same clean RCT can legitimately disagree. I'd like the two of them to work through at least one shared example in some numeric or structural detail — feel free to invent a clean toy scenario, or borrow something like the kidney-stone-treatment or graduate-admissions cases — rather than just trading slogans, and I'd like each to press the other on their strongest objections rather than talk past them. Neither should be a pushover, and if you think the methodological literature actually favors one side, let that side win on the merits rather than by some artificial even-handedness.
