I'm TAing a graduate seminar on assertion and self-knowledge, and this week's unit centers on Moore's paradox: sentences like "It's raining, but I don't believe it" are perfectly consistent, yet seem impossible to assert or believe sincerely and rationally, and the oddity survives even once the easy explanations are set aside — the speaker need not be lying, joking, or contradicting themselves in any strict logical sense.

For this week's materials, I'd like a short story (roughly 1,000 words) whose plot places a character in a position with exactly this shape: sincerely committed, in word or action, to both "P" and "I don't believe that P," without the narrative resolving the tension by revealing that the character was lying, self-deceived, or simply mistaken about their own mind. I want students to come away unsettled rather than reassured that the case reduces to something more familiar, so please avoid Orwellian doublethink and self-deception narratives more generally — both tend to dissolve exactly the tension the story should preserve. If it helps in constructing the plot, feel free to let the story bear on whether the paradox is ultimately about assertion, about belief, or about self-knowledge, but the story itself should carry that weight, rather than a moral appended to it.
