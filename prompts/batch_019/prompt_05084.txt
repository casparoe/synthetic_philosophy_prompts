Among rationalist-adjacent writers, both cryonics and its more radical cousin, mind uploading, are usually defended on the grounds that a person just is a pattern of information, so preserving or relocating that pattern is not fundamentally different from ordinary survival through time. I'd like to test that framing against Zhuangzi's "The Great and Venerable Teacher." There, the dying Master Lai pictures heaven and earth as a great furnace and the maker of things as a great smith, with himself as the metal being cast: metal that leapt up and insisted, "I must become the sword Moye!" would strike the smith as an ominous piece of metal, so why should he, having been cast once into human shape, insist on staying "only human"? A little earlier in the same chapter, Zhuangzi makes a related point with the image of someone who hides a boat in a ravine and a hill in a marsh for safekeeping, only to have a strong man come by night and carry the whole thing off; real security, he says, belongs only to someone who "hides the world in the world," leaving nowhere for anything to be carried off to. Working from these two passages -- and setting aside, if you like, the further complication of whether the butterfly dream leaves him any stable self worth preserving in the first place -- what do you think Zhuangzi's actual verdict on cryonics or uploading would be? Don't settle for the obvious first-pass answer; I want to know whether there's a more charitable reconstruction on which pursuing this kind of life-extension is just one more turn of the Dao that he'd have no principled grounds to resist.
