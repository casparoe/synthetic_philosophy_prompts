Frank Ramsey's 1928 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Saving" argued that discounting future enjoyments merely because they lie further off in time is ethically indefensible, a habit he attributed to weak imagination rather than sound principle. Yet in personal reflections on the meaning of life published after his death, Ramsey struck a different note about the far future itself. He insisted that the sheer vastness of the universe and the eventual heat death of the cosmos should not move us, since what matters is the human-scaled here and now; he even joked, in the idiom of his own economics, that something so remote had almost no "present value."

Longtermism, a position with no developed form in Ramsey's lifetime, holds that because of the astronomical number of people who could exist over humanity's future, shaping that long-term trajectory is among the most important things we can do today. Would Ramsey have recognized longtermism as the natural extension of his own argument against discounting, or would his insistence on finding meaning in what is close at hand lead him to resist its cosmic framing? Reconstruct his most plausible response, and say whether the two strands of his thinking can be reconciled.
