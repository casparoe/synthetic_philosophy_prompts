For nearly forty years my research concerned the comparative physiology of the vertebrate heart — how the same basic pump gets reorganized, chamber by chamber and shunt by shunt, from fish through crocodilians to mammals — and in all that time I used the word "function" the way every physiologist does, without a second thought. Since retiring I have been reading my way through the philosophy of biology, and I have become stuck on a long-running dispute in that literature. On one side are etiological (or "selected-effects") accounts of biological function, associated with Larry Wright and later sharpened by Karen Neander and Ruth Millikan; on the other are causal-role accounts in the tradition Robert Cummins set out, on which a trait's function is simply whatever it contributes to some capacity of a containing system that we, as investigators, have chosen to analyze.

I follow the two slogans well enough, but I cannot work out on my own how much daylight actually separates them once hard cases are on the table, and I would like you to compare the two accounts by working through several of these rather than simply restating the standard formulations. First, consider malfunction and disease: a heart with a stenotic valve still seems to have pumping blood as its function even while performing that job badly, and the causal-role view is routinely said to struggle here, since a token that fails to contribute to the relevant capacity looks, on a naive reading, as though it simply lacks the function; I would like to know whether the etiological view genuinely does better, or whether it merely relocates the problem onto facts about selection history that are often no easier to establish, which brings to mind the old "just-so story" worry about adaptationist reasoning. Second, consider vestigial and co-opted structures, where a feature either arose as a developmental by-product with no selected effect at all, or was selected for one effect and is now put to some unrelated current use; these strike me as the decisive wedge cases between the two camps, and I would like to see them actually adjudicated rather than merely gestured at. Third, and this is as much a check on my own memory as anything else: I recall an argument, I believe due to Amundson and Lauder, that working comparative morphologists lean on causal-role notions in practice precisely because assigning an etiological function commits one to a selective history that has not yet been established; if that is roughly right, does it count against the etiological account as a general theory of biological function, or only against invoking it too early in an investigation?

Please close with your own considered judgment: should one of these two notions be treated as fundamental, with the other reducible to or parasitic on it, or does biology genuinely need both concepts for different explanatory purposes? And if it is the latter, I would be curious which of my own two habits as a physiologist — diagnosing a heart that is failing, and explaining one that is currently working well — was quietly etiological thinking all along, and which was quietly causal-role thinking.
