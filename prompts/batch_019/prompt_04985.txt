So I'm TAing a seminar on self-knowledge and higher-order evidence this term, and grading a stack of papers on the Enkratic Principle has left me a little obsessed with a case I can't quite shake. Everyone in this literature (Christensen, Horowitz, Worsnip, Lasonen-Aarnio, etc.) frames the debate as being about how much weight misleading higher-order evidence should get relative to your first-order evidence, using cases like the reasoning-impairing pill: you might've taken a drug that messes with your logic, and the question is whether it's akratic (or even required) to keep your confident first-order belief anyway once you learn that.

But it occured to me that you can build a nastier version by making the higher-order evidence self-undermining -- i.e., evidence that specifically calls into question your ability to reason well about higher-order evidence itself, not just about the first-order domain. Once you do that, it starts to look like there's no doxastic state available to the agent -- full confidence, suspension, some in-between credence, any split-level combo -- that isn't akratic with respect to *some* part of their total evidence. I floated this to a couple people in my cohort over beers and got pretty mixed reactions, but my hunch is that it shows the Enkratic Principle can't be an exceptionless requirement of ideal rationality, just a norm that sometimes gets outweighed, or that is sometimes strictly unsatisfiable.

I'd like you to actually argue for that stronger claim rather than just gesture at the tension. Concretely: (1) construct a case with this self-undermining structure (something in the spirit of the reasoning-impairing-pill scenarios, but tweaked so the "pill" also degrades the subject's capacity to assess whether they've been dosed) -- feel free to invent the details; (2) go through the main candidate doxastic responses an agent could occupy and show why each one leaves them akratic relative to some portion of their evidence; and (3) use that to actually defend the conclusion that the Enkratic Principle fails as a strict, general requirement, rather than just noting the puzzle and punting on it. If you can, situate the argument relative to the more "anti-akrasia" camp (Horowitz and co.) versus the more permissive camp that thinks structural/coherence requirements can come apart from substantive correctness (Worsnip, or steadfast-ish views like Lasonen-Aarnio's) -- does the self-undermining case actually help the permissive side, or is there some clever move available to anti-akrasia theorists that I'm missing?
