This is a total side-quest from novel research, but I just read Misak's biography of Frank Ramsey and now I've got a character loosly based on him -- alternate history where he doesn't die at 26 and gets to watch his ideas play out over the next fifty years. I'm stuck on how he'd react to David Lewis's 1976 triviality results about conditionals: the proof that you basically can't treat an indicative conditional as an ordinary truth-valued proposition while also having its probability always match the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, except in degenerate cases. Which is awkward, because that whole debate grew directly out of Ramsey's own footnote in "General Propositions and Causality" -- the one that inspired both Adams's thesis and Stalnaker's semantics. So once the triviality results are on the table, does Ramsey go the Adams/Edgington route and just deny conditionals have truth values, or does he dig in and try to save the propositional picture? He was already kind of cagey about whether general propositions and laws are truth-apt in that same essay, so my hunch is it's not a coinflip, but I'd want it grounded in what he actually argued rather than just vibes about his philosphy generally.
