In *De Senectute*, Cicero has Cato the Elder meet the standard complaint that old age is undesirable because it lies so close to death by arguing that a death arriving in its natural season is as fitting as ripe fruit dropping from the tree, unlike the violence of fruit torn away green. Yet Cato also rejects the companion complaint that age necessarily saps body and mind alike, pointing to men whose faculties never dimmed — Sophocles still composing tragedy in deep old age, Cato himself taking up Greek literature after eighty — and insisting that much of what passes for the decay of old age is really the fruit of a badly spent life rather than of age as such.

Suppose Cicero learned of contemporary biomedical research aimed at arresting or even reversing bodily aging, so that the physical decline he treats as old age's one genuine hardship might eventually be engineered away for whoever could afford it. Drawing on the specific arguments Cato makes against each of the standard complaints, work out whether Cicero would welcome such research as finally delivering on his own claim that decline is not truly necessary, or would instead diagnose, in the very project of defeating aging, the same fault he condemns elsewhere in those who resent nature's fixed term for a human life. Does your answer change depending on whether the years such technology buys are actually spent on the pursuits — public counsel, study, farming — that he says alone make old age worth having?
