Quick one -- I've been falling down a David Hume rabbit hole for a piece on old economic ideas vs. modern ones. Turns out the causation-and-induction guy also wrote a ton on money, interest, and public debt back in the 1750s, stuff way more in the weeds than anything in the Treatise, and almost nobody talks about it. He had this theory that money is basically a useful fiction that facilitates trade rather then actual wealth, plus a genuinely alarmist take on public credit -- something like, a nation eventually has to choose between destroying its own debt or being destroyed by it. So here's what I can't stop thinking about: what would Hume make of Modern Monetary Theory? MMT's whole pitch is that a government issuing its own currency isn't budget-constrained like a household, and inflation (not solvency) is the only real limit on how much it can spend or "print." Would he see that as basically vindicating his own theory of money, or as exactly the credit-fueled delusion his public-debt essays were warning about? Don't just say "he'd have been a fiscal hawk" -- actually get into the mechanics of how he thought money and credit worked.
