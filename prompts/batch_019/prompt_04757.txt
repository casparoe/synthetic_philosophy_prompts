I'm on my company's "Architecture Review Board" (ARB) -- six staff/principal engineers who get final sign-off on any big, hard-to-reverse technical decision, like which of two competing internal frameworks becomes the company standard, or whether we migrate off our aging message queue. Lately theres a push, backed by two ARB members themselves, to scrap this setup for a "Technical Referendum": for any major either/or technical call, open it up to a binding, anonymous, company-wide vote among all ~450 engineers, simple majority wins. At the last all-hands someone put up a slide with actual napkin math to back this up: assume each engineer only has like a 55% shot at independently picking the "right" answer, and the probability that a majority of a few hundred of them still land on the right answer climbs into the high 90s. That slide basically won the room overnight -- it turned the debate from "do we trust six people or the whole company" into "the math says a big vote wins," and it's going up for a real vote at the offsite next month.

I'm skeptical, but I don't want to just say "trust the experts," because honestly the ARB has made some bad, insular calls before -- there's a running joke that ARB actually stands for "always rubber-stamps [some team]'s favorite tool." My gut says the slide's math is quietly smuggling in some assumptions that don't hold up here -- like, nobody's guess is really "independent" when everyone hashes it out in the same week-long Slack thread before voting, and I doubt "55% accurate" means the same thing for a call about our Kafka setup as it does for a call about a frontend build tool that most people have zero context on. But I can't fully work out whether that actually breaks the argument or just makes me feel better about being on the losing side of a popularity contest.

Could you write the memo I'd bring to that offsite? I need it to land on an actual recommendation -- keep the ARB as-is, go full-referendum, or some specific hybrid -- with a concrete plan for phasing it in (or not) over the next couple of quarters, not just "more discussion needed." And it has to genuinely engage with the "the math says big votes win" argument on its own terms, since that's what's actually persuading people right now, rather than dodge it by just asserting that small groups of experts are obviously better.
