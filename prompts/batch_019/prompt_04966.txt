Quick one for you, been chewing on this for a piece on mathematical platonism. Cantor spent a huge chunk of his later career convinced the continuum hypothesis was true and trying (and failing) to prove it, and he was enough of a realist about the infinite that he actually ran his transfinite numbers past Catholic theologians to make sure he wasn't trespassing on Gods exclusive claim to the "Absolute Infinite" -- so this was a guy who clearly thought there was a real fact of the matter here, not just a formal game. Given that, what do you think Cantor would've made of Gödel and Cohens later result that CH is independent of ZFC, i.e. neither provable nor disprovable from the standard axioms? Would he treat that as evidence the question was somehow ill-posed, side with Gödel that we've just got the wrong/incomplete axioms and the "real" universe of sets still settles it one way, or -- given his own theological hang-ups about which bits of the infinite are even knowable -- end up oddly sympathetic to today's set-theoretic multiverse crowd, who say CH just doesn't have one true answer? Skip the textbook definition of independence, I want your actual best reconstruction of how Cantor personally would've reacted, and whether his own theology cuts for or against his mathematical realism.
