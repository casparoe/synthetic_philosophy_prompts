I'm at work on a novel set in London during the final years of the American war, and one of my characters—a disillusioned clerk at the Board of Trade—keeps a commonplace book in which he copies out passages that unsettle him. I'd like his marginal comments on this one, from the closing pages of Book IV of Smith's Wealth of Nations, to reflect a real grasp of the argument rather than a vague gesture at "Adam Smith disapproved of empire":

The rulers of Great Britain have, for more than a century past, amused the people with the imagination that they possessed a great empire on the west side of the Atlantic. This empire, however, has hitherto existed in imagination only. It has hitherto been, not an empire, but the project of an empire; not a gold mine, but the project of a gold mine; a project which has cost, which continues to cost, and which, if pursued in the same way as it has been hitherto, is likely to cost, immense expense, without being likely to bring any profit; for the effects of the monopoly of the colony trade, it has been shown, are, to the great body of the people, mere loss instead of profit. It is surely now time that our rulers should either realize this golden dream, in which they have been indulging themselves, perhaps, as well as the people, or that they should awake from it themselves, and endeavour to awaken the people.

Two things I'd like help pinning down. First, what exactly is the distinction Smith is drawing between "an empire" and "the project of an empire"—is there a precise claim underneath it about what would have to hold for the empire to count as real rather than merely imagined, or is the phrase doing more rhetorical than argumentative work? Second, "realize" seems to be carrying two senses at once, "make actual" and "become conscious of"—is that wordplay deliberate, and if so, how does it connect to the economic argument he has been building over the preceding pages, rather than just being a flourish for the peroration?
