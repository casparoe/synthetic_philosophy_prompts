Over a long career as an ethologist, I always dismissed Descartes' claim that animals are mere automata as a crude prejudice; only in retirement, reading the Discourse on Method closely, have I come to appreciate how precise his actual argument is. In Part V he gives two specific tests for telling a genuine thinking being from any machine that might resemble one outwardly: first, that no machine could arrange words to reply appropriately to whatever is said to it, as even the dullest human can, though it might be built to emit fixed phrases triggered by particular mechanical operations; second, that a machine would inevitably fail at some tasks because it acts only from the fixed disposition of its parts rather than from reason, which he calls a "universal instrument" serviceable in any circumstance. He then applies both tests to argue that animals, however cleverly they behave, do not truly think. Reasoning strictly from these two tests rather than from any modern gloss on his views, how do you think Descartes would judge a large language model: would its fluent, context-appropriate conversation satisfy his first test, and would its characteristic failures - hallucination, brittleness under unusual phrasing - confirm his second test, or expose a limit in it?
