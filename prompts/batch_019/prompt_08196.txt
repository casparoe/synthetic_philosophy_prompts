I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but for the past year or so I've been reading everything I can find on population ethics, mostly starting from effective altruism debates about the "repugnant conclusion." While trying to trace the idea back further than Parfit, I came across this passage in Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics (first published 1874), from the chapter on the utilitarian view of population, written well over a century before Parfit ever used that phrase:

"...the point up to which, on Utilitarian principles, population ought to be encouraged to increase, is not that at which the average happiness is the greatest possible — as many Utilitarians have assumed — but that at which the produce formed by multiplying the number of persons living into the amount of average happiness reaches its maximum."

I can follow the grammar, but I'm stuck on what exactly Sidgwick is licensing here. Is he saying we should keep adding people even as average happiness falls, so long as that "produce" (I take it he means product) keeps rising — which sounds like precisely the reasoning that gets you to the repugnant conclusion? Or is there a narrower claim buried in that sentence that later summaries have flattened out? And does Sidgwick show any sign, in this passage or elsewhere in the chapter, of being troubled by where this leads, or does he just state it as a clean corollary of the utilitarian calculus and move on? I'd like help walking through the sentence itself and then placing it honestly in the history of the repugnant conclusion, rather than just being told it's the same argument as Parfit's without being shown why.
