So I fell down a rabbit hole this weekend after a coworker sent round a HN thread about Godel's incompleteness theorems, and one of the top comments went off on a tangent about "Skolem's paradox" as this other wierd thing set theorists apparently just have to live with. I spent way too long reading about it on wikipedia and a couple of stackexchange answers, and I think I basically get the setup now: if ZFC is consistent then by the Lowenheim-Skolem theorem it has a countable model, but ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem, that there's no bijection between the naturals and the reals (or the powerset of the naturals). So you get this situation where the model contains some set that the model itself thinks is uncountable, even though the model as a whole, viewed from outside, only has countably many elements total, so that "uncountable" set can't actually have more elements than the naturals do. The resolution I've pieced together is that the bijection which would prove that set is countable exists mathematically, it just isn't one of the sets that lives inside that particular model, so "uncountable" turns out to be relative to the model you're in rather than an absolute property.

Trying to actually pin this down for myself instead of just nodding along, I wrote a little analogy using stuff from my day job, and I want to know if it actually holds up or if I'm just pattern-matching to something familiar and fooling myself. Here it is: imagine a client that only ever gets to interact with a database through a fixed set of permitted views and queries. From inside that client session there might be no permitted query that joins some table against the integers table row by row, so relative to that session the table looks "unenumerable." An admin with raw filesystem access to the underlying storage can point at a perfectly good row-by-row enumeration sitting right there on disk, it's just not reachable through any query the client's session is allowed to run. So being "countable" isn't an intrinsic property of the table, its a property relative to which enumerations your current session has access to. And then the part that's been bugging me: doesn't this basically mean cardinality above the finite numbers is sort of made up, or at least entirely observer-dependent, kind of like how two files can look like "the same size" or "wildly different sizes" depending on what compression scheme you're allowed to measure them with?

Can you poke holes in this for me? Specifically: (1) is my explanation of why the paradox happens actually technically right, or have I fudged something important; (2) does the database-session analogy really track the underlying logical structure, or is there something about it that's secretly misleading (like, is "permission-restricted but still there somewhere" actually the right way to think about why the bijection is missing from the model, or is that importantly different from what's really going on); and (3) is "cardinality is basically observer-dependent fiction" a reasonable thing to take away from this, or is that the kind of overreach that people who actually work in logic/set theory would wince at. I'm not looking for a rewrite of the analogy, just an honest gut check on where it's solid and where it's shaky.
