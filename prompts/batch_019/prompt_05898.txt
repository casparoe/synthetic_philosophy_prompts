I'm putting together a graduate seminar on Leibniz for next term and, instead of actually writing the syllabus, I fell down a rabbit hole last night reading about this decision-theory idea called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) that's been floating around rationalist/EA-adjacent circles for a while now (Caspar Oesterheld's name keeps coming up in connection with it, I think he wrote the original piece on "multiverse-wide cooperation"). It's been rattling around in my head since, and I want to see what you make of it.

Quick and dirty version of ECL, for anyone who hasnt run into it: suppose the universe, or multiverse, is vast enough - maybe infinite - that there are huge numbers of decision-making agents out there who reason in ways similar to you but hold totally different values, and whom you can never causally contact or influence in any way, not even in principle. Because your decision procedure and theirs are correlated - not causally linked, just correlated, the way two seperately-run calculators executing the same program give matching outputs without talking to each other - the idea is that you should treat your own choice to act cooperatively toward value systems unlike your own as evidence that similarly-reasoning agents elsewhere are, right now, choosing to act cooperatively toward values like yours. It's basically Hofstadter-style superrationality scaled up to a whole multiverse of mutually inaccessible agents: no contact, no enforcement, no communication, just correlated dispositions, and yet (allegedly) everyone ends up doing better by their own lights than under pure local selfishness.

Here's what I can't shake: this rhymes awfully hard with Leibniz's monads - causally sealed ("windowless") substances whose internal states nevertheless mesh perfectly with the rest of creation, not through interaction but through a harmony fixed in advance. Obviously his mechanism for that harmony is God, front-loading the coordination at the moment of creation like two clocks built once to agree forever, rather than anything like correlated reasoning among the substances themselves - and his system is also weirdly committed to there being exactly one actual world, the uniquely best compossible one, rather than a genuinely existing plurality of causally walled-off worlds sitting side by side. So don't just hand me the cute surface parallel - actually use what you know of his real commitments (pre-established harmony versus occasionalism versus brute interaction, the principle of sufficient reason, compossibility, his optimism about this being the best of all possible worlds) to work out what his considered reaction to ECL would actually be. Would he recognize it as agents groping their way toward a secularized, DIY version of his own harmony, minus the deity needed to guarantee it actually holds? Or does his own framework give him solid grounds to reject the whole setup as resting on a kind of modal confusion? Give me a real verdict, not a "well, it's complicated," and show me the Leibnizian reasoning that gets you there.
