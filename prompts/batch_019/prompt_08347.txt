As a software engineer with an amateur interest in philosophy of science, there's a connection I keep coming back to that I've never seen spelled out anywhere. Hume's core argument was that we never perceive necessary connections in nature, only constant conjunction -- an idea that, generations later, hardened into the Mill-Ramsey-Lewis "best system" account of laws: a regularity counts as a law of nature just when it's a theorem of whichever true deductive systematization of the world's particular facts best balances simplicity against strength. That formulation sounds, to me, a lot like the minimum description length principle from computer science: laws as whatever a maximally short "compressor" of the universe's data yields as axioms. Hume died roughly two centuries before Kolmogorov, Solomonoff, and Chaitin made that idea rigorous, and before Lewis built it into his metaphysics of laws. A standard objection to the best system account, often pressed by anti-Humeans, is that "simplicity" is objectionably relative to an arbitrary choice of language, a worry serious enough that Lewis tried to fix it with his theory of "perfectly natural properties"; that same worry is mirrored almost exactly by the fact that Kolmogorov complexity is only well-defined up to an additive constant depending on the choice of universal machine. Had Hume lived to see this, would he have welcomed algorithmic information theory as finally cashing out "simplicity" in properly empiricist terms, or recognized in it the same unexplained primitive he spent his career trying to banish, just relocated into the choice of machine? Please reconstruct the reasoning you think he would actually give, engaging with the technical substance rather than restating his general empiricism.
