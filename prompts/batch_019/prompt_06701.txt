I chair my department's academic-integrity panel, which this year has mostly been adjudicating suspected cases of AI-assisted essay writing. Lacking any decisive test, we've settled into a heuristic of confirmation by absence: when an essay lacks the markers we associate with generated text (tonal flatness, suspiciously even coverage, vocabulary beyond the student's earlier work), we count that absence as evidence the essay is the student's own. One panel member, trained in philosophy of science, remarked over coffee that this is just Hempel's raven paradox in administrative dress: an essay lacking those markers is our non-black non-raven, and we're letting a vast, largely uninformative class of negative instances pass for evidence. She didn't stay to defend the point, so I'd like a more rigorous treatment than she gave it. Please describe the most important implications of Hempel's raven paradox, and of the standard responses to it, for how a committee like mine should actually weigh an 'absence of red flags' as evidence of a student's authorship.
