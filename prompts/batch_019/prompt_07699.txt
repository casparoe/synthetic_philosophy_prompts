I'm a novelist working on a campus novel set in a philosophy department, and there's a scene I keep botching. Two of my characters — Ines, a junior faculty member who has come to think that trusting someone else's moral judgment is, epistemically speaking, no different from trusting a doctor's diagnosis or a mechanic's verdict on your car, and Teodor, her old graduate-school rival, now on the faculty elsewhere and back in town to give a colloquium talk, who has spent his career arguing that something goes badly wrong whenever a person simply takes another's word for what is right or wrong — run into each other at the department's wine-and-cheese reception afterward and end up re-litigating an argument they have clearly had many times before. Neither of them should come across as a strawman: they have read the same literature, they respect each other, and the disagreement is genuinely hard to settle.

Could you draft their exchange? Have Ines press the "no relevant asymmetry" line: if we happily defer to experts about chemistry, history, or our own medical conditions without independently re-deriving their verdicts, and there's no obvious reason to think moral facts are less tractable to expertise than those other domains, why should ethics be treated any differently? Teodor shouldn't just answer "because ethics is personal." Instead, have him push on whether testimony can transmit the kind of understanding — a grasp of the relevant reasons, the ability to extend a verdict to new and harder cases, something like the sensitivity that seems bound up with actually caring about the right thing — that a merely true belief doesn't guarantee; this is roughly the line the philosopher Alison Hills has taken, and I'd like Ines to have a real answer to it rather than simply concede the point. At some point I'd like the two of them to test their positions against a concrete case: someone who becomes a committed vegetarian purely because a partner they trust told them eating meat is wrong, without ever working out for themselves what, if anything, is wrong with it. Have Ines and Teodor disagree about whether there is anything to criticize in that person's belief, and, if so, whether the defect is epistemic, moral, or something else again. Give the scene room for genuine back-and-forth — at least a dozen exchanges total, each of them responding to what the other has actually just said rather than to a caricature of it — and end it without a clean winner; I would rather they arrive at a sharper sense of exactly what they still disagree about than at a tidy resolution.
