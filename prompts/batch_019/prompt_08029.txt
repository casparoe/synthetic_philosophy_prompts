I cover science and ideas for a living, and a few years ago I profiled a close friend's research on Confucian role ethics for a magazine piece: the view, developed by Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, that a person just is their particular relationships -- parent, spouse, friend, colleague -- rather than some free-standing self that merely enters into relationships from the outside. She is getting married next month, and I have been asked to give one of the toasts. Roughly half the room will be her fellow philosophers, who will notice immediately if I reach for the standard toast move of urging the couple to "never lose who you are as individuals" -- precisely the picture of selfhood her work has spent a decade dismantling. The other half is family who have never heard of li or zhengming and just want to be moved. Her fiancé, who is not a philosopher, has more than once told her, quite sincerely, that all he wants is for her to "be herself" -- a phrase she finds quietly hilarious given everything her career has been built to argue against. Write the toast: about five minutes long, faithful to what her philosophy actually implies about marriage -- including what it implies about her new in-laws and both families, not just the couple -- and built around one genuine laugh line drawn from the gap between "be yourself" and role ethics, so that the room hears it as love for her, not a lecture about her.
