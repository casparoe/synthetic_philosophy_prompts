I've been in a little Quine reading group and we just got through the gavagai stuff in Word and Object, but honestly the part that's got us actually arguing isn't the original thought experiment, it's a side-debate we stumbled into afterwards. Apparently Chomsky's line against Quine (I think its from "Quine's Empirical Assumptions," though don't quote me on the title) was that indeterminacy of translation isn't some special new phenomenon, it's just ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence — the same Duhem-Quine problem you get anywhere in science, just relabeled for linguistics. And Quine's comeback (somewhere in his reply to Chomsky, or maybe it's the "Reasons for Indeterminacy" paper, my notes are a mess) was that there's a real difference: with rival physical theories we still assume there's some fact of the matter about which one is true even if we can never know it, whereas with rival manuals translating "gavagai" as rabbit vs. undetached rabbit parts vs. rabbit stages, there's no fact of the matter at all, even once you fix literally every disposition to observable behavior there could ever be. Our group is split on whether that's a real, principled distinction or just Quine smuggling in a metaphor to make the same old underdetermination sound scarier than it is.
