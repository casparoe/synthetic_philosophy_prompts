A friend and I got into a long argument last night that started as idle office gossip and ended with the two of us nearly re-deriving probability theory at the kitchen table. Here's the situation: my company is in the middle of a reorganization, and two separate things are up in the air. First, we're apparently going to merge with one of two competitors — everyone has taken to calling them "the Boston firm" and "the Chicago firm" — and nobody has given us any reason to favor one over the other, so we've been treating it as a coin flip. Second, our team will either move to a new uptown office or stay in our current downtown space — a question we'd always assumed was completely unconnected to the merger decision, and which we'd likewise been treating as a coin flip. So before any news came in, all four combinations — Boston-and-uptown, Boston-and-downtown, Chicago-and-uptown, Chicago-and-downtown — seemed equally likely to us, 25 percent each.

Then my friend's manager let something slip in a hallway conversation that got cut short before she could finish: "if it ends up being the Boston firm, we're something like three times more likely to move uptown than to stay downtown." She got called away before saying a single word about what happens under the Chicago scenario. My friend's position is that this can only sensibly bear on the uptown/downtown split conditional on Boston — since the tip was, in his words, "purely about the Boston branch," our odds on Boston versus Chicago have to stay exactly fifty-fifty, with only the internal split within Boston moving from fifty-fifty to seventy-five/twenty-five. That was my gut reaction too. But I took a probability course a few years back that covered a rule for exactly this kind of situation — updating on an "if-then" claim rather than on a flat statement of fact — and when I carefully applied that rule, trying to disturb our original beliefs as little as possible while still honoring the new three-to-one ratio, I kept getting an answer on which Boston becomes very slightly less likely than Chicago, not fifty-fifty at all. He's convinced I'm just butchering the arithmetic, since he finds it obvious that a statement "about" the Boston branch cannot possibly tell us anything about Boston versus Chicago itself. At this point neither of us trusts our own intuition anymore. Is there a fact of the matter about which of us is right, and if it really is the unintuitive answer, what would justify it?
