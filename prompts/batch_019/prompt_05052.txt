Last month I sat in on a decision-theory workshop for a piece I'm reporting, and after the formal talks ended, two of the attendees kept arguing over drinks about the old "Death in Damascus" puzzle for causal decision theory (CDT). Briefly, since it doesn't get the airtime that Newcomb's problem does: a man in Damascus is told that Death, a highly reliable predictor, will come for him tomorrow in whichever of two cities — Damascus or Aleppo — Death has already predicted he will be in. The man wants to be wherever Death is not. Because Death predicts so well, whichever city the man settles on choosing immediately looks like the more dangerous one, precisely because a good predictor would have anticipated that very choice; so neither staying nor fleeing is a stable, self-justifying ("ratifiable") recommendation, and CDT seems to have no definite verdict to give.

Here is roughly how the argument went (paraphrased from notes, not verbatim):

1. Renata opened by saying the puzzle shows CDT is simply broken: a decision theory that cannot settle on any stable recommendation isn't a theory of rational choice at all, whatever technical patches its defenders offer.

2. Dev replied that this is too quick, since CDT was never restricted to pure acts. Once the agent is allowed to randomize, there is a 50/50 mixture that is stable: choosing to flip a coin gives Death's predicted location no reason to shift, so the mixed act maximizes expected utility conditional on being chosen — which is just what "ratifiable" means. CDT does deliver a definite verdict; it's just not a pure one.

3. Renata countered that this fix only works if Death cannot predict the outcome of the coin flip itself. If Death is really the reliable predictor the story stipulates, there is no principled reason to exempt the randomizing device from that reliability, and if Death can predict the coin too, the mixed act is exactly as unratifiable as either pure act. Dev's move doesn't dissolve the paradox, she said; it just relocates it one level up.

4. Dev responded that every such puzzle has to fix some causal ordering by stipulation — Newcomb's problem itself only works because the predictor is stipulated to move first and to be insensitive to whatever the agent does afterward. Specifying that Death's prediction is likewise fixed prior to, and independent of, any later randomizing device isn't an ad hoc rescue of CDT; it's just filling in the case the way Gibbard and Harper intended, and on that reading the mixed strategy really is stable.

Neither of them landed a clean knockout, and I couldn't tell who was closer to right or exactly where the other's reasoning slipped. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
