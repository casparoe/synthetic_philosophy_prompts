I'm reporting a piece on why experts and laypeople alike so often dig in rather than update when confronted with new evidence, and a philosopher I consulted pointed me to the "dogmatism paradox" associated with Saul Kripke and Gilbert Harman. As I understand the argument, if I genuinely know some claim to be true, I thereby already know that any evidence which might later surface against it would have to be misleading; and since I'm rationally entitled to disregard evidence I know in advance to be misleading, simply possessing knowledge today licenses me to wave away tomorrow's contrary evidence without even examining it. That looks like a rigorous, logically airtight justification for intellectual stubbornness: the more solid a piece of knowledge is, the more rational it becomes to ignore whatever data might later contradict it. For the piece, could you unpack why this argument establishes that dogmatism is sometimes the epistemically correct response to future counter-evidence, and what it implies about how much credence readers should give experts who insist they've already accounted for objections still to come?
