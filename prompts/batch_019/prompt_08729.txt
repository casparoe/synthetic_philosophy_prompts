I help run a tiny volunteer fund in my town — six of us on a group chat basically — that gives neighbors one-time grants (a couple hundred bucks up to $1000ish) to cover back rent or a utility bill before it turns into a shutoff or eviction notice. We started out just winging it, but we get more applications every month than we have money for, and it's turned into a real fight about how we should decide who gets funded, so we finally have to write an actual policy down.

Right now, roughly half of us process applications in the order they come in and approve the first ones that clearly meet our basic bar (real shutoff/eviction notice, amount asked for is reasonable, hasn't gotten money from us in the last 6 months), until that month's pot is gone — so somebody who applies on the 3rd and obviously qualifies gets the money even if somebody worse off applies on the 8th, after the moneys already spent. The other half want us to hold every application for two weeks, score them all on a need rubric, and fund strictly worst-off-first. The first group's argument is that making someone with a shutoff notice wait two extra weeks so we can feel like we ranked everybody fairly is its own kind of cruelty, and that the rubric scores are close enough for most people that the "ranking" is mostly noise anyway. The second group's argument, which I honestly can't out-argue, is that if there's somebody visibly worse off sitting in the queue and the money hasn't gone out the door yet, "we already found somebody good enough" isn't a real reason not to help them instead — it's just us stopping early because stopping is easier. We also can't agree on whether the "clearly qualifies" bar itself should slide up or down depending on how many strong applications come in a given month, or whether that's just us moving the goalposts whenever it's convenient.

After the last meeting I fell into a bit of an internet hole reading about "satisficing" — started with a pop psychology book about maximizers vs satisficers, then some older economist guy (Herbert Simon?) on why organizations basically can't optimize and just settle for good-enough, then a philosopher — Slote, I think? — apparently arguing that settling for good-enough isn't just a fallback for when you're out of time, it can be the actually correct call. I don't fully know what to do with any of that, but I'd like to bring something better than vibes to our meeting in two weeks, where we're voting on making this official policy. Could you write the memo I'd send around beforehand? I want it to actually commit to one policy instead of splitting the difference, lay out the reasoning tight enough that people can push back on specific pieces of it, and genuinely deal with the "there's a worse-off person right there" objection instead of brushing past it.
