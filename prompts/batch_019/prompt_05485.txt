My friend Priya from my PhD cohort is getting married in a few weeks and asked me to give a toast, which is a little terrifying because she's finishing a dissertation on behavioral contract law and her fiancé Marcus is a public defender, so the wedding planning has apparently involved actual clause-drafting. The detail I can't get over: instead of normal vows they've written what they call (their words) a "Ulysses clause" — each of them has pre-authorized the other to invoke a mandatory cooling-off period and mediation before either one is allowed to bring up divorce mid-fight, and the clause can only be amended later if they both agree to it while explicitly NOT in the middle of a fight. It's basically a tiny constitution with it's own amendment procedure, and I think it's very funny, but also, because I'm a philosophy PhD student who has spent the last year buried in Elster and Parfit for my own dissertation on political obligation, I can't stop thinking about how this is a real-life version of the exact puzzle I work on.

Here's the thing that's been bugging me and that I want the toast to actually get at: a Ulysses contract is supposed to be your rational, sober self binding your future, weaker self for your own good, the way Odysseus has his men tie him to the mast. But the second you take seriously Parfit's point that personal identity over time is just psychological continuity coming in degrees, your 2am-fighting self isn't obviously "the same person," in the sense that matters, as your wedding-day self — which means the vow starts looking less like Odysseus and more like one generation writing a constitution that binds a future generation which never agreed to any of it. That's the exact "dead hand of the past" objection people raise against constitutions (Jefferson going on to Madison about how no generation has the right to bind the next one), and I don't think it's obviously wrong just because the "generations" here are two hours apart instead of two hundred years.

I want to actually use this in the toast instead of just being a smartass about it, but it also has to function as a real wedding toast: half the room is grad school and law school people who will catch every reference, and the other half is both sets of parents and assorted relatives who will just be listening for "nice things about the happy couple" and would be legitimately alarmed if I seemed to be building toward "and that's why your marriage may not be legitimate." Could you write me something like a five-minute toast that uses the Ulysses contract/constitutional precommitment idea as its actual spine, including the personal-identity wrinkle, but that resolves the tension into something warm and genuinely moving rather than landing as a philosophy lecture with "congrats you two" bolted onto the end?
