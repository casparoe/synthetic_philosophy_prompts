I spent part of last week at a small workshop on algorithmic information theory, reporting a piece on why "simplicity" remains such a slippery virtue in science. At the dinner afterward, I watched two of the invited speakers — a theoretical computer scientist and a philosopher of science — fall into a genuine argument about whether Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity actually rescue Occam's razor from the old charge that "simplicity" is just an artifact of whatever description language you happen to be using.

1. She opened by noting that although Kolmogorov complexity is defined relative to a choice of universal machine, the invariance theorem guarantees that any two universal machines assign a given string complexities differing by at most a fixed constant that doesn't depend on the string — so, she said, the worry that simplicity is merely a fact about our chosen language is essentially dissolved.

2. He answered that "fixed" only means the constant doesn't grow with the string, not that it's small: for the finite, modestly sized hypotheses scientists actually compare, that constant can dwarf the real differences in complexity, so two perfectly reasonable universal machines can rank the same hypotheses in opposite order of simplicity. Nothing has been rescued for any case anyone would actually apply this to.

3. She replied that this only shows Solomonoff induction describes an idealized reasoner with unlimited data rather than a working algorithm for finite science, and that any two sensible description languages will eventually agree, since a fixed prior gets swamped by the data as more of it arrives.

4. He said that's the sleight of hand: Solomonoff's convergence theorem earns that result only by assuming the true process is computable and already carries positive weight under the universal prior — so "they'll eventually agree" just re-imports, as an unexamined assumption, the very bias toward short programs that Occam's razor was supposed to earn on independent grounds.
