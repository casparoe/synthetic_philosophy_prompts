I'm a backend engineer at a small startup that fine-tunes open-source language models, and for the last few months I've been sitting in on an internal reading group a couple of our researchers started on mechanistic interpretability — working through the Anthropic line of work on toy models of superposition, the sparse-autoencoder "monosemanticity" papers, and the causal-scrubbing-style methods people use to check whether a proposed "circuit" is actually doing the work it's credited with. The group keeps splitting on a question nobody wants to table: when a paper says some direction in activation space "represents" a concept — sycophancy, the Golden Gate Bridge, whatever — is that a discovery about the network, comparable to discovering that a compiler's symbol table represents variable bindings, or is it closer to modern phrenology, where you ran a dictionary-learning algorithm with a sparsity penalty and a dictionary size you chose yourself, pulled out some directions, and then reached for the most flattering English label? This isn't purely academic for me either: I've started using some of these learned features as steering vectors in a production system, and I'd like to know whether I'm holding a real causal handle on the model or just a label I've projected onto it.

I'd like an essay aimed at someone with my background — comfortable with linear algebra and with how these models are trained, but with no formal philosophy — that actually takes a side rather than leaving me at "reasonable people disagree." Lay out the strongest realist case for representations in neural networks: why polysemanticity and superposition initially looked like they would sink any naive "one neuron, one concept" reading, and why proponents think causal interventions on a claimed feature, rather than merely training a probe that can decode it, are what should license calling it a representation instead of a mere correlate. Then lay out the strongest deflationary case: the instability of learned feature dictionaries across training runs and hyperparameter choices, the "interpretability illusion" results where a probe-decodable property turns out not to be what the network is actually using, and the older complaint — going back to critiques of symbolic AI's habit of "wishful mnemonics" — that attaching a plain-English gloss to a mathematical object doesn't make the object about anything. Bring in philosophy of mind or philosophy of science if it genuinely earns its keep, but keep the argument tied to how these systems actually work, and end by telling me, and defending, whether — and under what conditions — I should believe neural networks really do have representations.
