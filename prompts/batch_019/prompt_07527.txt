So I'm about three months into a feature on the recidivism-risk algorithms that courts use for bail and sentencing decisions (the COMPAS-style tools), and last week I accidentally refereed an argument between two of my sources that I still can't resolve in my own head. One, Elena, spent a few years building risk models at a criminal-justice-tech company before moving into academia. The other, Marcus, is a public defender who's spent years trying to get these tools thrown out in his state. I'd looped them onto the same call for a fact-check and they basically forgot I was on the line, which is not really how I planned that call to go. I've relistened to the recording an embarrassing number of times since. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Marcus opened with the old ProPublica line on the COMPAS tool used in Broward County: Black defendants who never went on to reoffend were flagged "high risk" at a much higher rate than white defendants who never reoffended, so the tool is biased against Black defendants. (2) Elena shot back that this leaves out the part that actually matters to a judge using the score: among defendants who land in the same risk decile, the real-world reoffense rate ends up roughly the same regardless of race, so the score means the same thing for everybody - which is the standard she'd use to call an instrument unbiased in the first place. (3) Marcus said both things are true at once, and that's exactly his point, not a rebuttal: once two groups reoffend at different overall base rates for reasons that have nothing to do with the algorithm, its a mathematical fact that you cannot have matching calibration and matching false-positive/false-negative rates across those groups at the same time, so "the score means the same thing for everybody" isn't some special virtue of COMPAS, it's a guarantee of the exact racial gap he's complaining about. (4) Elena said that's precisely her point too: if the disparity is the forced consequence of a coherent, race-blind scoring rule plus a genuine base-rate difference, you can't get from there to "the company built something unfair" - any well-calibrated model on this data would show the identical gap, including one his own side might build. (5) Marcus answered that this is a dodge, since nobody's accusing anyone of malice - the accusation is that calibration was chosen as the target, deliberately, over matching error rates, and that choice, not fate, decides who absorbs the model's mistakes; a company that picked the other target would build something just as internally consistent and just as "objective" by its own lights, it would just spread the errors the other way. (6) Elena's comeback was that his preferred fix has the identical problem in reverse: forcing matching false-positive and false-negative rates across groups with different base rates necessarily breaks calibration, so a given score would systematically overstate risk for one group and understate it for the other - meaning his option isn't the neutral one hiding behind her political one, it's a different political choice that just happens not to produce a scary chart in a news story.

I've talked myself into a different winner about four separate times now, so I'd genuinely love an outside read, partly so I can characterize this fairly when I write it up.
