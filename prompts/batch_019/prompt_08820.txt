I am a software engineer who hosts a small podcast on artificial intelligence and philosophy of mind; I have no formal training in philosophy, but I am comfortable with Turing machines and the incompleteness theorems themselves. Next month's guest is a philosopher known for defending the Lucas–Penrose argument that Gödel's theorems show human mathematical understanding cannot be the output of any single algorithm. Her characteristic move in debate is that whenever someone proposes a specific formal system as a model of her own reasoning, she constructs that system's Gödel sentence, declares that she can see it is true, and treats this as refuting the proposal. Draft eight to ten interview questions that make this move progressively harder to fall back on, including at least one question each on: how she can be confident that her own reasoning is even consistent, given that the argument only succeeds if it is; whether recognizing a Gödel sentence as true requires already knowing the exact system attributed to her, which seems to presuppose the very self-knowledge in question; and whether her position requires Penrose's further step into non-computable physical processes in the brain, or whether it can remain purely logical. Close with a question asking what she would accept as evidence against her own view, and give each question a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.
