I write about the history and philosophy of science, and I'm drafting a column that pushes back against the standard "Galileo versus the Church" story. Below is the current draft. Before I send it to my editor, I'd like your comments on three things: whether the historical and theological claims are accurate, and whether I've overstated anything historians would consider contested; whether the central argument actually holds together rather than just sounding clever; and whether, in trying to complicate the heroic version of events, I've swung too far toward letting the Inquisition off the hook. Where you're genuinely unsure whether a claim is right, say so rather than smoothing over it.

Draft:

What Galileo Actually Argued About Scripture

The Galileo affair is usually told as a fable about science defeating dogma: a lone empiricist insists the earth moves, and the Church, reading Joshua and the Psalms literally, insists it does not. The trouble with this version is that it gets Galileo's own argument backwards. He was not asking Rome to set scripture aside in favor of nature. He was making a theological argument about how scripture should be read, one that leaned heavily on Augustine and that Rome's own theologians did not, in the abstract, reject.

In his 1613 letter to Castelli, and the longer 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, Galileo's central claim was that scripture's purpose is to teach salvation, not astronomy, and that it therefore speaks in the accommodated language of everyday experience — the sun "rises," the earth stands still — the way a parent simplifies for a child. He borrowed this almost wholesale from Augustine's commentary on Genesis, De Genesi ad Litteram, which warns Christians against making confident claims about the natural world that could later be shown false by more careful observers, to the faith's embarrassment. Galileo even repeats, admiringly, a quip he attributes to Cardinal Baronius: that the Holy Spirit's intention was to teach us how to reach heaven, not how the heavens move.

None of this was heterodox. Cardinal Bellarmine, in his own 1615 letter to the Carmelite Paolo Foscarini, granted the identical principle: if heliocentrism were ever truly demonstrated, the Church would have to revisit its reading of the relevant Psalms rather than deny a proven fact. His dispute with Galileo was not over that principle but over whether Copernican astronomy had cleared the bar of "true demonstration," or remained what Andreas Osiander's unsigned preface to Copernicus's 1543 De Revolutionibus had already proposed: a convenient fiction for predicting planetary positions, adopted whatever one believed about the real arrangement of the heavens. Bellarmine bet no demonstration was coming; Galileo bet that one already existed or soon would. The 1616 Index ruling that eventually followed leaned on the Council of Trent's rule against reading scripture contrary to the unanimous consent of the Fathers — a rule Trent had aimed at faith and morals, and whose extension to a question of planetary motion is itself a debated leap.

Here the story turns less flattering to Galileo than the fable allows. His own best candidate for the demonstration he insisted was available — the theory of tides he lays out at length in the 1632 Dialogue, attributing them to the sloshing produced by the earth's combined rotation and revolution — is simply wrong; tides are driven by the moon and sun's gravity, a mechanism unavailable to him. The first real observational evidence for the earth's orbital motion, James Bradley's discovery of stellar aberration, did not arrive until the 1720s; the first measured stellar parallax came from Friedrich Bessel in 1838, nearly two centuries after Galileo's death. He was right, but he did not have, and could not have had, the demonstration he insisted Bellarmine was obligated to accept.

So the real disagreement in 1615 was not science against faith. It was a quarrel, conducted inside a shared hermeneutic, over what counts as a demonstration in natural philosophy, and over whether a mathematically successful model may be asserted as physically true or only used as a calculating device — a version of the argument between realism and instrumentalism that philosophy of science was still having three centuries later. None of this makes the 1616 ruling, or Galileo's 1633 trial, defensible. It does mean the standard morality tale flatters both the science and the certainty of the man usually cast as its hero.

Please be concrete rather than diplomatic: I'd rather have three sharp objections than a paragraph of praise.
