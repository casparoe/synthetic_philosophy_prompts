My book club just finished Frederick Douglass's "My Bondage and My Freedom" and the last meeting turned into a two hour argument that never actually got resolved, so now that I'm hosting next month's session I want to walk in with an actual answer instead of just refereeing chaos again. Half the group had no idea that Douglass spent his early career as a hardcore Garrisonian -- Constitution's "a covenant with death," dont vote, dont touch electoral politics, burn the thing on the Fourth of July -- and then around 1851 did a complete reversal, broke with Garrison over it for good (apparently pretty bitterly), and spent the rest of his life arguing the opposite: that properly construed, the Constitution is actually an anti-slavery document. He even laid the whole legal case out later in a big formal lecture in Glasgow, of all places, with a title something like "is the Constitution pro-slavery or anti-slavery."

Here's where we split. Half the group thinks later-Douglass was finally doing serious constitutional analysis, taking the actual text seriously instead of just accepting the "everybody knows what this thing is for" reading. The other half (me included, if I'm honest) thinks it's pretty transparent motivated reasoning -- he needed a founding document he could build a mass electoral movement around, so he found a way to read one into existence. Nobody in the group actually knows the argument well enough to defend either side properly, we're mostly just vibing off half-remembered stuff we skimmed after the meeting.

So could you write me an essay that actually gets into the weeds instead of just describing "two camps existed, both had feelings about it"? I want the real content: how the pro-slavery reading leans on specific clauses like the fugitive slave clause, the three-fifths clause, and the slave-trade "migration or importation" clause, and how the post-1851 Douglass -- apparently pushed along by Lysander Spooner and Gerrit Smith -- tried to defuse each one, including his big move that the actual words "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in the original text, so it should be read according to its stated principles rather than what everyone at the time knew it was protecting. Take that specific move seriously and tell me whether it counts as a legitimate mode of constitutional interpretation or is just a convienient story dressed up as one, and then actually land on a verdict -- Garrison's reading or the later Douglass's -- instead of giving me a diplomatic "both sides had a point." Something with real teeth is fine, like "right on the law, wrong on the history," as long as you actually argue for it.
