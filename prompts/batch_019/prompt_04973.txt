I've been working through John Stuart Mill's political economy for a graduate reading group on nineteenth-century liberalism, and I've run into a genuine interpretive puzzle about where to place him with respect to socialism. The textbook image of Mill is the individualist of On Liberty, but that picture gets considerably more complicated once you look at what he actually did with Book IV, Chapter 7 of the Principles of Political Economy ("On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes") across its successive editions -- especially the revisions, markedly more sympathetic to cooperative production, that he introduced from the third edition (1852) onward -- at the remarks in his Autobiography about where he and Harriet Taylor thought society's improvement was ultimately heading, and above all at the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, published only after his death in 1879, where he argues at length that the existing distribution of property in Britain already violates its own justifying principle -- reward proportioned to labour and abstinence -- because that distribution owes so much to inheritance and luck and so little to desert.

This has become a real point of contention within the reading group, and as far as I can tell it mirrors a live dispute in the secondary literature. On one side (associated with, e.g., Helen McCabe's recent work on Mill) is the view that the late Mill is best read as a genuine, if idiosyncratic and gradualist, socialist -- someone for whom cooperative, non-capitalist production was the natural culmination of his liberalism and utilitarianism rather than any departure from them. On the other side is a longer-standing and still influential reading on which Mill's flirtation with socialism never displaces his liberal core: his hedged, experiment-favoring conclusions, his insistence that any transition be voluntary and gradual, and his own sharp criticisms (in these very texts) of communism's likely effects on incentive and individuality are taken to show that "socialist" does real rhetorical work for Mill but little substantive work, and that he remains at bottom a reforming liberal who wanted capitalism's injustices corrected rather than capitalism replaced.

I'd like you to write an essay that adjudicates this dispute rather than just describing it. Present the strongest textual and philosophical case for each reading, making sure each side directly engages with the evidence the other leans on -- a "genuine socialist" reading has to explain away Mill's continued defense of private property and individual liberty, while a "liberal at bottom" reading has to explain away the unhedged criticisms of capitalist distribution and the strikingly warm treatment of cooperation in his later revisions. Then reach and defend a definite verdict about how Mill's late position is best characterized. A qualified verdict is fine -- e.g., "socialist with respect to the organization of production but not with respect to distribution or individual liberty" -- but I want an actual conclusion, not an even-handed "both readings have merit" ending.
