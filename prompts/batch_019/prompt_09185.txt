So I fell down an animal cognition rabbit hole this week — started with an EA Forum-ish thread about invertebrate welfare (the whole shrimp-stunning debate) and somehow ended up re-reading Descartes on the beast-machine stuff. Everyone who brings him up online just goes "lol Descartes thought animals were unfeeling robots" and moves on, but the actual argument in Part V of the Discourse is a lot more specific than that, and more interesting. He gives two concrete tests for whether some behavior is coming from a real rational soul instead of clockwork: (1) the language test — a machine could be built to produce words, even words tied to specific triggers, but it could never rearrange language to respond appropriately to whatever's said in its presence, the way even a "dull" human can, and (2) the versatility test — a machine or animal might outperform us at some particular thing (he actually uses a clock keeping time as his example), but it'll always fail at something else, because its working off a fixed arrangement of parts for that one action rather than reason, which he calls a "universal instrument" good for any situation. Then in the 1646 letter to Newcastle, and especially the 1649 one to Henry More, he gets weirdly cagey about whether animals feel anything at all — more cagey than the "unfeeling robot" caricature suggests, like he wants to deny them thought/language in his technical sense without fully committing to denying sensation too.

Obviously the guy died in 1650, so he never got to actually run this against a real test case. Now we've got New Caledonian crows spontaneously bending straight wire into a hook to solve a problem they'd never seen before (the Betty studies), parrots like Alex who gave correct novel answers about the color/shape/material of objects he'd never encountered, and, well, LLMs, which rearrange language to respond to almost anything you type at them — literally the thing Descartes says is impossible to fake. I don't want a "here's the modern philosophy of mind consensus on animal cognition" essay out of you; I want you to run his own argument forward, from his own commitments (the real distinction between mind and extended matter, reason as a unified faculty rather than a bag of narrow tricks, etc), and tell me what he'd be forced to conclude about the crow, the parrot, and the LLM if someone actually sat him down and demoed all three. Does he have any principled, non-ad-hoc way to draw a line between them, or does his own test prove too much for at least one case? Does the thought-vs-sensation dodge from the letters rescue him, and does it rescue him equally for all three, or just some of them? I'd rather have your honest best reconstruction of where he actually lands, warts and all, than a tidy answer that just confirms he was "wrong about animals" in the generic way everyone already says.
