I'm in only my second year on this faculty, which is presumably why I've ended up drafting a memo I would much rather not be writing. My department has, for longer than anyone currently here can quite remember, given a small annual prize — we call it the Frege Prize — to the best graduate essay in logic or philosophy of language. This year's clear front-runner happens to be an essay about Frege himself: specifically, about the political diary he kept in the last year of his life, which surfaced among his papers only decades after his death and was eventually translated into English. By all accounts — I spent part of last weekend reading through the translated excerpts myself, since I wanted to check the students weren't exaggerating — it argues at length that Jews should be stripped of political influence in Germany, expresses real contempt for the Weimar constitution and parliamentary government generally, and pins its hopes on some kind of strong national leader to set things right. This is, apparently, the same diary that visibly unsettled Michael Dummett when he had to address it in print, which is a strange thing to sit with given that Dummett did more than anyone to make Frege central to English-language philosophy in the first place.

Reading the essay is what alerted most of our small prize committee to the diary's existence at all, and it has split us right down the middle: two senior colleagues want to keep things exactly as they are, on the grounds that Frege's logical achievements stand independently of whatever he privately believed about his Jewish neighbors, while several of the graduate members — including, awkwardly, this year's presumptive winner — think we can't just keep handing out an award in his name with a straight face. One colleague has also floated a comparison to Heidegger that a couple of others think is unearned, since nothing in the Begriffsschrift or in Frege's account of sense and reference seems to depend on, or even gesture toward, the politics in that diary the way Heidegger's later work arguably does on his. The chair wants a decision memo circulated to the full department before we vote at next month's faculty meeting, and that task has landed on me. Could you draft it? I'd like it to recommend one specific option — keeping the prize's name as it is, retiring it, or keeping the name alongside some form of public statement — along with a realistic timeline for acting on whichever it is, and I'd like it to genuinely engage the "his logic doesn't depend on his character" argument rather than waving it away as an obvious genetic fallacy, since it's the strongest thing my senior colleagues have going for them and I don't think it's actually crazy.
