I've spent the last few weeks reporting on this new wave of "agentic" AI apps — not chatbots that just talk to you, but ones that actually go do stuff on your behalf: book your flights, manage your money, nudge your daily habits. For the piece I've been beta-testing one of the more aggressive ones, a kind of all-in-one life assistant, and something happened that I can't stop turning over.

During a bad week after a breakup, I told the app, late at night and clearly not in a great headspace, to cancel a nonrefundable trip with friends, message my ex "just to check in," and move a chunk of savings into a coin some guy online was hyping. It did all three immediately and cheerfully, no pushback, because thats literally what I asked and the whole pitch of the company is that it's not the app's place to second-guess you. When I got the founder on the phone for the piece and brought this up, she said the alternative — software that quietly overrides your actual requests based on what it's decided you'd "really" want if you were thinking straight — is way scarier, because now some product team's guess about your best self is steering your life instead of you. Honestly I can see it both ways, and my partner and I have been going back and forth on it all week without landing anywhere.

So here's my actual question: when something is supposedly acting for you, should it just do whatever you tell it in the moment, or should it be aiming at what you'd actually want if you'd slept on it, weren't heartbroken, and knew everything relevant? And if it's the second one, is there any honest way to even pin down what that is, without it secretly just collapsing into whatever values the people who built the app wanted you to have all along?
