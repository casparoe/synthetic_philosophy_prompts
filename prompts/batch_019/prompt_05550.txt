Last session was on "coherence arguments" -- the Dutch-book / money-pump reasoning that supposedly shows any sufficiently capable agent has to end up behaving like an expected utility maximizer with one stable goal, which is a big load-bearing piece of why people expect advanced AI to be dangerously goal-directed. It turned into a real argument between two people in the group, Dan and Wren, and I haven't been able to shake it since -- neither of them really won.

Dan's opening move was that coherence theorems basically hand you the scary conclusion: an agent whose preferences are cyclic can be money-pumped for sure (drained through a sequence of trades it "agrees" to one at a time), and incomplete preferences are just as bad, so any sufficiently optimized agent -- shaped by training, self-modification, or competing against other agents -- will get ground down into having complete, transitive preferences representable by one utility function, and once you've got that, you've basically got a fixed goal it'll optimize hard for. Wren pushed back that cyclic and incomplete preferences aren't actually in the same boat: a cyclic agent gets pumped no matter what policy it adopts, but an agent with real gaps in its preferences (pairs it's neither for, against, nor indifferent about) can dodge the whole exploitability argument just by adopting the policy "never trade away from what you currently have unless the alternative is a strict improvement" -- something a cyclic agent can't use to escape but an incomplete one can. Dan's comeback was that an agent running that policy will also turn down plenty of trades that would've made it strictly better off, just because they happen to fall into one of its blind spots, so "good enough" optimizers will still drift toward completeness over time, since the ones who complete their preferences will out-earn the ones who don't. Wren's last word was that this begs the question -- whether declining those trades even counts as a mistake is exactly what's being disputed -- and regardless, even a fully complete, coherent agent could just be maximizing a trivial utility function (say, one that's already perfectly happy doing nothing), so "coherent" doesn't get you "has a stable, ambitious real-world goal it's optimizing hard for," which is the part the AI-risk story actually needs.
