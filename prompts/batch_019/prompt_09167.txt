I've been working on an independent research project on possible-worlds metaphysics for my school's philosophy club, and I've run into a puzzle about Leibniz that I haven't been able to resolve on my own. In his correspondence with Arnauld, he argues that every individual substance has a "complete concept" containing all of its predicates, so that a Caesar who never crossed the Rubicon, or an Adam who never sinned, would strictly speaking be a different individual altogether, rather than our Caesar or Adam living an alternate life. This sounds very close to David Lewis's counterpart theory, which denies that one and the same individual is ever literally present in more than one possible world. Yet elsewhere, in the Theodicy, Leibniz has God compare whole possible worlds containing different versions of "Sextus," as though weighing different careers open to a single person before deciding which one to actualize — which sounds much closer to Kripke's insistence on genuine transworld identity. If Leibniz had to take a side in this twentieth-century debate, which do you think he would choose, and can his doctrine of complete concepts really be reconciled with the way he talks about God comparing possible individuals?
