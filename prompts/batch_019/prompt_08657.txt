For thirty-five years I ran an experimental condensed-matter physics laboratory, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than in stolen half-hours between grant deadlines. A former postdoc of mine, who now works in computational neuroscience, has taken to sending me papers on Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory of consciousness, and I have worked through enough of the formalism to follow the central claims: that a system's degree of consciousness corresponds to Φ, its irreducible integrated cause-effect power, and that the so-called exclusion postulate picks out a single maximally irreducible complex, at one spatiotemporal grain, as the thing that is conscious, rather than any of the many overlapping candidate systems one could carve out of the same physical substrate.

What I have not been able to settle in my own mind is how seriously to take the theory once one pushes on its edges, and three things I have since read seem to pull in different directions. There is the "unfolding argument," which I believe originates with Doerig, Herzog and colleagues: a recurrent network can be "unfolded" into a feedforward network with an identical input-output function but a very different Φ, so that no behavioral test could ever distinguish the conscious version from its behaviorally indistinguishable, supposedly unconscious twin -- which its authors take as evidence that IIT's central claims are empirically idle. Then there is the older exchange between Scott Aaronson and Tononi, in which Aaronson described a simple, densely connected combinatorial circuit -- I recall it being built from something like an expander graph -- with an enormous Φ, and Tononi's reply was to bite the bullet and grant it rich consciousness, which struck many observers as the opposite problem: not that IIT is untestable, but that it issues a definite and, on most people's intuitions, absurd verdict. Finally, there was the 2023 open letter, signed by a substantial number of consciousness researchers, that drew on considerations of this kind to brand IIT outright as "pseudoscience," a label that in turn provoked a fairly heated round of counter-argument about whether it was fair or well-motivated.

I would like an essay that actually resolves this for me rather than merely surveying it. Please weigh these three lines of attack against one another and against the strongest replies IIT's defenders have offered -- including, if you think it succeeds, some account of why the unfalsifiability charge and the overgeneration charge are not simply in tension with each other -- and then commit to a real verdict on the theory's standing. Something in the spirit of "this is respectable metaphysics dressed up as bad science" or "the core formalism survives the objections and deserves to be treated as a live scientific theory" is the kind of judgment I am after; a conclusion that amounts to "more research is needed" will not do.
