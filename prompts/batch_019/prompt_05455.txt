Scott Aaronson has argued that computational complexity theory, rather than indeterminism as such, may supply the missing ingredient in the free will debate. His proposal centers on "freebits": hypothetical bits of physical information, plausibly seeded by quantum indeterminacy and amplified by chaotic dynamics in the brain, whose values are unpredictable in a way that is not merely probabilistic but Knightian -- following economist Frank Knight, uncertain in a sense that cannot be captured by any well-defined probability distribution at all. On this view, a choice counts as free to the extent that no predictor, however computationally powerful and however much it knows about the laws of physics, could have assembled in time the physical information needed to predict it, since doing so would require cloning or extracting microscopic data that thermodynamics and the quantum no-cloning theorem make physically inaccessible. Crucially, Aaronson intends this as fully compatible with physical determinism: what is supposed to matter for freedom is not whether a choice was caused, but whether it was predictable in principle by any physically realizable agent.

Write an essay assessing whether this complexity-theoretic proposal succeeds in capturing something philosophically important about free will, or whether it merely redescribes a kind of unpredictability that obviously unfree chaotic systems -- the weather, a double pendulum -- can equally possess. In particular, consider whether the traditional worry about free will is best understood as a worry about predictability at all, rather than a worry about authorship or rational control. Weigh the strongest form of each side, and end with a definite, argued verdict rather than a hedge.
