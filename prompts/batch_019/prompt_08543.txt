I'm a backend engineer who ended up on a project doing causal inference over product-analytics data -- basically, trying to estimate the effect of shipping a feature on user retention from observational logs rather than a clean A/B test. That work forced me to learn the basics of structural causal models and Judea Pearl's do-operator, and out of curiosity I started reading around the philosophical edges of it. I gathered that Pearl's "graph surgery" treatment of interventions is essentially a formalized version of what philosophers call the interventionist (or manipulationist) theory of causation, associated especially with James Woodward, and that this is usually presented as an improvement on David Lewis's older counterfactual theory of causation, on which (very roughly) c causes e when e counterfactually depends on c.

What I can't get straight is why these count as genuinely different theories rather than the same idea in different clothes, since a claim like "if we were to intervene and set X to x, Y would take value y" is still, on its face, a counterfactual conditional. My best understanding is that the real issue shows up in cases like a barometer whose needle drops shortly before a storm: on a naive, similarity-based reading of counterfactuals, "if the barometer hadn't dropped, the storm wouldn't have arrived" threatens to come out true, wrongly making the barometer a cause of the storm, and Lewis needed a fairly elaborate and contested system of weights for ranking how similar possible worlds are (favoring small, localized "miracles" right before the counterfactual's antecedent over any divergence further back in history) specifically to rule that out. My hazy impression is that Woodward and Pearl think the intervention-based approach gets the right verdict "for free," because the causal asymmetry is baked directly into what counts as a legitimate intervention, so you never need the whole apparatus of ranking possible worlds by overall similarity. What I don't understand is the actual mechanics of why restricting yourself to intervention-counterfactuals buys you this so much more cheaply than Lewis's approach does -- or whether it's really cheaper at all, once you spell out what a "legitimate intervention" has to be.

That last worry is the part that's been nagging at me most: I've come across the objection that specifying which manipulations count as genuine, "surgical" interventions (as opposed to sloppy ones with side effects on other variables) seems to already presuppose the very causal facts the theory is supposed to explain, which would make the whole account circular, or at least non-reductive in a way Lewis's theory at least aspired not to be. Could you walk me through, at a fairly precise level, what's actually at stake between these two camps -- not just how each one is defined, but the specific technical reason interventionism is thought to have the upper hand on cases like the barometer -- and give me your own read on whether it genuinely earns that advantage or just relocates the problem somewhere less visible?
