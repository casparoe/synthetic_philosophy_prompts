Two friends of mine, neither of them philosophers, spent most of a dinner arguing about whether it's wrong to have children, after one of them had just finished David Benatar's "Better Never to Have Been." I don't have any formal training in this either — I write backend infrastructure for a living — so I wrote down the exchange afterward because I couldn't tell who was actually getting the better of it.

(1) Mara laid out Benatar's asymmetry: pain is bad, and its absence is good even if no one exists to enjoy that good; pleasure is good, but its absence is bad only if some actual person is deprived of it. Since an unconceived person is deprived of nothing, while a conceived person will certainly suffer some real pain, Mara concluded that coming into existence is always a net harm to that person, even when their life is happy overall.

(2) Devon objected that this proves too much: it implies a life of near-total bliss with one stubbed toe in it is still worse than never existing at all, and that we'd have equal reason to painlessly end a happy person's life right now to spare them all future pains — which Devon was pretty sure even Benatar denies. If "good even though nobody benefits" is enough to justify not creating someone, it should equally justify killing someone, and since nobody wants that conclusion, Devon thought the premise must be flawed.

(3) Mara replied that Benatar has an answer: an existing person has real interests — in staying alive, in finishing what she's started, in the people she loves — that death frustrates, whereas an unconceived person has no interests at all, so never creating her frustrates nothing. That, Mara said, is why the two cases aren't parallel.

(4) Devon answered that this only relocates the problem: Mara needs "no one's there yet" to cancel the unconceived person's forgone pleasure, but not to cancel that same unconceived person's potential pain, even though the whole argument in (1) depends on that pain counting as a real harm to someone. Devon didn't see, and didn't think Mara had actually said, what justifies treating the not-yet-existing person's stake in future pain as real while treating their stake in future pleasure as nothing at all.

Neither of them budged. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) one at a time, say which parts hold up and which don't, and then give your own verdict on whether Benatar's asymmetry survives Devon's last move or actually breaks down there?
