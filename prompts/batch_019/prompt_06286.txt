I've been slowly making my way through Nietzsche's "On the Genealogy of Morality" (got curious after a youtube video mentioned it, wasnt expecting to actually finish the thing but here we are), and the second essay, the one about guilt, punishment, and "bad conscience," kinda messed with my head. His whole argument seems to be that guilt as we experience it didn't come from some pure moral instinct, it grew out of literal creditor-debtor relationships, where punishment was originally just a creditor extracting pain as payment from someone who owed a debt they couldn't repay, and it was only way later that this got internalized and dressed up as "conscience" once society caged in all our natural aggressive instincts and turned them back against ourselves.

This got me thinking about restorative justice, the programs some schools and even a few courts are using now, where instead of just punishing someone who did something wrong, you actually sit the offender down with the person they hurt (sometimes literally in a circle) and the group works out together how to repair things, no detention or jail time involved. Everyone I know who's into this stuff talks about it like it's this obviously more compassionate step forward from old school punishment. I brought this up with a friend who volunteers for a restorative justice program and she said Nietzsche would clearly be against punishment altogether so he'd love it, but that felt like a lazy answer that doesn't actually engage with his argument. Based on what he actually lays out in that book, do you think he'd see restorative justice as us genuinely moving past "bad conscience," or would he say its just slave morality/herd control in a gentler costume, maybe even more effective at taming people since now they internalize the harm on a personal level instead of just doing time or paying a fine? Genuinely want to know how this actually works out through his framework, not just a surface-level guess.
