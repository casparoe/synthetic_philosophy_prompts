I've been working through Kant's Groundwork on my own, and one step in the universalizability test has been bothering me. With the false-promise example, Kant says the maxim fails because you cannot even coherently conceive of it as a universal law: universal false promising would destroy the very practice of promising that the lie depends on. But with the example of refusing to help others in need, he grants that such a world is perfectly conceivable — humanity, he says, could carry on well enough without mutual aid — and holds only that no rational being could will it, since anyone might someday need the very help they would be legislating away. Commentaries label these two failures a "contradiction in conception" and a "contradiction in the will" and treat that split as mapping neatly onto perfect versus imperfect duties. What I can't work out is why the second test still counts as a formal test of universalizability at all, rather than a substantive claim about what needy, vulnerable beings like us must want for ourselves. Could you explain what is actually doing the argumentative work in the contradiction-in-the-will cases, and whether Kant, or his best interpreters, have a convincing reply to the suspicion that this half of the procedure smuggles in exactly the kind of substantive premise that a purely formal universal-law test was supposed to avoid?
