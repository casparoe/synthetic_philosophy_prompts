My friend and I got into a solid two-hour argument on the drive back from a wedding this weekend about the Doomsday Argument, of all things, and I don't think either of us came away understanding it better, just more dug in. I brought up the standard version: you treat yourself as basically a random sample from everyone who will ever live (the "self-sampling assumption," I think its called), and conclude that a future with some enormous number of additional humans in it is suspicious, because then you'd be a weirdly early sample instead of a typical one -- so something like doom relatively soon starts to look more likely than you'd think. My friend, who's been on a big rationalist-blogosphere kick lately, shot back that this move can't be trusted, because a close cousin of it -- reasoning about how likely you are to exist at all under different theories, rather than about your rank among the people who do exist -- leads straight to something called the presumptuous philosopher: physicists have two theories that fit the evidence equally well, one implying the universe contains something like a trillion times more observers than the other, and a philosopher interrupts to say don't even bother running the deciding experiment, since the theory with more observers is already a trillion times more probable, just because there'd be that many more "people like me" to possibly be. He says that's obviously deranged, so we shouldn't trust that whole genre of reasoning about your own existence -- doomsday argument included.

I pushed back that this seemed too quick, since I'm pretty sure that second move (self-indication, he called it) was originally floated specifically to cancel out the doomsday conclusion, not to prop up the same kind of mistake -- so they're rivals competing for the same job, not two examples of one bad habit, and it feels off to use one's absurd result to discredit the other. We also both kept tripping over this "reference class problem" thing -- something about nobody being able to say what set of observers you're supposed to count as a random sample from in the first place -- without being able to say whether that's a unique problem for the doomsday-style reasoning or whether the presumptuous philosopher has an equivalent headache buried in it too.

Could you actually untangle this properly for me? I want the real gears-level story, not just headline versions of each puzzle: why these two ways of reasoning about your own existence pull toward such different conclusions, whether my "rivals, not cousins" pushback actually holds up or my friend's instinct was onto something, and whether anyone's found a way to reason about self-location that dodges both messes instead of just making you choose which flavor of weird conclusion you can live with.
