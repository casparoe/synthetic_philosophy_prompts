Ok so I fell down a total rabbit hole this weekend that started on a wiki page about game theory and ended with me reading an actual 1997 economics paper at 1am, don't judge me. The thing that hooked me was this "absent-minded driver" puzzle from a couple of economists (Piccione and Rubinstein I think). Setup: a guy is driving home and has to take the second of two exits off a highway. Taking the first exit dumps him somewhere bad, missing both exits means he just ends up sleeping at a motel (meh but not terrible), and only the second exit actually gets him home. The catch is he's absent-minded enough that when he's sitting at an intersection he genuinely can't tell if its the first one or the second one, no landmarks, no memory of having passed one already. So whatever rule he uses for "exit vs keep driving" has to be literally the same rule both times, because from the inside the two moments are indistinguishable. Apparently if you work out the best plan beforehand (sitting at the bar before he even starts driving) versus what looks smart in the moment once he's actually at an intersection re-deriving the odds of "am I probably on pass one or pass two," you can talk yourself into two different answers, and there's a genuine unresolved argument in the decision theory literature about which one, if either, is the "correct" way to think about it.

This is scratching an itch that shows up in my actual job too, weirdly enough - like when you've got a retry loop or a worker process that has no reliable way of telling "this is attempt #1" from "this is attempt #2 of the same thing," and you still need one policy that's supposed to behave correctly no matter which attempt it secretly is.

Anyway, I think there's a good short story hiding in this and I'd love it if you wrote it. Doesn't have to literally be a highway, or even a human - could be an AI process, somebody stuck in a weird facility, whatever setting works best - but I want the actual bind to survive the move: a character who keeps landing on what might be the same decision point or might be a later one in a sequence, can't tell which, and has to commit to one policy that covers all of them. What I really want dramatized is the disagreement itself, the gap between "the plan I'd have picked in advance" and "what seems right now, updating on the fact that I've found myself here at all", rather than just a passing nod to the puzzle. It doesn't need to resolve neatly - I'm mostly curious if you can make that specific flavor of confusion feel real from the inside instead of just explaining it.
