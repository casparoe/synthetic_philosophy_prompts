Okay so this has been rattling around in my head since a LW meetup a few weeks back, where it turned into an arguement that never really got resolved. Someone brought up Roko's basilisk as a joke, which somehow spiraled into a much more serious conversation about acausal blackmail and threats more generally -- the kind of thing that comes up in s-risk discussions about what a sufficiently capable but misaligned agent (a future AI, or some other mind out there in the multiverse doing updateless-decision-theory-style reasoning) might be incentivized to threaten us with. Somebody brought up Tobias Baumann's old "surrogate goals" post as basically the standard proposed defense against this kind of thing, and that's where my two friends started disagreeing and never really stopped.

As I understood the pitch (correct me if I'm butchering it): instead of trying to credibly precommit to never give in to threats against the thing you actually care about -- which is psychologically almost impossible to pull off once a threat that severe is actually on the table -- you deliberately adopt some other, basically manufactured goal, and commit in advance to responding to threats against *that* exactly as if it were the real thing, while treating your actual values as simply not up for negotiation, ever, full stop. The hope is that a rational extortionist figures out threatening your true goal gets them nothing, redirects to the decoy instead, "extracts" something from you there, and you've lost nothing you actually cared about.

One friend thinks this is a genuinely clever, underated bit of applied game theory that more people -- and arguably more AI labs -- should take seriously as an actual policy, not just a thought experiment. The other thinks it's a self-deceiving parlor trick that doesn't solve the problem so much as relocate it one level up: his line was "the moment you're actually willing to pay a real cost to protect the fake goal, it's stopped being fake, and you're back to square one." They argued about it for an hour in the parking lot afterward and neither one budged. Could you write out a dialogue between them picking this back up -- say, a week later, still at it -- where they actually engage with each other's strongest moves instead of just restating their own position louder? I want both sides played by someone genuinely smart, not a strawman standing there to get knocked down; ideally I finish reading it still not sure who's actually right, and I want it to go deep enough into the actual mechanics that it's not just two people trading one-liners about "credible precommitment."
