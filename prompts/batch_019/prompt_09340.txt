I spent my career as a field biologist studying animal foraging behavior, and only in retirement did I start reading up on the economics side of "revealed preference" -- turns out its close to the same math we used to check whether a rat's diet choices were internally consistent. Anyway, at dinner last week two friends of mine (she's an economist, he trained as a clinical psychologist) got into it about my brother in law, who says he wants to quit smoking but buys a pack every single day.

(1) She said: whatever someone consistently chooses just IS their preference -- if he keeps buying cigarettes over patches, then by revealed preference he prefers smoking, and its confused to insist he "really" wants to quit. (2) He said that's nuts, because it would make it conceptually impossible to ever act against your own considered desires, and people obviously do that all the time, that's basically what addiction and akrasia mean. (3) She answered that economics isn't denying second thoughts, it just refuses to call something a preference unless it shows up in a choice -- "I want to quit" is cheap talk unless he actually buys the patch and uses it. (4) He shot back that economists invoke exactly this "he doesn't really want this" idea themselves, e.g. to justify cigarette taxes as protecting smokers from their own addiction, so apparently the profession does think there's a "true" preference apart from the addicted choice. (5) She conceded there are rival models here -- some treat the addiction itself as rationally chosen with eyes wide open, others split the person into something like a "wanting" self and a "liking" self -- but said picking one of those models is a modeling decision, not a fact about what he "really" wants. (6) He said that just concedes his whole point: there's no fact of the matter about "the" revealed preference without first assuming some model of which self is doing the choosing.

Both of them turned to me afterward and honestly I don't have the background to referee this properly. Could you go through moves (1) to (6) and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Then end with a one line verdict, formatted exactly as \verdict{Economist} or \verdict{Psychologist}, for whoever comes out ahead on balance.
