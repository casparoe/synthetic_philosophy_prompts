A while back I came across a video essay walking through Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument, and I found myself more persuaded by it than I expected to be. The basic move — start from any distribution of wealth that people already agree is fair, let a huge number of separately voluntary exchanges take place (say, a million fans each choosing to pay a little extra just to watch Wilt play), and end up with a wildly unequal result that nobody can object to without also objecting to particular free choices people made along the way — struck me as a genuinely sharp argument against the idea that justice just means matching some pre-approved pattern of who owns what.

What has been nagging at me since is that almost everything I have come across on this argument — videos, forum discussions, even a couple of encyclopedia-style overviews — stops right there, at what Nozick calls justice in transfer, and quietly skips over the third and much less flashy leg of his theory: rectification. As I understand it, entitlement theory holds that a holding is only truly just if it also traces back to a just original acquisition, and that if somewhere upstream there was theft, slavery, conquest, or fraud, something is owed to correct for it. What actually stopped me in my tracks was discovering that Nozick himself concedes that, since we usually cannot reconstruct exactly how history would have unfolded without those injustices, the best available substitute might be something like Rawls's difference principle — arranging society to maximize the position of whoever ends up worst off — used as a rough stand-in for genuine rectification. That struck me as an enormous concession, given that the rest of the book reads like one long argument against exactly that kind of engineered, "patterned" distribution.

So here is what I would like your help with: write me an essay that actually grapples with this tension rather than just re-explaining the Wilt Chamberlain case one more time. Given how much of the world's actual property and wealth traces back to conquest, dispossession, and slavery rather than to clean chains of just acquisition and voluntary exchange, can entitlement theory take its own rectification principle seriously and still deliver the anti-redistributive conclusions it is famous for — or does it end up quietly committed to something close to the Rawlsian redistribution it was designed to defeat? Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but do not settle for "it depends" — tell me which way you think this actually comes out, and defend that verdict.
