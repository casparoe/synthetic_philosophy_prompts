I'm currently taking a seminar cross-listed with the PPE program that has spent the last few weeks on decision-making under moral uncertainty, using William MacAskill, Krister Bykvist, and Toby Ord's book on the topic as our main text. A disagreement broke out between two people in my study group, Priya and Owen, over whether a mutual friend of theirs -- who eats meat but openly admits she is unsure whether animals have significant moral status -- is being irrational, and neither of them would let it go. Priya argued that their friend should follow the book's "maximize expected choiceworthiness" rule: since she assigns a real, non-negligible credence -- Priya guessed somewhere between twenty and thirty percent -- to moral views on which factory-farmed animals suffer enormously and that suffering matters a great deal, and only a modest residual credence to views on which it doesn't matter much at all, the expected disvalue of continuing to eat meat swamps the mild inconvenience of changing her diet, so she should stop. Owen replied that this smuggles in exactly the "intertheoretic comparison" problem the book itself flags as unresolved: there is no principled common scale on which to weigh "this much credence that eating meat is a serious wrong" against "that much credence that it's morally neutral," so Priya isn't really running a calculation -- she's just assigning numbers that already assume the conclusion she wants.

Priya's answer was that you don't need precise cardinal units when the stakes are this lopsided: severe, large-scale suffering on one side and mere dietary preference on the other, so almost any non-arbitrary way of comparing the two views still favors abstaining. Owen countered that this is precisely the reasoning behind what the book calls "fanaticism" -- if a small credence in a high-stakes theory can always outweigh a much larger credence in a low-stakes theory, then any moral view can be made to dominate a decision simply by claiming sufficiently enormous stakes, which should make us suspicious of the whole approach rather than confident in this particular conclusion. Priya pushed back that fanaticism, properly understood, is specifically about credences that shrink toward zero while claimed stakes grow toward infinity, in the style of Pascal's mugging, and that a real-world credence of twenty to thirty percent in a theory with large but finite stakes is nothing like that pattern, so Owen was importing a worry from a completely different kind of case. Owen's response was that even granting Priya that distinction, she still owes some account of how much credence, at what stakes, is enough to count -- and until she has one, insisting that her case is safely on the right side of an unspecified line is not much of an answer either.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each specific point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether you think the book's own framework actually gives Owen the resources he is reaching for at the end, or whether that is a genuine, unresolved gap in the "maximize expected choiceworthiness" proposal that both of them are talking past.
