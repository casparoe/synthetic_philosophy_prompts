So this semester I got roped into chairing our department's hiring committee, and something happened that reminded me alot of the discursive dilemma material I cover in my social epistemology seminar (probably too well, in hindsight). Five of us each formed a judgment on three "premises" for every finalist — fit with the department's research needs, teaching effectiveness, and strength of the letters — and then a seperate up-or-down judgment on whether to extend an offer, under the "official" rule that you extend an offer iff all three premises are satisfied. When we tallied things issue-by-issue (majority vote on each premise, then apply the rule) one candidate came out on top; when we instead just had everyone vote holistically on the final question, a totally different candidate won. Nobody was being irrational or strategic — people were just applying the rule to their own honest judgments — but the two procedures spat out different winners. I sort of waved my hands in the meeting and muttered something about Kornhauser and Sager's old doctrinal paradox, but I never actually made anyone, including myself, sit down and work through why this happens or what a committee is supposed to do about it. We just re-voted until the awkwardness went away, which is obviously not a principled solution to anything.

Now that grades are in and I've got some breathing room, I'd like to actually think this through instead of just gesturing at the literature. Could you walk me through the most important implications of the discursive dilemma, and the broader judgment-aggregation impossibility results (List and Pettit's work, mainly, though feel free to bring in others), for how a committee like mine should structure its deliberation and voting procedure going forward? I'm especially interested in whether there's any principled, non-arbitrary way to choose between premise-based and conclusion-based procedures, rather than it just coming down to whichever one the chair happens to call for on the day, and whether the systematicity condition that drives the impossibility result is something a hiring committee could just reject in good conscience, given what these committees are actually for.
