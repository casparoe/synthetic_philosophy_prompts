I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and the mathematical foundations of AI safety, and one of our recent sessions was devoted to Löb's theorem and the so-called Löbian obstacle to building agents that can rationally trust their own future reasoning, or the reasoning of a successor system meant to replace them. As I understand the core difficulty: if an agent's decision procedure is supposed to license deferring to whatever a successor proves (something like "if my successor proves that action A is good, then A is good," for arbitrary A), Löb's theorem shows that adopting this schema forces the agent to already be able to prove A outright whenever the successor can, which collapses the intended chain of trust into either triviality or an outright refusal to ever defer to the successor's proofs. Our instructor sketched three proposals meant to soften this: Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's "parametric polymorphism," which indexes trust to a strictly weaker proof system at each successive generation instead of a single fixed one; Christiano-style probabilistic reflection principles, which replace binary provability with calibrated, approximately self-referential credences; and reflective-oracle constructions, which let an agent reason about its own and others' dispositions while avoiding the diagonalization that would otherwise make such reasoning paradoxical. Our instructor stopped short, though, of saying whether any of these genuinely dissolves the obstacle rather than just re-describing it one level up, and a friend from the seminar and I have been arguing about it ever since: he thinks the obstacle becomes basically cosmetic once you give up on proof-based certainty, and I'm not convinced.

Could you help me settle this by writing an essay that first reconstructs the Löbian obstacle precisely enough that someone who already knows Gödel's and Löb's theorems can see exactly where a naive successor-trusting schema breaks down, and then weighs the leading proposed escapes against the position that something essential about the obstacle survives in each of them, so that no formal reasoner can fully and non-trivially certify a successor's proof-based trustworthiness using only tools of the same general kind? Please don't leave this as an even-handed survey: take a side on whether the obstacle has actually been overcome, has merely been pushed back a level and remains in force in a weaker guise, or deserves some more precise intermediate verdict, and defend your position against the strongest objections you can raise against it.
