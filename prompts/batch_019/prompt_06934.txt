so i'm plotting a novel where a minor character has survived four separate near-fatal things (car wreck, a bad fall, a botched surgery) and starts telling everyone he must be "quantum immortal" -- and completely coincidentally, this exact topic blew up into a real argument at a dinner party last month that i still can't stop replaying in my head. My friend Owen (self-taught physics obsessive, reads way too much pop-sci) was defending the idea; my friend Cass (phd in something physics-adjacent) went after it hard, and honestly neither of them said anything dumb.

1. Owen: if many-worlds is true, a repeated quantum-suicide setup always leaves some branch where you keep surviving, and since there's no "you" left in the branches where you die to be disappointed, you should subjectively expect to keep finding yourself alive no matter how bad the odds get.

2. Cass: that's confusing "a surviving branch exists" with "you should expect to land in it." Nearly all the quantum-mechanical weight sits on the branches where you die, and if weight is what fixes rational expectation -- the whole premise behind treating the Born rule as a real probability instead of a bookkeeping trick -- then you should expect to die, same as under ordinary collapse.

3. Owen: expectation over what population, though? There's no crowd of "yous" sitting in the death-branches to be shortchanged, so weighting by how much of the multiverse dies doesn't track anything anyone could actually anticipate experiencing. Only surviving ever gets experienced, so only surviving should count.

4. Cass: that move proves too much -- a lottery player could say the exact same thing, since the only tomorrow he's around to enjoy is the one where he wins. We don't let that excuse ignoring lottery odds, so "only survival is experienced" can't excuse ignoring quantum-suicide odds either.

5. Owen: lotteries aren't a fair comparison, because collapse-world has exactly one future that actually happens, so odds describe which single future gets picked. In many-worlds every branch is equally actual -- nothing is "selected" -- there's just a guaranteed continuer of me in the surviving branch, and that's who im identifying with, not gambling on which world wins.

6. Cass: they can be equally actual and you'd still owe an explanation for why you should anticipate becoming the thinly-weighted survivor rather than one of the overwhelmingly more numerous dying ones. Weight does exactly the job probability does for everyone else, and you can't credit Born-rule weights for why quantum computers beat classical ones and then drop them the second they say something inconvenient about your own odds.

Nobody backed down and I went home no closer to knowing who'd actually won, which matters to me because whichever guy is "right" is the one I want to come off smarter in the scene. Can you go through moves 1-6, say who had the better of each exchange, and then tell me who came out ahead overall plus anything both of them are still missing?
