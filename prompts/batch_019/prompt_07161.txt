I've spent a fair amount of time on LessWrong and the EA Forum following the literature on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) — the idea, growing out of Caspar Oesterheld's earlier work on "multiverse-wide superrationality," that if the universe (or multiverse) is large enough, there will be many causally disconnected agents whose decision procedures correlate with mine even though nothing I do can affect them, and that I should therefore act as though I were party to a vast, acausal bargain with them across differing value systems. A friend and I got into a good-natured argument last week about which dead philosopher would have made the most interesting interlocutor for this literature, and we kept circling back to David Lewis, for reasons pulling in two opposite directions at once. On one hand, no one in twentieth-century philosophy was more metaphysically committed to a plenitude of really-existing, causally isolated worlds than Lewis was in On the Plurality of Worlds (1986) — if anyone should find ECL's "large world" premise congenial, in an even stronger form than most ECL proponents actually need, it's him. On the other hand, Lewis was one of causal decision theory's most careful defenders, and in his 1979 paper "Prisoners' Dilemma Is a Newcomb Problem" he explicitly bit the bullet that a rational agent should defect even against a perfect twin whose choice is guaranteed to match her own, precisely because that match is correlational rather than causal — which is more or less the mirror image of the move ECL now asks us to make, just run on a single twin instead of across a whole multiverse of imperfect ones.

What I'd like from you is a careful, textually grounded reconstruction of how Lewis would most plausibly have responded to ECL had he lived to encounter it (he died in 2001; Oesterheld's work dates from the mid-2010s onward). Rather than jumping straight to a verdict, I'd like you to work through this in stages. First, would Lewis's Humean denial of necessary connections between distinct existences lead him to resist even the correlational premise ECL needs — that decision procedures can resemble each other closely enough, across causally disconnected regions, to carry genuine evidential weight — independently of any question about payoffs? Second, assuming he grants that much, does his reply to what's since become known as the "why ain'cha rich?" objection, in the Newcomb and twin-prisoner's-dilemma cases, give him the resources to resist ECL's practical conclusion, or do ECL's added ingredients — an enormous, perhaps infinite, population of only imperfectly correlated agents with heterogeneous values, rather than a single perfect twin sharing your own values — change the argument in ways his framework doesn't obviously anticipate? And third, does his own modal realism pose a special problem, or a special opportunity, that a more modest "large-but-ordinary-universe" ECL proponent doesn't face, given that similarity of decision procedure across logically rather than merely physically disparate worlds looks like a considerably bigger evidential stretch? I would rather have an argument that stays close to Lewis's actual, specific commitments than a general survey of the existing ECL debate.
