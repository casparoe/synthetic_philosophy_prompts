I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but over the past few months I've been slowly working through a secondhand copy of Robert Nozick's "Anarchy, State, and Utopia" on train rides, and the part that's really stuck with me is the Wilt Chamberlain argument from the chapter on distributive justice. As I understood it: you start by picking any distribution of wealth you're willing to call fair, call it D1. Now suppose Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract so that 25 cents from the price of every ticket to his team's home games goes straight to him, and a million fans happily keep buying tickets over a season just to watch him play. By the end of the season he has collected $250,000, a fortune that sits far outside whatever pattern D1 represented, even though every single transfer along the way was voluntary and nobody was cheated. Nozick's point is that you can't keep any such "fair pattern" stable over time without constantly interfering with what people freely choose to do with what's already theirs, and he uses this to argue for what he calls entitlement theory: a distribution is just as long as everybody's holdings trace back through legitimate acquisitions and voluntary transfers, whatever pattern that ends up producing.

I brought this up over dinner with two friends, Marcus and Dana, and it turned into a real back-and-forth that I haven't been able to settle since. Roughly reconstructed, it went like this. (1) Marcus said the argument shows that taxing Wilt back down to the D1 pattern amounts to the government reaching into a chain of clean, voluntary transactions and undoing them by force, so any tax scheme designed specifically to restore a "fair" pattern is really just a standing license to override free choices. (2) Dana said the argument only works if D1 already grants everyone an unlimited right to funnel as much of their share as they like to a single person; nothing stops a "fair" distribution from including a rule, from day one, that caps how much any one recipient can receive as a gift, with the excess flowing into a shared pool automatically. The fans could still hand over their quarters, but Wilt would never end up outside the pattern in the first place, so nobody would ever need to reach in afterward and claw anything back. (3) Marcus replied that this just relocates the interference instead of removing it: if a cap stops two people from doing what they want with a quarter that is, at that moment, already legitimately theirs, someone is still overriding a free choice, whether the override happens the instant the money changes hands or twenty years later at tax time. (4) Dana answered that "already legitimately theirs" is exactly what's being smuggled in there: property rights aren't handed down from nature, they're partly defined by whatever rules the money comes attached to in the first place, including limits on transfer, the same way we don't call it "interference" when someone is stopped from selling their vote. A gift ceiling built into D1 from the outset isn't overriding anything, because nobody was ever entitled to give away more than the ceiling allowed. (5) Marcus said fine, but that move seems to prove too much: if any restriction whatsoever can be justified just by declaring it "part of the original terms attached to the money," then a 90% income tax could be defended in exactly the same way, and there's no longer any real daylight between entitlement theory and whatever redistributive scheme you like, which seems like far too cheap a way to rescue "fair patterns" from the argument.

Neither of them budged, and honestly I go back and forth on it every time I replay the conversation in my head. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) one at a time and say who has the better of it at each individual step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's a shared assumption behind the whole exchange that neither Marcus nor Dana ever thought to question.
