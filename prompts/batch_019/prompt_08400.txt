I'm at work on a novel set in the English countryside in the summer of 1914, in the last unclouded season before the war, and I've reached a scene I can't quite get right. Two of my characters — Mrs. Aldous, a widowed schoolteacher who spent several years at one of the small Tolstoyan colonies that had sprung up around England since the eighteen-nineties, and Gerald Fenn, a young painter newly back from Paris and full of talk of "significant form," picked up from his Bloomsbury friends — find themselves seated together at a dinner party shortly after Mrs. Aldous has been rereading "What Is Art?" Their hostess, hoping for a quiet evening, instead gets a running argument about what art is actually for.

I'd like your help drafting this scene as a dialogue, because I want the argument itself to carry the chapter rather than just gesture at "two opposing views" in the abstract. Mrs. Aldous should press the real substance of Tolstoy's position: that a work's worth lies in how sincerely and clearly it transmits a feeling, and in how far that feeling can be shared by every kind of person, labourer and aristocrat alike, rather than only by those with a cultivated eye. At some point — and I believe this is a conclusion Tolstoy actually drew himself, though a novelist's memory for footnotes is not to be trusted — she should say, without flinching, that by this same test War and Peace belongs among the bad art of his own class, with only his simplest peasant tales passing muster. Gerald should give just as serious an account of why a work's value might lie entirely in relations of line and colour rather than in any feeling communicated at all, and he should press Mrs. Aldous on the awkward results of her own position rather than simply insisting that beauty needs no excuses. Neither should have the easier time of it, and neither should convert the other by the end; I would rather they end up seeing exactly where their disagreement actually bites than have one quietly flatten the other. Could you draft this scene for me as a dialogue, something like eight to twelve exchanges long?
