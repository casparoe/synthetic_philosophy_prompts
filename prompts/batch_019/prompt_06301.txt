I'm finishing a magazine feature on split-brain research and the unity of consciousness, and I keep running up against how flat most popular coverage leaves the philosophical question. The standard treatment reports the classic Sperry-era dissociations — a commissurotomy patient shown a word only in the left visual field can't say what they saw but can retrieve the matching object with their left hand, and in Levy, Trevarthen and Sperry's chimeric-faces experiments patients verbally name one half of a split face while pointing to the other — and then simply asserts that the surgery leaves "two people in one skull," as if the neuroscience alone settled a question philosophers have been arguing over since Nagel's 1971 essay on brain bisection. Rereading that essay alongside the more recent literature, including Pinto and colleagues' 2017 paper reporting that split-brain patients retain far more cross-field integration than the textbook picture allows, has only convinced me the popular version stops exactly where the hard part begins.

My editor has agreed to run a sidebar in which two philosophers of mind fight this out directly rather than having me adjudicate it, so I'd like you to draft that dialogue. One speaker should defend something in the neighborhood of the two-streams view associated with Roland Puccetti: that under experimental conditions, at least, a split-brain patient houses two distinct subjects of experience, and that the dissociations are evidence of this rather than artifacts of how information happens to get reported out. The other should defend an integrationist or unity view of the kind the Pinto results and Tim Bayne's "switch model" have been used to support: that there is one subject throughout, whose access to information is bottlenecked rather than doubled. I don't want this to just restage the 1981 Behavioral and Brain Sciences exchange over Puccetti's paper in period costume — both speakers should be reckoning with the newer cross-integration findings as well as the classic dissociations. Please give both positions at their strongest: the two-streams theorist has to say something real about the seamless coordination these patients display outside the lab, the unity theorist has to say something real about the documented cases of simultaneous, conflicting intentions between the two hands, and each should press the other on loaded terms like "subject" and "stream of consciousness" rather than letting them pass unexamined.

Keep it a genuine back-and-forth of roughly 900 to 1300 words, weighted toward argument rather than exposition, and don't force a resolution — I'd rather it end with the real fault line exposed than with an artificial synthesis.
