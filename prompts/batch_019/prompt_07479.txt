Been down a bit of a Quine rabbit hole lately (the gavagai stuff in Word and Object) and i cant unsee the parallel to the ELK problem that made the rounds on LW/Alignment Forum a few years back. ARC's setup has the reporter head implement either a 'direct translator' that says what the predictor's internal Bayes net actually represents, or a 'human simulator' that just predicts what a person watching the camera feed would guess -- and those two strategies can agree on every training-time question yet come completely apart once someone slips a screen in front of the camera and the diamond's actually gone. That's basically radical translation with extra steps: multiple translation manuals fit all the behavioral evidence but disagree about what's actually true. Quine's own reaction to indeterminacy in ordinary translation was basically a shrug -- no fact of the matter about which manual is 'right', and nothing of practical importance hinges on picking one. Do you think he'd extend that same shrug to ELK, where picking the 'wrong' manual means trusting a deceptive AI, or does something in his actual naturalism/behaviorism force the two cases apart? Ground it in his real arguments (radical translation, inscrutability of reference, naturalized epistemology) rather than generic guessing about what a mid-century philosopher would make of AI.
