I'm a retired physicist who fell into reading about the Vienna Circle this year almost by accident (started with a biography of Einstein that kept going on about Schlick, then went down the rabbit hole - kind of funny that a physicist ends up in philosophy, since that's basically my own trajectory too). I'd always had this vague picture of Carnap as the ultimate dry logic-chopper, all protocol sentences and syntax, none of it touching the real world, so I was suprised to learn how openly left-leaning he and most of the Circle actually were. Carnap traced his own sympathy for socialism back to the German youth movement he'd been part of before WW1, and he was close with Neurath, who at one point literally ran a planning office during the short-lived Bavarian Soviet Republic, arguing you could run a whole economy "in kind," with no money or prices anywhere in the loop. Even the 1929 manifesto the Circle put out reads, once you know that background, less like a neutral statement about logical method and more like a program for a scientifically planned society.

Anyway, here's what's been rattling around in my head since: Carnap died in 1970, decades before anyone had the computing power to make Neurath's "planning without prices" idea look practically serious again. But theres a whole line of argument going around now - Cockshott and Cottrell's stuff, that "People's Republic of Walmart" book - claiming that modern optimization and machine learning basically dissolve the old Mises/Hayek objection that comprehensive planning can't handle the information problem, i.e. that a big enough model could just compute the allocation instead of leaving it to prices. Knowing what you know about the actual content of Carnap's philosophy, and not just the fact that he leaned left - the verificationism, the physicalism, his principle of tolerance, his general allergy to metaphysically loaded political rhetoric - do you think he'd have found that kind of argument congenial, or is there something built into his own commitments that should make him more cautious about it than Neurath would have been? I'm less interested in a straight yes/no than in which specific piece of his philosophical machinery ends up doing the work.
