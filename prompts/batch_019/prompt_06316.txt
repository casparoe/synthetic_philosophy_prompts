I'm a retired analytical chemist, and since I hung up my lab coat a couple of years back I've been reading my way trough philosophy of science for fun, on the assumption that Popper had basically settled the "what counts as science" question -- which I'm now discovering is very much not the consensus once you get into Lakatos and the rest of the people arguing with him. This week I got stuck on a case I half-remembered from an old astronomy elective decades ago, and it turns out to be a favorite illustration in this literature: Le Verrier used literally the same move twice on two unexplained orbital wobbles. In the 1840s he took the perturbations in Uranus's orbit, kept Newtonian gravity fixed, and calculated where an unseen planet would have to sit to cause them -- and Neptune turned up almost exactly there, one of the great predictive triumphs in the history of science. About a decade later he ran the identical play on Mercury: there was a small leftover precession in its perihelion (something like 40-odd arcseconds a century, though I could be misremembering the exact figure) that Newtonian gravity couldn't account for even after subtracting the pull of all the known planets. So he confidently postulated another unseen planet, "Vulcan," orbiting inside Mercury. It was never found, and it took Einstein's general relativity, decades later, to explain the precession with no extra planet required.

What's been bugging me is that I can't find a clean answer for what, if anything, made the Neptune move good science and the Vulcan move bad science, given that structurally they look like the identical maneuver -- an auxiliary hypothesis about an unseen mass, protecting the same background theory from what looked like a falsification -- and both were, in principle, testable just by going and looking. The standard line I keep running into is that Neptune's prediction was "novel" and Vulcan's wasn't, but that feels like it smuggles in hindsight: at the moment Le Verrier proposed it, Vulcan looks exactly as novel and falsifiable a prediction as Neptune did. So could you actually walk through where naive falsificationism breaks down on this pair of cases, and whether Lakatos (or anyone else's patch on Popper) gives a principled way to tell the two apart before the fact, rather than retroactively, once we already know which one panned out?
