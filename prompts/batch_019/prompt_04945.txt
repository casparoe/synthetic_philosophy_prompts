The question the students were set is this: "In the Lectures on Religious Belief, Wittgenstein discusses the case of a believer who holds that there will be a Last Judgement. Explain what Wittgenstein means when he denies that this belief is 'grounded' in evidence in the way an ordinary empirical belief is grounded, and explain why he nonetheless refuses simply to call the believer unreasonable. And here, in full, is the student's answer:

"Wittgenstein's discussion of the man who believes in a Last Judgement is meant to bring out that this kind of belief does not function as an empirical hypothesis that could be confirmed or disconfirmed by evidence in the ordinary sense. If it were that kind of hypothesis, we would expect the believer to treat contrary evidence as at least relevant to whether he keeps holding it, and we would expect a disagreement with a non-believer to look like an ordinary factual dispute, one where a party might eventually concede the point. Wittgenstein's point is that nothing is allowed to count as evidence against the belief, and, crucially, that this is not a mark of the believer's irrationality: it is a sign that 'believing' has a different grammar here, since the belief functions to regulate the whole of the believer's life rather than to state a fact he expects to observe or fails to observe. This is also why Wittgenstein neither straightforwardly agrees with the believer nor contradicts him: to say 'yes, there will be a Judgement' or 'no, there will not' would treat the disagreement as though it sat on the same plane as an ordinary factual disagreement, whereas for Wittgenstein believer and non-believer are, strictly, talking past one another, holding the picture in quite different ways. It is exactly this analysis of religious utterances as belonging to their own 'language-game,' with its own 'form of life,' that Wittgenstein spells out over the course of the Lectures on Religious Belief, using those very terms to mark religious discourse off from empirical discourse.

Because Wittgenstein treats religious belief as sealed off within its own language-game, insulated from the kind of evidential appraisal appropriate to empirical claims, his position looks like a textbook case of what Kai Nielsen called 'Wittgensteinian fideism': roughly, the view that a form of discourse carries its own internal criteria of intelligibility and so cannot be rationally criticized from outside. D. Z. Phillips, the philosopher most associated with this reading, accepted Nielsen's label and built his account of religious belief around it, arguing that because religious and scientific discourse are logically distinct language-games, it is simply a category mistake to hold religious belief to standards imported from science or from ordinary empirical reasoning. So on this last point I think the 'fideist' reading of Wittgenstein is basically right: once belief is relocated from the plane of fact to the plane of grammar, there is no room left to ask whether it is rational by any standard external to the practice itself."
