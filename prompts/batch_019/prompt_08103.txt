I'm a retired analytical chemist (34 years split between university and industry labs) who fell down a philosophy rabbit hole after retiring, and I could use some help thinking through something that's been bugging me for weeks. I joined a little philosophy discussion group that meets at the library, and last month we spent two sessions on the epistemology of testimony -- specifically the fight between "reductionists" and "anti-reductionists" over whether believing what other people tell you is its own basic source of knowledge, or whether it always has to be cashed out in terms of more fundamental stuff like perception, memory, and induction over who's proven reliable in the past. It hit close to home because as a scientist I spent my whole career trusting numbers I never personally re-measured -- calibration constants from someone else's lab, a competitor's reported yield, a grad students late-night reading of a gel -- and I'd never really examined what justifies that kind of trust until now.

From what I've read, Hume is the classic reductionist: he thought our confidence in testimony has to be earned the same way confidence in any other regularity is earned, by repeatedly observing that testimony and truth go together. Reid pushed back hard, insisting we have something like a built-in "principle of credulity" that makes testimony basic, on a par with perception, not something bootstrapped from experience after the fact. In the contemporary literature it seems Coady picks up Reid's side, while Fricker tries to rescue something reductionist-ish with her "local reductionism," where each individual testimonial exchange still has to be monitored and assessed by the hearer even though the general practice doesn't need some grand inductive proof standing behind it. Honestly I'm still fuzzy on whether local reductionism is really doing different work than anti-reductionism, or whether that's a merely verbal disagreement wearing two costumes and not two genuinely seperate positions.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed reductionist and a committed anti-reductionist working through this? I want real back-and-forth, not two prepared speeches passing by each other -- every dialogue like this I've read online has one side quietly written as the smart one, and I'd like both of these two to actually hold their own and press each other on their strongest objections. Please have them get into the regress/bootstrapping worry (you'd need a mountain of testimonially-acquired background belief just to run the induction reductionism demands), the case of small children trusting what they're told long before they could have any track record to go on, and that nagging question of whether local reductionism collapses into anti-reductionism or is a genuine third option. Feel free to have them hash it out over a concrete case, maybe something like trusting a colleague's reported measurement, if that helps keep it from floating off into pure abstraction.
