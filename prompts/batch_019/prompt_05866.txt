My father was moved into the memory care wing of an assisted-living facility three months ago, and partly because of that I have just been asked to join the facility's newly formed technology ethics committee. Our first substantial case is a "preference-adaptive" companion robot the facility wants to pilot for residents with mild-to-moderate dementia. Rather than asking residents to rank activities, foods, or music directly -- something many of them can no longer do reliably -- the vendor fits a reward function to each resident using inverse reinforcement learning over logged behavior: how long a resident lingers at an activity, whether they finish a meal, when they turn away from a suggested game, and so on. That inferred reward function then drives what the robot offers going forward, and the vendor's pitch is that this "lets residents' own choices speak" more honestly than self-report ever could.

One committee member with a machine-learning background raised something that has been nagging at me since: this is not simply an engineering wrinkle that more logging would smooth out. Any such procedure has to assume some model of how much apparent inconsistency in a resident's behavior counts as meaningful signal versus noise, and, depending on that assumption, quite different reward functions -- some crediting a resident with almost none of their apparent likes -- can rationalize the exact same behavioral record equally well. Whoever fixes that assumption is quietly deciding, on behalf of people who often can no longer tell us themselves, which of their reactions revealed what they actually want and which were just fatigue, confusion, or an artifact of the limited menu of options on offer in the first place.

I have been asked to draft the position our committee will vote on ahead of the pilot's ninety-day review, and I would like your help with a first pass. Please write it as a short decision memo, addressed to colleagues who are not machine-learning specialists, recommending approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions. The memo should take the vendor's strongest argument seriously -- that some form of behavioral inference is plausibly better than the realistic alternatives on offer, such as a one-off intake questionnaire or staff intuition -- rather than dismissing it, while being precise about exactly where the technical assumptions inside the inference procedure are doing ethically consequential work that residents and their families never see and cannot contest. If you recommend approval, with or without conditions, specify what should be monitored, by whom, and what findings would justify revisiting the decision at the ninety-day mark.
