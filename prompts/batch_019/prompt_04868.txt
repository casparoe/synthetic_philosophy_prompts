I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mostly on moral psychology, and a friend and I run this small, pretty amateurish podcast where we get grad students and visiting speakers to talk through their current work. Our guest next month just published a book defending a coincident-reasons-style account of moral worth -- the view that what makes an act morally worthy isn't that you did it "from duty" or under the concept of morality at all, just that the reasons that actually moved you happen to match, in content, the reasons that make the act right. I've been TAing intro ethics this semester and the Groundwork's sorrowful-philanthropist passage reliably starts a small riot in section every single time, so I've been chewing on whether this view actually disolves that problem or just relabels it, and I don't wanna have the episode be forty minutes of her restating the thesis in slightly different words.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions? I want at least one on Kant's own case: the man who helps only because duty commands it once grief has burned off all his natural sympathy, versus the warm-hearted philanthropist Kant famously denies worth to. Does her view actually flip that verdict, and if so, how does it stop "coincidence of reasons" from certifying basically any pleasant motive as worthy -- my guess is she'll say the philanthropist's real motivating reason is other-regarding concern, not self-regarding pleasure, so the follow-up should press on how fine-grained the "content" of a reason has to be before that move either trivializes the view or makes almost nothing ever coincide. I want one comparing her to Arpaly on Huck Finn -- since Arpaly's story about responsiveness to right-making features and the coincidence story both want the agent's operative reasons to line up with the reasons that make the act right, whats the actual difference supposed to be, and does it survive contact with a guy who can't even consciously articulate the right-making features because his explicit conscience says the opposite thing. And I want one on what I've been privately calling the fair-weather helper problem: someone who'd only help because it feels good to him, and this time it happens to coincide with what morality requires -- that seems to lack moral worth even though the reasons match on paper, so how does she rule him out without just re-importing some version of the old Henson overdetermination test through the back door. Add a follow-up for each of those three keyed to whatever answer she reaches for first, and throw in a couple more questions of your own if you spot a sharp angle, but please skip anything generic like "what drew you to this topic."
