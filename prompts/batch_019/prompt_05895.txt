I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on the political commitments lurking behind the Vienna Circle's official doctrine of value-neutral "unified science." Working through Carnap's intellectual autobiography in the Schilpp volume alongside Reisch's and Uebel's work on the Circle's politics, I keep coming back to how much more entangled it was with practical politics than its logic-chopping reputation suggests: Neurath ran the short-lived Bavarian Soviet Republic's central planning office before taking on Mises and Hayek in the socialist calculation debate with his proposal for "calculation in kind," while Carnap, quieter about it, describes genuine socialist sympathies dating to the German Youth Movement that sit awkwardly next to his official position that logic and unified science are neutral among rival forms of social organization.

A friend in the economics department recently sent me, half as a joke, the "People's Republic of Walmart" argument -- the claim, descending from Cockshott and Cottrell's older computational-planning proposals, that firms like Amazon already plan enormous swathes of the economy in kind using modern logistics software, and that machine-learning-scale data processing has finally dissolved Hayek's knowledge problem, making economy-wide algorithmic planning newly credible again. It struck me as a direct descendant of the fight Neurath had with Mises, and it left me wanting to know how Carnap specifically, not the Circle in general, would have sorted it. I'd like you to work this out using Carnap's actual apparatus rather than a generic "positivists trusted science" gesture: does the internal/external-question distinction from "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" let him treat "AI planning can outperform markets" as a straightforward empirical question for unified science, while cordoning off "we ought to plan the economy this way" as a merely practical matter on which the Principle of Tolerance forbids him from taking sides -- or would his own admitted sympathies, plus his view that value-statements only express or direct rather than assert, mean that tolerance quietly gives way? Please commit to an actual verdict on where Carnap would land, and say whether it leaves him closer to, or further from, Neurath's technocratic optimism than his official doctrine allows.
