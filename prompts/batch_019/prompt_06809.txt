I cover science and ideas for a living, which last month meant sitting in on a small academic workshop on the foundations of rational choice, followed by a dinner at which one of the organizers — mostly for his own amusement — decided to demonstrate the day's central idea, the money pump, on the guests themselves. He asked a good-natured postdoc named Toby to compare the three wines open on the table, pairwise, and to say which he'd prefer if a dollar or two rode on the difference. Toby's answers turned out to be cyclic: he'd pay a dollar to trade his Malbec for the Rioja, another to trade the Rioja for the Chianti, and a third to trade the Chianti back for the Malbec he'd started with. Three trades later he was holding the exact bottle he'd begun the evening with, three dollars poorer, and the table laughed — but the laughter didn't survive the next round of coffee.

(1) Marcus, who works on the philosophy of action, said the demonstration had proven something real: Toby's preferences were irrational, full stop, because that is exactly what a money pump is supposed to show — that an agent whose preferences don't resolve into one consistent ranking can be milked indefinitely and get nothing back for it. (2) Elena, a decision theorist, disagreed: the trick indicted Toby's procedure, not his preferences. He'd been a "naive" chooser, taking each trade on its own; a "sophisticated" chooser, seeing the whole loop coming, would simply have refused the very first trade and kept the bottle he already had, with exactly the same underlying preferences intact. (3) Marcus replied that this just relocates the problem: at the moment of that first offer, Toby, by hypothesis, actually preferred the trade. Refusing an option you currently prefer, because of where taking it eventually leads, doesn't rescue your rationality so much as require you to act against your own preferences on cue — which is no obvious improvement. (4) Elena answered that this is no stranger than a dieter turning down one bite of cake: we assess choices as parts of plans, not one at a time, and a rational agent picks the best available sequence rather than the best next step. That, she said, is what the literature calls "resolute choice" — a matter of diachronic rationality, of what it takes to be rational over time rather than just at an instant — and it isn't a dodge. (5) Marcus's rejoinder was that resolute choice, taken seriously, makes the whole argument toothless: if any exploit can be waved away by appeal to some plan the agent supposedly adopted in advance, then no pattern of preferences, however wild, can ever actually be convicted of irrationality by a money pump — and the entire argument was supposed to be a test that some preferences could fail.

Nobody at the table, myself included, settled this before the check came. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each, who has the better of that specific point, rather than just who wins on balance? I'm especially interested in whether Marcus and Elena are circling a genuinely open problem in the theory of rationality over time, or whether one of them actually has a clean answer that the back-and-forth obscured. Please give a short verdict and justification for each numbered move, then close with a paragraph on anything you think both of them are missing; if you're willing to also say who came out ahead on points overall, save that for the very last line.
