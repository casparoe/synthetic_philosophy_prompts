A friend and I got into a long argument after watching a government spokesperson field questions at a press conference: every answer was a shifting, self-serving story with only a loose relationship to what had actually happened. I called it a textbook case of political lying; my friend insisted "lying" was the wrong word, since nobody in the room seemed to care what was true in the first place, and that something else entirely was going on. We never agreed on what that something else was, or whether the distinction even matters.

Compare Hannah Arendt's account of political lying, developed in "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics," with Harry Frankfurt's account of bullshit in "On Bullshit." Both are concerned with the fate of factual truth in public speech, but they locate the core failure differently: Arendt through the liar's relationship to fact and to the human capacity for action, Frankfurt through the speaker's indifference to truth as such. Spell out precisely where the two accounts diverge, then argue whether the modern, organized political lie that Arendt herself describes—practiced by image-makers and public-relations professionals, rather than by someone concealing a single discrete fact—should still count as lying in her sense, or whether it has actually collapsed into bullshitting in Frankfurt's.
