Hey, I'm working on a piece about medieval resistance theory and fell down a rabbit hole with Aquinas on tyranny. The popular version everyone repeats is basically "Aquinas said its fine to kill tyrants," but then I dug up this bit from the Summa (II-II, Q.42, the question on sedition, old Dominican translation so forgive the clunky phrasing) and now I'm second-guessing that whole narrative:

"A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler... Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government itself. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely."

This reads to me less like a permission slip for assassination and more like Aquinas doing something sneaky with the definition of "sedition" itself so the hard question kind of evaporates. Can you unpack the actual logical move he's making here? And how does it fit with the more cautious stuff in De Regimine Principum about not trusting private citizens to take out a tyrant themselves — is there one coherent doctrine underneath both texts about who's actually allowed to act and when, or is Aquinas just hedging differently depending on what he's writing in the moment?
