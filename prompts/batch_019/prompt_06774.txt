Nick Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher case has physicists on the verge of a costly experiment to decide between two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, which fit all existing evidence equally well but imply wildly different total numbers of observers in the universe (say, a trillion under T1 against a trillion trillion under T2). A philosopher who accepts the Self-Indication Assumption — reason as if you are a random sample from all possible observers, not merely actual ones — can announce from her armchair, before the experiment is run, that T2 is a trillion times likelier than T1 simply because she exists. This is standardly treated as a decisive objection to the Self-Indication Assumption and a point in favor of its rival, the Self-Sampling Assumption (reason as if you are a random sample only from the observers who actually exist within your reference class). Please argue that the objection fails: show either that the very "presumptuousness" it trades on is already built into observation-selection reasoning we accept without complaint elsewhere, or that it confuses an update's being extremely confident with its being illegitimate. Your argument should also explain why the Self-Sampling Assumption's own escape from this problem, purchased at the price of licensing the Doomsday Argument, does not simply amount to reaching the same conclusion more cheaply.
