Twice in the past six months, colleagues have told me that something I wrote "reads like ChatGPT" — once because a grant report of mine used the word "delve," once because a newsletter draft had two em dashes in the same paragraph. I hadn't used any AI tool either time; those are just words and habits I've had for years. Since then I've caught myself, and several people I work with, quietly editing out "delve," "boundaries," "tapestry," "meticulous," and long em-dash constructions before sending anything out, purely to dodge the accusation. It is strange to watch a shared, unwritten rule take hold this quickly: a whole set of ordinary words becoming suspect within about two years, for reasons that have nothing to do with the words themselves.

I am currently auditing a seminar on philosophy of language, and we recently spent two sessions on David Lewis's 1969 book Convention. His account of a convention — a regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, sustained by precedent and common knowledge, where some other regularity would have done equally well — seemed at first like a perfect fit for what's happening with these words. The longer I sit with it, though, the less sure I am. Lewis builds his theory on situations of coincidence of interest, of the kind behind everyone driving on the same side of the road, or the old rule about who calls back after a dropped telephone line. The word-avoidance case looks more adversarial than that: writers want to avoid looking synthetic, language models (and whoever is prompting them) may or may not want to be caught, and detectors and "tell"-word lists are visibly racing to stay a step ahead of both. When I raised this in the seminar, opinions split on whether that adversarial element rules Lewis's framework out entirely.

Since Lewis died in 2001 and never encountered anything like this, I would like your best reconstruction of how he would have analyzed it, reasoning strictly from the apparatus he actually built in Convention — and, if it genuinely bears on the case, from his later remarks on truthfulness and trust as linguistic conventions in "Languages and Language." Would he count today's vocabulary-avoidance as a genuine convention in his technical sense, or does its adversarial structure disqualify it, and if so, what would he say it is instead? What, on his view, would have to be true about common knowledge and precedent among writers, readers, and model developers for it to qualify either way? And given how quickly the list of suspect words shifts as detection methods and model outputs adapt to one another, what would he predict about whether this ever settles into a stable convention rather than continually dissolving before it can?
