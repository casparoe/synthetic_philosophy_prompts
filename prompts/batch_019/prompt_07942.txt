Since retiring from a career in physics and picking up a serious philosophy habit, I've fallen down a rabbit hole on Thomson's lamp -- the lamp that gets switched on then off infinitely many times inside two minutes (once at 1 min, again at 1.5, again at 1.75, and so on forever), where the puzzle is whether it's on or off once the two minutes are actually up. Thomson used this to argue that "supertasks" are flat out logically impossible. But then I read that Benacerraf wrote a reply back in '62 basically saying Thomson's argument doesn't work, because nothing about the sequence of states before the two minute mark logically fixes what the lamp is doing AT the two minute mark -- the setup just under-specifies things, so there's no real contradiction, only an open question. That felt very familiar from my old life doing physics: its the same shape of problem as a sequence like 1,0,1,0,... simply failing to converge, rather then converging to some contradictory value.

What I can't work out for myself is whether Benacerraf actually kills the paradox stone dead, or whether there's still something genuinely troubling left over about supertasks once you grant his point -- maybe to do with whether "the state at the limit" even has to be a well-defined thing, or whether a lamp like this could ever be physically built, even in principle. Could you write me an essay that lays out Thomson's original argument and Benacerraf's reply in some real depth, weighs up whether the reply actually succeeds, and then commits to an actual verdict -- are lamp-type supertasks logically coherent, physically possible, both, or neither? Don't just leave it hanging at "reasonable people disagree."
