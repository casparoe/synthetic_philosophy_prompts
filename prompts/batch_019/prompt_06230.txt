After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy in earnest, and I keep returning to David Lewis's brief 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", whose plain, step-by-step style of argument I find especially congenial. Lewis's puzzle is this: we recoil at the thought of a devoted friend serving a burglar's prison term or a murderer's death sentence, yet we raise no objection when a friend simply pays an offender's fine on his behalf, even when that fine is a serious punishment rather than a nominal fee. He briefly entertains, and then dismisses in a couple of sentences, what strikes me as the most natural way of defusing this puzzle without conceding any ground to penal substitution itself: that fines are simply a second-rate, compromised form of punishment (compromised precisely because a third party's gift can so easily discharge them), so that tolerating substitution there is just the known cost of an imperfect institution, not evidence of any real ambivalence about the principle that only the guilty may permissibly suffer for their guilt. Lewis sets this resolution aside mainly on the ground that, as a matter of fact, we do not complain about fines in quite that way. Please construct the strongest possible case for the position Lewis dismisses too quickly, and then assess whether, if your argument succeeds, it also undermines his closing suggestion that our shared wavering over fines is itself some evidence that penal substitution might, somehow, make sense after all.
