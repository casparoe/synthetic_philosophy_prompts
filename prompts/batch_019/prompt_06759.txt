So I'm a PhD student in cognitive science (my own work is on auditory stuff, totally unrelated) and I've been re-reading a bunch of the old Sperry/Gazzaniga split-brain papers for my quals reading list, and there's one phenomenon I can't stop chewing on outside of work hours. Standard setup: corpus callosotomy patient, a word or picture gets flashed to just the left visual field so only the mute right hemisphere "sees" it, and the body does something in response (the left hand picks up an object, or the patient gets up and starts walking) - but when you ask the patient why, the talking half of the brain, which never received the information, just invents a reason on the spot and says it with total confidence. Gazzaniga called this module "the interpreter." There's also older case-report stuff about intermanual conflict right after surgery - like one hand buttoning a shirt while the other is undoing the buttons - which I guess is less consistently documented, but it's exactly the kind of detail that makes this feel less like an abstract though experiment and more like theres actually something it's like to be the hand that keeps losing that argument.

It's messing with my head a bit because it suggests the feeling of being one continuous "I" might just be a story the left hemisphere tells after the fact, even in people with an intact callosum - like maybe we're all narrating over a messier committee of subsystems, and callosotomy patients just make the seams visible. I brought this up in my advisor meeting hoping for some insight and got "sounds like a limitations section, not group therapy," so now I'm bringing it here instead.

Could you write me a short story that actually sits inside that disorientation, rather than reaching for the easy "twist: there were two people in one skull all along!" reveal? I don't have strong opinions on setting - lab, home, somewhere else entirely, whatever serves the story - but I'd really like it if the mute, non-verbal side of things got some kind of interiority or voice on the page, seeing as in real life it never gets to argue back.
