I spent 35 years running a nematode lab on lifespan-extension mutants, and since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy of medicine rabbit hole. Here's what's been stuck in my head: is aging itself a disease, or just life doing what life does? I read up on Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory hoping a hard statistical definition would settle it, but his reference-class move -- health as statistically typical function for your age-sex-species group -- seems to make most age-related decline (arthritis, cataracts, and so on) come out as not-disease, since its exactly typical for old people. He apparently once floated fixing this by measuring everyone against young-adult function instead, which then makes basically all of old age pathological by definition. Neither horn feels like "just reading off the biology" to me, which is what's got me thinking the normativists are onto something.

Could you write me a dialogue -- say they run into each other at a biogerontology conference -- between a committed naturalist defending something like Boorse's view and an equally sharp normativist, hashing out whether aging counts as a disease? I want real back and forth, each one engaging the others actual moves rather than talking past them, and I especially want that reference-class dilemma pressure-tested, since it's the bit that's been bugging me most. Doesn't need to resolve neatly if it wouldn't resolve neatly in real life.
