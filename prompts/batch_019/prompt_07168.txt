So I've been re-reading Tolstoy's What Is Art? for an essay I'm procrastinating on, and it's dragged me into an argument with my critique partner that I can't resolve on my own. Context: I write pretty serious literary fiction, the kind that gets nice little reviews in journals nobody reads and sells maybe 600 copies, and she writes self-published dragon-riders-falling-in-love romantasy that she serializes on Royal Road and then cleans up for Kindle Unlimited, and outsells me something like 400 to 1. We've been half-jokingly arguing for weeks about who the "real" artist is, and then I made the mistake of bringing Tolstoy into it.

Here's what's bugging me: Tolstoy's case in that book rests on a pretty specific, and honestly kind of extreme, set of criteria -- not just "he liked simple moral stories." Art, for him, has to "infect" the audience with a feeling the artist actually, sincerely felt, and how infectious it is depends on the individuality, clarity, and above all sincerity of that feeling -- and on top of that, real "good" art has to be accessible accross class lines, not just to an educated elite (he goes on for pages about how a simple folk tale or hymn beats out sophisticated stuff aimed at connoisseurs). He's also not just picking on other people to make this point -- he trashes King Lear as basically a fraud, and in the same book more or less throws his own War and Peace and Anna Karenina under the bus as failed, upper-class "counterfeit" art, keeping only a handful of his simplest, folk-style stories as the real thing.

So, taking all of that seriously rather than the cartoon version -- what do you think Tolstoy would actually say about the romantasy/fanfic boom happening right now? These serialized romances are about as sincerely, unashamedly infectious as fiction gets, and they reach a genuinely massive, unpretentious, cross-class audience in a way my little literary novel never will. But they're also mass-produced for a subscription and ad-revenue market, wildly formulaic, and dismissed by critics in almost exactly the language Tolstoy uses for "counterfeit" art made to entertain rather than to express. Would he have to admit, on his own logic, that my critique partner is making the more genuine art? Or is there a way of applying his actual criteria that still lets him condemn this stuff, and if so, what would that argument look like? I'd rather have his real reasoning worked through than just "Tolstoy = liked simple things, romantasy = simple, therefore Tolstoy approves."
