For a dissertation chapter on rational agency and AI corrigibility, I've spent the last few weeks working through the recent back-and-forth on the "shutdown problem": the challenge of designing an advanced agent that will always let itself be shut down, without thereby creating an incentive to manipulate that very mechanism. The proposal I keep returning to is Elliott Thornley's: train the agent to have incomplete preferences, so that it simply lacks any preference between trajectories that differ only in when shutdown occurs, and have it obey something like a "timestep dominance" principle instead. The hope is that such an agent never pays a cost to shift probability mass toward a longer or shorter run, and so has no reason to touch the button either way. But whenever I bring this up in my reading group, someone raises the standard worry about incomplete preferences in general: that an agent with genuine gaps in its preferences can be walked through a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off by its own lights, and that a sufficiently capable, reflective agent would therefore either get exploited or iron out the gaps by self-modifying -- in which case the timestep-dominance fix collapses right back into the manipulation incentive it was meant to remove. This matters to me beyond the technical point, since it bears on whether shutdownability can be grounded in a coherent theory of rational preference at all, or whether it is structurally at odds with being a rational agent in the first place.

I'd like to see the two sides actually argue this out, rather than read yet another literature summary. Could you write a dialogue -- maybe set in the bar after an AI safety workshop -- between a researcher who defends building shutdownable agents through incomplete preferences and a colleague who presses the exploitability objection? I want several real exchanges rather than two speeches talking past each other: the critic should sketch, at least in outline, the kind of sequential-choice scenario that would actually exploit a timestep-dominant agent, and the defender should have to respond to that specific scenario -- whether by biting the bullet on some cost, by restricting what trades a real-world counterparty could actually offer such an agent, or by denying that the exploit does what the critic thinks it does -- rather than just retreating to "it's the best proposal anyone has." Neither of them should be easy to satisfy, and I'd like to come away genuinely unsure which one has the better of it.
