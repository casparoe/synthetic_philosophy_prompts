I have been asked to give this year's commencement address at the law school, in a spring when several graduating students -- a few of them from my own seminar on political obligation -- were arrested during a wave of campus protests. Some accepted the citations that followed without contest; others did not. Within the year, most of these graduates will be admitted to the bar and sworn to uphold the very laws some of them spent the spring breaking, and several of my faculty colleagues, who taught these same students Rawls's conditions for justified civil disobedience -- that the society already be nearly just, that the act appeal publicly to the community's sense of justice, and that fidelity to law be shown precisely through willingness to accept punishment -- will be in the audience listening for whether I get the theory right, even as most of the parents present have never read a page of Rawls. Please write the address: it must neither scold the students who were arrested nor lionize them regardless of how they conducted themselves, it needs to be fully intelligible to the relatives in the room, and it has to do real work with the distinction between civil disobedience and ordinary lawbreaking rather than resting on something generic about standing up for one's convictions.
