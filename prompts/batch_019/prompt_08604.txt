I cover science and ideas for a magazine, which usually means these debates arrive on my desk pre-packaged in journal articles; it's rarer that I get to watch one unfold live, over something as low-stakes as a pub quiz. That happened a few weekends ago, when two friends of mine, Farah and Julian, spent the better part of an hour arguing about a third friend, Nora. Nora captains our trivia team and enforces a strict house rule — no cancellations inside 24 hours, because it leaves the rest of us scrambling for a substitute — with a mildly scolding group text to whoever breaks it. Three weeks ago, Nora herself dropped out of a match nine hours ahead of time, for a reason that, by her own later admission, amounted to a better offer for the evening. Last week, when our teammate Diego canceled with about 20 hours' notice for what sounded like a genuine scheduling conflict, Nora sent him the usual text about respecting everyone else's time. Diego shrugged it off, but Farah and Julian kept arguing about it long after he'd left the group chat.

As best I can reconstruct it: (1) Farah said Nora had no standing to send that text, full stop — you don't get to enforce a rule on someone else that you broke yourself three weeks earlier, whatever the merits of Diego's excuse. (2) Julian said that conflates two separate questions: whether Diego's cancellation was inconsiderate, and whether Nora is a hypocrite. Nora's own record doesn't make Diego's excuse any thinner, and refusing to credit a true criticism because of who delivered it is just shooting the messenger. (3) Farah answered that she wasn't disputing the facts of the case, only who gets to press them: there's a difference between a complaint being correct and someone having the right to be the one voicing it, and Nora forfeited that right the moment she started enforcing on others a rule she quietly excuses herself from. (4) Julian said that can't be right as a general principle, because applied consistently it would disqualify almost everyone from ever criticizing anything — nobody lives up to every norm they endorse, all the time, and if a single lapse were enough to permanently forfeit the right to object, we'd have to give up on holding each other to anything. (5) Farah said Nora's case wasn't a single lapse: it was carrying on enforcing the rule on everyone else without ever acknowledging that her own cancellation had been the very same offense. It's the unacknowledged double standard that costs Nora her standing, Farah said, not the bare fact that she, too, once broke the rule.
