Q. (a) [7 pts] State Zorn's Lemma precisely, and use it to prove that every vector space has a basis.
(b) [8 pts] It's a theorem (due to Andreas Blass) that "every vector space has a basis" isn't merely implied by the Axiom of Choice — it's fully equivalent to it over ZF, since assuming every vector space has a basis is enough to derive AC back. Is the same true of the Banach-Tarski paradox? That is, could you run an analogous argument to recover AC from the existence of Banach-Tarski-style decompositions? Explain your reasoning, and say what it would even mean for two statements to be "equivalent" in this sense.
(c) [5 pts] AC, and results that depend on it like the Well-Ordering Theorem, get called "non-constructive" a lot. Using the Well-Ordering Theorem as your example, explain what that actually means.

Student's answer:

(a) Zorn's Lemma: if (P, ≤) is a nonempty poset in which every chain has an upper bound in P, then P has a greatest element. Apply this to a vector space V: let P be the set of linearly independant subsets of V, ordered by inclusion. Given a chain {A_i} in P, the union ⋃A_i is still linearly independent — any dependence among finitely many vectors in the union would have to already sit inside one single A_i, since the A_i are nested, contradicting that A_i is independent. So the union is an upper bound and lies in P, every chain has one, and Zorn's Lemma gives us a greatest element B of P. B has to span V: if some v wasn't in the span of B, then B ∪ {v} would still be independent and would properly contain B, contradicting that B was the greatest element of P. So B is independent and spans V, i.e. B is a basis.

(b) I actually think Banach-Tarski comes out equivalent too, for basically the same reason. The way Blass's argument works is you build a specific vector space out of the family of sets you're choosing from, so that any basis for it just is a choice function for that family. Banach-Tarski leans on the same kind of move: to get the paradoxical decomposition you need to pick a representative point from each of continuum-many orbits of the rotation group acting on the sphere, with no rule for doing it, which is the same unrestricted choice-making that bases require. So I'd bet you could build an analogous family of sets out of a paradoxical decomposition and pull a choice function back out of it, meaning Banach-Tarski and AC are equivalent, not just AC implying Banach-Tarski. And "equivalent over ZF" here just means each statement, added to plain ZF, is strong enough to prove the other one — its not about the statements meaning the same thing, just about what they're each able to derive.

(c) The Well-Ordering Theorem says every set — including something uncountable like R — can be well-ordered, meaning there's a total order on it where every nonempty subset has a least element. The usual order on R isn't like that (R itself, or an interval like (0,1), has no least element), so whatever well-order AC promises already has to look totally unlike the order we're used to. And nobody has ever actually written one down for R — the theorem just says one exists, it doesn't hand you a rule for deciding, given two real numbers, which one the well-order says comes first. That's what "non-constructive" means here: it's a pure existence proof with no algorithm attached, which is exactly the kind of move that people with constructivist objections to AC are objecting to.
