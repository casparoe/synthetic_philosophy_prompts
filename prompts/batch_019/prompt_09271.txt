Until her death last month, my closest friend of thirty years was an experimental physicist who spent her career running tests of Bell's inequality, including in one of the loophole-free experiments of the 2010s. I am a retired chemist myself, and it was our long conversations about what her results actually meant that pushed me toward reading philosophy in retirement. She had no patience for the mystical gloss people reflexively put on her work at dinner parties -- that it proves everything in the universe is secretly connected, or that it licenses some kind of faster-than-light influence -- and she corrected it every time, gently but firmly. Yet a few weeks before she died she told me, quite deliberately, that something genuinely startling does follow from what she had spent her life demonstrating: that the world cannot be both local and 'real' in the sense that she and Einstein once hoped it was, even though nothing in her results permits a signal to outrun light. She asked that whoever spoke at her funeral get exactly that right, no more and no less, and the task has now fallen to me. Please write the eulogy, for a room split evenly between her old laboratory colleagues, who will catch any overclaim at once, and family who have never heard of a Bell inequality and are simply grieving -- it needs to comfort both without a single sentence she would have flagged as sloppy.
