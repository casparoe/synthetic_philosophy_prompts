I am drafting a novel set during the July Crisis of 1914, and a purely technical question of craft has pulled me into philosophy of history. Hempel's covering-law model holds that to explain the war's outbreak is, at bottom, to show that it followed from antecedent conditions together with general laws of political or social behavior, even though, as Hempel himself conceded, working historians typically offer only "explanation sketches" rather than the fully worked-out argument this requires. Danto's narrativist alternative denies that this is what real historical understanding involves: on his account, explanation proceeds through devices such as the narrative sentence, which redescribes an earlier event (say, the assassination in Sarajevo) in language only available in light of what came later, so that explaining and storytelling turn out to be much the same activity. I would like you to compare these two accounts of historical explanation in some depth, and then take a stand: when a historian such as Christopher Clark explains the war's outbreak in The Sleepwalkers by emphasizing contingency and the interlocking miscalculations of many actors rather than deep structural causes, is he tacitly relying on covering laws he never states, or does his narrative achieve genuine explanation without them? Defend your answer against the strongest objection you can construct from the opposing side.
