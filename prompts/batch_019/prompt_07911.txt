"Patients who undergo a commissurotomy have the corpus callosum, the main bundle of fibers connecting the two cerebral hemispheres, surgically cut in order to stop epileptic seizures from spreading from one hemisphere to the other. Because the hemispheres can no longer communicate directly, Sperry and Gazzaniga were able to show that each side can process information on its own. In their classic experiment, a word is flashed only to a patient's left visual field, so it is only sent to the right hemisphere. Patients typically say they saw nothing, because the right hemisphere usually cannot produce speech, but when asked to reach into a bag of objects with their left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere), they can correctly pick out the object that was named, showing that the right hemisphere did register and understand the word even though it couldn't say so.

Philosophers disagree about what to make of this. In "Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness," Nagel argues that these results show that split-brain patients really do have two separate streams of consciousness running side by side, meaning there are, strictly speaking, two minds under one skull. Bayne pushes back against this two-minds picture with what he calls the "switch model": rather than two consciousnesses coexisting, there is only one stream of consciousness at any given moment, but which hemisphere is "hosting" that stream switches rapidly depending on the task, which he thinks fits better with how ordinary and coordinated split-brain patients seem outside of lab settings.

My own take is that because the two hemispheres are completely cut off from each other with no way for information to pass between them, the two-minds view has to be correct, and unity of consciousness is therefore not some deep fact about the mind but just an accident of the brain being wired a certain way."
