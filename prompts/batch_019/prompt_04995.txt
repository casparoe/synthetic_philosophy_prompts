I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of language this term, and we've just moved from Frege's classic puzzle about identity statements to Kripke's discussion of belief reports in "A Puzzle about Belief." I feel like I have a decent grip on the original puzzle: "Hesperus is Phosphorus" seems to convey real information while "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial, and Frege's proposed fix is that co-referring names can differ in sense, or mode of presentation, even while sharing a referent, which is supposed to explain the difference in cognitive significance. Where I've gotten stuck — and where a few classmates and I ended up arguing past each other after class — is Kripke's Paderewski case, which is meant to complicate this tidy picture.

As I understand the case, Peter is a competent, monolingual English speaker who hears reports of a brilliant pianist named "Paderewski" and, impressed, sincerely assents to "Paderewski has musical talent." Later, quite separately, Peter hears of a Polish statesman also called "Paderewski" — in fact the very same man, though Peter has no inkling of this — and, reasoning that a mere politician is unlikely to have that kind of gift, comes to sincerely assent to "Paderewski has no musical talent." Ordinary disquotational principles then seem to force us to attribute to Peter both the belief that Paderewski has musical talent and the belief that he does not, even though Peter is perfectly rational and is not equivocating in any obvious way: unlike in Kripke's related Pierre/"Londres"-London case, there is no translation at issue here and no second name in play, just one name used twice within a single idiolect.

What I'd like help with is this: since only a single name is involved, and there is no obvious linguistic ambiguity of the sort Frege's apparatus was designed to locate, why exactly does Kripke take the Paderewski case to be a problem even for a fully convinced Fregean, rather than a straightforward instance of the very phenomenon the sense/reference distinction was invented to handle? I'd like you to reconstruct Kripke's reasoning for treating the puzzle as neutral between Fregean and Millian theories of names, and then assess a possible Fregean comeback on which the two uses of "Paderewski" express distinct senses after all — say, because Peter tacitly associates each occurrence with a different "mental file" or ground of introduction, even though the public linguistic meaning of the name is unchanged. Does that move genuinely save the Fregean account of cognitive significance, or does it just relabel the puzzle without resolving what's actually doing the work?
