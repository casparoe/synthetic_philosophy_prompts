Okay so this has been bugging me for like a week now. In AP Environmental Science we spent a big chunk of a unit on Aldo Leopold's land ethic - this idea that what's actually morally right or wrong depends on whether it keeps the whole ecosystem healthy and balanced, not on what happens to individual animals. Our teachers example was that culling an overpopulated deer herd, or just letting wolves rip apart an elk, can be totally fine (even good) if thats what keeps the system in balance. Then over the weekend I fell down a youtube rabbithole about "wild animal suffering," which I guess is a whole thing in effective altruism circles - the idea that trillions of wild animals live short, brutal lives full of starvation, disease and getting eaten alive, and that this suffering matters morally just as much as human suffering does, so maybe we even have some obligation to intervene in nature to reduce it.

These feel like they're coming from totally opposite starting points to me: Leopold's thing seems to care about the species/ecosystem/"land" as the real unit that matters, where individual pain is kind of beside the point, while the wild animal suffering people seem to think individual conscious experience is basically ALL that matters, and that ecosystems can't really be helped or harmed in themselves at all. I brought this up with my friend Maya and she insisted the two views "obviously" don't actually conflict, that conservation and animal welfare are just two sides of the same coin, but ngl that feels like its dodging the real disagreement instead of resolving it. Could you actually compare these two views properly for me - like what do they disagree about at the most fundamental level, would someone who buys into the land ethic even recognize wild animal suffering as a real moral problem in the first place, and is there some way to reconcile them, or is one of them just kind of wrong?
