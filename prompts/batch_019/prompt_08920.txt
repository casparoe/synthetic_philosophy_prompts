I serve on my university's Faculty Senate Governance Committee, which reviews proposed bylaw amendments before they reach the Senate floor. We are currently considering a proposal, brought by a coalition of senior colleagues still shaken by a round of program closures several years ago, to entrench our tenure and post-tenure review standards in the Faculty Handbook. Under the current rule, any Handbook change passes by simple majority of the Senate followed by simple majority ratification of the full voting faculty; the proposed amendment would instead require a two-thirds vote of the full faculty to weaken those specific provisions, and would extend that same two-thirds threshold to any future attempt to repeal the entrenchment clause itself. The declared purpose is to stop some future Senate — smaller, more reliant on contingent faculty, or under sharper administrative pressure than ours — from doing by ordinary vote what this faculty, right now, would never agree to.

At our last meeting, the committee's resident political theorist raised an objection I haven't been able to answer to my own satisfaction, and since I'm the closest thing the committee has to a philosopher, I've been asked to take a first pass at a response. Her point had two parts. First, unlike a national constitution, our Handbook has no court standing behind it: if a future Senate majority simply decided to disregard the entrenchment clause and vote the old-fashioned way, the only thing standing in their path would be whatever the Board of Trustees chose to honor, and the Board's charter authority over the university does not run through our internal amendment rules at all. Second, and more fundamentally, she argued that even a perfectly self-enforcing version of the clause would still bind colleagues not yet hired, and perhaps not yet out of graduate school, to a settlement they had no part in reaching — Jefferson's objection to any generation legislating for the next one, and structurally the same problem faced by any precommitment device, going back to Ulysses ordering his crew in advance to disregard whatever he says once the ship is alongside the Sirens. Roughly half the committee thinks this settles the matter against entrenching anything; the other half thinks the objection proves too much, since something evidently does distinguish a genuine precommitment from a piece of paper, and constitutions, marriages, and rehab programs all seem to manage it after a fashion.

The full Senate takes up the amendment at the April plenary, and the Governance Committee is expected to circulate a recommendation beforehand. Could you draft the roughly two-page memo I'll bring back to the committee? I would like the memo to reach a clear recommendation — send the amendment forward as written, send it back for redesign, or withdraw it — to meet the political theorist's objection on its own terms rather than simply asserting that stability is valuable, and, if some version of entrenchment survives your analysis, to specify concretely what its design would need to include (an enforcement mechanism, particular thresholds, sunset or renewal provisions, or anything else) in order to bind without collapsing into either a dead letter or a life sentence imposed on people who never agreed to it.
