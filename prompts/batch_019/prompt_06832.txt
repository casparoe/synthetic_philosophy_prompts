I'm a novelist at work on a book set in the 1850s, and one of my secondary characters — a clergyman's daughter drifting away from orthodoxy — is meant to echo the intellectual shape of George Eliot's own religious trajectory rather than the stock "Victorian loses faith to Darwin" arc. Before I draft the scene, I've written myself a short note on how I understand Eliot's actual position, and I'd value your comments on whether it's accurate or too tidy.

My note: Eliot's break with orthodox Christianity in the early 1840s had little to do with science; it grew out of biblical criticism, above all her work translating Strauss's "Life of Jesus" and, later, Feuerbach's "Essence of Christianity." What she took from Feuerbach wasn't a simple debunking atheism but the idea that doctrines like the Incarnation are unconscious projections of real human needs and attachments onto a divine screen. So instead of discarding religion wholesale, she tried to salvage what she took to be its ethical and emotional core — sympathy, duty, self-renunciation — while setting aside the supernatural claims she saw as merely that core's imaginative disguise. That, on my reading, is why her fiction can be so tender toward devout characters like Dinah Morris even though she herself no longer believed: their piety reads as displaced fellow-feeling rather than error.

Does this hold up as a fair account, or am I flattening real tensions in her position — for instance, between this "salvaged religion" reading and the much harder edge of an essay like her review of Dr. Cumming? I'd welcome comments on where the note gets Eliot right, where it oversimplifies, and whether I'm leaning on the Feuerbach connection more than the evidence really supports.
