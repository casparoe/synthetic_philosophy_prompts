I'm taking a formal epistemology seminar this term, and a few weeks ago we spent a whole session on Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle: roughly, that your current credence in a proposition A, conditional on your credence in A at some later time being r, must itself equal r. Our professor motivated it two ways: first via a diachronic Dutch book argument, on which failing to align your current and anticipated future credences this way commits you to a sequence of bets, each fair by your own lights at the time it's offered, that jointly guarantee a loss; second via an idea she said van Fraassen adapts from Gaifman, that a rational agent should treat her own future opinion the way she'd treat an expert's, deferring to it absent specific reason not to. She also flagged that van Fraassen himself worried about this going too far -- in a later paper he reaches for the myth of Ulysses and the Sirens to describe a case where you know a future version of yourself will be swayed by something that has nothing to do with the evidence, and concludes that self forfeits its "expert" status, so Reflection shouldn't apply to it. I followed all of this fine in isolation, but the "expert" qualifier always struck me as doing a lot of unexplained work, and a dispute at our last study session convinced me I wasn't the only one confused.

It started because a friend of ours, Elena, mentioned she's spending spring break at a residential "manifestation and abundance" retreat her cousin swears by. Elena currently puts about 5% credence in manifestation (the idea that focusing your thoughts on a goal helps bring it about through some channel other than its effects on your own effort and attention), but the retreat's own alumni surveys -- which she has no reason to think are faked -- show that roughly 90% of attendees who arrive as skeptics leave with credence above 80%, averaging around 85%. Two people in my seminar group, Marcus and Dana, got into an argument over whether Reflection means Elena should raise her credence now, before hearing a single argument. (1) Marcus said that on the principle as stated, Elena's credence conditional on "next week I'll be around 85%" just is 85%, full stop -- the principle doesn't care why the update happens, only that it's coming. (2) Dana said this is exactly the case the "expert" clause exists to block: a process that converts skeptics at a fixed rate no matter what is fed into it isn't tracking the truth, so future-Elena isn't an expert and present-Elena owes her nothing. (3) Marcus replied that this escape hatch is too convenient unless Dana can say, in advance and without just consulting whether she likes the answer, how Elena is supposed to know the retreat's arguments are all bad -- and if Elena is already that sure, she didn't need Reflection to tell her what to think in the first place. (4) Dana answered that it isn't ad hoc: the conversion rate is reportedly the same across different coaches teaching mutually contradictory versions of the doctrine, which is exactly the pattern that would make you distrust a diagnostician whose verdicts didn't track anything about the patient either, and that fact is available to Elena beforehand. (5) Marcus said that if it all cashes out in independently checking the reliability of the belief-forming process, Reflection isn't adding anything to ordinary Bayesian updating on testimony, despite being sold as a further constraint with its own Dutch book argument behind it -- so either it has independent force, and the retreat is a real counterexample to it, or it doesn't, and the "expert" talk was just relabeling reasoning we already had. Neither conceded. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (5), and say who has the better of it at each step rather than just who wins overall -- and point out anything about Reflection, or about van Fraassen's own way of handling cases like this, that you think both Marcus and Dana are missing?
