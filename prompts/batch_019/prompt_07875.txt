"The logical problem of evil argues that an omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good God is logically incompatible with the existence of evil — if all three properties were true, evil couldn't exist, so since evil obviously does exist, such a God can't exist either. This is the version J.L. Mackie pushed. The evidential problem is different: it says that even if evil doesn't technically disprove God, the amount and kinds of evil we actually see (like animals suffering horribly for millions of years before humans even existed) make it unlikely God exists, even if its not impossible.

Plantinga's free will defense responds by arguing God couldn't force people to freely choose good without them stopping being free. So a world with free creatures who sometimes choose evil is better than one with no free will at all, which is why an all-good God allows moral evil like murder. Plantinga extends this to natural evil too, like earthquakes and disease, by saying these can also come from free human choices, similar to how bad infrastructure or pollution causes a lot of harm.

I think the free will defense succeeds, since it shows God does have a morally sufficient reason to allow evil — protecting free will — which answers both the logical and evidential versions of the problem. Its basically the same idea as the 'vale of soul-making,' where going through hardship helps us grow moral character, which is why God set things up this way."
