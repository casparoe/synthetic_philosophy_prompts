I am writing a dissertation chapter on diachronic rationality and keep circling back to a puzzle in the money-pump literature. The standard argument concludes that intransitive or incomplete preferences are irrational, since an agent with such preferences can be strung along through a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off than where it started, with no offsetting gain. McClennen's well-known reply is that an agent who chooses 'resolutely' -- fixing on the best overall plan at the outset and sticking to it even when a later trade looks locally tempting -- can dodge the pump without its preferences ever becoming transitive or complete. What I have not seen clearly explained is why so many decision theorists still treat this as a Pyrrhic victory rather than a genuine solution: resolute choice seems to require the agent, at some later choice node, to pass up what looks like the locally preferred option purely because of an earlier commitment. Could you spell out, in some depth, the strongest version of this objection -- why binding yourself to a plan against your own later, locally arising preferences might carry a rationality cost no smaller than the cost of remaining exploitable -- and how a defender of resolute choice would try to answer it?
