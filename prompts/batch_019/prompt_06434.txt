This happened in a mechanical keyboard Discord I'm in (I lurk there during standup when I should be paying attention to actual work). One regular, I'll call him Denny, spent about a month trading his way through boards: first he gave up his walnut-case board plus $20 for "Board B" becuase he'd decided he liked B's layout better; a couple weeks later he gave up Board B plus $25 for "Board C" because C had hot-swappable sockets; then a week after that he gave up Board C plus $15 to get his original walnut board back, because apparently he'd been missing the case the whole time. So three trades later he owned exactly the board he started with, minus $60 and a bunch of shipping hassle. He seemed totally unbothered by this and said every single trade felt worth it in the moment.

Two other regulars got into a whole thing about whether Denny had actually done something irrational, and I've been chewing on it since (I even googled "diachronic rationality" afterward and only half understood what I read). Roughly, the exchange went:

(1) Nick said this is a textbook "money pump": Denny's trades reveal a preference cycle (original < B < C < original), and that's exactly the sort of thing philosophers point to when they say cyclic preferences are irrational -- a cycle is basically a standing invitation for someone to drain your wallet using nothing but trades you'd willingly make anyway.

(2) Farah pushed back: there's no real cycle here, because Denny wasn't comparing the same fixed options each time -- he had different reasons at each step (layout, then hot-swap sockets, then nostalgia for the case). His preferences just changed over the month, and preferences are allowed to change; that's not irrational, that's just having a life.

(3) Nick replied that this excuse doesn't work if the "new reasons" are really just the same shiny-object reflex firing every time, in a predictable direction that happens to always cost Denny money -- that's not honest preference change, that's a bias being farmed, and that's precisely what a money-pump argument is supposed to catch.

(4) Farah said Nick was quietly moving the goalposts: from "some individual trade was irrational" (which she says he can't show, since Denny was happy with every single trade given what was actually on offer at the time) to "the sequence was bad for him overall," which is a much weaker claim. It might show Denny has an exploitable quirk, sure, but that's not obviously the same as being irrational, since irrationality is supposed to be a defect in a choice at the time it's made, not just a fact about how a string of individually fine choices happened to net out.

(5) Nick's comeback was that this is exactly why people distinguish "synchronic" rationality (being sensible in the moment) from "diachronic" rationality (being sensible across time) -- the whole point of a money-pump argument is to show that being locally sensible at every single step is NOT enough. Denny's trading loop, he said, is a live demonstration of why you need some further constraint on rational agents across time, not a counterexample to the idea that cyclic preferences are irrational.
