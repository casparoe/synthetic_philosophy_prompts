Last month the chair of my co-op board formally reprimanded me at a meeting for subletting my unit without prior written approval. I don't dispute that I broke the rule. But a departing board member has since told me, in confidence, that the chair has knowingly let a close friend on the board do the identical thing for two years without objection. Since then I've been reading around in the philosophical literature on hypocrisy and the standing to blame, and two accounts keep recurring: R. Jay Wallace's view that hypocritical blame is defective because it violates the equal standing that the practice of moral address presupposes between blamer and blamed, and Patrick Todd's proposal that loss of standing always comes down to the blamer presupposing something false about her own relation to the violated norm -- for instance, that she doesn't also commit it. My situation sits awkwardly with both: the chair doesn't sublet her own unit, so nothing obviously false is presupposed about her conduct, yet the favoritism seems like exactly the sort of thing that should undermine her standing to reprimand me. Compare the two accounts, and tell me which, if either, correctly diagnoses a standing-defeating problem in a case of selective enforcement like mine, where there is favoritism but no shared guilt. Justify your answer, and say what each view would need to add or revise to accommodate a case like this.
