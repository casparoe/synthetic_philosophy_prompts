I serve on my university's Faculty Senate Governance Committee. Under our current bylaws, any general-education course proposed to satisfy the Quantitative Reasoning requirement is first vetted by a three-member Subcommittee of statisticians, then ratified by a vote of the full Senate. The Senate has twice overturned the Subcommittee's recommendations, both times by votes that tracked departmental representation rather than any stated disagreement with the statistical reasoning itself; a survey the Subcommittee commissioned afterward found that most sitting senators could not correctly interpret a p-value or a confidence interval. The Subcommittee has now proposed a bylaw amendment making its determinations final, appealable only on procedural grounds. Two senators object that both overturned courses were in fact rejected over whether the material was pedagogically accessible to non-majors, not over the soundness of the statistics — a question on which the Subcommittee has no special claim to authority. As the committee's resident philosopher, I have been asked to draft the brief memo we will bring to next month's floor debate: recommend adopting the amendment, rejecting it, or replacing it with some alternative allocation of authority; state the criteria that justify drawing the line where you do; and give a genuine, non-dismissive account of the strongest reason to decide otherwise.
