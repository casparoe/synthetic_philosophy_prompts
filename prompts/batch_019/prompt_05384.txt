I teach a graduate seminar on population ethics, and this week -- right after we finished up Parfit and moved into the long-termist, infinite-future stuff -- we hit the unit on aggregating welfare over infinite populations (infinite future people, infinite universe, whatever cosmology you like best). Every single year some student says "well, you just add up everyone's utility and see which total is bigger, obviously," and I like to have a genuinely clean counterexample loaded up to slow that down. Thought I'd test it on you before I spring it on them.

Here's what I want. Two "worlds," each with the exact same population of infinitely many people, where every person has some finite welfare level, and the two worlds contain the exact same multiset of welfare numbers -- literally the same collection of levels handed out, just to different specific people. So world B is just world A with the welfare levels reshuffled around among that very same infinite set of people: nobody's identity is doing any special work, and no welfare has been created or destroyed anywhere. Intuitively that should force the two worlds to come out exactly equally good -- same welfare overall, just relabeled who has it. What I want from you is an actual example, with real numbers (not "imagine some positive and negative utilities and wave your hands"), where the natural way of cashing out "total welfare" -- line everyone up in some order, take the running total, see what it converges to -- gives two different totals for A and B, or converges for one and just doesn't for the other at all.

Once you've built it, tell me straight which of the two things in tension should actually give: the idea that mere reshuffling among the same fixed population can't possibly change how good a world is, or the idea that total utilitarianism's "total" just is whatever the summed-up limit happens to come out to. And here's the part I'm honestly unsure about myself: would it save the total utilitarian to only insist on "reshuffling doesn't matter" for reshufflings that move finitely many people at a time, rather than the wholesale kind you'll need for your example -- or is that just a technical dodge that doesn't touch what's actually bugging my students?
