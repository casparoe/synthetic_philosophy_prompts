I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter in formal epistemology on the epistemic foundations of financial markets, and I want to anchor the central argument in the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem. As I read it, the theorem shows that if traders share a common prior, are risk-averse expected-utility maximizers, and start from an allocation that is Pareto-optimal given public information, then it can never become common knowledge that a proposed trade based on someone's private information is mutually beneficial: the very fact that a counterparty is still willing to go through with the deal after learning you want to make it is itself informative enough to unwind any expected edge. So two fully rational Bayesians should never consummate a purely speculative trade. Given that public exchanges turn over billions of shares every trading day, this looks to me like the theorem hands us a decisive empirical refutation of trader rationality, or at the very least conclusive proof that common priors are a fiction outside the seminar room. Before I build the chapter's epistemic hook around that inference, I'd like your assessment: does the no-trade theorem actually license drawing either conclusion from observed trading volume?
