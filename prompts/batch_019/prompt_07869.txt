I'm a backend engineer at a crypto exchange, and in my spare time I've been reading Aristotle on money — specifically Politics I.9–10, where he distinguishes natural household management (oikonomia) from the "art of wealth-getting" (chrematistike), and singles out interest (tokos) as the most unnatural form of acquisition of all, because it makes money itself "breed" more money rather than merely mediating an exchange of real goods, a point he underscores by noting that tokos also literally means "offspring."

I'd like you to pressure-test that argument against two mechanisms he obviously never had the chance to consider:

1. Proof-of-stake staking rewards, where locking up a cryptocurrency causes a protocol to automatically mint and pay you new units of that same currency, in proportion to the amount staked rather than to any labor performed (the validation work required is minimal and roughly constant regardless of stake size), with no individual counterparty involved at all.
2. Liquidity provision on an automated market maker like Uniswap, where you deposit a pair of assets into a pool and earn a cut of the fees paid by traders who swap through it — you're compensated for facilitating other people's exchanges, and your income depends on how much genuine trading activity passes through the pool.

Reasoning strictly from Aristotle's actual stated grounds for condemning tokos, rather than his general reputation as a critic of profit-seeking, would he have principled reason to treat these two income streams as differently "unnatural"? And is there a case that staking rewards are an even purer instance of what he was objecting to than ordinary interest ever was, precisely because they dispense with the lender–borrower relationship entirely?
