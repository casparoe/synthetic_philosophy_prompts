A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing over whether Benatar's case against procreation can really be kept separate from the older Epicurean argument about death, and neither of us came away satisfied. My friend's challenge was this: Epicurus holds that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, since by the time death arrives there is no subject left to be harmed — so why does Benatar still treat the absence of pain in someone who is never conceived as a good, given that there is likewise no subject there to enjoy it? Compare the logical structure of Benatar's asymmetry argument with the Epicurean argument against the badness of death, and assess whether Benatar's usual reply — that ceasing to exist and never existing are relevantly disanalogous — actually defuses this parity challenge or merely relocates it. Please indicate which premise in each argument is doing the real work, and whether that argument can survive without it.
