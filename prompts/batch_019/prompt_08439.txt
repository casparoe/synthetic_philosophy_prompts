Okay, this ones gonna sound weirdly specific, but it's been bugging me for weeks. At work I've been building a little internal tool that tracks claims we care about (stuff like "vendor X ships by Friday" or "this migration won't break the payments service"), and instead of just flagging them true or false it assigns them credences and updates them Bayesian-style as new info comes in. Building it got me curious about the theory behind it, so I started tagging along to a friend's formal epistemology reading group, and a couple weeks ago we covered the "problem of logical omniscience" - apparently going back to Hintikka's original possible-worlds treatment of knowledge, where if an agent knows some set of facts, the logic automatically has them knowing every logical consequence of those facts too, including every unproved theorem, which is obviously nuts for any real reasoner.

My reaction was that this seems like a total non-issue once you go Bayesian, which is basically what my tool already does: instead of insisting an agent either fully knows a hard theorem or doesnt, you just let it hold some medium credence in it, so the "knows everything that follows from what she believes" absurdity quietly disappears. My friend thinks Bayesians inherit almost the exact same problem in a different costume - something about tautologies needing probability 1 and logically equivalent claims needing identical probabilities no matter how limited the agent computing them is - and apparently there's still live research (something with the AI safety people at MIRI?) into building systems that don't just assume this away. That feels like pedantry to me though, since I thought the whole point of using probabilities instead of flat belief was to handle exactly this kind of partial, resource-limited cognitive access. So genuinely: is my friend right that my tool doesn't actually escape the problem, or is this a scholastic wrinkle that stops mattering once you're doing something practical like what I'm doing?
