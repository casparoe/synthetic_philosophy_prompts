I convene a small monthly colloquium on formal epistemology and rational agency, and next month's guest is a decision theorist whose recent work defends "surrogate goals" as a hedge against acausal threats: roughly, the idea that because a sufficiently capable reasoner can be threatened by an extortionist with whom it shares no causal contact at all -- provided the extortionist can predict or correlate with it closely enough for the threat to bind -- the reasoner is best served by cultivating in advance a visible, dispositionally firm attachment to some cheap substitute value, so that any rational threat-maker will aim at the decoy rather than at what the reasoner actually cares about. I've been turning the proposal over since a graduate student presented it in my reading group last term, and the more I read the more it strikes me as either a genuinely clever patch for agents that cannot fully self-modify, or an acausal dress-up of ordinary reputational bargaining, and I still can't decide which. In print, the guest tends to meet most objections with some version of "that only shows we need a better-designed surrogate, not that the strategy itself is unsound," and I'd like the questions to make that move progressively harder to reach for.

I'm moderating the Q&A and would like your help drafting eight to ten questions that go beyond having him restate the proposal. At minimum I want one question on the credibility problem: if it is common knowledge that the target adopted a decoy precisely because it is cheap to sacrifice, why wouldn't a sophisticated extortionist simply threaten the real goal, or both at once? One on the regress this seems to invite, since a surrogate that is itself known to be a surrogate needs some further guarantee of its own credibility, and it is unclear where that chain is supposed to bottom out. One on how the whole proposal relates to the updateless ideal of simply being the sort of agent against whom threats never pay to be issued in the first place -- is the surrogate goal a stopgap for agents that cannot achieve that disposition outright, or is it in tension with it? And one on whether stipulating a goal purely for its deterrent value, all the while knowing it to be a stand-in, is psychologically or rationally coherent, rather than an elaborate exercise in self-deception. For each of these, please add a brief follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give: on the credibility question, for instance, I expect him to retreat to saying that the surrogate only needs to be indistinguishable from a real goal from the threatener's epistemic position, and I would like the follow-up to press on how much that concedes.
