After a thirty-year career as a research chemist, I have spent part of my retirement working through the abolitionist literature of antebellum America, a period I never had time to study properly while I was still in the laboratory. My reading group recently took up Frederick Douglass, and I have just finished his 1852 Rochester address, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" Knowing that Douglass began his public career as a protégé of William Lloyd Garrison, and that Garrison's circle famously denounced the Constitution as "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," I had assumed that Douglass's blistering account of the nation's hypocrisy in that speech was simply an application of the same Garrisonian premise: that the republic's crimes against the enslaved flow directly from a founding charter that is corrupt at its core. This makes it all the more puzzling to me that Douglass is remembered today, above all, as the abolitionist who broke publicly with Garrison over exactly this question, arguing instead that the Constitution, properly read, was an anti-slavery document that Black Americans could rightfully claim as their own.

What I would like help understanding is the shape of that change. Was Douglass's later constitutional optimism essentially a strategic pivot -- a way of clearing the ground for anti-slavery voting and party politics once moral suasion alone had plainly failed to move the country -- with the interpretive argument constructed afterward to justify a decision already made on other grounds? Or did he have a genuine argument about the text and history of the Constitution, one that could in principle be assessed on its merits independently of the political convenience it offered? And since I have just read the 1852 oration as though it belonged to Douglass's Garrisonian period, I would be grateful if you could tell me whether that assumption survives scrutiny, or whether the speech in fact already belongs to a later, and rather different, chapter of his thinking.
