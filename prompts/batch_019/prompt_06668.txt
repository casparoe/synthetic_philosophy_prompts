Working through Augustine's two treatises on lying — De Mendacio and the later, harder-line Contra Mendacium — for a substack post that's loosely tied to the new book, and I want a sanity check before it goes up.

Augustine's definition of a lie isn't "saying something false." Its saying something you believe to be false, with the will to make someone else believe it — the intention is doing basically all the work. Which is how he gets to the two exemptions I find interesting: jokes and fiction. A joke that's literally false still isn't a lie for him, because both people are in on the register — the joking tone announces itself, so nobody's actually being deceived, whatever the content is. He treats invented stories and figurative speech (fables, allegories, a poet's inventions) more or less the same way: the fictive frame does the work that honesty usually does. Nobody thinks Aesop is lying about foxes talking.

Here's my worry, and the actual point of the post. That exemption only runs on the frame being legible — on the audience knowing which game is being played. And a lot of what's interesting about the novel right now is writing that either doesn't announce its frame or half-disguises it: autofiction that keeps the narrator's real name and most of the facts, a book sold as memoir that quietly invents dialogue and merges two people into one character (I don't just mean the famous scandals, plenty of very respectable memoirs do this), or a novel that only admits to being fiction on the copyright page and nowhere in the text itself. If Augustine's whole case for why fiction isn't lying depends on both sides tacitly agreeing on the register, then a memoir that invents scenes while trading on being read as true isn't fiction that happens to resemble a lie — on his own terms it just is one, full stop, regardless of how good the prose is. Which means the morality of a huge amount of contemporary literary practice is hanging off a title-page label and some catalog copy, not off anything in the writing itself. That seems like a strange place for a book's ethics to live, but I can't find the hole in the reasoning.

One more thing, more research question than argument: I keep half-remembering that Augustine somewhere praises a bishop (Firmus? not sure I've got the name right) who hid a fugitive, refused to lie to the men hunting him, but also refused to just hand the guy over — apparently by not answering rather than asserting anything false — and held out until they gave up. If you actually know that story I'd love the accurate version, might want to use it as a footnote.

What I want from you: is the Augustine I'm describing (the intent-based definition, the joke/fiction exemption, why it holds) actually right, or am I flattening something he'd object to? And separately, does the autofiction/fake-memoir argument work as honest Augustinian reasoning, or am I just borrowing his name for a point he'd never have signed off on? Be blunt, it doesn't need to be gentle.
