I'm writing a term paper for my high school's philosophy elective on whether genuine moral dilemmas can exist—cases like Sophie's Choice, where every option open to the agent is morally wrong. I think I've come across an argument that settles the question for good. In standard deontic logic, if someone ought to do A and also ought to do B, then they ought to do both A and B (this is called the agglomeration principle). But it's a basic truth that no one can be obligated to do something impossible. So if A and B genuinely cannot both be done, that just shows one of the original "obligations" was never real to begin with. Doesn't this mean that philosophers such as Ruth Barcan Marcus and Bernard Williams, who insist that real, unresolvable moral dilemmas exist, have simply overlooked an elementary point of logic that dissolves the whole puzzle?
