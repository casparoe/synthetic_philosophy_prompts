In my seminar on early modern science and religion, we've been comparing Galileo's 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina with Cardinal Bellarmine's 1615 letter to Foscarini, and I was struck by how much hermeneutic common ground the two men actually share. Both accept the broadly Augustinian idea that scripture is accommodated to the understanding of ordinary people, and both agree that if a natural-philosophical claim ever met the standard of genuine demonstration, the received reading of scripture would have to yield rather than the demonstrated fact. Where they part ways is over whether Galileo's case for heliocentrism actually met that standard, and over whether the earth's motion counts as the sort of "faith and morals" question that, per the Council of Trent, scripture may not be read against the unanimous consent of the Fathers.

Could you write a dialogue between Galileo and Bellarmine, set in the immediate aftermath of Bellarmine's letter to Foscarini, in which the two of them work through these two specific points of disagreement? Please have each one engage directly with the other's actual arguments rather than lapsing into a generic science-versus-religion exchange, and don't let either come out as the obvious winner: I'm interested in seeing exactly where their shared premises run out and the real disagreement begins.
