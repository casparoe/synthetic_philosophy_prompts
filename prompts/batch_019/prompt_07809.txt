I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of science, and we've spent the last two sessions working through Larry Laudan's list of once-successful theories -- phlogiston, caloric, the humoral theory, the various nineteenth-century ethers -- that were later abandoned wholesale, and the "no miracles"-style realist attempts to blunt the resulting pessimistic meta-induction. Next week we turn to what I think is the cleverest of these attempts: the selective, or "divide et impera," strategy associated with Kitcher and Psillos, on which a discarded theory's posits split into those that were genuinely doing the explanatory and predictive work and those that were merely idle background assumptions never really underwritten by the evidence, so that realist commitment was only ever owed to the working posits. One of my sharper students pushed back using Fresnel's wave theory of light: the mechanical, elastic-solid ether looks, on Fresnel's own understanding and that of his contemporaries, to have been doing real work in generating the novel prediction of the Poisson-Arago bright spot, yet selective realists now want to say the ether's mechanical properties were idle and only the wave equations were working. Generalized, her worry is that the working/idle line gets drawn only in hindsight, by projecting our current theories back onto the old ones, so the strategy tells us which bits of a dead theory to feel good about only once we already know how the story ends -- which looks like conceding the pessimistic induction rather than blocking it.

I'd like your help thinking this through before I finalize my lecture. Please write an essay that treats this as a live controversy rather than a solved problem: reconstruct the strongest version of the selective-realist strategy (draw on Kitcher, Psillos, or others as you see fit, though you needn't worry about getting every citation exactly right), then reconstruct the strongest version of the hindsight-circularity objection just sketched, and adjudicate between them. Please keep the discussion focused specifically on selective, divide-et-impera realism rather than sliding into a general defense of structural realism -- my students already conflate the two, and I'd like an essay that keeps them apart. Above all, don't leave things on a "considerations on both sides" note: reach a definite verdict on whether the working/idle distinction, at its best, actually defuses the pessimistic meta-induction or merely relocates it, and defend that verdict against the strongest available objection to it.
