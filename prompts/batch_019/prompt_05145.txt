Okay, this has been bugging me for a while and I've never actually seen it nailed down properly, as opposed to just gestured at. You know the money-pump argument that gets thrown around constantly in the rationalist blogosphere — if your preferences aren't transitive (you like A more than B, B more than C, but C more than A), a sufficiently clever bookie can offer you a sequence of trades, each one a strict improvement by your own lights at the time, that cycles you right back to where you started, minus a small fee every lap. Yudkowsky leans on this pretty hard in the "coherent decisions imply consistent utilities" type posts to argue any sufficiently optimized agent ends up behaving like a transitive expected-utility-maximizer, or else it's just leaking money to whoever's paying attention.

What I've never gotten a straight answer on is the "resolute choice" escape hatch — pretty sure that's McClennen's term, could be misremembering. The idea, as I understand it, is that the agent doesn't have to be naive/myopic about the whole thing: if it surveys the entire decision tree up front, commits to the single best overall plan, and then just refuses to deviate at the later nodes even when its in-the-moment preferences say otherwise, it never actually bleeds any money, while its underlying preferences stay just as cyclic as before. So my actual ask: build this for me concretely, not just describe it in the abstract. Give me three options with a genuine preference cycle, spell out exactly what trades the bookie offers a naive version of the agent and how much it looses, then walk through the identical sequence of offers for a resolute version of that same agent, same cyclic preferences, and show precisely why it comes out even. Then give me your actual take: does this mean rationality is more like a property of a whole plan across time than something you can judge choice-by-choice in the moment, or is "being resolute" secretly just smuggling the consistency requirement back in at the level of plans — in which case the money pump never really touched the preferences at all, just our naive way of acting on them?
