I've been reading al-Ghazālī's attack on natural causation in the seventeenth discussion of *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, and I keep getting stuck on a worry that goes beyond the famous fire-and-cotton example itself. Al-Ghazālī insists that there is no necessary connection between fire and burning, only a habitual pairing that God freely institutes and could suspend at will. But if fire contributes nothing of its own to the outcome, why does the habit track contact between fire and cotton specifically, rather than, say, water and cotton, or fire and stone? It seems like the pairing still has to be keyed to some real difference between things, which sounds very close to the causal powers he is trying to deny - unless he is willing to say the pattern is simply a brute, unexplained fact repeated at every instant, which strikes me as a heavy cost to pay. He does seem to leave himself a fallback position, allowing that God might be continuously placing a created "power" in the fire itself rather than dispensing with it entirely, but then I don't see what is left of his disagreement with someone like Ibn Rushd, who wants things to have real natures that function as secondary causes under God. Could you write me a dialogue between the two of them in which Ibn Rushd presses exactly this challenge and al-Ghazālī tries to hold on to a genuinely occasionalist position, without either conceding that the pattern is arbitrary or quietly readmitting the very causal powers he set out to reject? I would like both of them arguing at full strength, actually responding to each other's moves rather than restating their opening positions, and I am especially curious whether they end up with a real disagreement or just two different ways of describing the same picture.
