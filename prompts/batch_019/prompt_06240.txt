Last week, a friend of mine was agonizing over two job offers -- one in the city, one in the country -- that she insisted were simply incomparable rather than equally good: not that she was indifferent between them, but that there was no fact of the matter about which was better. I made an offhand remark that decision theorists treat this kind of incompleteness as a potential source of exploitation, in much the way intransitive preferences are, and that remark turned into a long argument that neither of us really won.

Here is where the argument left off. The classic money-pump argument for transitivity assumes a naive (or myopic) agent who walks unwittingly into a cycle of trades; the standard reply, going back to McClennen's work on resolute choice and Hammond's on sophisticated choice, is that a forward-looking agent can avoid the pump either by refusing the first trade once she foresees where the sequence leads, or by committing to a plan in advance and sticking to it at every node even where, taken in isolation, a different choice would now be preferred. This is usually presented as a diachronic vindication of what looks like a diachronic vulnerability: what gets rescued is the rationality of a whole policy extended over time, not any single synchronic preference at a moment. My friend wanted to extend the same defense to her own case: since she has no ranking between the city job and the country job, she could simply resolve in advance how she would choose at every relevant node of some decision tree built around offers and side-payments, and thereby escape any sequence of trades that would otherwise guarantee her a loss. My objection was that resoluteness earns its keep differently for intransitivity than it does for incompleteness. In the classic case, the agent always has some determinate preference to fall back on at the pump's crucial node -- she prefers the third option to the first, she is just declining to act on that preference for the sake of the plan -- so the diachronic commitment overrides a preference that is already there. In my friend's case, by hypothesis there is no preference at all between the city and country jobs at the relevant node, so sticking to the plan cannot be a matter of overriding an existing preference; it has to manufacture a tie-breaking disposition from nothing, and it is unclear what makes the resulting policy count as rational by her own lights rather than an arbitrary rule bolted onto her preferences after the fact -- which looks uncomfortably like smuggling completeness back in to do the very work incompleteness was supposed to be exempt from.

I would like your honest assessment: does this disanalogy between the transitivity case and the completeness case actually hold up under pressure, and is there a good way for someone who wants to keep both incomplete preferences and resolute choice available to block the argument? I would also genuinely welcome correction if I have set up the naive/sophisticated/resolute distinction itself imprecisely, since I am reconstructing it from memory of a graduate seminar rather than checking it against McClennen's or Hammond's actual formulations.
