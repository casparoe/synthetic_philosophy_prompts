I'm several drafts into a novel whose second narrator is a philosophy don, long retired from Cambridge to a house on the Suffolk coast, who confesses partway through the book that he no longer believes he — or anyone he ever examined in forty years — has followed a rule correctly in his life. I want his reasoning to survive real scrutiny rather than read as decorative erudition, so I've gone back to the sources rather than working from memory of old seminars: Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations itself, Kripke's book on it, and Baker and Hacker's rebuttal of Kripke. I've gotten stuck on a tension inside a single passage and would like help thinking it through, since I don't have anyone nearby who works on this professionally.

Here is the passage, Philosophical Investigations §201, in the Anscombe translation:

"This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule. The answer was: if everything can be made out to accord with the rule, then it can also be made out to conflict with it. And so there would be neither accord nor conflict here.

It can be seen that there is a misunderstanding here from the mere fact that in the course of our argument we give one interpretation after another; as if each one contented us at least for a moment, until we thought of yet another standing behind it. What this shows is that there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases."

Kripke reads the first paragraph as the statement of a genuine sceptical paradox about meaning — no fact about me, dispositional or otherwise, fixes that I mean addition rather than the deviant function he calls "quus," which agrees with addition on every case I've actually computed and diverges beyond it — and he offers his own community-based "sceptical solution" as the only way to live with that paradox rather than answer it. Baker and Hacker read the second paragraph as Wittgenstein already dissolving the paradox on the spot, with no sceptical solution needed, on the grounds that the demand for an interpretation was itself the mistake. What I can't settle for myself is whether "a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation" is doing real philosophical work in that second paragraph, or whether it just restates the problem in more comforting language while leaving Kripke's question about what determines my meaning exactly as open as before. Could you walk through what that phrase is actually supposed to accomplish, and say which side has the stronger claim on this specific passage — and if you think both Kripke and Baker/Hacker are missing something in it, I would much rather hear that than a verdict that politely splits the difference.
