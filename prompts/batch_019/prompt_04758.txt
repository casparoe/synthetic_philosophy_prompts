In my early modern philosophy seminar we've just moved from Berkeley's Principles of Human Knowledge and Three Dialogues to his later economic tract The Querist, where he argues that money is best understood as a ticket or counter for conveying power rather than something valuable in virtue of the metal it's made of. This looks like a direct application of his immaterialism to me: given his prior denial that bodies exist independently of being perceived, the gold in a coin was never doing any metaphysical work to begin with, so of course he'd think paper credit or bare denominations could serve just as well. If the monetary conclusion really does follow straight from the metaphysical premise, would it be fair to say that Berkeley counts as an early ancestor of fiat-currency theory simply in virtue of being an immaterialist, and that anyone who wants to resist the economics of the Querist has to first find a flaw in the Principles?
