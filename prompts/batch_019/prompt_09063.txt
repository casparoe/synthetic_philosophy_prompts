So my school's EA club (effective altruism, not the video game company, I always have to clarify that lol) had a meeting last week that sent me down a total rabbit hole and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since. Someone brought up this old paper by Nick Bostrom (the "Superintelligence" guy) called something like "Infinite Ethics," and the part that broke my brain is what he calls "infinitarian paralysis." The basic idea: if the universe is actually infinite (which apparently cosmologists think is a live possibility) and it already contains an infinite amount of good and bad stuff spread out across it, then any finite thing you personally do — saving a kid from drowning, or torturing someone, whatever — literally cannot change the total amount of value in the universe, because infinity plus or minus any finite number is still just infinity. So on the standard utilitarian "add up all the value and compare the totals" approach, it seems like every possible action you could take is exactly, precisely as good as every other one, which is insane and kind of guts the entire premise of EA (that some actions do way more good than others and you should go find those).

My friend Marcus is extremely smug about this and has decided it basically proves consequentialism is broken and we should all go be virtue ethicists instead. I don't think its that easy of a win for him though — I vaguely remember reading that economists have some workaround for comparing infinite sequences where you look at whether one sequence's running totals eventually get and stay ahead of another's forever, instead of comparing the (undefined) final totals directly, but I have no idea if that actually rescues anything or just hides the problem one level down.

Can you make the strongest possible case that infinitarian paralysis is NOT actually fatal to consequentialism — i.e., that there's some legitimate way of ranking actions by their consequences even in an infinite universe that (a) doesn't collapse into "everything is equally good" and (b) still counts as a genuinely consequentialist solution rather than a "well let's just stop being consequentialist" cop-out? Please actually engage with why the naive summing approach breaks instead of just asserting that some fix "obviously" works.
