For my EPQ (an independent research project I'm doing alongside my A-level Philosophy course), I've been drafting a short essay on disjunctivism as a response to the argument from illusion, since my class only spent about half a lesson on it before moving on to Berkeley. The essay's core move is this: the classic argument from illusion gets from cases like the bent stick in water to sense-data by assuming that veridical and illusory experiences must share some fundamental common nature — what Byrne and Logue label the "Common Kind Assumption" — and disjunctivism blocks this by denying exactly that, insisting that genuinely perceiving something and merely seeming to perceive it are two fundamentally different kinds of mental state, even when they feel identical from the inside. I try to argue that this move works reasonably well against the original illusion cases, where there's still a real object doing some of the explanatory work, but that it gets much more expensive once you push it onto hallucination cases, since there "naive" disjunctivists like Martin seem forced to say the hallucinatory state has no positive character of its own at all beyond being indiscriminable from a genuine perception — which strikes me as an odd thing to have to say about something like a full-blown drug-induced hallucination. I then bring in Pritchard's epistemological disjunctivism as a way of dodging that cost, since it relocates the disjunction from the experience itself to the epistemic warrant a subject has, but I argue that this quietly gives up the most attractive part of the original view: the idea that perception itself, and not merely the justification it gives us, puts us in direct contact with the world.

Before I show this to my teacher, I'd really value some honest outside pushback on three things: whether I'm being fair to disjunctivists in treating hallucination as the harder case, or whether there's a standard move (from Martin himself, or from someone like Fish) that avoids the "no positive character" cost without collapsing into Pritchard's more modest position; whether my complaint about Pritchard's view amounts to a real philosophical cost or whether I'm just assuming metaphysical disjunctivism was supposed to deliver more than it actually was; and whether this whole line of argument is genuinely worth pursuing further, or whether it's a fairly standard observation that any decent perception course would already make. I'd rather hear specific objections than general encouragement.
