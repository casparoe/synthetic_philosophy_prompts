I'm taking a seminar on Plato's Laws this term, and I also sit on my residential college's committee that rules on alcohol-policy exceptions for events billed as "curricular programming." The Classics department has asked us to approve a one-time "Symposium Night": as the capstone of a course on the Republic and the Laws, of-age students would reenact an ancient drinking party, wine included, with a faculty member staying sober to preside over the evening.

Two committee members are both invoking Plato, on opposite sides. One cites the Athenian Stranger's argument, early in the Laws, that just as controlled exposure to fear can test and train courage, wine can test and train self-mastery: it loosens a person's guard and exposes their real character in a way sober life never does, provided someone sober and experienced is running the occasion. The other member responds that Plato's own defense is hedged in ways this event doesn't satisfy: he restricts real license to drink to people whose character is already settled, treats the practice as a standing institution a city regulates over a lifetime rather than something staged once for a class, and warns that wine without such regulation is among the most dangerous things a lawgiver can permit. On this reading, "Symposium Night" borrows Plato's vocabulary while skipping the conditions that were doing all the work in his argument.

We vote next week. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate beforehand: a recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions -- with reasoning that seriously engages the losing side's argument rather than just overruling it?
