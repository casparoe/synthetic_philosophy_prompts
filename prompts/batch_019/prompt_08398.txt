I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter on twentieth-century theories that locate meaning in life in some notion of autotelic activity, i.e., activity that is valuable in itself rather than as a means to something further. Two texts I keep circling back to are Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life" and Bernard Suits' *The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia*. As far as I can tell, they are essentially never read together in the secondary literature, yet they seem to me to be converging on a strikingly similar move from very different starting points, and I would like help thinking through exactly how similar that convergence really is.

Schlick's argument, briefly, is that purposive "work" — activity performed for the sake of some further end — cannot be where meaning ultimately resides, on pain of an infinite deferral: if the value of each act is only ever borrowed from the act it serves, then a life composed wholly of such acts is worthless taken as a whole, since nothing in the chain is ever valuable in its own right. He locates that missing intrinsic value in "play," explicitly borrowing from Schiller's notion of the Spieltrieb, and treats childhood, together with the absorption of the artist, or of the scientist who has found a genuine vocation, in activity that looks like work only from the outside, as the closest adults get to it. Suits, writing half a century later and, as far as I know, with no debt to Schlick, arrives at game-playing — defined with real formal precision as the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles, via lusory means constrained by constitutive rules and sustained by what he calls the "lusory attitude" — as the one kind of activity that would remain worth pursuing in a technological utopia where every genuinely necessary problem has already been solved for us.

Could you work through how far this parallel actually holds up? I am especially interested in whether Suits' apparatus supplies exactly what Schlick's account lacks — a non-question-begging criterion for when an activity counts as play, rather than just an observation that some activities happen to feel autotelic to whoever is engaged in them — or whether the two are quietly talking past one another, given that Suits' "unnecessary obstacles" clause seems to disqualify absorbed vocational work from counting as game-playing in a way that Schlick's looser notion of Spiel never does. I'd also value your judgment on which account survives more easily an objection that neither philosopher confronts directly: that some of the most obviously meaningful human activities — raising a child, sustaining a decades-long research program, organizing politically for a cause whose success one may never live to see — seem to be neither play by Schlick's lights nor games by Suits', without thereby collapsing into merely instrumental "work" either.
