Over the winter break I helped organize my grandmother's funeral. She had been a devout Catholic all her life, and in the months since, I've watched my mother and aunts keep lighting candles for her, praying the rosary "for the repose of her soul," and speaking with complete certainty about seeing her again "on the last day." I'm not religious myself, and I went along with all of it without much thought at the time, but it has left me with a philosophical itch rather than a theological one: what exactly is going on when they say they believe she will be judged and reunited with the rest of the family? Nobody in my family treats this as a hypothesis that could be disconfirmed, nobody looks for evidence the way they would for a claim about, say, next week's weather — and yet they would be genuinely offended if you suggested it was merely a comforting story they don't actually believe.

A friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD pointed me toward Wittgenstein's 1938 lectures on religious belief, where he makes almost exactly this observation about someone who believes in the Last Judgement: that the belief is not held "on grounds" the way an empirical claim is, and that the whole business of weighing evidence for and against it may simply be the wrong framework to bring to it in the first place. But she also warned me that this move is notoriously controversial among philosophers of religion. Kai Nielsen famously accused views like this of amounting to "Wittgensteinian Fideism" — the charge, pressed especially against D.Z. Phillips, being that treating religious language as a self-contained practice with its own internal standards of sense doesn't so much explain what my grandmother thought she was doing as change the subject, so that her claim about an afterlife never actually gets assessed as true or false at all.

I would like to see this disagreement actually play out rather than just summarized secondhand. Could you write a dialogue between two contemporary philosophers of religion — one developing and defending the Wittgensteinian position, the other pressing the Nielsen-style objection — in which each responds directly to the other's strongest move rather than talking past them? I am especially interested in whether the conversation can get traction on a further question: is this really a disagreement about what is true — about whether a judgment is coming — or is it, at bottom, a disagreement about how words like "belief," "reason," and "evidence" ought to be used in a religious context? It is fine if the two characters end up mainly clarifying what divides them rather than resolving it, but I would like the dialogue to earn that conclusion rather than simply assert it.
