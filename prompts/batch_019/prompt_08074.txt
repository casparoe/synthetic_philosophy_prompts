I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I spend a good deal of my working life building and debugging neural networks, and over the past couple of years I've followed mechanistic interpretability research closely enough that one particular worry has started to nag at me: whether that research is discovering something that was already there in the network, or whether it's imposing a convenient story on top of it. I wrote up my thinking below and would appreciate your comments on it.

Are We Discovering Features, or Inventing Them?

Interpretability researchers have started talking about neural networks the way anatomists talk about bodies. A model "has a feature for the Golden Gate Bridge." A circuit "performs indirect object identification." This is realist language: it treats the feature or the circuit as something that was already there, waiting to be found, independent of whatever probe or decomposition method was used to find it. I want to ask whether that language is earned, or whether it's smuggling in more than the evidence supports.

The strongest case for realism is causal. When Anthropic's interpretability team located a Golden Gate Bridge feature inside Claude and then artificially clamped it to a high value — the "Golden Gate Claude" demo — the model didn't just mention the bridge more often; its behavior reorganized around that theme in exactly the way you'd expect if the feature were a load-bearing part of the computation rather than a label pasted on after the fact. There's a name for this move in philosophy of science: Ian Hacking's argument that we become entitled to believe in an unobservable entity once we can reliably manipulate it to produce predicted effects elsewhere in the system — an argument he made about electrons, and one that seems to transfer cleanly to this case. There's also an independent, convergence-based argument, sometimes called the "Platonic Representation Hypothesis": the claim that models with different architectures, training objectives, and even different modalities are converging, as they scale, toward a similar representational geometry. The name is a nod to Plato's cave: models trained on different "shadows" of reality are said to be recovering a shared model of the reality that casts them. Independent methods landing on the same answer is usually treated as evidence that the answer tracks something real, rather than being an artifact of any single method.

But there's a serious complication: superposition. Because networks are pushed to represent more independent features than they have neurons, individual neurons end up polysemantic, each responding to several unrelated concepts at once. To get anything interpretable out of this, researchers fit a sparse autoencoder on top of the raw activations — essentially learning an overcomplete dictionary of directions — and call the resulting directions "the features." But that dictionary is a choice, not a measurement, and the choice doesn't stabilize: make the dictionary bigger and single features reliably "split" into several more specific ones, with no principled place to stop. That looks less like finding the joints of the network and more like picking a zoom level — you can always describe a coastline more finely without ever hitting bedrock.

My tentative way through this borrows from Daniel Dennett's "real patterns" move rather than picking a side outright. On that view, a pattern earns the label "real" not by being the one uniquely correct carving of the underlying system, but by letting you compress and successfully intervene on the system better than the alternatives available to you — the way a center of gravity, or the equator, is real and useful without being a fundamental joint in nature. Given feature splitting, full realism — the claim that there is one true feature basis waiting to be found — looks too strong. But given that clamping these features actually does causal work, dismissing the whole enterprise as an arbitrary story also looks too strong. So: these features are real in the same modest, observer-relative sense that Dennett's patterns are real — a genuine result, just a smaller one than the "we mapped the network's true anatomy" framing suggests.

I'd like your honest reaction to this, especially on three points. Does the move to Dennett's "real patterns" actually resolve the tension I set up, or am I just using it to avoid committing to an answer? Is there a stronger argument for realism, or for instrumentalism, that I'm not engaging with? And is there anything technically off in how I've described superposition, feature splitting, or the interpretability results — something that would undercut the philosophical point once it's corrected?
