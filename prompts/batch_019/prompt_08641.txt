Until this spring I would have struggled to define "pramāṇa" at all; a mid-career sabbatical led me, on something of a whim, to enroll in a part-time seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and I have since become unexpectedly invested in a dispute that keeps resurfacing in our discussion sections. The question is whether upamāna ("comparison" or "analogy") really deserves to stand alongside perception, inference, and testimony as one of Nyāya's four independent pramāṇas, or whether it quietly collapses into inference once you look closely. Two classmates got into an extended version of this argument after class last week, using the textbook example everyone in the seminar now has memorized: someone who has never seen a gavaya (a kind of wild ox) is told by a forest-dweller that a gavaya resembles a cow, and later, encountering the actual animal in the forest, recognizes it as a gavaya purely on the strength of that resemblance. I have a short position paper due on exactly this dispute and still can't decide who got the better of it, so I wrote the exchange down afterward and would like an outside read.

(1) He opened by arguing that upamāna does no independent work: the final cognition "this is a gavaya" is just an ordinary inference from two things the person already has -- testimony that gavayas resemble cows, and a fresh perception of cow-like resemblance in the animal in front of them -- so there is no need for a fourth pramāṇa to cover it. (2) She replied that this can't be a garden-variety inference, because Nyāya inference requires a previously grasped invariable concomitance between mark and target, of the kind built up from repeated observation, as with smoke and fire; by hypothesis, this person has no prior grasp of any link between "resembling a cow" and "being correctly called a gavaya" -- that link is exactly what gets established for the first time in this encounter, so it must be its own kind of cognitive achievement. (3) He came back by noting that plenty of uncontroversial inferences rest on general connections a person has only ever taken on someone else's word rather than personally verified -- you can infer a diagnosis from a lab report without ever having checked the underlying correlation yourself -- so the mere fact that the connection here was borrowed from testimony, rather than personally established, can't be what makes this not-inference. (4) She answered that the real individuating criterion Nyāya uses isn't where the general connection came from, but whether the causal instrument (karaṇa) producing the cognition is of a distinct kind: perceiving a similarity while recalling a verbal description is a different sort of instrument from perceiving a mark while recalling a concomitance, whatever that concomitance's origin -- and if that distinction is erased, the same reductionist move would just as easily dissolve inference itself into perception-plus-testimony, leaving Nyāya only one pramāṇa in the end. (5) He closed by granting that this is Nyāya's official line, but objected that it looks suspiciously ad hoc: there's no independent test for when two causal processes count as genuinely different kinds rather than variants of the same kind, so it protects upamāna only by stipulation -- and if that flexible a standard were legitimate, he didn't see why it wouldn't equally justify treating ordinary recognitional memory as its own pramāṇa, which Nyāya explicitly and for independent reasons refuses to do.

Neither of them budged, and the instructor's only intervention was to look pleased that we were fighting about it. Could you go through the exchange move by move, in the order numbered above, and say who has the better of it at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also value your sense of what, if anything, both of them are missing -- including, if it's relevant, whether the dispute looks different once you bring in how schools other than Nyāya, the Mīmāṃsakas for instance, analyzed this very same gavaya example.
