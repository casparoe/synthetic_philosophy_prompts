Because my last novel spent three hundred pages inside Frederick Douglass's head, our federal courthouse has asked me to give the keynote at this year's Fourth of July naturalization ceremony, where dozens of new citizens from thirty-odd countries will take the oath on the very holiday Douglass once used to ask a Rochester crowd what American independence could possibly mean to someone the nation had not yet promised to include. I don't want the easy version of that speech, the denunciation everyone quotes from "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" but the harder argument Douglass reached only after breaking with William Lloyd Garrison: that because the Constitution's text never once says "slave" or "slavery," it has to be read by its letter, not by the nation's practice, as a promise the country broke rather than a bargain that was rotten from the start. The presiding judge has asked every speaker to stay clear of anything readable as present-day politics, which leaves that distinction — text against practice — as about the only honest thing I can find to say to a room holding both these new citizens and a couple of Douglass scholars from the local university who will notice if I get it wrong. Write the speech: eight minutes, meant to be read aloud, that holds the promise and the betrayal together without letting either cancel the other out.
