My friend Theo and I got into a whole argument last night that I still can't quite let go of. It started because he'd read some article about a company using an AI system to help redesign the next version of itself, and his take was basically: no big deal — the AI just runs the new version through a bunch of tests, proves to itself that it's safe, and hands off the reins, same as any good engineering practice. I tried to explain this thing I half-remember from some AI safety reading about "embedded agency" — the idea that a real agent can't actually stand outside the system it's reasoning about and check it from a safe distance, because its made of the exact same stuff as that system, running on hardware that's just another part of the world it's trying to steer. Which means it can never fit a complete model of that world, including itself, inside itself — there's always some piece left over that isn't accounted for.

Where it got heated is when I tried to argue this isn't just an abstract worry — even something as basic as an agent trusting its own future self, or a successor it's building, turns out to be genuinely hard to guarantee from the inside. Apparently there's a real result (something to do with Löb's theorem, if I'm remembering that right) showing that a reasoner generally can't fully certify that something as capable as itself is trustworthy, without either an infinite regress of proofs or just taking a leap of faith somewhere along the line. Theo's take was that I was dressing up a mundane practical problem — test it more, basically — in fancy language to make it sound like a deep philosophical bind. I don't think it's mundane at all, and honestly I think about this stuff alot more than is probably normal for a random Tuesday argument. It was 1am and neither of us budged.

I want to see this worked through as fiction instead of argued about after a few beers. Write me a short story, a page or two, built around some agent — an AI, sure, but it could just as easily be a person, a company, an institution, an ant colony, I don't know — that's materially embedded in and made of the same stuff as the world it's trying to act on, and has to make a real decision that hinges on trusting itself, or a successor, or its own read of the situation — with no outside vantage point to double-check any of it from. Please don't make the twist "and then it became conscious and turned evil" — I want the actual dramatic tension to come from that no-outside-view, can't-fully-model-yourself bind itself, not from a bolted-on villain. Go wherever setting makes that bind feel most real — doesn't have to be sci-fi at all.
