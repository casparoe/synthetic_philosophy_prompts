So I had this whole argument last night with my friend Theo (he's a jazz guitarist, really into theory) about whether music actually "means" anything beyond itself, and at some point I dragged out Schopenhauer because I remembered he has this theory where music is somehow more directly metaphysically real than every other art form — like paintings and novels are copies of copies, but music skips a step and copies the thing itself. Theo wasn't having it, he kept saying it's basically just vibrating air pressure that we've culturally decided to get emotional about, but I think theres something to Schopenhauer's move even if the way he argues for it is completely unhinged by modern standards. So afterward I dug out my copy of The World as Will and Representation (the old Haldane and Kemp translation, book 3, near the end of section 52) to find the bit I was half-remembering, and honestly rereading it just confused me more, especially the specific analogy he draws between the different "voices" in a piece of music and the levels of nature.

Here's the passage:

"I recognise in the deepest tones of harmony, in the bass, the lowest grades of the objectification of will, unorganised nature, the mass of the planet... Now, further, in the whole of the complemental parts which make up the harmony between the bass and the leading voice singing the melody, I recognise the whole gradation of the Ideas in which the will objectifies itself. Those nearer to the bass are the lower of these grades, the still unorganised, but yet manifold phenomenal things; the higher represent to me the world of plants and beasts. The definite intervals of the scale are parallel to the definite grades of the objectification of will, the definite species in nature. The departure from the arithmetical correctness of the intervals, through some temperament, or produced by the key selected, is analogous to the departure of the individual from the type of the species. Indeed, even the impure discords, which give no definite interval, may be compared to the monstrous abortions produced by beasts of two species, or by man and beast... Lastly, in the melody, in the high, singing, principal voice leading the whole and progressing with unrestrained freedom, in the unbroken significant connection of one thought from beginning to end representing a whole, I recognise the highest grade of the objectification of will, the intellectual life and effort of man."

Could you help me actually work through this? Two things in particular are bugging me. First, is there meant to be some real, load-bearing reason that bass / middle voices / melody map onto minerals / plants-and-animals / humans specifically, or is he just reaching for a poetic-sounding analogy that doesn't do any actual argumentative work in his system? And second — the bit about musical "temperament" (the small tuning compromises you have to make so a keyboard instrument is playable across different keys) being compared to "the departure of the individual from the type of the species," and badly tuned intervals being like "monstrous abortions" from crossbred animals — I can't tell if that's supposed to connect rigorously to his ideas about the Will, the Platonic Ideas, and the principium individuationis, or whether it's just a flourish he got carried away with. Is there a way to make that analogy actually do real philosophical work, or should I just concede to Theo that Schopenhauer lost the plot a bit here?
