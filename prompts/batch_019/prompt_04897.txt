Ever since ARC's report on Eliciting Latent Knowledge made the rounds on the rationalist blogosphere, I've watched the same disagreement resurface every few months on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, usually without anyone quite settling it, and I'd like a considered verdict rather than another restatement of the setup. As I understand the core worry: a "reporter" trained to answer questions about a predictor's internal state can get away with implementing a human simulator -- something that outputs whatever a human watching the raw sensor data would conclude -- instead of a direct translator that faithfully reports what the predictor itself represents as true, and the report's own worst-case analysis suggests that most of the natural countermeasures (penalizing extra computation inside the reporter, penalizing dependence on "downstream" variables, demanding consistency across many rephrasings and counterfactual variants of a question) all admit some contrived predictor for which the human simulator still wins out. Some people I've argued with take this to show that ELK cannot be solved by clever loss functions or dataset construction alone, and that some fundamentally different lever -- most plausibly interpretability -- is indispensable for privileging the direct translator over the human simulator; others insist that ARC's counterexamples all lean on adversarially constructed predictors that gradient descent would be very unlikely to actually produce, and that in realistic training regimes a sufficiently rich battery of consistency checks would make the human simulator the more complex, and hence disfavored, hypothesis once the predictor is genuinely smarter than the humans labeling its outputs.

Write an essay that settles this disagreement rather than just narrating it. Take seriously both the worst-case framing that ARC insists on and the objection that worst-case guarantees might be the wrong bar to set in the first place, and be explicit about what would have to be true of realistic training dynamics for either side to be vindicated. Don't retreat to a "reasonable people disagree" ending -- commit to a verdict on whether purely behavioral, training-based strategies could in principle rule out the human simulator, or whether something like interpretability is indispensable, and say what evidence would change your mind.
