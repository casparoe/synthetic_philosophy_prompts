I am at work on a novel whose narrator spends most of the book flatly denying something the reader can already see is true of her, and I want to end with a scene in which she comes as close as prose allows to holding the forbidden thought and disbelieving it in the same breath — genuinely, silently, without ever putting either half into words even to herself. Before I write it, I need to know whether that is something a mind can actually do, or only something a sentence can pretend to describe.

G. E. Moore's puzzle about sentences like "it's raining, but I don't believe it" — what's come to be called Moore's paradox — is standardly explained as a fact about assertion: to assert "P" commits you to believing P, so the second clause cancels a commitment the first has just incurred, and this holds whether the assertion is spoken aloud or only silently avowed to oneself. That explanation says nothing directly about belief itself, considered apart from any act, public or private, of putting the two thoughts together in words.

Argue for a definite answer to the question this leaves open: can a person genuinely, occurrently believe both halves of "P, and I don't believe that P" at once, as a single integrated state rather than two beliefs merely held at different moments — or does whatever rules out the sentence and the silent avowal reach all the way down to belief itself, so that any character who seems to manage it is really doing something else (self-deception, compartmentalized belief, plain ignorance of her own mind) that only resembles the genuine article? Defend your answer against the strongest form of the opposing view, and give a straight verdict rather than a survey of the possibilities.
