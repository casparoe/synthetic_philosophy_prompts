For my political philosophy elective this semester, our final project asks us to take one thinker from the syllabus and use their actual arguments -- not just their general reputation -- to work through a problem they could never have anticipated. I've chosen John Stuart Mill, and I want to test his ideas against a proposal called "AI safety via debate." I came across it while reading about a problem in AI safety known as scalable oversight: roughly, the difficulty of a human overseer reliably judging the output of an AI system that may already know more than they do about the question at hand. The debate proposal, from a 2018 paper by Irving, Christiano, and Amodei, tries to get around this by having two copies of a system argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge who cannot verify the answer directly -- the idea being that it should be much harder to win the debate by lying than by telling the truth, since a dishonest argument can be exposed by a competent opponent even when the judge alone couldn't have caught it.

My first instinct was that Mill would be enthusiastic about this: it looks like a mechanized version of the argument he makes in On Liberty, that a truth never allowed to collide with error hardens into what he calls a "dead dogma" instead of a living, reasoned conviction, and that even a false or foolish position deserves a hearing because contesting it is how the true position earns its justification. But going back through the rest of the syllabus complicated that answer. In Utilitarianism, Mill argues that when the question is which of two things is more valuable, only someone with direct experience of both is in a position to judge -- which seems to describe exactly what the human judge in a debate is not. And in Considerations on Representative Government, he's surprisingly willing to give some citizens' political judgment more institutional weight than others', on the grounds that people without relevant education aren't equipped to assess certain questions on equal footing with those who are. Both of these sit oddly next to the free-marketplace-of-ideas Mill I expected, and both seem to bear directly on whether he'd trust an ordinary, non-expert human to referee a contest between two systems that both know more than she does.

So here is what I would like your help with: working only from what Mill actually argued, rather than his reputation as "the free speech philosopher," what do you think his verdict on AI safety via debate would be, and why? When I raised the plural-voting point in class, my teacher pushed back that Considerations on Representative Government is making a narrow, practical point about suffrage qualifications and shouldn't be stretched to cover something as different as judging an AI debate, so that On Liberty ought to get the interpretive weight here. I'm not sure that fully works, since the worry that people without relevant expertise shouldn't be treated as final authorities on questions requiring it seems like exactly the worry that would also apply to a debate judge -- but I'd rather have your independent read than just take my own side. Please don't simply conclude that Mill would approve because he liked open argument; I want the actual reasoning spelled out, including any respects in which his own framework would predict that debate could go wrong.
