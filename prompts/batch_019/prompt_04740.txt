I've spent a good deal of time working through John Stuart Mill's On Liberty on my own, and one argument that has stayed with me is his claim that no one should be permitted to sign a valid contract selling themselves into slavery: his reasoning is that the whole point of protecting a person's liberty is to let them pursue their own good in their own way, so a contract that permanently signs away all future liberty defeats the very purpose that justified respecting it in the first place. This has left me wondering what Mill would make of modern "Ulysses contracts" — for instance, a compulsive gambler who has themselves legally barred from every casino in a state, or a psychiatric patient who signs an advance directive consenting in advance to involuntary treatment during some future crisis, even overriding their objections at the time. These are nothing like slavery, but they share its basic structure: a present self permanently or semi-permanently binding a future self who may not consent when the time actually comes. Would Mill's own argument against self-enslavement contracts commit him to opposing this sort of arrangement as well, or does his framework offer a principled basis for treating them differently — say, on grounds of revocability, the narrowness of what is being given up, or the fact that the arrangement is meant to preserve rather than extinguish the person's longer-term agency? I would like to see how his specific reasoning actually holds up here, rather than a general summary of his views on liberty and paternalism.
