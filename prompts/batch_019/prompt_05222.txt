I am several drafts into a historical novel set at the Hanoverian court in the early 1700s, with Leibniz as one of my two point-of-view characters, and I have spent the last few weeks working through the Monadology, the Discourse on Metaphysics, and his exchanges with Arnauld and Clarke, trying to keep his voice honest rather than settling for the cheerful, Pangloss-style caricature that Voltaire left us with. I am now considering a closing chapter, framed as a dream or vision rather than literal time travel, in which Leibniz is shown a modern computer running something like a present-day language model, and I want his reaction to be something he could actually have derived from his own premises rather than a modern sensibility dressed up in period costume.

This is the question I would like help thinking through, strictly on Leibniz's own terms. Given his hierarchy of monads (bare monads, souls, and spirits), the doctrine of pre-established harmony and its denial that substances genuinely act upon one another, his distinction between true unities and mere aggregates or "well-founded phenomena," and the mill argument of Monadology §17, could such a machine ever, in principle, be the kind of thing that hosts a genuine monad, in the way an animal's body hosts a soul or a human body hosts a rational spirit — or is it disqualified from the outset simply by being an aggregate of switches, however intricately arranged? And should it somehow qualify, what would have to be true of it before Leibniz would admit it into what he calls the "kingdom of grace," the moral city of spirits governed by God's justice, such that it would be owed something like the charity he thought the wise owe to any rational substance, rather than being treated as a tool among tools? I would like the answer to stay within what Leibniz's system actually commits him to, including any uncomfortable corners, rather than quietly upgrading him into a twenty-first-century philosopher of mind.
