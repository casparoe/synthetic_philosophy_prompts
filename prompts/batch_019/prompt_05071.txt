I'm about two-thirds through a novel where the whole plot hinges on a woman's report of a break-in (and worse) getting waved off by the local cops, basically because of who she is—poor, a "known troublemaker," with a history of being called hysterical by everyone from her family doctor to her own mother. My partner's sister is doing a philosophy PhD, and when I described the plot to her over christmas she said "oh, that's basically Miranda Fricker" and lent me her copy of Epistemic Injustice. Funny enough, Fricker leans on novels for her own case studies too—Tom Robinson's trial in To Kill a Mockingbird is the big one, but there's also, if I'm remembering this right, a bit about Marge in The Talented Mr Ripley, who's correct about Ripley the entire time and gets waved off as a jealous hysteric for her trouble. Felt like a weird nod from the universe that I was onto something.

Anyway, here's the actual thing I can't untangle, and its more specific than the basic testimonial/hermeneutical injustice split, which I've got down fine. Fricker is adamant the injustice is structural—it tracks a society's whole distribution of credibility along lines of race, gender, class, etc, not just one cop having a bad day. But then when she gets to the actual fix, it's pure virtue epistemology: the individual hearer is supposed to cultivate a corrective disposition, basically train themselves to notice and discount their own prejudicial gut reactions in the moment of judging someone. People like Elizabeth Anderson have pushed back hard on exactly this move, arguing that if the diagnosis is structural then pinning the remedy on individuals becoming more virtuous is close to a category error—what you actually need is institutional redesign (how juries get picked, how complaints get processed, who's even in the room), especially since individual virtue is exactly the kind of thing that tends to buckle under situational pressure anyway. This isn't purely academic for me either, since it bears on how I want to end the book: a redemption arc where one detective finally learns to listen properly is a very different animal from an ending where the whole department's procedures have to get overhauled for the resolution to feel earned instead of sappy. I brought this to my writing group last week and it split the room right down the middle.

So here's what I want: write me an essay that actually stakes out a position on this. Lay out the strongest version of "the individual-virtue remedy is adequate, if it's genuinely cultivated" and the strongest version of "a structural diagnosis demands a structural remedy, full stop," but don't let yourself off the hook at the end with a mushy "both matter, in their own way"—if that ends up being close to your real view, fine, but then commit to some specific account of how they fit together rather than just gesturing at both.
