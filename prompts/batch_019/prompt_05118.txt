Neither of us really won, we just got more wine and kept going in circles, so now I want to actually work out my position properly instead of just vibing in an arguement.

Here's roughly my view, typed up the next morning: the fact that biologists have like six or seven competing "species concepts" (biological, phylogenetic, ecological, cohesion, etc.) that carve up the same organisms differently, and that none of them generalize to all of life - the biological one breaks down for anything asexual, the phylogenetic one massively inflates species counts and arguably just tracks how much DNA data happens to have been sequenced for a given group - seems like pretty strong evidence that "species" isn't a natural kind we're discovering so much as a real-but-human-imposed sorting that tracks several different, only loosely correlated things (reproductive compatibility, ecology, shared ancestry, morphology) which happen to line up often enough to be useful but don't line up perfectly, and there's no further fact about which one is "the real" boundary when they come apart. Even Darwin seemed to basically think this - I'm pretty sure he says flat out somewhere in Origin that he doesn't think there's a principled line between "species" and mere "variety," and that he mostly uses the word as a convenient label for taxonomists. My friend's comeback was something about Richard Boyd's "homeostatic property cluster" idea - that a kind can be totally real and objective without necessary and sufficient conditions, as long as the properties cluster together for a causal reason (gene flow plus selection, in this case) - and I didn't have a good response to that one, I just kind of mumbled something and changed the subject.
