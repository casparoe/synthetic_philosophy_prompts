So this whole thing started because of a kind of stupid argument at work. We were doing a code review and I'd written a `Point` value class where `equals()` just compares the fields, and a teammate who did a philosophy minor before switching to CS said something like "so if two Points have the same x and y, they're just... the same point? what does 'two of them' even mean then?" That sent me down a rabbit hole into something called the identity of indiscernibles, which is apparently the Leibniz-descended claim that if x and y share literally every property, they have to be numerically one thing, not two. The classic objection is Max Black's 1952 paper, written wierdly as an actual dialogue between two people, where he describes a hypothetical universe containing nothing at all except two solid iron spheres, perfectly symmetric, same size, same composition, same everything, some fixed distance apart. Black's whole point is that these two spheres share every single property, including relational ones like "being some number of miles from a sphere just like itself," and yet there are clearly two of them, so the principle is false.

Here's what's been bugging me for days now. The first response everyone reaches for, including me, is "ok but their positions are different, and position is a property, so they ARE discernible." But then I found some old forum posts arguing that this doesn't actually work, because in a perfectly symmetric, featureless space, the two locations are exactly as indiscernible from each other as the spheres were — you've just moved the same problem up one level, from the spheres to the points they occupy. Somebody in the thread compared it to the "hole argument" from general relativity (which I did not follow at all, something about spacetime points not having identities independent of whatever is sitting at them), which made me think the "just cite spatial position" move might be a dead end unless you're willing to say points have some kind of raw, brute identity that has nothing to do with any of their properties — which felt like giving up on the whole principle rather then saving it.

So here's what I want: write me the strongest possible argument that the identity of indiscernibles is still defensible, even granting that the "just appeal to position" move collapses into that same regress. I don't want the cheap answer where you just define a property like "being this particular sphere and not that one" and call it a day — that seems to make the principle unfalsifiable and trivial, which can't be what people were actually arguing about for 300 years. I want an argument that confronts the regress problem with the points head-on, and either shows where it's legitimate to stop it, or makes the case for why stopping exactly there, instead of at the spheres, is principled rather than just an arbitrary place to plant a flag.
