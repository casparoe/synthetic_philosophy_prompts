I'm teaching our grad seminar on conditionals again this term, and every year I hit the same wall with Lewis's triviality result. Everyone can recite the headline — if the probability of "if A, then B" always equals the conditional probability P(B|A) (Adams' Thesis) for every reasonable credence function you might have, the whole thing collapses into triviality, so an indicative conditional apparently can't just be a garden-variety proposition sitting in the same probability space as A and B. Fine. But when we actually go through Lewis's 1976 proof line by line, the step where you conditionalize on the conditional itself (and separately on its negation) to figure out what happens to B given A always gets a few students objecting that it's a slight of hand, like we've built in exactly the assumption we needed. I know it's technically licensed by requiring Adams' Thesis to hold not just for your current credence but for every credence function you could reach by learning some further proposition, but I have a hard time making that closure requirement feel like anything other than a technicality nobody would've signed up for in advance, rather than the thing that's actually doing all the work.

So what I'd love from you is the gut-level version, not another run through the algebra: why is that closure-under-conditionalization requirement really the load-bearing assumption, and why isn't conditionalizing on the conditional itself question-begging? I'd also like to understand, in plain terms, what feature of ordinary conditionals — the kind that are supposed to track correlations, like "if you strike the match it'll light" — actually gets destroyed once you accept the premises. And finally, is Lewis's own moral, that indicative conditionals must not express propositions in the relevant sense, really forced on us, or is there a more conservative place to get off the train that I'm not giving my students enough credit for? You don't need to give me the fully general 1986 version — I just want something sturdier than "trust me, the math works out" to hand to a room full of second-years.
