I'm a novelist, and I've recently joined the three-person judging panel for a small endowed fiction prize whose trust deed, drawn up by a benefactor who was a devoted reader of Tolstoy, binds us to the tests he sets out in What Is Art? rather than to "literary merit" at large. For each finalist we must weigh the individuality, clarity, and sincerity of the feeling it conveys, since together these determine how strongly a book infects a reader; separately, the deed instructs us to withhold the prize from any book whose feeling, however intensely conveyed, fails to unite readers — in its words — "without distinction of education or class," since for Tolstoy a work only the cultivated few can enter is suspect no matter how deeply it moves them. The deed permits "no award" in a year when nothing clears both bars.

My co-judges are deadlocked over this year's two finalists. One champions The Absence Room, a fragmented novel about a mother's dementia that most ordinary readers abandon confused, though the small circle who finish it call it the most exact rendering of grief they know; she dismisses our other finalist's broad appeal as manufactured. The other champions All Our Winters, a family saga that has reduced readers of every background to the same tears at the same reconciliation scene; he argues The Absence Room is disqualified outright, however sincerely felt, because needing the right training to feel something is precisely the defect Tolstoy diagnosed in the art of his own class.

Please draft the recommendation memo I'll bring to our final meeting: name a winner, or argue for no award, and engage honestly with the strongest objection to whichever call you make.
