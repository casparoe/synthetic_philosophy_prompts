I have spent the past few weeks reporting on how newsrooms decide whether a single, uncorroborated anonymous source justifies running a major story, and the reporting sent me back to a piece of epistemology I had not properly thought about since a seminar years ago: the dispute between "reductionists" about testimony, who hold that trusting what someone tells you is rational only insofar as that trust can be cashed out in independent, non-testimonial evidence of the speaker's reliability, and "anti-reductionists," who follow Thomas Reid in treating our default trust in testimony as a basic source of justification, on a par with perception or memory, that does not need to be earned case by case. What struck me on closer reading is how quickly the popular version of this debate flattens into slogans, when the real disagreement is much more specific: Elizabeth Fricker's "local reductionism" holds that a competent hearer is always monitoring a speaker for cues to sincerity and competence rather than running some global induction over "testifiers in general," whereas Tyler Burge's a priori "Acceptance Principle" and C. A. J. Coady's writings on testimony counter that almost nobody could ever assemble the independent track record reductionism seems to demand — a small child certainly could not, and arguably neither can an adult, whose "independent" evidence about the wider world turns out, on inspection, to be mostly testimonial as well.

With that in mind, could you write me a dialogue of roughly 700–1000 words between a contemporary reductionist and a contemporary anti-reductionist about testimony? Stage it concretely: an editor has just run a major story on the strength of one anonymous, unverifiable source, and two philosopher friends who happened to be in the newsroom that night stay on after everyone else has left, arguing about whether the editor's trust was rational. Have the reductionist press the anti-reductionist on where exactly the line falls between warranted default trust and plain gullibility, and have the anti-reductionist press the reductionist on the regress problem: where the independent evidence for a source's reliability is even supposed to come from, given that so much of what passes for "independent evidence" turns out itself to rest on somebody's word. Both characters should be arguing at full strength, genuinely engaging each other's objections rather than restating their own position twice over, and I would rather you not adjudicate a winner at the end — let the disagreement stand unresolved.
