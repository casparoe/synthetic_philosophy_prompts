I'm a philosophy PhD student mostly working on early modern epistemology (lots of Descartes and Arnauld), but this term I'm auditing a comparative skepticism seminar, and this week's session got kind of heated over the very end of the Qiwulun chapter of the Zhuangzi (chapter 2, "Discussion on Making All Things Equal") — the butterfly dream bit. Here's the passage as we had it, in Burton Watson's translation or near enough to it:

"Once Chuang Chou dreamed he was a butterfly, a butterfly flitting and fluttering around, happy with himself and doing as he pleased. He didn't know he was Chuang Chou. Suddenly he woke up and there he was, solid and unmistakable Chuang Chou. But he didn't know if he was Chuang Chou who had dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he was Chuang Chou. Between Chuang Chou and a butterfly there must be some distinction. This is called the transformation of things."

One guy in the seminar is convinced this is basically proto-Cartesian, i.e. a fully general "you can never tell from the inside whether your dreaming or awake" argument, same structure as the First Meditation, just older and prettier. I'm not so sure, mostly because of the last two sentences, which nobody wanted to actually sit with. If the whole point was that Chou can't tell which side of the dream he's really on, why does the passage then just flatly assert that there IS a real distinction between Chou and the butterfly, and file that under "the transformation of things" like thats a solution rather than a new problem? is that Zhuangzi quietly smuggling back in the very distinction the thought experiment was supposed to make unstable, or is "transformation of things" not an epistemic claim at all, but a metaphysical one about identity surviving change — in which case the passage might not even be doing the skeptical work everyone in the seminar assumed it was doing. Also, if its relevant: earlier in the same chapter there's that bit about how there's no neutral third party who could ever adjudicate a dispute between two people with opposing views — does the butterfly ending extend that same perspectival relativism to a single self across different mental states, or is something else going on there? Would love an actual close reading here, not the standard "haha maybe we're all just dreaming" gloss everyone gives this passage.
