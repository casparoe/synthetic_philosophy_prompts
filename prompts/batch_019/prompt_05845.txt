So I tagged along with my uncle to one of his vintage watch meetups last weekend (he's way into it, I'm just watch-adjacent through him) and ended up watching two of the regulars, Marcus and Ollie, get into an argument that I haven't been able to stop turning over in my head since. Marcus had brought in a watch from the 60s to show off, and Ollie told him flat out it was what collectors call a "Frankenwatch" - the case, dial, hands and movement are all genuine and all correct for that exact model and year, but they didn't start out together, somebody built the watch later by combining parts pulled from a few different donor watches to make one more complete-looking piece. Marcus got pretty defensive about it.

Ollie's argument was that even though every single part is 100% real, the watch as a whole is basically fake, because what makes an old watch valuable isn't just "the parts are correct," it's that its supposed to be one specific object that's had a single continuous history since 1965 - and this thing doesn't have that, it's got four different histories stitched into one case, so calling it "a 1965 [model]" the way you would an untouched one is a kind of lie, even with zero counterfeit parts anywhere on it. Marcus shot back that this is a nuts standard, because literally every sixty year old watch has had parts swapped at some point, even ones with full paperwork - crystals, crowns, sometimes the dial or the mainspring, replaced during routine repairs, sometimes by the brand's own service centers - so if "one unbroken history" is the bar, nothing on earth clears it, and Ollie's just arbitrarily decided that a shop replacing something is fine but a shop combining things isn't. Ollie's comeback was that there's a real difference between a manufacturer or authorized center swapping a broken part under its own name, which is a sanctioned continuation of that watch's story, versus a private seller quietly marrying parts from unrelated watches to fake up a rare configuration and charge more for it - one is maintenance, the other is manufacturing a past that never happened. Marcus's answer to that was that basically nobody can actually verify most "fully original" watches were only ever touched by authorized hands either, so Ollie's just trusting a paper trail that's exactly as unprovable as anything else in this hobby, and that if the actual problem is Marcus not disclosing this to a future buyer, fine, that's an honesty issue - but it doesn't mean the watch itself is somehow worth less or less "real" once everybody involved already knows exactly what it is.

They went back and forth like this for a solid twenty minutes while my uncle just quietly ate his sandwich. It kind of reminds me of stuff I already half-think-about with old photographs and reprinted books, but I'd never heard it argued out this hard before and I honestly can't tell who's right. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who had the better of it at each specific point (not just an overall winner), and point out anything you think both of them are missing?
