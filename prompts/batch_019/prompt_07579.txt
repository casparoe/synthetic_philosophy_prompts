I am currently at work on a novel set in Restoration London, and one of its two point-of-view characters is Thomas Hobbes himself: elderly, notoriously stubborn, and still smarting over the new Royal Society's refusal to admit him as a fellow. I want to write a scene in which he holds forth at a private dinner, defending to a table of increasingly skeptical natural philosophers his lifelong conviction that civil philosophy could, in principle, be rendered every bit as certain as geometry, provided one starts from sufficiently careful definitions and reasons from them with real discipline. A friend who read an early draft, and who knows the political philosophy far better than the mathematics, warned me not to reduce Hobbes to a cardboard rationalist -- so before I draft the dinner scene in earnest, I want to make sure I understand the actual grounds of his confidence rather than a textbook simplification of it.

My working understanding is that Hobbes arrived at this conviction more or less empirically: having spent decades absorbed in geometry, working through proof after proof to his own satisfaction, he concluded from that hard-won personal experience that any body of reasoning built up from self-evident definitions and pursued with sufficient logical discipline is thereby guaranteed to yield certain truths. On this reading, it was precisely his track record of demonstrated mathematical success that emboldened him to attempt the same method on the commonwealth in Leviathan.

Assuming that is the right way to understand his motivation, could you help me identify two or three of the actual geometrical proofs or discoveries for which Hobbes was best known among his contemporaries? I would like my character to gesture toward one of them at the dinner table, fairly casually, as settled proof that his method had already passed its test in mathematics well before he ever turned it on politics.
