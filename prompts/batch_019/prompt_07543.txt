I'm drafting a scene, set at a royal debate assembly in classical India, in which a Naiyayika logician tries to dispose of anekantavada in a single stroke rather than with the usual dismissal of it as mere fence-sitting. His argument is that syadvada's governing claim -- that every assertion is true only "in some respect" (syat), as formalized in the sevenfold predication (saptabhangi) -- cannot itself be syat-qualified without losing its universal force, yet cannot be asserted unconditionally without becoming a counterexample to itself. I don't want him to be a strawman, so before he yields the floor, his argument needs to anticipate and dismantle the two moves Jain philosophers actually use to escape this dilemma: the naya/pramana distinction between a partial verbal expression and the manifold object it expresses, and the appeal to a kevalin's unconditioned omniscience as the doctrine's ultimate ground. Please write the strongest version of this argument, showing precisely why, on his view, neither move saves syadvada from self-refutation.
