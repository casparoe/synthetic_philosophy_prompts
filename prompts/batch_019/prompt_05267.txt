A friend of mine and I spent a long evening after our ethics reading group — and a couple of drinks afterward — arguing about whether the category of supererogation can be made philosophically stable, and neither of us was able to convince the other. We had just been discussing Scheffler's agent-centered prerogative and Slote's satisficing consequentialism, which, in different ways, try to license some permitted degree of self-favoring below full impartial maximization, so that acts that go beyond that baseline — say, giving away most of one's income to effective charities rather than the conventional ten percent — count as praiseworthy but strictly optional. My friend thought this gave supererogation the right kind of foundation. I kept raising a demandingness-style objection modeled on the one Kagan brings against such views in The Limits of Morality: if agents are permitted to weight their own interests more heavily than strict impartiality would allow because full impartiality would be too costly to demand, that very rationale seems to grow stronger, not weaker, as compliance becomes more costly, and I couldn't see what was supposed to fix the baseline at some moderate, "reasonable" point rather than letting it slide toward near-total self-preference — or, if you block that slide, collapsing back down toward full maximization and leaving nothing for supererogatory acts to exceed. We went in circles on this for well over an hour without either of us managing to articulate a non-arbitrary stopping point.

I'd like to see the argument conducted properly, by two people who actually know the terrain. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of equal ability: one defending a prerogative- or satisficing-based account of supererogation along the lines my friend was gesturing at, and the other mounting the "why stop here" challenge against it, with effective giving as the running example throughout. I'd like the second philosopher's pressure to escalate in three stages — first asking exactly where the permitted baseline is set and on what grounds, then asking whether that line can be defended on principle rather than by appeal to what commonsense morality already happens to believe, and finally asking whether the view can avoid collapsing into either full maximizing demandingness or a permission so broad that it becomes barely distinguishable from ordinary self-interest — with the first philosopher giving the strongest available reply at each stage rather than simply restating the original position. Please have both of them genuinely engage with each other's actual moves rather than talk past one another, and feel free to let the dialogue end in real impasse rather than forcing a tidy resolution.
