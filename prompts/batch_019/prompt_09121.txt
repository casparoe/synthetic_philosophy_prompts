I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but for the past few months I've been reading around the edges of the sorites paradox in my spare time -- the old puzzle about heaps and grains of sand, and more generally about words like "tall" or "bald" that don't seem to have a sharp cutoff. Most of what I've come across lays out two rival technical answers to the puzzle, called epistemicism and supervaluationism, but they're always presented as two separate summaries, one after the other, rather than as an actual disagreement where each side thinks the other has made a specific mistake. So I understand roughly what each side claims, but not where the real pressure points are once they're put in the same room.

As I understand it, the epistemicist -- I keep seeing Timothy Williamson's name attached to this -- holds that a word like "tall" really does have a precise cutoff, some exact height at which a person switches from not-tall to tall, and that we simply can't ever know where that cutoff falls, because our grasp of the word's meaning through ordinary usage isn't fine enough to pin it down. The supervaluationist, by contrast, denies that there's any hidden fact of that kind to be ignorant of: our use of "tall" is compatible with many slightly different ways of making it precise, and a borderline case is simply one where those different ways disagree, so the sentence comes out neither true nor false rather than secretly one or the other.

Could you write me a dialogue between the two of them, set at the front of a line of a thousand people arranged from shortest to tallest, arguing over exactly where "tall" starts to apply? I'd like the epistemicist to have to answer for how a fact that fine-grained could exist at all, fixed by nothing more than ordinary usage, and I'd like the supervaluationist to have to answer for whether "borderline case" isn't itself just another vague predicate that puts the very same problem back one level up -- I gather this second worry has a name, something like "higher-order vagueness," though I've never actually seen anyone spell out how a supervaluationist is supposed to escape it. Please have each of them respond directly to the objection just raised, rather than mainly restating their own position.
