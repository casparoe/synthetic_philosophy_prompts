I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and I keep arriving at the same stalemate whenever I try to adjudicate the debate on my own — most recently after workshopping a draft of the chapter in my department's reading group. The situationist side, following Gilbert Harman and John Doris, leans heavily on the classic experimental literature: Hartshorne and May's early honesty studies with schoolchildren, Isen and Levin's demonstration that finding a dime left in a phone booth made passersby substantially more likely to help a stranger shortly afterward, and Darley and Batson's "Jerusalem to Jericho" study, in which seminarians in a hurry were far less likely to stop for a stranger apparently in distress, regardless of how they scored on independent measures of compassion. On the strength of this evidence, situationists argue that cross-situational behavioral consistency is too low — correlations rarely climbing much above what Mischel called the "personality coefficient," often put at around .30 — for anything like a robust Aristotelian virtue to exist as a trait that could genuinely explain and predict conduct across dissimilar situations. Defenders of virtue ethics respond with two moves that I find individually plausible but jointly a little too convenient: an aggregation argument in the spirit of Gopal Sreenivasan's work, holding that noisy individual acts can reveal a stable underlying disposition once averaged over enough occasions, and a "content of character" argument in the spirit of Rachana Kamtekar's work, holding that the mere fact of variable behavior across situations doesn't show a lack of virtue, since virtue itself partly consists in situational sensitivity — the real question being whether that sensitivity tracks genuine moral reasons or merely arbitrary pressures like being in a hurry.

What bothers me about deploying both moves together is that the resulting position starts to look unfalsifiable: any inconsistency gets absorbed either as noise that aggregation will smooth out, or as exactly the situational sensitivity that virtue would predict, leaving me unsure what evidence could possibly count against the relevant virtues existing at all. I'd like you to write a dialogue between a committed situationist and an equally well-informed virtue ethicist — one who has actually engaged with the empirical literature rather than just cited Aristotle — in which this worry is taken seriously by both sides rather than brushed aside. The situationist should press the unfalsifiability objection as hard as it will go, and the virtue ethicist should have to answer it with something more than a restatement of the aggregation and content-of-character moves; in turn, the virtue ethicist should get to press the situationist on whether the low correlations found in any single study really license the strong conclusion that the relevant traits don't exist, given standard aggregation principles from psychometrics, and the situationist should have to meet that challenge on its own statistical terms rather than waving it away. Please end the exchange with at least one genuine concession or point of clarification from each side, rather than a simple standoff.
