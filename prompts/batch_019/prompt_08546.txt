I never studied philosophy formally, but over the last year or so I've gotten fairly absorbed in reading about formal logic and how it gets used to model reasoning in fields like game theory and AI, mostly through blog posts, some old lecture notes I found online, and a secondhand logic textbook I've been slowly working through. One idea that has really stuck with me is what's apparently called the "problem of logical omniscience": in the standard possible-worlds systems that logicians use to formalize knowledge and belief (the sort that goes back to Hintikka), any agent modeled this way automatically ends up knowing every tautology and every logical consequence of whatever it already knows, no matter how many steps of derivation that would take, and regardless of whether any real mind could ever get there. From what I can tell, people have proposed various fixes, such as letting an agent simply fail to be "aware" of certain propositions, or building "impossible worlds" into the semantics, where the ordinary laws of logic can break down, so that failing to know something doesn't automatically generate a contradiction. None of these patches strikes me as fully satisfying, and the debate seems to still be very much open among people working in formal epistemology and multi-agent systems.

While reading about all this, I kept thinking about Leibniz, who, as far as I understand, was the first philosopher to talk about "possible worlds" in something close to the modern sense, who dreamed of a universal characteristic and a calculus of reasoning precise enough that two people could settle any dispute simply by sitting down and calculating, and who also drew a sharp line between truths of reason, which he thought could always be resolved into identities through an analysis that might have to run to infinity, and truths of fact, which he thought only God's mind could grasp all at once, in a single intuition, rather than through any finite chain of steps. It seems to me he was already, in his own vocabulary, grappling with something like the gap between a mind that sees every entailment of a proposition instantaneously and minds like ours, which can only inch toward the same truths through fallible, time-consuming reasoning.

Given all that, I would like you to work out, as carefully and specifically as you can, what you think Leibniz's own considered position would be if he were confronted with the modern problem of logical omniscience and the standard proposals for handling it. Would he most likely treat the possible-worlds model of knowledge as a defensible idealization of a finite intellect straining toward something like God's-eye omniscience, or would he think it fundamentally misdescribes what it is to be a limited reasoner? And given that he is the very figure who gave "possible worlds" their philosophical standing in the first place, how do you think he would react to "impossible worlds" being invoked to fix the problem: would that strike him as a natural extension of his own framework, or as something closer to a contradiction in terms, given how he understood possibility, contradiction, and the structure of God's understanding? I am less interested in a quick verdict than in seeing you actually trace how his specific commitments pull in different directions on this, including anywhere you think it is genuinely unclear how he would come down.
