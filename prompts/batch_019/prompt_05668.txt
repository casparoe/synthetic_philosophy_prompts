A few weeks ago I had a long, increasingly technical argument with a friend of mine, a biostatistician, about what it even means to say that a particular patient has a given percent chance of surviving five years. He maintained that any such figure is hostage to the reference class used to compute it: that a given patient belongs to indefinitely many equally legitimate comparison classes, that there is no non-arbitrary way to privilege one over the rest, and that a clinician who quotes a single number to a patient is dressing up a convention as a measurement. I pushed back that medicine cannot simply throw up its hands at this, since patients still have to make real decisions and need some number to act on. The disagreement has become more than academic, because I sit on the shared decision-making committee at the hospital where I work — the body that sets standards for how prognostic statistics are presented in patient decision aids — and a case matching our argument almost exactly has landed on the agenda.

A 43-year-old patient with a rare sarcoma subtype is choosing between an aggressive resection with significant expected morbidity and a palliative approach, and wants a five-year survival estimate to help her decide. Her surgical oncologist quoted 61 percent, computed from a large national registry's broad category for her tumor site and stage. A specialist she consulted for a second opinion quoted 22 percent, computed from a pooled case series of fourteen patients matched on her specific genomic subtype and comorbidities. Both figures are correctly calculated from real data; they differ only in which population of "similar patients" was treated as the relevant comparison class, and a third reasonable clinician could very plausibly produce a third number from a third defensible class. The patient's family has concluded that someone on her care team is incompetent, and is demanding that the hospital simply state "the real number." On the committee itself, opinion has already split three ways: some members want to mandate the narrowest available reference class on the theory that specificity trumps everything; others want to mandate the broadest adequately powered class on the theory that small samples are unreliable; a few want to abandon point estimates for ranges entirely.

Please draft the short memo I will circulate ahead of next week's meeting, recommending a concrete policy for how our oncologists should present survival statistics in cases like this one. I want the memo to commit to an actual recommendation rather than merely cataloguing the options already on the table, and I want it to engage seriously with my friend's underlying objection — that no such rule can be anything more than a convention dressed up as a methodology — rather than treating the choice of reference class as a narrow technical detail that any competent statistician could resolve.
