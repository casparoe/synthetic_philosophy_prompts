In my decision theory seminar we've been covering the Kelly criterion and what's sometimes called "geometric rationality" -- the ergodicity-economics case, associated with Ole Peters, for treating log-optimal betting as objectively correct rather than as just one utility function among others -- and the material set off an argument between two friends from that seminar that I haven't been able to settle myself.

1. Maya argued that once you accept the ergodicity point -- that wealth compounded through repeated betting evolves multiplicatively and is therefore non-ergodic, so the growth rate an individual actually experiences over time diverges from the expectation-value (ensemble) average of the same bet -- betting the Kelly fraction stops being one reasonable policy among others and becomes the objectively correct choice for anyone who cares about their own long-run wealth.

2. Dev objected with the classic Samuelson line: even granting the ergodicity point, being almost surely the best strategy as the number of bets goes to infinity doesn't make Kelly betting optimal over any finite number of bets, since that depends on the agent's utility function, and nothing about ergodicity forces that function to be logarithmic.

3. Maya replied that this misdescribes the disagreement: on the ergodicity-economics framing, the log growth rate isn't one utility function competing with others, it's a fact about the dynamics, so treating any deviation from it as a cost-free preference is the real mistake. A deviation needs to be earned by something about the agent's actual situation -- a bounded horizon, say, or a serious risk of ruin -- not simply asserted as a brute taste for risk.

4. Dev answered that this concedes his point rather than rebutting it: if departing from Kelly can be justified by horizon length or ruin risk, then the "objectively correct" strategy is only objectively correct in an idealized limit, and Maya has just reintroduced the agent's particular situation and goals -- exactly what a utility function is supposed to encode -- under a different name.
