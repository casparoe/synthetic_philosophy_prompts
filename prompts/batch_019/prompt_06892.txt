I'm one of four members on the rotating governance council of an income-sharing group house that grew out of the Bay Area rationalist and EA meetup scene. For about five years, everyone among our eight residents has signed a bylaw committing to pool all outside earnings above a fixed personal stipend into a common fund, split evenly each quarter, on a rationale -- borrowed from mutual-aid arrangements I'd first read about on various rationalist and EA forums -- that this lets people take on real career risk (an unfunded research year, a startup attempt, a return to grad school) without anyone falling through the cracks. The arrangement worked smoothly until this year, when one of our housemates, who does instructional design, started running paid workshops on applied rationality and decision-making, first as a favor to friends of friends, then as a real business after a well-known blogger linked to her site. Housemates and total strangers alike now pay for a seat, and her income from this is roughly six times the house median.

She's made her case to the council, informally and in writing, that money strangers hand her voluntarily for a skill she built and marketed on her own time isn't the kind of thing anyone else has a claim on, whatever the bylaw says. She's fond of citing Nozick's old point about Wilt Chamberlain and the fans who each drop an extra quarter into the till of their own free will, and says treating her workshop income as poolable is the same move, in miniature, as a state taxing away a windfall to preserve a distribution it prefers. Two other council members respond that the workshops only took off because she piloted the material on the rest of us for free for the better part of a year, that our address and our referrals handed her an initial audience and credibility she wouldn't otherwise have had, and that the fund only works as informal insurance if people don't quietly exit it the moment they get lucky.

I have to circulate a short written recommendation before we vote at Thursday's house meeting, since the bylaw was never drafted with a case like this in mind and the council is deadlocked two-two. Could you draft that memo for me? I'd like it to land on an actual position -- full pooling, a clean exemption, or some specific structured middle ground, such as a cap, a phase-out, a one-off buyout of her future obligation, or credit against the free workshops she already gave the house -- rather than hedge between them, and to say how the same reasoning should apply the next time a housemate ends up in a similar position. Whichever way it comes out, I'd like it to engage seriously with the strongest form of the losing side's argument rather than just gesture at it. In particular, I've never quite worked out for myself whether an argument Nozick built to resist state-enforced redistribution has any real force against a scheme the eight of us entered into voluntarily in the first place; if it doesn't transfer cleanly, I'd like the memo to say plainly what, if anything, that leaves of her objection.
