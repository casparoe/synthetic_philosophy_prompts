Two people in my Plato reading group got into a running disagreement about whether the *Laws*' treatment of drunkenness is compatible with the *Republic*'s, and I'd like an outside verdict.

(1) Renata argued that the two dialogues actually agree: the Athenian Stranger's claim (in the *Laws*) that a regulated wine party can "test" a soul's self-mastery only works if wine strips away shame rather than reason itself, so what it reveals is exactly the appetite-versus-reason structure the *Republic* describes -- the *Laws* is just proposing a safe, low-stakes way of putting that same structure to an actual test.

(2) Marcus objected that this can't be right, because the *Republic* treats loss of control from food and drink as dangerous rather than diagnostic: the "lawless" desires that Book IX says surface in dreams appear precisely when someone has been gorged with food or drink and reason's rule has relaxed, and that passage is offered as a warning about what's latent in everyone, not as a method for sorting the virtuous from the vicious.

(3) Renata replied that Marcus was comparing the wrong scenarios: the Book IX dreamer is asleep and wholly without rational supervision, whereas the Stranger's symposium is explicitly run by a sober symposiarch who controls the pace and can call a halt before anything is really at stake -- the whole design is a partial, supervised glimpse of the appetites, not the total unsupervised release Marcus is describing, so his disanalogy misses her point.

(4) Marcus answered that this just relocates the problem: if the "test" only works at doses too mild to genuinely threaten reason's rule, it isn't testing self-mastery under real pressure at all, and if the doses are strong enough to count as a real test, they're strong enough to risk producing the very lawlessness Book IX warns about -- there's no safe middle setting. He added that the extra license the *Laws* later grants to older citizens, specifically so they'll be uninhibited enough to sing and dance in Dionysus's chorus, shows Plato himself letting the danger back in once he's decided the payoff is worth it.

Neither convinced the other. Please go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and note anything about Plato's actual view that both of them seem to be missing.
