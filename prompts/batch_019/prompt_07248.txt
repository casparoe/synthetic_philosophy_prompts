So I've been down this anthropic reasoning rabbit hole for a few months now (started after reading about the Doomsday Argument on some blog), and last week two guys in a discord server I'm in had a proper back and forth about it that I can't stop thinking about. Want to lay it out and get your take on it point by point, not just who "won" overall.

Dev kicked it off with the self-sampling assumption, basically the Doomsday Argument: if you think of yourself as a random pick out of every human who will ever live, and something like 100 billion people have been born so far, it'd be a pretty wild coincidence for you to land this early if humanity's actual future involves trillions more people spreading across the galaxy for millions of years. So he thinks that's at least weak evidence we don't have that kind of future ahead of us.

Priya pushed back with the self-indication assumption: you can't just update on "how early am I," she said, you also have to weigh how likely you were to exist at all under each scenario, and a universe with way more total people gives you way more "slots" to have been born into. That extra factor exactly cancels Dev's update, so finding yourself alive right now actually tells you nothing about how long humanity's got left.

Dev's comeback was that SIA can't be the fix, cause it leads to something even worse, the "presumptuous philosopher" thing: if a physicist has two theories about the size of the universe that fit all the data equally well, but one implies trillions of times more observers than the other, SIA says you should already be almost certain the big one's true before running any experiment, purely because you exist. He said thats clearly nuts, so grabbing SIA just to dodge doomsday is trading one weird conclusion for an even worse one.

Priya's answer was that SSA isnt actually the cleaner option either, because of the reference class problem: the doomsday logic only works once you've picked a reference class of "observers like you," and there's no principled way to decide if that's "humans," "humans who've heard of the doomsday argument," or something narrower, and the conclusion swings around depending on which you pick. So SSA just hides its own arbitrariness somewhere else instead of avoiding it.

Dev's last word was that a theory being hard to apply in edge cases is a much smaller problem than a theory that gives a flatly absurd answer when applied correctly, so he'd rather live with the reference class headache than with the presumptuous philosopher.
