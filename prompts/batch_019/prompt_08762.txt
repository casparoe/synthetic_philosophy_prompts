A friend of mine and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about just how anti-imperialist Adam Smith really was, after she described him in passing as one of the Enlightenment's earliest and most systematic critics of European empire. I objected that most of Book IV of The Wealth of Nations -- including the long chapter "Of Colonies" -- is really an attack on the mercantile system and on the monopoly privileges granted to chartered trading companies, not on colonization or conquest as such, and that Smith's own proposal, floated in the middle of the American crisis, for giving the colonies proportional representation in Parliament looks a lot more like a plan to reform and preserve a liberal, free-trading empire than like a case against empire in general. She pushed back with his insistence that the costs of governing and defending colonies almost always exceed what they return to the nation as a whole, and with his pointed remarks on the injustice and violence European conquest visited on native peoples in the Americas and in Bengal, arguing that these add up to something closer to a systematic indictment than a narrow trade complaint. We kept trading examples -- the East India Company's misrule in Bengal, the comparative flourishing of the North American settler colonies, his description of Britain's whole colonial project as more a costly dream than a paying concern -- without ever quite settling whose Smith was the real one.

Could you write a dialogue between two Smith scholars who each defend one side of this disagreement: one who reads Smith's economic and moral arguments in Book IV as together amounting to a genuine, principled anti-imperialism, and one who reads him as a critic only of a specific, badly managed form of empire who remained open to a reformed imperial arrangement built on free trade and colonial representation. I'd like both characters to be equally sharp -- each should respond to the other's strongest point rather than simply restating their own view -- and I'd rather the exchange end with the disagreement clarified than artificially resolved.
