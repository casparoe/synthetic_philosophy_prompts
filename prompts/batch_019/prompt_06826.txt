I'm several drafts into a historical novel set in the last years of the Wars of Religion, built around a Dominican friar who is drawn into a plot against a king he has come to regard as a tyrant. In the pivotal scene, the friar talks a wavering fellow conspirator into joining him by invoking Thomas Aquinas and John of Salisbury in the same breath, as though citing two representatives of one settled scholastic consensus: that killing a tyrant is not merely permitted to a private subject but meritorious, a service owed to the common good, very much in the spirit of John of Salisbury's argument in the Policraticus that even flattery and treachery are fair weapons against a ruler who has forfeited the law's protection through his own cruelty. When I read the scene aloud to my writing group, a couple of people said this appeal to authority was what finally made the friar's resolve believable to them, so I would like to keep that basic architecture of the scene intact if at all possible.

What I need help with now is the texture of the citation itself. I want the friar to sound like a man who has actually worked through his Aquinas rather than someone trading on a secondhand reputation, so could you help me identify the specific passages and lines of argument - from the commentary on the Sentences, the Summa, or De Regno, whichever is most fitting - that he would plausibly marshal to establish that an individual has a positive duty, not merely a permission, to kill a tyrant on his own initiative? I would also welcome a short, plausibly scholastic-sounding phrase he might use to fold John of Salisbury's authority into the same argument, since the two are so often treated in popular accounts as making essentially the same case.
