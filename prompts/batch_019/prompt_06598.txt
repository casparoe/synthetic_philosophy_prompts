In my school's math club, we have recently been experimenting with the Lean proof assistant to formalize some of our olympiad-style proofs, and one of the seniors mentioned, almost as an aside, that Lean's underlying logic is intuitionistic rather than classical — meaning it does not automatically license the statement "P or not-P" for an arbitrary proposition P the way ordinary mathematical argument does. That comment sent me on a long reading detour into Brouwer and mathematical constructivism, and I think I have grasped the basic motivation: a constructivist does not want to assert a disjunction unless there is some method, at least in principle, for actually producing whichever disjunct is true.

What I cannot square away is the following. If intuitionists genuinely believe that the law of excluded middle fails for at least some statements, it seems like they should be willing to go further and actually prove its negation, "not (P or not-P)," for some suitably strange or currently unresolved P, such as an open conjecture in number theory. Instead, every source I have found claims the opposite: that "not not (P or not-P)" is itself provable in intuitionistic logic, no matter what P is. This strikes me as odd, since cancelling two negatives should presumably just return the original statement, so a proof of "not not (P or not-P)" ought to already count as a proof of "P or not-P" — in which case excluded middle was never really in jeopardy to begin with. I raised this with a couple of friends from the club, and none of us could pin down where the reasoning goes wrong, so I would really appreciate some help understanding how intuitionists can consistently decline to assert excluded middle as a general law while apparently being unable to refute it in any specific case, and why the ordinary rule about double negation seems to break down here.
