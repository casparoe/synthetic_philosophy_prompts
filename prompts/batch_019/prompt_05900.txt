After a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I've spent my retirement sitting in on metaphysics seminars, and I've been asked to lead the Q&A after a colloquium talk. The speaker will argue that Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment doesn't refute the identity of indiscernibles, since the spheres are 'weakly discernible' in the sense Quine made precise: each stands in an irreflexive relation (being some fixed distance from the other) to its counterpart, even though no monadic property and no order-sensitive relation distinguishes them. Please draft nine or ten questions that push past a mere restatement of this position: I want at least one question on whether letting 'weak' relations do the discerning work trivializes the principle, since almost any two distinct things stand in some irreflexive relation to each other; one that asks her to extend the account to genuinely indistinguishable quantum particles, since the case for fermions seems to trade on the Pauli exclusion principle, and I'd like to know whether the same resource is available for bosons occupying a single shared state, where no such exclusion applies; and one on Ian Hacking's rival strategy of denying there are really two objects at all, by redescribing the scenario as a single sphere reappearing within an unusual spatial topology. Add a short follow-up to each of these three, keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, and close the sequence with a question about what would actually count as evidence against her view.
