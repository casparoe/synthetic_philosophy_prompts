I'm working on a longer feature about philosophers who, once in their careers, set aside their signature technical work to weigh in directly on a live policy dispute, and one case I keep returning to is Michael Dummett. He's remembered almost entirely for the anti-realism debates, intuitionistic logic, and his Frege scholarship, and separately for some quite technical work on voting procedures — so it's a little jarring that in 2001 he also published a short, plainly argued book called "On Immigration and Refugees" (one of Routledge's "Thinking in Action" volumes for general readers) that reads more like a moralist's brief than anything in the realism/anti-realism corpus. I read it years ago for a review that never got commissioned, and I don't have a copy to hand while drafting this section, so what follows is reconstructed from memory — which is exactly why I'd like a second pair of eyes on it before it goes near an editor.

Here is the paragraph as it currently stands: "Dummett's case has two parts that don't obviously sit well together. The first is corrosive: he argues that almost every argument actually offered for restricting immigration into a country like Britain turns out, on inspection, to be either question-begging or a rationalization for keeping the population's ethnic composition roughly as it is, and that refugees specifically have a claim — grounded in the 1951 Refugee Convention and in ordinary morality — that a receiving state has very little standing to override. The second part is a concession: a state can, in principle, legitimately restrict immigration if admitting more people would genuinely outrun its capacity to sustain them, provided the restriction is reached impartially rather than reverse-engineered from a wish to exclude particular groups. The trouble is that the concession seems to quietly undo the polemic. 'Capacity' is elastic and politically contestable enough that it hands every restrictionist government a respectable, resource-based vocabulary in which to redescribe exactly the motives Dummett spends the first half of the book unmasking — so the impartiality test meant to keep the two parts apart may not be doing much work at all."

I'd like your comments on three things in particular. First, accuracy: does this look like a fair reconstruction of Dummett's actual argument, or have I flattened or misremembered something important — and, separately, am I perhaps attributing to Dummett a move that really belongs to the broader liberal literature on immigration (Walzer, Carens, David Miller, and so on) rather than to his book specifically? Second, substance: is the tension I've described a genuine problem for the framework, or is there a natural way to block it that I'm missing? Third, framing: is this even the most interesting fault line in the book, or would the piece be stronger built around a different one? I'd rather find the problems now than after it's in print, so please don't hold back.
