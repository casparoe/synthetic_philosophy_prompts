Here's the question, worth 20 points:

"Parfit uses the Branch-Line Case to argue that personal identity is not what matters in survival. In your answer, explain: (a) how the Branch-Line Case differs from the ordinary Teletransporter Case; (b) why Parfit thinks the person on Earth is not identical to the Replica on Mars; (c) what Relation R is; and (d) what practical or emotional conclusion Parfit draws from all this about how we should regard our own deaths."

And here's the answer I'm stuck on:

"In the ordinary Teletransporter Case, the Scanner destroys your body on Earth at the exact moment the Replicator builds a new body out of new matter on Mars, so there is only ever one person alive at a time who has your psychology. The Branch-Line Case changes this: instead of destroying you, the Scanner merely damages your heart, so you go on existing on Earth, badly hurt, for a few more days, while the Replica is already alive and well on Mars. For those few days there are two people around, both fully psychologically continuous with the person who originally stepped into the Scanner.

Parfit thinks the Earth-person is not identical to the Replica, and his reasoning is basically a closest-continuer argument: identity should go to whichever candidate is the uniquely best continuation of the original, and here the Replica and the dying Earth-person turn out to be equally good continuations of the person who stepped into the Scanner, so neither one has a stronger claim than the other. Since there's no non-arbitrary way to award identity to one over the other, Parfit concludes that the identity facts in the Branch-Line Case are indeterminate -- there's simply no fact of the matter about whether the original survives.

What Parfit thinks actually matters is Relation R: psychological connectedness (direct links like particular memories or persisting intentions) and/or psychological continuity (overlapping chains of connectedness), together with the right kind of cause. Because the Replica is fully R-related to the Earth-person, Parfit thinks the Earth-person can regard his own approaching death with nearly the same equanimity he'd feel about ordinary survival, since everything that actually matters to him -- his memories, his projects, his character -- carries on regardless of what we say about identity. This is supposed to be consoling: once you accept the Reductionist view, losing your identity is not something to fear, so long as Relation R is preserved. Parfit does stop short of saying we therefore have no more reason to care about our own future than about anyone else's -- he explicitly rejects that stronger claim -- but he does think the boundary between surviving and not surviving matters much less than we ordinarily assume."
