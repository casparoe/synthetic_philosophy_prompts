My mother, a theoretical physicist, died five weeks ago, and the small annual workshop on the foundations of quantum mechanics that she helped run for nearly twenty-five years is going ahead as scheduled next month, opening with a short tribute in place of the usual welcome address. The organizers — her friends and her sparring partners, in roughly equal measure — have asked me to deliver it, on the theory that a novelist in the family must obviously know how to write something like this, which is generous of them but not quite true: I've written any number of deathbed scenes, but always for characters I was still free to revise.

She spent her career an unapologetic Everettian, and was, for as long as I can remember, engaged in a running, entirely affectionate argument with two people who will be sitting in the front row: a Copenhagenist who thought she'd rather invent a fresh universe every few seconds than admit that measurement might just be special, and a Bohmian who thought both of them were too squeamish to say plainly that the particle has to be somewhere the whole time. I'd like one line in the tribute that rewards the three of them for having conducted this argument, with real stakes and no malice, at more or less every family dinner I can remember.

Here is the actual problem. I know exactly what the room wants to hear, because at least half of them have read the same one popular book: that somewhere, in some branch, she pulls through. I don't want to say that — not only because it's a poor description of what her own theory implies about the branch we're actually sitting in, but because correcting people out of exactly that comfort was something she did, patiently and often, in the public-facing part of her career. At the same time, I don't want to spend five minutes at my mother's memorial being right about physics at the expense of being kind to my aunt, who will also be sitting there and has no opinion whatsoever on the preferred-basis problem. Could you write the tribute for me? Something that could actually be read aloud in about five minutes, that finds whatever real comfort the many-worlds picture, honestly held, has to offer — rather than the comfort it is popularly mistaken for.
