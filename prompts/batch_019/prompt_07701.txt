A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about something that started with her asking why I still bother picking individual stocks instead of just buying an index fund, and ended up turning on the no-trade theorem. Her view was that the theorem, together with Aumann's result that rational agents sharing a common prior cannot agree to disagree, shows that the heavy daily trading volume on real exchanges cannot be rationalized as informed agents trading on private information: if everyone truly shared a prior and had common knowledge of everyone else's rationality, a counterparty's mere willingness to trade should immediately unwind any perceived edge, so persistent trading must reflect irrationality or diverging priors rather than genuine information. I countered that the theorem's idealizations — a common prior, common knowledge of rationality, an already Pareto-efficient starting allocation, no transaction costs — are doing so much of the work that the result tells us little about actual markets, and that Grossman and Stiglitz's argument about the impossibility of fully efficient prices suggests some of that "irrational-looking" trading has to survive for prices to carry any information at all. Neither of us gave ground.

Write a dialogue of roughly 500–700 words between two equally sharp interlocutors defending these two positions: one takes the no-trade/Aumann result at face value as a genuine rationality challenge to ordinary trading behavior, the other defends heavy trading volume as compatible with markets remaining decent, if imperfect, aggregators of information. Have them respond to the actual move the other just made rather than restate their own position, get concrete about whether disagreement over priors itself counts as a form of irrationality, and end with one side granting a narrow, specific concession rather than the whole argument.
