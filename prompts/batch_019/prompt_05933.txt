Each spring I teach a graduate proseminar on nineteenth-century American political and constitutional thought, and this year the term will close with a "living history" exercise: one of my doctoral students, who has spent the better part of two years immersed in the abolitionist press, has agreed to be interviewed on camera in character as Frederick Douglass -- specifically, Douglass as he presented himself in and shortly after his 1860 Glasgow lecture on whether the Constitution is a pro-slavery or an antislavery instrument. I will be conducting the interview myself, and I want to avoid the obvious failure mode of letting him simply re-narrate the familiar biographical arc from Garrisonian disunionist to constitutional abolitionist under the influence of Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner, since the seminar has already read enough Douglass and Garrison to have that story cold.

What I need instead are eight to ten questions that put real pressure on the actual argument at stake, namely that because the Constitution's text never uses the words "slave" or "slavery," and because its Preamble commits the document to justice and liberty, ordinary canons of legal interpretation require reading it as antislavery whatever individual framers privately intended or federal marshals and courts actually did with it in practice. I want at least one question pressing him on why textual silence should be read in favor of liberty rather than as the kind of face-saving circumlocution -- "such Persons," "held to Service or Labour" -- that everyone at the Philadelphia Convention understood perfectly well; one that asks him to reconcile this hermeneutic with what we know of the framers' actual bargaining over apportionment and the slave trade; one that puts Garrison's charge that the document is "a covenant with death" to him directly and asks exactly where he thinks that inference goes wrong; and one that poses this hypothetical: if the slave states were one day defeated and the nation then adopted a formal amendment abolishing slavery outright, would that show his reading of the unamended text had been mistaken all along, or merely that the amendment made explicit what sound interpretation already implied? For each question, please add a brief follow-up tuned to whichever response he is most likely to give, so the conversation doesn't simply stall once he supplies the answer any careful reader of the 1860 speech would anticipate.
