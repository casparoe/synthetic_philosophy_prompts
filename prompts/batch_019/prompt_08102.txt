In my philosophy elective, we just spent a week on Augustine's writings on lying, and it turned into the most contentious discussion we've had all year. We read excerpts from Contra Mendacium, the treatise he addressed to a man named Consentius, who had proposed having someone pose as a convert to infiltrate the Priscillianist heretics and expose their secret teachings from the inside. Augustine's answer was an unqualified no: even a lie told to defend the Church against heresy is still a sin, and he goes on to rank lies from most to least sinful without ever declaring even the mildest ones blameless. What struck several of us in class was our teacher's point that, for Augustine, what actually makes an utterance a lie has less to do with whether the other person ends up deceived or harmed than with a kind of inner contradiction between what you believe and what you assert — a disorder located in the liar's own soul. That is apparently why even a tactful, well-meant lie that harms no one still counts as a sin for him, just a lesser one.

That got me wondering about a case Augustine obviously never had to consider: what would he say about lying to an AI chatbot? I don't mean anything dramatic, just ordinary things like inventing a fake identity or backstory in a prompt to get a language model to produce something it's designed to refuse, or feeding a customer-service bot a false story to get around its policy. Since his whole theory of why lying is wrong seems to locate the sin in the speaker's own heart-and-tongue mismatch rather than in whether the listener is actually deceived, it isn't obvious to me whether deceiving a machine would count as the full offense, a lesser one, or nothing at all for him — especially since he might just as easily say a chatbot has no soul to be deceived in, so there would be nothing there to sin against in the first place. Could you reconstruct how Augustine himself, reasoning from his own stated grounds for condemning even beneficial lies like the one he refused to permit against the Priscillianists, would most plausibly come out on this? I'd like a specific verdict defended on his terms: would he treat deceiving an AI system as a genuine lie, a lesser offense, or no moral offense at all?
