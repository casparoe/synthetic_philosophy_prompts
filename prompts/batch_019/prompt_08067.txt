I spent about thirty-five years as an experimental physicist (condensed matter, mostly) before retiring, and since then I've been trying to fill in all the philosophy I never had time for. Lately that's meant decision theory: I did the standard tour through Newcomb's problem, one-boxing vs two-boxing, and that lead me into this "updateless decision theory" business that seems to come out of the AI-alignment/LessWrong crowd (Wei Dai, Yudkowsky, and that lineage). My grandson Callum, who's doing a PhD in machine learning, is the one who got me started on it, and when he brought his flatmate Deniz round for dinner last week the two of them ended up in a proper argument over dessert about one of the standard thought experiments, "counterfactual mugging," that I still haven't been able to shake.

For anyone who doesn't know it (I only just learned it myself, so tell me if I've mangled a detail): Omega, a predictor who's never once been wrong, flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega walks up and asks you for $100, no explanation, and there's nothing in it for you that you can see. If it lands heads, Omega would instead have handed you $10,000 -- but only if Omega had already predicted that you'd be the sort of person who'd hand over the $100 in the tails-case. Say the coin actually lands tails, and Omega's standing in front of you asking for your hundred bucks. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Callum said refusing is obviously correct. The coin already landed tails, there's no possible world left in which paying gets you the $10,000, so handing over $100 for nothing in return is just a straightforward loss. Being rational means doing the best you can from where you actually are, not from where you might have been.

(2) Deniz said that reasoning is exactly what guarantees you walk away with nothing. Omega is a perfect predictor, so if you're the type who'd back out once tails actually comes up, Omega would've known that in advance and never paid out on heads either. Refusing doesn't get you the best of both worlds, it just makes you the kind of agent who nets zero every time, while the type who pays nets $4,950 on average.

(3) Callum said fine, that's a good argument for becoming the kind of person who'd pay, before the coin gets flipped -- take an oath, sign something, whatever. But he never did anything like that, and he's not bound by some policy that only exists in Deniz's head. It can't be rational to make someone honor a commitment they never actually made just because making it in advance would've been smart.

(4) Deniz said Omega isn't predicting a signature on a piece of paper, its predicting what Callum would actually do in the moment, full stop -- and if Callum is disposed to wriggle out of it once tails shows up, that disposition is exactly what a perfect predictor would already have detected, contract or no contract. "Being the kind of person who pays" and "having precommitted" aren't different things from Omega's point of view, so Callum doesn't get to claim credit for one without the other.

(5) Callum said that's a sleight of hand -- Deniz is telling him to act against his own interest in the only world that's actually real, for the sake of some other version of him in a coin flip that didn't happen, and "the kind of agent who wins on average" describes a whole population of possible Callums, not a reason for the one actual, sitting-right-here Callum to hand over real money for nothing.

Neither of them budged, and I went back and forth all evening without landing anywhere. Could you go through this turn by turn, 1 through 5, and say who's got the better of it at each individual step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know if you think there's a consideration neither of them raised that actually matters here. And if you had to give a one-line final verdict -- Callum, Deniz, or a genuine draw -- what would it be?
