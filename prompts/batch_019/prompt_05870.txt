My father is retiring this spring after forty-one years at the same stamping plant, and I've been asked to say a few words at his retirement dinner. I have no background in economics or philosophy, but I've spent years reading Keynes on my own, and the piece I keep coming back to is his 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," where he guessed that within about a hundred years — which lands almost exactly now — the economic problem would be solved, and worried that ordinary people, bred over generations to work rather than enjoy, wouldn't know what to do with leisure once it arrived. My father has never heard of Keynes, so the toast can't turn into a lecture over his head; but my brother-in-law, who teaches economics at the community college, will be at the table and will notice if I flatten the idea into a greeting-card line. Please write the toast — about three minutes read aloud — so that it takes Keynes's worry about leisure seriously enough to matter, and still ends up as a warm, honest tribute to my father rather than a seminar.
