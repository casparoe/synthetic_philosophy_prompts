I've long followed how Zahavi's handicap principle gets invoked across the rationalist blogosphere — Robin Hanson's Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, Kevin Simler and Hanson's The Elephant in the Brain — as the biological warrant for treating status display, philanthropy, and ritual as costly signals. Next month I'm interviewing an evolutionary biologist who defends a strong, close-to-original Zahavian version of the principle and also popularizes it for human behavior. She tends to fold any counterexample back into the theory by insisting it counts as a handicap once described precisely enough. Draft eight to ten interview questions that don't let her simply restate the informal story. I want at least one question on the gap between Zahavi's verbal proposal and Alan Grafen's game-theoretic vindication of it — specifically the differential-cost condition (marginal signaling cost falling as quality rises) his models require but that Zahavi's own writing never specified; one on how she distinguishes a genuine handicap from a mere index signal, like a stag's roar, whose pitch is mechanically tied to body size without being costly at all; and one on what the Sir Philip Sidney game's equilibria, where most signalers pay little or nothing along the realized path of play, do to the popular image of handicaps as visibly wasteful ornaments. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the deflection she's most likely to reach for, and close with a question asking what observation would actually change her mind.
