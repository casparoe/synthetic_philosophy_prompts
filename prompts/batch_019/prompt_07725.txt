This term I am taking a graduate seminar on classical Islamic philosophy, and this week's readings paired the Seventeenth Discussion of Al-Ghazālī's Tahafut al-Falasifa with Ibn Rushd's rebuttal in the Tahafut al-Tahafut. I can follow the basic shape of Ghazālī's argument: that there is no demonstrable necessary connection between what we call a cause, such as fire touching cotton, and its supposed effect, the cotton's burning, and that the true agent behind the burning is God, who acts according to a fixed custom ('āda) rather than endowing fire itself with any causal power. What I am less sure of is how much weight this deserves as metaphysics, as opposed to theologically motivated maneuvering meant to protect the possibility of miracles. My professor also mentioned, almost in passing, that some commentators treat Ghazālī as an unacknowledged forerunner of Hume's account of causation, while others think that comparison flattens a crucial difference between the two positions -- and I have not managed to work out on my own which side has the better of it.

Could you write an essay that, first, reconstructs Ghazālī's argument against the philosophers' (chiefly Avicenna's) claim that causes necessitate their effects, including his appeal to habitual custom, his claim that genuine agency requires will and choice (which an inanimate thing like fire lacks), and his use of counterfactual scenarios such as a fire that God simply declines to let burn; second, reconstructs Ibn Rushd's central objections to this position, especially the worry that stripping things of real natures and causal powers would make scientific knowledge, and perhaps even the recognition of miracles as miracles, impossible; and third, takes a stand on whether Ghazālī's occasionalism is rightly read as anticipating Hume's regularity theory of causation, or whether that comparison obscures a fundamental difference in the kind of necessity each thinker denies. Please do not simply lay out the considerations on both sides and leave the matter open -- tell me where you come down, and defend it, on both questions: whether occasionalism survives Ibn Rushd's rebuttal, and whether the comparison to Hume is apt or misleading.
