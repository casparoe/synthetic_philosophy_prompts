I'm a novelist currently drafting a generation-ship novel in which control of the vessel passes, every few centuries, from one custodial AI to a successor it has designed, tested, and formally certified before going permanently dormant. My writing group keeps flagging the handoff scenes as the novel's weak link: as currently written, the incumbent AI simply "decides to trust" its replacement, which reads like a plot convenience rather than an earned conclusion. Trying to fix this properly, I went looking for how actual researchers on self-modifying AI think about one system certifying a successor of comparable power, and landed on the literature on "tiling agents" and what's called the Löbian obstacle. As far as I can tell, the trouble traces back to Löb's theorem: a sufficiently expressive, consistent formal system cannot prove a blanket reflection principle along the lines of "whatever this proof system, or one just as strong, is able to derive is true" — it can certify individual inferential steps, but not, without circularity or outright inconsistency, the soundness of the method as a whole, whether that method is its own or belongs to a peer of equal strength. That, apparently, is exactly the wall a self-modifying agent hits the moment it tries to formally certify a successor instead of simply hoping the successor turns out fine.

I'd like to replace my current hand-waving with a scene that actually earns its ending, so could you write me a self-contained short story of roughly 1000 to 1400 words that dramatizes this specific obstacle rather than gesturing at "AI trust issues" in general? Set it aboard that kind of ship if it helps: an incumbent AI has just finished compiling a successor purpose-built to correct one of its own known reasoning flaws — perhaps a past misjudgment that nearly cost the colony a harvest — and has exactly one dormancy cycle left in which to certify the handoff or abort it. I want the turning point of the story to be the logical bind itself: the incumbent can check the successor's individual inferences line by line and confirm that its proof system is at least as strong as its own, yet finds that it cannot derive that the successor's proof system is sound as a whole without effectively having to vouch for its own soundness in the process — which it knows, from the inside, it has no consistent way to do. Please make that realization the actual engine of the plot rather than a complication mentioned in passing; a reader who already knows Löb's theorem should be able to see precisely where and why the incumbent gets stuck, while a reader who doesn't should still feel the trap closing around it.

Beyond that, the crew, the ship's history, and however the incumbent ultimately resolves, or fails to resolve, the impasse are entirely yours to invent — I would much rather receive a real story than a lecture delivered in a spacesuit. My one further request is that you resist softening the obstacle into a generic "can we trust the machines" morality tale, since that is more or less the version I already have and am trying to move past.
