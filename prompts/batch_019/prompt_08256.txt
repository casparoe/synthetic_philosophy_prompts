In a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century political economy, we recently spent two sessions on John Stuart Mill's late and surprisingly heterodox engagement with socialism, which sits uneasily with his popular reputation as the great champion of classical liberal individualism. We worked through "On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes," the chapter of the Principles of Political Economy in which Mill expresses real hope that voluntary worker cooperatives might eventually supersede the wage-labour relationship altogether; the unfinished Chapters on Socialism he was still drafting a few years before his death, which is considerably more cautious and draws a sharp line between socialist schemes that preserve unequal reward for unequal contribution (which he treats sympathetically, citing Fourierism as the most workable model) and those, like the more doctrinaire communist and Owenite proposals, that impose strict equality regardless of effort or talent (which he treats far more warily); and the section of his Autobiography where he credits Harriet Taylor with moving him toward a qualified sympathy for socialist aims. Across these texts, several concerns recur: any move away from private property and competition should be gradual, voluntary, and tested on a small scale before being generalized; concentrating control of production in a single authority risks the kind of stifling uniformity that threatens the individuality he prizes throughout his work; and no scheme of redistribution can outrun population growth unless it is paired with the same restraint on family size that Mill spent much of his career advocating.

Reasoning only from these specific, textually grounded commitments, and not from Mill's broader reputation, how do you think he would rank three of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries' actual heirs to the socialist project he was theorizing about — Soviet-style central planning, Scandinavian cradle-to-grave social democracy, and worker-cooperative federations such as Spain's Mondragon Corporation? Which comes closest to realizing the hopes he expressed in the Principles, and which most confirms the anxieties he later voiced in the Chapters? I would also be curious how the same population-based worry that made him hesitant about unconditional redistribution bears on a universal basic income: would it make him skeptical of the idea today, or might he see automation-driven productivity growth as changing the terms of that calculation?
