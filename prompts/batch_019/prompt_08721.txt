Three weeks ago, a comment I made during a graduate seminar was screenshotted and posted—without my name attached, but with enough identifying detail that everyone on my floor knew exactly who it meant—to the anonymous, geofenced campus app that has become the main forum for undergraduate opinion here (whichever one is current by the time you read this; Yik Yak, Fizz, and their imitators seem to cycle every few years). The thread ran for four days and drew several hundred replies, nearly all of them jokes at my expense, none of them signed. When I brought this up at a meeting of the Faculty Senate's ad hoc Committee on Anonymous Platforms, which I sit on as an at-large representative, a colleague from Comparative Literature pulled out her copy of Kierkegaard's Two Ages—specifically the closing section usually excerpted in English as "The Present Age"—and read aloud his account of "leveling" and of "the public" as a kind of phantom conjured into being by the press. She then told me, in more detail than I'd known, how the satirical paper The Corsair (Corsaren) spent a long stretch of 1846 turning him into a laughingstock on the streets of Copenhagen, after he more or less dared it to attack him. It was an unnervingly precise description of my month.

The Provost's office has asked the committee to produce an actual recommendation by the time we reconvene next Wednesday: whether the university should petition IT to block the app's traffic on the campus network (which a number of universities tried with Yik Yak roughly a decade ago, generally without eliminating the underlying behavior); leave things as they are on free-expression grounds; or pursue a middle path, such as setting up a formal, expedited channel between the university and the app's operators for removing posts that target identifiable individuals, short of any blanket block. The committee is split down the middle, and the strongest objection from the anti-block faction is one I find genuinely hard to answer: the same anonymity that let a few hundred strangers mock my voice is also, on their account, what has let first-generation students post honestly about exploitative advisors, and let at least one student describe an assault without attaching her name to it.

Could you draft the memo I'll bring to that meeting? I'd like it to land on one concrete recommendation among those three options (or a clearly specified alternative), with reasoning the anti-block faction could actually engage with rather than dismiss, and I'd like Kierkegaard's specific diagnosis of the press and the public—not a generic "social media is toxic" gloss—to do real analytical work in it, including an honest reckoning with whatever in his account doesn't survive the trip from a nineteenth-century broadsheet aimed at a single philosopher to an app aimed at no one in particular.
