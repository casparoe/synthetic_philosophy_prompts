I've been on a pretty big Borges kick this year (third read-through of Labyrinths) and I keep noticing how obsessed the guy was with the idea that time isn't "real" in some deep sense — theres the refutation of time essay where he leans on Berkeley and Hume to argue that if there's no soul and no external matter, then there's no succession either, theres the circular ruins with the man who dreams another man into existence and then realizes he himself is being dreamed too, the garden of forking paths, and even an older essay of his about the "doctrine of cycles" where he goes after Nietzsche's eternal return. Anyway, a friend of mine who's way more into physics than I am lent me Julian Barbour's book The End of Time, and it kind of blew my mind, because Barbour is an actual working physicist arguing dead seriously that time doesn't exist at the fundamental level of reality — there's just this enormous space containing every possible instantaneous arrangement of the universe (he calls the whole space "Platonia" and each arrangement a "Now"), and some of those Nows just happen to contain records and memories arranged in a way that tricks us into feeling like time passed, even though nothing ever actually moved from one Now to the next.

It felt so close to what Borges was doing that I had to ask somebody who actually knows this stuff. Obviously Borges died in '86 and Barbour's book came out in '99, so he never got to hear about Platonia. But based on everything he actually wrote, do you think he would've taken Barbour's theory as basically a scientific vindication of his own "refutation," or would he have found something importantly different going on — like, Borges gets rid of time by getting rid of the self and the external world, which is a very Berkeley/Hume idealist move, whereas Barbour keeps a very real, mind-independent Platonia sitting there timelessly, no idealism required. Would a difference like that have mattered to him, or would he just have grabbed the physics as one more piece of evidence for the kind of thing he'd been saying all along? Also, is there some other essay or story of his, besides the ones I mentioned, that you think would actually be the most relevant one to bring into this? Sorry if this is a bit rambly, I never studied any of this formally, I just read a lot on my own and think about it on long walks.
