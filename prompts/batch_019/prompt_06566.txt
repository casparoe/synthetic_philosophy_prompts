Two friends of mine, Maya and Owen, belong to the same knitting group, and after last week's meetup they ended up in a surprisingly involved disagreement that I haven't been able to stop turning over in my head. Maya had spent a long weekend knitting an elaborate cabled sweater for her cat out of a single skein of expensive hand-dyed merino yarn. The cat wanted nothing to do with it, so a month later she unraveled the whole thing and used the same yarn to knit herself a scarf instead. When she told the story, Owen teased her for "destroying" such a nice piece of work, and Maya said that was absurd, since not one inch of yarn had actually been destroyed -- it was all still right there in the scarf. From that offhand remark, the conversation escalated into something neither of them could quite let go of:

(1) Maya argued that the sweater was never anything over and above the yarn: "sweater" is just a convenient way of describing the yarn while it happened to be arranged sweater-wise, the same way "scarf" describes it now. Nothing new came into being when she finished knitting, and nothing was destroyed when she unraveled it, because the very same stuff persisted the whole way through.

(2) Owen disagreed. The skein of yarn, he said, could have become any number of different sweaters, or a blanket, or nothing at all, and would still count as "the same yarn" throughout; but that particular sweater, once unraveled, is gone for good -- you cannot re-knit that very sweater from the recovered yarn, only a new one, even if it came out identical stitch for stitch. Since the yarn can survive things the sweater could not possibly survive, he argued, the two cannot be one and the same thing; there must have been two coincident objects the whole time, the yarn and the sweater, sharing all the same matter.

(3) Maya replied that this only shows they were using two different kinds of words for one and the same portion of matter -- "yarn" tracks the stuff, "sweater" tracks a shape or function it temporarily has -- not that some second physical object was somehow stacked on top of the first, taking up its own additional space. She compared it to a wall and the bricks that make it up: nobody thinks a wall is some further thing piled on top of the bricks, even though the wall could not survive being scattered across the yard while the bricks could.

(4) Owen pushed back that the comparison doesn't hold, because the sweater and the yarn did not merely differ in what they could survive -- they differed in their actual, present properties the whole time the sweater existed. It made sense to say the sweater took her eleven hours to make and had a flattering neckline, but not to say either of those things about the yarn; conversely, it made sense to say the yarn cost forty dollars and was one hundred percent merino, but rather less sense to say that of the sweater. If it were really just one thing wearing two names, he said, every true description of one ought to be a true description of the other, and that plainly is not so.

Neither Maya nor Owen budged, and I went back and forth all evening about who was actually winning. Could you work through the exchange move by move, (1) through (4), and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether, in your assessment, there is a standard philosophical treatment of cases like this one that ends up favoring one side more than the other, and what, if anything, you think both Maya and Owen are overlooking.
