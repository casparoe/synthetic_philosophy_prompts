I'm assembling a cycle of short stories, each built around a single philosophical thought experiment, and for the next one I want to draw on G. E. Moore's discussion of the ideal in Principia Ethica. Moore holds that personal affection and aesthetic appreciation are each, in his terms, an organic unity — a true perception of real excellence combined with the emotion properly due to it, worth more than the sum of its parts — and that the two together are the highest goods we can conceive. Elsewhere in the same book, he asks us to imagine one world of surpassing beauty and another that is hideous, stipulates that no being will ever perceive either, and argues it would still be irrational to deny that the first is better. He never quite asks what happens when the two unities are stacked: when beauty is appreciated together, by friends, rather than alone. Write me a short story, under 1,500 words, about two old friends who turn out to be the last people who will ever see or finish some beautiful thing — once they part, no one will know it existed. Without having either character say so outright, let the story turn on whether doing this together is worth more than the beauty and the friendship would each have been worth apart, and on whether finishing the thing is even worthwhile once being its last witnesses amounts, in practice, to no witnesses at all.
