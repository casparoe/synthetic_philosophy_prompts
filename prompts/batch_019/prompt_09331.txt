I'm a senior backend engineer, and for the last two years I've also sat on my company's Incident Review Board, a five-person group that meets monthly to sign off on whatever corrective actions come out of a Sev-1 or Sev-2 postmortem. Like most engineering orgs our size, we've had a strict "blameless postmortem" culture since before I joined: you fix the gap in the process that let the error through, and you deliberately don't attach consequences to a name, because that's what actually gets people to report and diagnose problems honestly instead of covering for themselves. That culture is now running into an awkward, specific case. One of our senior engineers — I'll call him Marcus — has caused three Sev-1s in eight months: a schema migration pushed around a required review that locked a production table for the better part of an hour, a payments bug that shipped because he had "high confidence" and skipped the canary stage, and, three weeks ago, a flaky-but-actually-correct integration test he disabled to unblock a release, which was masking a real defect and led to a multi-hour outage. The first two postmortems closed as purely systemic, per policy, with no individual follow-up. For the third, leadership is asking the board to recommend an actual performance consequence — realistically, a formal improvement plan — for the first time, and I've been asked to draft the memo the board will vote on Tuesday, with the vote currently split two-two.

The complication is an objection one of Marcus's teammates raised at the review meeting, which I haven't been able to shake since. He argued that singling Marcus out now, after two structurally identical incidents were waved through as "the system's fault," requires us to say what makes Marcus the true author of this decision in a way he wasn't the true author of the last two — and that we can't, because a decision like this always traces back to a temperament, a risk tolerance, and a set of habits Marcus didn't choose, which were themselves produced by earlier decisions made by that same not-yet-chosen temperament, all the way back. I assumed this was an improvised rationalization until I looked into it and found it's a serious, well-known position: Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility, which holds that being truly (not just usefully) responsible for anything would require being the cause of yourself — causa sui — in a way Strawson argues is impossible whether or not the universe turns out to be deterministic. Two board members now want to adopt "no individual consequences, ever" as standing policy on the strength of this; two others think it's just an unusually well-read way of saying "don't blame me," and want to proceed with the plan on the ordinary grounds that Marcus was already warned twice. For what it's worth, someone who minored in philosophy mentioned, mostly as trivia, that Strawson's own father was one of the twentieth century's best-known defenders of the opposite view — which hasn't broken the tie, but has stuck with me.

Could you draft the memo? It should reach a concrete recommendation with a timeframe rather than punting the decision back to the full board, it should engage directly with the argument Marcus's teammate raised rather than answering it with a company-values slogan, and it should state the strongest form of the "this is no different from the last two" position honestly before explaining why the board should, or shouldn't, rule that way.
