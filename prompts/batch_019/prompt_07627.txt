So this is a weird one but here goes. I'm doing a PhD in philosophy (dissertation's on Locke's associationism, so I've spent way too much time this year in "Some Thoughts Concerning Education"), and through a series of bad life decisions I'm now also on the discipline subcommittee of the tiny independent school my daughter attends. The school's mission statement literally cites Locke on habituation and on learning being the least important of the "virtue, wisdom, breeding, and learning" a good education is supposed to aim at, which is cute until it isnt: a parent just filed a formal complaint after her son got "swatted" (paddled, per the consent form she signed at enrollment) for a third demerit, and her letter basically says: you quote this guy in the handbook and then paddle my kid AND bribe the little ones with a treasure box for good behavior -- Locke was against both of those for basically the same reason, so pick a lane. She's not wrong that Locke thought whipping a kid and sweet-talking him with treats are two versions of the same mistake, and that esteem and shame (the good or bad opinion of people the child actually cares about) are supposed to do the real work instead, alongside slow habituation rather than posted rules. But a couple of our longtime teachers think that's naive for six-year-olds who don't have a stable enough sense of reputation for shame/esteem to grip yet, and honestly some of what I've read on shame specifically (as opposed to guilt) in kids makes me nervous that the "Lockean" alternative isn't obviously the safe, humane option it sounds like either.

We meet next Tuesday and I got roped into drafting the actual recommendation memo for the full board, since apparently a PhD on this guy makes me the resident expert now. Can you write that memo for me? It needs to land on one concrete policy -- what happens to the paddle, what happens to the treasure-box/marble-jar system, whether the answer's the same for a 6-year-old and a 12-year-old, and on what timeline/review point -- rather than just listing options and bailing. And it actually has to engage with the teachers' worry about the younger kids and with the shame-vs-guilt worry, instead of just letting Locke's name win the argument by association. A page or so is fine, doesn't need headers or anything fancy, just something I can read out loud on Tuesday without embarrassing myself.
