I'm taking a graduate seminar this term called "Theories of Consciousness," and we've just spent three weeks working through Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (IIT), from the five phenomenological axioms down through the exclusion postulate and the claim that a system's level of consciousness corresponds to the maximally irreducible cause-effect structure it generates over itself, the size of which is measured by Φ. What has stuck with me more than the positive theory, though, is the ongoing fight over whether IIT even counts as science.

In one session we worked through Scott Aaronson's well-known argument that certain simple, highly-connected networks built from expander graphs receive absurdly high Φ values under IIT's own formalism despite obviously lacking anything like a mind, together with Tononi's willingness to "bite the bullet" and accept that such systems really are highly conscious. In another, we covered the "unfolding argument" (associated with Doerig, Schurger, Herzog and colleagues), which contends that because every recurrent network has a feedforward twin that produces identical behavior and verbal reports but a different cause-effect structure, no possible experiment could ever distinguish the two -- so IIT's central claims about which systems are conscious, and to what degree, are untestable even in principle. A couple of classmates think this unfalsifiability worry is also what was really driving the widely reported 2023 open letter, signed by well over a hundred consciousness researchers, that branded IIT pseudoscience.

For my term paper I have to actually take a stand rather than just lay out both sides, and I'd like your help thinking through where the strongest position lies before I write my own draft. Please write an essay addressing the following question: in light of the unfalsifiability-style objections to IIT -- the unfolding argument and Aaronson-style reductios especially -- should IIT be classified as pseudoscience, as a legitimate but currently flawed scientific theory, or as something else entirely (for instance, a valuable explanatory framework that was never meant to be held to strict falsifiability standards)? Engage seriously with IIT's own resources for answering these objections -- such as the role the exclusion postulate plays in picking out a privileged spatiotemporal grain, and the independent empirical track record claimed for integration/differentiation measures like the perturbational complexity index -- rather than only presenting the case against the theory. Weigh the considerations on both sides, but don't end up hedging: defend one specific verdict, and say what would have to turn out to be true for you to abandon it.
