A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about vagueness and parted without resolving anything. It started from the sorites paradox — the heap, the bald man — and quickly became a dispute between Timothy Williamson's epistemicism, which my friend defended, and supervaluationism, which I defended. My friend's position was that predicates like "heap" and "bald" have perfectly sharp cutoffs we simply cannot know, our ignorance explained by something like a margin-for-error principle governing how meaning gets fixed by patterns of use. Mine was that a sentence like "n grains make a heap" is true only if it comes out true on every admissible way of precisifying "heap," so borderline cases are genuine truth-value gaps rather than unknown facts, with penumbral connections keeping the precisifications from being arbitrary. Neither of us landed a decisive blow: my friend kept pressing the higher-order vagueness objection, that supervaluationism's own "definitely" operator seems to need just the kind of sharp boundary it was invented to avoid, while I never got a satisfying answer to how an unknowable sharp cutoff is compatible with meaning being nothing over and above use. Write an essay that reconstructs the strongest form of each objection, assesses what genuine resources epistemicism and supervaluationism have for defusing the objection aimed at it, and defends a definite verdict about which view comes out ahead — or, if you think both ultimately fail, about what should replace them. Don't let it end in a stalemate.
