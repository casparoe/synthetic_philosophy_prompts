I've been reporting a piece on how forecasters and AI systems are supposed to revise their beliefs in light of new evidence, and while preparing for an interview with a formal epistemologist I spent a few evenings working through Bas van Fraassen's 1981 "Judy Benjamin problem," the thought experiment loosely inspired by the film Private Benjamin. A lost army patrol is somewhere in a war-games area divided into Red and Blue territory, each of which is further split into a "Headquarters Company Area" and a "Second Company Area," so that the soldier starts out assigning a flat one-in-four probability to each of the four regions. Over the radio, headquarters tells her, in effect, that they don't know whether she has wandered into Red territory, but that if she has, it is three times more likely that she is in the Red Headquarters Area than in the Red Second Company Area — nothing at all is said about the Blue side. Yet when you update using the "minimum relative information," or cross-entropy, rule that is often presented as the natural extension of Jeffrey's "probability kinematics" to this sort of conditional input, her credence that she is still safely on the Blue side actually rises, from one-half to a bit above 0.53.

That looks to me like a genuine reductio: a piece of evidence that is, by construction, entirely about how Red territory breaks down internally has somehow dragged her credence in Red-versus-Blue around with it, which suggests that Bayesian conditionalization manufactures information out of thin air the moment the evidence it is fed happens to be conditional in form. If a decades-old thought experiment already shows that, I am curious why formal epistemologists and Bayesian statisticians still teach conditionalization as the default, gold-standard answer to how a rational agent should update on new evidence, rather than treating the Judy Benjamin case as having quietly sunk it.
