So I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I've spent most of this semester buried in Kepler's astrological writings for a dissertation chapter on the astrology/astronomy split around 1600, and I keep getting stuck on how to characterize what he's actually doing in the Tertius Interveniens (1610). He's positioning himself as a "third man" between Roeslin, who defends the traditional horoscopic stuff, and Feselius, who wants to chuck the whole enterprise -- and Kepler's own position is neither. He throws out most of the "vulgar" apparatus (houses, zodiacal rulerships, the idea that you can read specific worldly events off of any old planetary configuration) but keeps a hard core: the aspects, i.e. the geometric angles the planets make as seen from earth, really do act on some shared faculty or "soul" in the earth and in us, continuous with the harmonic/geometric picture he lays out later in the Harmonice Mundi. (He also apparently once described astrology as astronomy's foolish daughter who nonetheless pays her wise mother's bills, though I read that in a secondary source and can't vouch for the exact wording.)

A friend in the philosophy department and I got into it last week about whether this makes Kepler a kind of proto-falsificationist about astrology speficially -- he's willing to prune away huge chunks of the tradition on basically empirical/explanatory grounds while defending what's left as more defensible. So here's what I'm actually curious about: what do you think Kepler would say if you could show him the 20th century debunking literature, like the double-blind matching studies (the Carlson one in Nature is the one everyone cites) or the rise and fall of Gauquelin's "Mars effect" once people ran bigger, more controlled follow-ups? Would he read that as basically finishing the job he started, vindicating his instinct to cut astrology down to size? Or would he say those studies are debunking exactly the predictive, event-level astrology he'd already thrown out in 1610, leaving his aspects-and-soul theory untouched because it was never really in the business of the kind of individual predictions those tests check? And given how ruthlessly he'd abandon his own pet hypotheses when the numbers didn't cooperate (RIP perfect circular orbits) -- do you think the "core" theory he kept was actually the kind of thing that could have been killed by data like that, or was it set up in a way that made it safe from that sort of test from the start?
