I've been down a bit of a Gödel rabbit hole for a piece I'm working on (the Budiansky biography from a few years back is partly to blame), and I keep running into the same flattened version of a story that I suspect is way more interesting underneath. Every pop-science writeup mentions, almost as a punchline, that the guy who proved the incompleteness theorems also sincerely believed in an afterlife -- and then just moves on, like its a quirky-genius factoid on par with him being scared of germs. But from what I can tell he didn't believe this out of religious upbringing or blind faith -- he apparently laid out something like an actual argument for it in letters to his mother, built out of his general premise that the world is fundamentally rational and meaningful rather than absurd, plus some idea about it being wasteful for a person's whole development to just switch off right as its reaching maturity. Seperately, I know Hao Wang's book has that numbered list of Gödel's core convictions (fourteen items, I think?) where an afterlife-ish claim shows up almost as a corollary of his metaphysics rather than a religious add-on.

So what I'd love is for you to actually reconstruct that argument as precisely as you can -- the premises and how the inference is supposed to go, not just "he was a rationalist so he believed in God-ish stuff." Can you tie it to his Platonism about mathematical and logical concepts and his rejection of materialism, and explain how the afterlife claim is supposed to follow from those rather than being a separate leap he tacked on? I'm also curious how this squares with his own incompleteness results -- there's something about him thinking minds can't be reduced to mechanical, rule-bound systems that I think is doing real work here, and I'd love to know exactly how that connects. If you can, say which of the items on that numbered Wang list are actually carrying the weight of the afterlife claim, versus just being background metaphysics. And to be clear, I don't want the ontological proof of God's existence that always gets recycled in these articles -- I mean specifically the separate, much less quoted stuff about personal survival after death.
