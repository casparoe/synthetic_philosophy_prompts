A while back I got heavily into sports betting and poker, and like most people who take position-sizing seriously in that world, I eventually worked through the Kelly criterion: the result that, given a repeatable favorable bet with known odds, there is a specific fraction of your bankroll to stake on each round that maximizes the long-run growth rate of your wealth, provided you keep reinvesting your winnings. I read Poundstone's "Fortune's Formula" and a couple of Thorp's old papers, and the derivation made sense to me on its own terms: you're maximizing the expected value of the logarithm of your wealth because, for a bettor who compounds over many rounds, that is mathematically what long-run growth reduces to. I also ran into the old Samuelson objection to this whole way of thinking, and it stuck with me: Samuelson argued that expected-utility theory only tells you to maximize expected log-wealth if your utility function happens to be logarithmic, and that appeals to "you'll almost surely end up ahead after enough rounds" quietly smuggle in a preference for long-run growth that isn't forced on a rational agent by anything in the axioms.

More recently I've been reading rationalist-community blog posts (on LessWrong, mostly) that use the label "geometric rationality" for something that sounds like a much larger claim than anything Kelly himself made: that maximizing a geometric expectation, rather than an ordinary arithmetic one, is in some sense the more defensible way to aggregate outcomes quite generally, and that this shows up not only in repeated betting but in one-off decisions, and even in how two people should split the gains from a negotiation. That's a big jump from "bet this fraction if you're compounding a bankroll over many rounds," and given that Samuelson's objection was already aimed at the comparatively modest, repeated-betting version of the argument, I'd like to understand whether the broader version survives it. Could you compare Kelly betting with this more general idea of geometric rationality: what exactly does Kelly betting share with the wider proposal, and in what specific ways is it a special case rather than a direct illustration of it? Then tell me, with an actual verdict rather than just a list of similarities and differences, whether the reasoning that makes Kelly betting compelling for repeated, reinvested bets genuinely extends to justify geometric-expectation reasoning in a decision you'll only ever face once, or whether something gets smuggled in along the way that Samuelson would have objected to just as strongly.
