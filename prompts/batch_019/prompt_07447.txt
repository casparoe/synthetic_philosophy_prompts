I am writing a novel in which a present-day theoretical physicist finds herself in dialogue with a fictionalized Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī, and I want their exchange to rest on the actual structure of his occasionalism rather than a caricature of medieval theology. In the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers), Ghazālī denies that fire has any intrinsic power to burn cotton: he holds that God alone produces the burning on each occasion, and that the regularity we observe is merely a divine "habit" (‘āda) that He could suspend at will, as happens in a miracle. Suppose Ghazālī were confronted with Bell's theorem and the modern case for genuine quantum indeterminacy: would he treat it as vindicating his rejection of necessary connections between created things, or would brute, undirected chance be just as alien to his picture as the necessitating natures of the philosophers he opposed? Give a definite answer, and defend it by engaging with the specific commitments of his position rather than the general idea that he merely denied causal necessity.
