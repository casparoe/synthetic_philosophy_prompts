I've been thinking about paradoxes that involve completing infinitely many actions within a finite stretch of time. The best-known case is Thomson's lamp: its switch is flipped at ever-shrinking intervals, so that by the two-minute mark infinitely many flips have occurred, and there seems to be no coherent answer to whether the lamp ends up on or off. A less familiar case is the Ross-Littlewood vase-and-balls problem: at each step, ten numbered balls are placed in a vase and one is removed, and if the ball removed is always the lowest-numbered one still present, the vase turns out to be provably empty after infinitely many steps — even though the number of balls inside it grows without bound at every finite stage. Both are supertasks, yet one seems to end in genuine indeterminacy while the other ends in a single, well-defined but deeply counterintuitive outcome. What is the underlying structural difference that explains this contrast, and does the Ross-Littlewood case — where the supertask does yield a definite answer — weaken Thomson's claim that completing a supertask of this kind is logically impossible?
