(a) Reconstruct the argument that leads him to this claim, being clear about what he means by 'propaganda' here: is he simply urging Black writers to build an explicit political or racial message into their work, or is he making a more general claim about art as such? Below is one student's response to this question. Before I enter a grade, I'd like an independent assessment, since I want to make sure I'm not being too harsh or too lenient anywhere:

"Du Bois's claim that 'all Art is propaganda' is best understood against the backdrop of decades of anti-Black propaganda in American culture, from minstrelsy to D. W. Griffith's Birth of a Nation, which had degraded and dehumanized Black people in the public imagination. Since white America had already weaponized art and popular culture against Black people, Du Bois thought Black artists had an obligation to counter this by using their own art to advance the race: to insist on Black beauty, dignity, and full humanity. So when Du Bois says art is 'propaganda,' he essentially means that Black writers and artists should build an explicit political or racial message into their work, rather than writing 'art for art's sake' and leaving questions of racial justice to activists and politicians. He is not saying art may therefore be crude or artless - he insists elsewhere in the essay that Black art must still meet the highest standards of beauty - but he does think message and beauty need to go together.

This put Du Bois at odds with other figures in the New Negro movement. Alain Locke, for instance, took roughly the opposite position: in his introduction to The New Negro, Locke argued that Black art should be free of propaganda, valued for its own aesthetic self-expression rather than enlisted in a social program, since he worried that treating art as a tool for racial advancement would trap Black artists in a defensive relationship to their own culture. A similar split shows up in the 1926 Nation exchange, where George Schuyler's 'The Negro-Art Hokum' directly rebuts Du Bois's propaganda thesis, insisting that art should carry no racial or social agenda at all, while Langston Hughes's reply, 'The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain,' defends the artist's right to depict any aspect of Black life - however 'low-down' or embarrassing to the Black middle class - without worrying about whether it serves racial uplift.

In my own view, Du Bois's argument does not fully collapse the distinction between art and propaganda, because he never says that propagandistic intent is sufficient for something to count as art - he still requires that it satisfy independent standards of beauty and craft. Propaganda is necessary, on his view, but not sufficient: a pamphlet or a slogan is propaganda without being art. So a meaningful, if narrower, distinction survives, even though he thinks every genuine instance of art will also turn out to be propagandistic in its effects."
