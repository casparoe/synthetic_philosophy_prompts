Explain (a) how the risk function r allows REU to accommodate the Allais preferences, (b) why REU acts still satisfy statewise dominance despite this, and (c) how REU's treatment of risk aversion differs from how risk aversion is handled within ordinary EUT."

Here's the student's answer, copied as submitted:

"REU keeps the same two ingredients as ordinary EUT -- a utility function u over outcomes and a probability measure over states -- but adds a risk function r that reweights the probability of each outcome before its multiplied by utility, similar to how Kahneman and Tversky's weighting function reweights probabilities in prospect theory. Because the best and worst outcomes in a gamble can get pushed up or down independently by r, an agent can prefer a certain $1 million to a gamble that has higher expected value under the unweighted probabilities but a small chance of getting nothing -- that's the Allais pattern. On Buchak's view this comes out rational because r is basically capturing loss aversion: how averse the agent is to landing on the worst outcome, which seems like a perfectly legitimate thing for a rational agent to be sensitive to. Dominance still holds because r never changes which outcomes are ranked above which -- an act that does at least as well as another act in every state, and strictly better in at least one, is still going to be preferred, whatever shape r takes. This is what makes REU different from ordinary EUT, where the only way to be risk averse is a concave u, i.e. diminishing marginal utility of money; REU treats risk aversion as a further, independent parameter that isn't reducible to the utility function. So instead of treating Allais preferences as a mistake that expected utility theorists have to explain away, Buchak concludes they can be perfectly rational."
