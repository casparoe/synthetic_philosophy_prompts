I spent the better part of four decades doing comparative work on sensory physiology and animal behaviour before retiring, and since then I have been filling my time with philosophy of mind, partly at the urging of a former colleague who now teaches the subject at our local university. This has recently led me into teleosemantics: the cluster of views, associated above all with Ruth Millikan and David Papineau, that try to fix the content of a mental or sub-mental state by appeal to its selection history. On Millikan's version, roughly, a state represents whatever condition it has the "proper function" of indicating, where a proper function is whatever effect of a mechanism explains why that mechanism was selected for, and thereby reliably reproduced, in the population that bears it.

What I cannot get past is a sharpened form of Fodor's old disjunction problem. Even once content is restricted to whatever a state was actually selected for indicating, rather than to everything that happens to trigger it, selection history itself seems blind to the difference between several candidate descriptions of that very condition: a narrow one pitched at the level of the organism's actual prey or predator, a wider one pitched at whatever proximal cue the detector is really keyed to, and assorted levels in between, since in the ancestral environment all of these descriptions would have been equally well correlated with the relevant fitness benefit. Teleosemanticists usually reach for a proximal/distal distinction, or a clause about "Normal" conditions, to settle which grain of description constitutes the real content, but I have not been able to convince myself that these moves do more than stipulate a preference for one grain over another.

Could you construct a fresh example of your own that makes this problem concrete? I would like it to involve a real or biologically realistic detection mechanism -- please avoid the frog-snapping-at-flies-and-BB-pellets case and the magnetotactic-bacteria case, both of which I have now seen recycled in every introductory discussion I have come across -- and I would like you to spell out at least two or three genuinely distinct candidate contents that a selected-effects theory cannot obviously choose among on the basis of selection history alone. Finally, please say whether the standard proximal/distal move, or whatever other resource teleosemantics typically offers, actually resolves the indeterminacy in your example, or merely relocates it.
