For a year-end column rounding up thinkers who "saw it coming," I've been rereading Kierkegaard's 1846 Two Ages: A Literary Review, particularly the closing section usually excerpted in English as "The Present Age." What strikes me on this pass is how specific his diagnosis is, and how neatly it maps onto complaints about online discourse today: he isn't just grumbling that the press degrades public life in some vague sense, he builds an actual mechanism. The press manufactures "the public" as an abstraction that supposedly speaks for everyone yet can never be located or held responsible for anything it says; that abstraction then erodes real distinctions between people through what he calls "leveling," which works not by open conflict but by constant, low-grade comparison; and the quiet engine behind the leveling, on his account, is envy rather than malice, dressed up as reflection so that it never has to announce itself as envy. It's a strikingly ungenerous piece of social theory — and, read a certain way, a strikingly funny one — for something written more than a century and a half before anyone had heard of a comment section.

I'd like to close the column with a short joke that trades on this specific machinery rather than the easy version of the idea. Could you write one about Kierkegaard's critique of the press and "the public"? I don't want the "cranky philosopher yells at newspaper" joke that anyone could produce after reading a two-line summary of his work; I want the humor to hinge on something a reader would only get if they knew, say, that "the public" is supposed to be everyone and therefore no one in particular, that envy is doing the real work under cover of reflection, or that leveling proceeds by comparison rather than confrontation. If it also happens to land as a comment on present-day social media, so much the better, but that should fall out of the Kierkegaard material itself rather than being bolted onto it.
