I have no background in philosophy or cognitive science, but I've spent six years as the parent representative on my school district's accommodations review committee, and one case on our agenda for Thursday has been keeping me up at night. A ninth-grader I'll call Eli suffered a severe episode of autoimmune encephalitis two years ago that left him with a lasting deficit in forming new long-term memories. Since then he has kept an obsessively detailed log on his phone -- covering everything from class material to what his friends said at lunch -- which he updates himself, in his own words, as things happen. By his own account and his neurologist's, he doesn't experience consulting it as "looking something up," any more than you or I experience recalling a childhood address as looking it up: he reaches for it automatically, trusts what it tells him without double-checking, and has done so every day for two years.

His mother has asked the committee to let him consult the log during the district's closed-book final exams, which currently prohibit personal devices and notes on the grounds that finals are meant to certify what a student has internalized, not what they can retrieve from something outside their own head. Her position is that taking the phone away for three hours during a final isn't leveling the playing field, it's disabling part of the system that does his remembering, the same way confiscating another student's glasses before a vision-dependent task would. Our assessment coordinator, for whom I have a great deal of respect, disagrees just as sincerely: she points out that glasses don't contain the material being tested, whereas Eli's log does, so allowing it doesn't correct a deficit against a shared baseline, it changes what the exam measures; and she worries that "this is really just part of how I think" is exactly what any sufficiently diligent student could say about a very good cheat sheet, so the committee needs some principle that doesn't just collapse into whatever a family sincerely believes.

We reconvene Thursday, and since I was the one who asked for more time, I'm the one who has to circulate something beforehand. Could you draft that memo for me? I need a clear recommendation -- allow the log, deny it, or allow it under specific conditions -- along with reasoning the rest of the committee could actually engage with point by point. I know my own sympathies already lean toward the mother, so I'd especially like the strongest, least dismissible version of the coordinator's worry represented fairly, not a straw version of it. Finals are five weeks away, and since we're supposedly using this case to set a general policy rather than a one-off ruling, I'd also like the memo to say what would distinguish Eli's situation from that of a student who simply wants to bring notes into the exam room.
