I am at work on a novel whose pivotal chapter is set in a 1949 West German courtroom, where a judge must pass judgment on a woman who, in 1944, denounced her husband to the Nazi authorities for a disparaging remark he made about Hitler while home on leave; he was sentenced to death, though the sentence was later commuted to service at the front. I want the judge's private reasoning to track an actual jurisprudential fault line rather than an invented one, so I need to understand precisely where H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller part ways on this "grudge informer" case, a central example in their 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange. As I understand it, Hart holds that the wife acted under statutes that, however odious, were validly law, so that punishing her honestly demands frank retroactive legislation; Fuller holds that the Nazi regime's contempt for law's inner morality had already stripped its enactments of any claim to the name "law," making retroactivity beside the point. Please compare these two positions: is their disagreement substantive, or merely a dispute over which label to attach to a shared moral conclusion? And whose reasoning would equip my judge to write the more intellectually honest verdict?
