I've spent more than a decade reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and, in the early days, Overcoming Bias, and I recently found an unexpected use for all that reading: I was asked to join the small working group that administers the forecasting program at the policy-research nonprofit where I work. The program is modeled loosely on Tetlock's Good Judgment Project: staff volunteer to attach probabilities to questions relevant to our work (will a given bill clear committee by a certain date, will a particular country hit a stated emissions target, and so on), and it currently evaluates everyone using the Brier score. The resulting leaderboard is mostly just for fun, but the same numbers also feed into quarterly performance reviews, which is where a real disagreement has opened up.

Half the working group wants to replace the Brier score with the logarithmic score before the next quarter starts. As best I can reconstruct their position, it leans on the accuracy-first epistemology that gets discussed on rationalist blogs — the idea, associated with Jim Joyce and developed at length by Richard Pettigrew, that only a strictly proper scoring rule can be given a principled epistemic justification — together with the further observation that the log score, unlike Brier, is "local": your score depends only on the probability you assigned to whatever actually happened, not on how you distributed the remaining credence among alternatives. They take this to settle the question in the log score's favor. The other half isn't moved, for a more practical reason: because the log score is unbounded, one confidently wrong call (say, 3 percent on something that then happens) can wipe out a whole quarter of otherwise excellent forecasting, and they claim — anecdotally, but plausibly — that our best forecasters already hedge toward 50 percent on the highest-stakes questions to protect their standing, which seems to defeat the entire purpose of scoring people to begin with. Two meetings have now ended without a resolution, largely because the discussion keeps sliding between "which rule is theoretically justified" and "which rule produces good behavior" without anyone quite noticing the slide.

I've been asked to write the short memo the group will vote on next week, and I'd like your help drafting it. It needs to come down on a specific policy — keep the Brier score, switch to the log score, or some third option (a cap on extreme values, different rules for the public leaderboard and the performance-review figure, or something else) — and it needs to lay out the reasoning so that whichever side loses can see that its strongest argument was actually engaged with, rather than granted a sentence and set aside.
