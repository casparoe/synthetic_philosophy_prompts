In my philosophy of religion seminar we've been discussing David Lewis's short paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" It tests the coherence of the doctrine by pressing an analogy with debt: just as someone else can pay off what I owe and thereby discharge my debt, the thought goes, Christ can be punished in my place and thereby discharge my liability to punishment. Lewis's worry is that punishment, unlike a sum of money, is supposed to be deserved specifically by the wrongdoer, rather than being a fungible burden that anyone's suffering can absorb on the debtor's behalf. For my seminar response paper, I want to defend the stronger claim that no repair to the debt analogy (not the substitute's consent, not stipulating an equivalent quantity of suffering, not an appeal to divine authority to arrange the transfer) can close this gap, because desert, unlike a debt, is not the kind of relation that can be satisfied by redirecting the relevant burden onto a different bearer. Please construct this argument as rigorously as you can: first grant the debt analogy its strongest form, and then show exactly where, and why, it must give out.
