Random Frege-Geach question that's been bugging me while I prep for comps. I feel solid on the classic modus ponens case -- needing some higher-order attitude, à la Blackburn, to explain why an argument like "lying is wrong; if lying is wrong then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; so getting your little brother to lie is wrong" comes out valid. But I keep running into people (Unwin, Schroeder) treating "the negation problem" as this whole seperate obstacle that needs it's own fix, and I don't really get why. Isn't negation just a degenerate, simpler case of the conditional problem? Like, if Blackburn or Gibbard already have machinery telling you which combinations of attitudes toward "wrong" are consistent across a conditional, shouldn't "not wrong" just fall out of that automatically, instead of needing a whole separate solution?
