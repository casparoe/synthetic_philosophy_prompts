I'm a PhD student in mathematical logic, and for the past semester I've also been sitting in on a philosophy of mathematics reading group that has spent most of its time on the literature that grew up around Cantor's continuum problem after Cohen's independence result. Nobody in the group disputes the technical fact that CH is independent of ZFC; what we can't agree on is what attitude a set theorist or philosopher should actually take once that's granted, and the going positions don't sort neatly into "realist" versus "anti-realist" camps the way an introductory course might suggest. Our last session ended in a genuine stalemate about how these positions even rank against one another, so I'd like a considered outside opinion, ideally from a source that isn't just echoing whichever view happens to be dominant in a given department.

Below are nine stances that have actually been defended in print (I've tried not to strawman any of them):

1. Gödel's original view: CH has a determinate truth value, which he took to be almost certainly false and expected future, not-yet-discovered axioms to settle -- despite the fact that his own consistency proof went via V=L.
2. V=L: adopt Gödel's axiom of constructibility outright, which settles CH (indeed GCH), at the cost of capping the large-cardinal hierarchy well below a measurable cardinal.
3. Forcing axioms (e.g., PFA, or Martin's Maximum from Foreman-Magidor-Shelah): imply that the continuum has size ℵ2, hence ¬CH, by generalizing the genericity intuitions already built into Martin's Axiom.
4. Woodin's Ω-logic case (circa 2001) that CH is false, resting on the still-open Ω-conjecture and a notion of generic absoluteness for the theory of H(ω2).
5. Woodin's later Ultimate-L program, which, if its conjectures are borne out, points to a canonical model satisfying GCH -- in apparent tension with (4).
6. Hamkins' set-theoretic multiverse view: CH has no absolute truth value; the substantive mathematics lies in how its truth varies across the multiverse of models related by forcing.
7. Freiling's "axiom of symmetry": an informal dart-throwing symmetry argument against CH, plus the standard rejoinder that it smuggles in, under cover of "randomness," exactly what was at issue.
8. Maddy's set-theoretic naturalism: candidate axioms (and so, derivatively, CH) should be judged by internal mathematical fruitfulness -- her "maximize" and "unify" maxims -- rather than by presumed insight into a single intended universe of sets.
9. Feferman's indefiniteness thesis: CH is not even a definite mathematical problem, because the quantifier over arbitrary subsets of the reals ranges over an inherently indefinite totality.
