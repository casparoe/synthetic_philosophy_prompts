Here is the answer I need graded:

"Fitch's paradox starts from two claims that look individually very plausible. The first is the knowability principle (KP): for every truth p, it is possible that p is known by someone at some time -- formally, p → ◊Kp. The second is the non-omniscience assumption: there is at least one truth that nobody ever actually gets to know, i.e., ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp). Anti-realists about truth, who want to deny that there can be truths that permanently outrun our capacity to verify them, are typically drawn to something like KP, while also wanting to hold on to the obvious fact that plenty of truths go unknown.

The proof works by plugging a particularly nasty instance into KP. Since by non-omniscience there is some truth of the form p ∧ ¬Kp, we can apply KP directly to this very conjunction: if p ∧ ¬Kp is true, then it is possible for someone to know that p ∧ ¬Kp. But knowledge distributes over conjunction, so anyone who knew p ∧ ¬Kp would thereby know both p and that p is not known. And since knowledge is factive -- Kx entails x -- knowing that p is not known entails that p really is not known. So the supposed knower would have to know p while it is also true that p is not known: a flat contradiction. So it is not possible, after all, for anyone to know p ∧ ¬Kp. But KP said it was possible. Since assuming there is an unknown truth leads to contradiction, we're forced to conclude that every truth is in fact known -- a much stronger claim than any anti-realist wants. This is the paradox: KP plus the truism that not everything is known together entail the absurd result that everything is known, and most philosophers now take this to be a decisive refutation of anti-realism about truth rather than merely a puzzle about how to state the knowability thesis correctly.

Dummett's response is to restrict KP so that it only applies to what he calls "Cartesian" propositions -- roughly, propositions that don't become self-defeating once known -- which rules out plugging in p ∧ ¬Kp in the first place and blocks the derivation. I find this fairly convincing, since it costs the anti-realist very little: they only give up knowability for a strange class of self-referential propositions that nobody cares about anyway, while keeping the intuitive core of the view intact for everything else."
