I spent my working life as a condensed-matter physicist, mostly on spin glasses and other disordered systems, so words like "ergodic" and "broken ergodicity" mean something pretty specific and hard-won to me -- a system is ergodic if a long time-average along one trajectory converges to the average over the whole ensemble, and in glassy systems that equivalence famously breaks down because phase space splits into valleys seperated by barriers you'll never cross in any realistic observation time. Since retiring I've filled the hours with two new habits that apparently were destined to collide: reading philosophy (never took a class in my life, now I can't stop) and sports betting (an excuse to keep doing statistics on something). The betting habit dragged me straight into the Kelly criterion -- bet the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the expected log of your wealth, because thats what maximizes your long-run growth rate -- and the math there is easy enough once you set it up as an optimization over expected log-wealth.

Where I got stuck is the philosophical add-on that comes bundled with the Kelly criterion these days. I fell down a rabbit hole of blogs and forum threads about "ergodicity economics" and something people are calling "geometric rationality," and the argument, as best I can reconstruct it, goes: an individual's wealth under repeated multiplicative bets is a "non-ergodic" quantity, so the time-average growth rate (the Kelly criterion) is the objectively correct thing to optimize, and ordinary expected-value maximization is basically a category mistake that economists have been papering over for decades by bolting a "risk aversion" utility curve on after the fact. Some of them want to push this even further into a whole general "geometric" rival to expected-utility rationality that's supposed to apply well beyond gambling. As a physicist this smells funny to me, like it's borrowing the word "ergodic" for its prestige more than its content -- but I can't get precise about why, and I suspect I might just be pattern-matching on vague unease rather than a real objection.

Could you actually walk me through this properly, in a way that doesn't just repeat the slogans from either side back to me? Three things I'd like sorted out in particular: first, whether the "non-ergodic wealth" claim is doing genuine mathematical work that's actually analogous to ergodicity-breaking in physics, or whether it's really just a restatement of the gap between the expectation of a product and the log of that expectation, dressed up in borrowed vocabulary; second, whether there's a real difference between "the dynamics force you to maximize expected log-wealth" and "you happen to have a log utility function" -- I vaguely recall Samuelson accusing the Kelly crowd of a fallacy of large numbers along these lines, and I want to know if that old objection still lands against the ergodicity-economics version; and third, whether "geometric rationality" actually generalizes into a coherent rival foundation for rational choice outside of repeated multiplicative betting, or whether that's smuggling a narrow, well-understood special case into a much bigger philosophical claim it hasn't earned. I want an actual opinion out of this, not a survey -- tell me which side is smuggling in what.
