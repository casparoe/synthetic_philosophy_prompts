Quick decision theory one: the "Death in Damascus" case from Gibbard & Harper. A guy runs into Death in Damascus, Death tells him "I'm coming for you tomorrow," so that night the guy skips town for Aleppo. Turns out Death works off an appointment book filled in weeks ago based on highly reliable predictions, so wherever the guy actually ends up is exactly where the book says he'll be, and he dies either way. Causal decision theory famously says neither "stay" nor "flee" is ratifiable here -- whichever one you pick, the fact that you picked it is evidence Death predicted it, so the other option looks like it would've been better. The standard fix is that flipping a coin turns out to be self-ratifying, so thats supposedly "the rational thing to do" -- but tweak the case to be asymmetric (say Aleppo's a brutal week-long trek, or Death is a lot more thorough in one city than the other) and coin-flipping starts looking pretty nuts as a recipe for rational choice.

Write an essay on whether randomizing really is the rational solution to Death in Damascus, or whether the case (especially asymmetric versions of it) exposes a real, unpatched problem for causal decision theory. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but actually commit to a verdict about what a rational agent should do -- dont just end on "well, it's genuinely unclear."
