I live in a five-person group house that's been together for about three years now, and somewhere in the first six months a household norm crystallized that nobody ever explicitly proposed. We don't have a chore wheel or a rota; instead, the rule that emerged is that whoever is the first person to find the communal kitchen intolerably messy is the one who cleans it. No one designed this — it's just what happened after enough repetitions that everybody now orients around it as though it were policy, even though if you asked any of us in the abstract whether "whoever cares first does the work" sounds like a reasonable way to allocate a chore, I doubt anyone would defend it.

The trouble is that my housemate Priya and I seem to have very different thresholds for "intolerable," and I increasingly suspect hers isn't just naturally higher than mine but has adjusted over time, consciously or not, now that she knows I'll crack first. When I raised this with her, she didn't deny the pattern; she just didn't see the problem with it. Her position, roughly, was: "Nobody is stopping you from leaving the dishes as long as I do. You're choosing to clean up every time, and I'm choosing not to — those are two equally free choices, and it's strange to say I'm treating you unfairly just because your choice and mine keep coming out different." I find this hard to rebut on its own terms, since it's true that she has never ordered me to do anything, and yet the conclusion still feels wrong to me, as though her description of "two equally free choices" is smuggling in something that isn't actually symmetric between us.

I've read enough of the more social-science-minded rationalist blogs over the years to suspect that this exact shape of disagreement — an unplanned regularity that everyone now treats as binding, defended by the person who benefits from it as though it were just neutral personal freedom — has a real name and has been picked apart somewhere more rigorous than our house group chat, but I can't reconstruct the argument well enough to know who's right. So: is Priya's "we're each just making a free choice" defense actually adequate, or is there a legitimate fairness complaint hiding underneath the way this arrangement came about? And if I do have a case, what, structurally, would it even take to renegotiate a rule that, as far as either of us can tell, nobody ever actually made?
