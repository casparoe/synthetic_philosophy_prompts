My younger sister is getting married in six weeks, and as maid of honour I've been asked to give the toast at the reception. She and her fiancé met three years ago at a small, slightly nerdy weekend workshop for people who like arguing about decision theory as a hobby, which tells you most of what you need to know about the two of them. I have no academic background in philosophy myself -- I trained as a dental hygienist -- but over the past year, largely so I could follow their dinner-table conversations, I worked through a secondhand copy of some of Douglas Hofstadter's old Scientific American writing and got stuck on his idea of "superrationality": the claim that two reasoners who are relevantly alike, facing a genuinely symmetric one-off prisoner's dilemma, should each cooperate -- not out of trust, and not because anything forces them to, but because each can see that whatever conclusion is correct for a reasoner in their position must be the very same conclusion the other one reaches, so that choosing to cooperate effectively chooses it for both of you at once.

The more I turned this over, the more it struck me as a truer account of my sister's relationship than the usual wedding-toast language about trust and soulmates. She and her fiancé spent their first year together on different continents, each one perfectly free to stray without the other ever finding out, and by their own account neither spent that year anxious about it -- not from blind faith, but because each had separately reasoned the whole thing through and landed exactly where the other one had, with no way to check in advance that they would agree.

I'd like the toast to be built around that idea rather than dropped in as a clever aside, since it is genuinely the best explanation I have for why the two of them work. My worry is the room: roughly half the guests are people from that same workshop who will notice immediately if I get the idea wrong, and the other half are aunts, uncles, and family friends who have never heard the phrase "prisoner's dilemma" in their lives and just want something short and warm at a wedding. Could you write me a toast of about five minutes in which the decision theory genuinely does the emotional work of the speech, rather than sitting decoratively on top of it, while still landing perfectly well for the guests who won't catch a single reference?
