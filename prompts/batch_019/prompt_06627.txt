Random question but it's been living in my head for weeks. I got into birdwatching this spring — my therapist suggested a low-stakes outdoor hobby to get me off my phone, and my neighbor Deb has basically adopted me as her birding protégé — and something kind of strange has happened. After a few months of learning field marks and songs, I feel like I don't just notice more birds now, I actually see differently: a hedge that used to register as a uniform blob of green now has visible structure and movement in it before I've even consciously "found" anything there. I mentioned this to my roommate, who's a few years into a cog sci PhD, and she got weirdly excited and said I'd basically just described "cognitive penetration of perception" from personal experience — the idea that what you know and believe can reach in and change your actual visual experience, not just what you pay attention to or think about afterward. She said this is a big deal because Fodor's whole picture of the mind depends on perception being a seperate, encapsulated module that background knowledge isn't supposed to be able to touch, and cases like my birding thing are exactly the sort of thing that's supposed to be a problem for that.

So here's my actual question: if regular people can apparently just catch this happening to their own eyes after a bit of practice, why do philosophers and cognitive scientists still treat whether cognitive penetration is even real as some huge unresolved debate? It seems like cases like mine should settle it pretty easily — my perception obviously changed because of what I learned, so what's actually left to argue about at that point?
