I co-host a small podcast on free will and moral psychology for a listenership that overlaps heavily with the rationalist blogosphere, and I'm prepping questions for the next episode in a short series we're doing on moral responsibility. The guest defends a broadly Strawsonian compatibilism -- the view, from "Freedom and Resentment," that our practices of resenting, thanking, blaming, and praising people are grounded in the "reactive attitudes," natural responses to the perceived quality of will behind what someone does, and that the truth or falsity of determinism is largely beside the point, since those attitudes were never premised on indeterminism to begin with. She's a serious, well-read defender of the position rather than a straw version of it, but in past talks and in our pre-interview she's shown a consistent habit of deflecting hard cases with some version of the line "that's just the objective attitude creeping back in under a different name" -- treating almost any pressure on the view as itself proof that the questioner has covertly stepped outside the participant stance the view is meant to vindicate, rather than as an objection she actually has to answer on its own terms.

I'd like eight to ten questions that don't just let her restate the framework, and that make that particular move progressively harder to reach for as the interview goes on. Please build in at least one question each on: (a) Gary Watson's use of a real death-row case (Robert Harris, whose horrific childhood is often cited in this context) to ask whether resentment and compassion can both be tracking the agent's actual quality of will, rather than one of them just being the attitude we reach for once the causal story gets too uncomfortable; (b) Pereboom's four-case manipulation argument, pushing her to locate a principled difference between a manipulated agent and an ordinarily determined one rather than simply asserting there is one; (c) R. Jay Wallace's amendment that it's only fair to hold someone to a demand they had the power to grasp and act on, and whether that "power" talk quietly reintroduces the kind of robust control the reactive-attitudes story was supposed to let her avoid needing; and (d) whether the framework has any principled basis for saying whether reactive attitudes toward sufficiently capable AI systems could ever be fitting, given that quality-of-will talk seems to presuppose exactly the kind of agent we're unsure such systems are. For each, add a plausible follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close with a question asking her to specify what, short of giving up the framework outright, would actually count as evidence that our reactive attitudes are misfiring across the board rather than just in this or that hard case.
