A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about Gottlob Frege, of all people -- specifically, about whether his philosophy should be read any differently in light of the diary he kept during the last year of his life. Frege died in 1925; the diary surfaced among his papers only decades afterward and wasn't published until the 1990s, and it turned out to be full of monarchist, anti-parliamentary sentiment and outright antisemitism, including the claim that Jews should be stripped of political rights or else leave the country. My friend's position was that none of this touches the philosophy in the slightest: you assess the sense/reference distinction, Frege's definition of number in terms of extensions of concepts, and his case against psychologism about logic strictly on their own merits, and treating a dead man's repellent private opinions as evidence against his arguments is moralized ad hominem dressed up as scholarship -- no different, she said, from citing Newton's biblical chronology and alchemical writings against the Principia. I wasn't satisfied with that, especially once I recalled that Michael Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to install Frege as a founding father of analytic philosophy, has written about how badly the diary shook him once he learned of it -- which suggested that someone who knew the corpus far better than either of us sensed a real tension here. Neither of us got past restating our starting positions, though, and the argument just fizzled by the time the check came.

I'd like you to make the case my friend wouldn't accept. Construct the strongest available argument that the diary is philosophically relevant, and not merely biographically damning. That means supplying an actual thesis connecting specific commitments in Frege's logic and philosophy of thought -- his hostility to psychologism, his insistence that the laws of logic bind every thinker regardless of standpoint or culture, and his positing of a timeless realm to which thoughts belong, apart from both the physical world and any individual mind, which he himself called, in his own German, a "third realm" (drittes Reich), years before that phrase acquired its more infamous later meaning -- to specific commitments in the diary: his contempt for parliamentary bargaining among competing interests, and his treatment of Jews as a foreign element that a healthy nation should expel rather than accommodate. Tell me explicitly what, if anything, you think that terminological echo is worth, rather than silently leaning on it or silently ignoring it, and then take on the obvious counterexample: other logicians just as hostile to psychologism in that same period, Bolzano and Husserl among them, held nothing resembling Frege's politics -- which looks like evidence that the logic can't be doing the work your argument needs it to do.
