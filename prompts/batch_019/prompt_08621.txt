For my final paper in my high school philosophy elective, I've been studying the "Bad Faith" chapter of Sartre's Being and Nothingness, including his examples of the café waiter who overplays his role and the woman on a date who refuses to acknowledge the meaning of her companion's advances. Around the same time, in economics class, I learned about "nudge theory": Thaler and Sunstein's idea that choice architects can reliably steer people's decisions through defaults and framing alone, such as auto-enrolling employees in retirement plans or making organ donation opt-out, without removing any option or using coercion. Sartre died in 1980, decades before nudging became an influential policy tool, so I'm curious how he would have judged it: would he treat a well-designed nudge as just another feature of our facticity, fully compatible with radical freedom since we remain free to opt out at any moment, or would he see it as a distinctively modern and more insidious form of bad faith, in which the choice architect and the "nudged" person tacitly collude to obscure the nudgee's freedom and responsibility for the outcome? Please argue for one of these positions using Sartre's own concepts, and explain specifically where the analogy to his waiter and woman-on-a-date examples breaks down once the "deceiver" is an institution or algorithm rather than an individual practicing self-deception on their own.
