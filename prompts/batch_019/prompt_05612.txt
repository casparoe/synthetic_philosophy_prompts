I'm a philosophy PhD student, though my own work is in a different subfield (philosophy of language), so this isn't really my area -- but it's been nagging at me since a dinner last week. A friend, Ana, stopped buying eggs after her housemate Owen spent twenty minutes describing battery-cage conditions to her one afternoon; she's since said she never looked into the matter herself, and simply found Owen credible. When this came up at dinner, two other people at the table, Leah and Marcus, got into a real disagreement about it.

(1) Leah said Ana deserves genuine moral credit: she heard that a practice causes serious suffering, believed it, and stopped participating. Demanding more than that before crediting her just penalizes people for not being moral philosophers.

(2) Marcus said that's too fast. Ana did the right thing, but not for the reasons that make it right: swap Owen for a housemate who insisted factory eggs are fine, and Ana would have kept buying them just as readily. Compare a patient who trusts a doctor's diagnosis without understanding it: we don't think you need medical expertise to be credited with taking your health seriously, but we do expect a minimally decent person to see for themselves why gratuitous suffering matters, rather than just taking a friend's word for it.

(3) Leah answered that this proves too much: nearly all our early moral opinions are absorbed exactly this way, from parents and teachers, well before we're able to defend them, and nobody thinks that makes inherited moral belief worthless. If secondhand moral belief is fine in the ordinary case, Ana's isn't special just because we can name her one source.

(4) Marcus replied that there's still a difference between a belief absorbed early that gradually becomes anchored in a person's own outlook -- so that they'd notice if it clashed with their other commitments -- and a belief held purely on continuing say-so, with nothing else propping it up. Ana's, he thinks, is the second kind: remove Owen from the picture, and nothing is left holding the belief up.

Neither budged, and dinner moved on before Leah could respond. I'd appreciate a move-by-move adjudication: for each of the four numbered turns, say whether it holds up, partly holds up, or fails, with a brief justification -- please don't just adjudicate the overall winner -- and close with a short paragraph on what you think both of them are missing.
