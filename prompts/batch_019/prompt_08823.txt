I never studied philosophy or logic formally — I service industrial refrigeration systems for a living — but for the last several months I've been working through the literature on conditionals in whatever spare time I can find, mostly via the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and a few PDFs that a friend from an online philosophy reading group sent me. That friend is the one who first pointed me toward David Lewis's 1976 triviality result, and I haven't been able to leave it alone since.

As I understand it, the starting point is what's called Adams' Thesis: your degree of belief in "if A, then B" should just equal your conditional probability of B given A. Lewis showed that if this equation is also required to survive conditionalizing on new evidence — not merely to describe your credences as they happen to be right now — something strange falls out. Conditionalize first on B, then on not-B, and track what each move forces the probability of the conditional to be; when you do, you can derive that the conditional probability of B given A has to equal the plain, unconditional probability of B. Since A and B were arbitrary, that generalizes into the claim that any two propositions whatsoever come out probabilistically independent of one another, which is absurd for anything resembling a normal set of beliefs. I can follow the algebra well enough, but I keep going back and forth on what to actually conclude from it, and I suspect I'd understand my own reaction better if I saw the dispute acted out between two people instead of summarized in an encyclopedia entry.

So here is what I'd like: a dialogue between two people who both fully accept that Lewis's proof is correct — neither one thinks he made a mathematical error — but who walk away from it with opposite morals. One should take the route associated with Ernest Adams and Dorothy Edgington and conclude that indicative conditionals were never in the business of expressing propositions to begin with, so of course the "probability of the conditional" can't be squared with ordinary probability theory: there was no proposition there for the equation to be about. The other should hold onto a broadly Stalnaker-style, possible-worlds semantics on which conditionals do express propositions, and try to block the argument the way I gather some truth-conditional theorists have — by denying that Adams' Thesis needs to survive arbitrary conditionalizing, and insisting it only has to hold for whichever credence function an agent actually has at the time. I'd like the second person to press the first on the obvious weak spot: if a conditional isn't true or false, what is even going on when it's embedded under negation, sitting inside a conjunction, or serving as the antecedent of a further conditional? And I'd like the first person to press back just as hard on whether restricting the thesis to "only my actual credences" is a principled move or just a patch stitched on to dodge the proof — one that no longer explains why the thesis felt so secure across ordinary reasoning and updating before Lewis came along. Please have them respond to each other's specific points as the exchange unfolds, the way two people who respect each other's competence actually argue, rather than each delivering a self-contained monologue.
