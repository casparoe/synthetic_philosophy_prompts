I have no formal background in philosophy, but over the last year or so I've spent a lot of my free time reading about metaphysics and philosophy of mind, mostly through online essays, forum threads, and whatever recorded lectures I can track down. One argument that keeps coming up in discussions of whether the mind is "just" the brain runs roughly like this: if a mental state such as pain were truly identical to some physical brain state — the textbook example always seems to be the firing of certain neurons called "C-fibres" — then the two would have to share absolutely every property in common, since that is simply what it means for two things to be one and the same thing rather than two. Because people can apparently point to at least one property where pain and any candidate brain state diverge, this is treated as a serious, sometimes decisive, reason to doubt that mental states are literally identical with brain states. The principle behind this move is usually credited to Leibniz.

Separately, I ran across a much-discussed thought experiment by the philosopher Max Black, in which he asks us to imagine a universe that contains nothing whatsoever except two solid iron spheres, exactly alike down to the last detail, floating some fixed distance apart, with no other objects, landmarks, or observers anywhere to help tell one from the other. Black's point, as I understand it, is that such a universe is perfectly conceivable, and yet it obviously contains two distinct spheres rather than one, despite the fact that the two share every single property in common. This thought experiment is presented as a serious, still-unresolved problem for the very same Leibnizian principle that gets invoked so confidently in the mind-brain case.

What I cannot work out is how both of these can be respectable moves within the same field. If Black's spheres really do show that two things can share every property and still be numerically distinct, that would seem to undercut the exact inference the mind-brain argument depends on, since maybe pain and its neural correlate are like the two spheres: perfectly indiscernible from one another and yet still two things rather than one. But if the mind-brain argument is sound, then sharing every property really does seem to force identity, which is precisely what Black's spheres are supposed to refute. This has been nagging at me for weeks, and a friend I discussed it with insisted that the two cases must somehow be about different things, but neither of us could say what that difference actually is. Is one of these two arguments simply mistaken, or is there some way for both to stand side by side that I'm not seeing?
