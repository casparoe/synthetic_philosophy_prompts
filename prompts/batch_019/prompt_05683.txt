I'm drafting a novel in which a character comes to hold two flatly contradictory beliefs about the same person without ever being irrational -- not the usual dramatic-irony trick of letting the reader in on something he doesn't know, but a case where nothing in his own reasoning ever goes wrong. Trying to work out whether such a mind is even coherent, I kept running into discussions of Frege's puzzle about why identity statements like "Hesperus is Phosphorus" are informative, and then into a supposedly tougher variant involving the pianist-turned-statesman Paderewski, where the same feat is achieved with only a single name in play and no identity claim ever doubted. Could you give me an intuitive sense of why philosophers treat the Paderewski case as a genuine escalation of Frege's original puzzle rather than just a dramatic retelling of it, and specifically why Frege's own fix -- distinguishing a name's sense from its reference -- stops being an easy answer once there are no longer two distinct names available to carry two distinct senses? I'd rather have the underlying intuition than the formal semantics.
