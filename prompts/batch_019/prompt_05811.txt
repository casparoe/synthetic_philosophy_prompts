A few of us who run the philosophy club's podcast at my high school have arranged a short recorded interview with a metaphysician who is known in particular for defending the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles against Max Black's famous 1952 thought experiment involving two qualitatively identical spheres. Her position, as I understand it from a paper our faculty advisor had us read before the interview, is that the spheres are still "weakly discernible" even though neither one has any purely qualitative or ordinary relational property that the other lacks: the move is to point to an irreflexive relation, something like being a fixed distance from the other sphere, that holds between the pair in each direction but could never hold between either sphere and itself, so the mere fact that it holds at all forces there to be two objects rather than one. We read Black's dialogue closely enough beforehand to see that he already has one of his two speakers head off the more obvious relational reply — that each sphere is "a mile from a sphere," a property they share equally — so I think I follow why the weak-discernibility move is supposed to be a cleverer, different maneuver than the one Black already anticipated and dismissed.

Since none of us wants the interview to consist of her simply restating the position for an audience that has never heard of it, could you draft eight or nine questions that press on how well the move actually holds up? I'd like at least one question asking her to explain why weak discernibility isn't just as question-begging as the reply Black already rejects, since calling one sphere "a mile from the other" already seems to presuppose that there are two things to begin with; one asking whether rescuing the principle this way secretly trades it in for something weaker than what Leibniz intended, given that the spheres still end up with no qualitative difference between them, only a guarantee that there are two of them; and one asking her to compare the spheres to the way I've seen this same "weak discernibility" idea invoked for indistinguishable particles in quantum mechanics, a comparison I don't understand well enough to evaluate on my own. For every question, please add a short note on the answer she is likely to give and a sharper follow-up keyed specifically to that answer, the way a good interviewer would if she tried to wave the objection away.
