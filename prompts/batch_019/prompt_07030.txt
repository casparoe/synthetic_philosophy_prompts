I sit on my university's research integrity committee, and we've got a case that's turned into a real arguement about something our policy doesn't actually settle. Two years ago Dr. A reviewed a grant proposal from Dr. B under standard confidentiality, and B's proposal laid out a pretty specific hypothesis about why the effect they'd both been studying disappears outside the lab. About 18 months later A publishes a paper built on that same hypothesis, different methods, no citation to B. B calls it scooping enabled by the review process; A says the idea was already floating around the subfield (this happens constantly in science -- Newton/Leibniz, Darwin/Wallace), that nobody can 'own' a hypothesis this general anyway, and that there's no proof he even consciously remembered B's proposal rather than just absorbing it -- cryptomnesia is a real thing, its well documented in the memory literature. Our policy defines plagiarism broadly enough to cover ideas, not just text or data, but a finding also requires the conduct to be intentional or reckless, not an honest mistake. Half the committee wants to censure A on the theory that this pattern is almost never innocent; the other half thinks that standard would basically criminalize ordinary convergent discovery. Can you draft the decision memo I'll bring to the committee -- a real finding plus concrete next steps -- that engages the convergent-discovery problem honestly instead of just waving it away?
