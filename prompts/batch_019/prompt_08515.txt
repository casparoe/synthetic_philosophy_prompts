In my seminar on decision theory, we've spent the last few weeks on multi-agent problems that come up in discussions of advanced AI, and we recently moved from ordinary Newcomb-style problems into the stranger territory of acausal threats: cases in which an agent — another person, an institution, or a future AI system — can benefit merely from your believing that they would carry out a punishment against you, with no ordinary causal channel of enforcement required, simply because you are able to predict what they would do. One proposed defense against this that came up in the readings is the idea of a "surrogate goal": you publicly and credibly commit to caring enormously about some cheap, otherwise-irrelevant outcome, so that a threatener who only cares about extracting concessions efficiently will prefer to threaten that surrogate rather than the things you actually value, since redirecting the threat costs them nothing. The strategy is billed as a kind of free lunch — a "safe" improvement that leaves you no worse off, since you never really cared about the surrogate, while still giving the threatener everything they wanted out of making the threat in the first place.

When I raised in discussion section that this seemed too good to be true, my professor agreed that whether surrogate goals actually survive contact with a sufficiently sophisticated adversary is genuinely contested among people who work on this, but we ran out of time before getting into why. Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp decision theorists, perhaps arguing it out over coffee after class, one of whom defends adopting a surrogate goal as a rational and nearly costless defense against extortion, and the other of whom thinks the strategy quietly falls apart once you take seriously how a sophisticated threatener would reason about it? I'd like the skeptic to press on at least the following: whether a commitment to value the surrogate can be credible once it is common knowledge that it was adopted purely as a strategic patch rather than as a genuine value; whether a threatener could simply route around the surrogate by threatening the commitment mechanism itself; and whether openly adopting the strategy might make you a more attractive target for threats overall rather than less. I'd like the defender to have substantive, non-strawmanned replies to each of these rather than simply folding, and I'd like both sides to actually engage with what the other has just said rather than merely restating their own view in turn. Feel free to let the conversation end in genuine disagreement rather than forcing a tidy resolution.
