Thomas Schelling argued in *The Strategy of Conflict* that a bargainer gains power precisely by destroying their own options: whoever commits first, and most irrevocably, to a demand forces the other side to do the accommodating, since only one party can still afford to back down. Schelling was equally alert to the dangers of pushing this logic too far, warning that automated or otherwise irrevocable retaliation systems can remove the very flexibility that keeps deterrence from tipping into catastrophe. Researchers in AI safety have recently described a structurally similar worry under the name "commitment races": because advanced AI systems might be able to bind their future conduct with a credibility no human promise can match (for instance, by provably committing via their own source code), each system faces pressure to lock in an aggressive bargaining stance as early as possible, before it has reasoned the situation through, on the logic that whoever commits first and hardest wins the ensuing standoff. Because such commitments could be far more binding than any commitment device available to human negotiators, incompatible early commitments threaten to produce not stable deterrence but guaranteed mutual disaster.

Drawing on Schelling's own arguments about commitment, credibility, and the risks of automated retaliation, how do you think he would have judged this scenario? Would he have regarded it as a natural, if extreme, extrapolation of his own theory, or would he have pointed to some disanalogy between human and machine commitment that blocks the extrapolation? And do any of the stabilizing devices he proposed for human conflict, such as focal points or open communication, have plausible analogues that could defuse a commitment race between AI systems?
