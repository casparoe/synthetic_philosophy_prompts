Over the past few months I've been slowly working through Kant's Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals on my own time. I never studied philosophy formally - I picked up a used copy after a friend kept recommending it - but one passage has stuck with me more than any other. Kant describes a naturally warm-hearted, cheerful person who helps others simply because doing so brings them joy, and says that however right and amiable this kind of help is, it has no genuine moral worth. He then has us imagine that same person's sympathy extinguished by grief, so that they no longer feel anything for other people at all, yet they force themselves to help anyway, purely because they recognize it as their duty - and only then, he says, does the act finally acquire real moral worth. I brought this up with a friend who studied philosophy in college, and she told me Kant "doesn't mean it quite that literally," but when I pressed her on what he means instead, she couldn't really say.

As far as I can tell, this means natural warmth and good cheer actually count against a person on Kant's view, since they crowd out the one thing that supposedly makes an action good in the first place. So if I have two friends who both spend every Saturday volunteering at a shelter - one because she loves the people there and couldn't imagine doing anything else, the other who feels nothing for them but drags herself there anyway because she believes it's the right thing to do - Kant seems committed to saying that only the second friend's action has any moral worth, and that the first friend would become more moral, not less, if she lost her capacity for warmth altogether. Is that really the implication of his view? And if so, doesn't it follow that the most virtuous person imaginable would be someone who has trained away all feeling for the people they help, so that absolutely nothing they do for others comes from anything but cold duty?
