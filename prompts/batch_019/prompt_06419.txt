Hempel's own fix for the raven paradox is to just bite the bullet: a white shoe really does confirm 'all ravens are black' (if only by a tiny amount), and the reason that feels wrong is that we're smuggling in background knowledge about how many more non-black things there are than ravens -- a psychological illusion, not a real logical problem with Nicod's criterion. I.J. Good later cooked up a scenario where background knowledge is arranged so that observing an actual black raven *lowers* your credence in 'all ravens are black.' Argue that this counterexample is fatal to Hempels diagnosis -- i.e. that its not just the size of the confirmation an instance provides that depends on background assumptions, but potentially the sign, even for ravens themselves, which is more than his psychological-illusion story can absorb.
