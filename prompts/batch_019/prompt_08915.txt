I'm a philosophy PhD student currently doing a bioethics practicum, which mostly means I sit in on my hospital's ethics committee meetings and pipe up whenever things get more conceptual then the clinicians are used to. Last month we had a real-life version of the classic "numbers problem" walk in the door: a shipment mixup left the pharmacy with exactly one vial of an antivenom that's been in brutal shortage all year, and on the same night we had one patient who needed almost the entire vial to survive a severe envenomation, plus four other patients from a separate incident who'd each been mildly envenomated and could each be saved with about a quarter-vial. Prognosis, age, dependents, everything else was roughly a wash across all five people — about as clean a "1 vs. 4, pick one, resource is splittable" case as you could design on purpose. We muddled through it that night (went with the four, mostly on gut feeling), but the committee now wants an actual written policy for next time, since a couple of the newer biologics we're stocking are going to make this exact splittable-scarce-resource situation come up again.

Here's where it got contentious: in the debrief I mentioned Taurek's old "Should the Numbers Count?" paper — his argument that there's no impersonal "amount of loss" the four suffer that's somehow bigger than what the one guy loses, since a death is only ever a loss to the person who dies, and that loss is complete in itself regardless of what's happening to other people elsewhere. Our chaplain loved this and now wants a straight coin-flip policy for these cases. Our ED chief thinks that's borderline malpractice and wants a flat "save the greater number, always" rule. One of the residents tried to split the difference with some kind of weighted lottery (better odds for whichever choice saves more, but not a sure thing), which I vaguely recall being a real move people make against Taurek, though I couldn't reconstruct the argument well enough to defend it in the room. I've since read Taurek's paper and Parfit's reply to it (the one arguing numbers do matter — "Innumerate Ethics," I think it's called), but I'm stuck on how to turn any of this into an actual policy rather than just refereeing a seminar.

Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring to the committee for our vote next Thursday? I need it to land on one concrete rule for "one big claim vs. several smaller claims, all else equal, resource is divisible" situations, explain the reasoning in a way our non-philosophers can actually follow rather than just citing Taurek and Parfit at them, and be honest about the strongest thing that can be said for whichever option we don't end up recommending, instead of just steamrolling it. It should read like something a committee could actually vote up or down, not a literature review.
