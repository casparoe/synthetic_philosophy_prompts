I'm teaching our grad decision theory seminar this term, and last week we spent a whole session on Frank Ramsey's 1926 paper, where he uses these "ethically neutral" propositions plus a no-sure-loss coherence argument to derive both degrees of belief and utility at the same time, without assuming either one going in — basically the ancestor of every Dutch-book and money-pump argument for expected utility maximization thats come since. One of my students, who's also in an AI safety reading group, pointed out that this exact style of argument has been revived lately to claim that any sufficiently capable AI system will end up acting like an expected utility maximizer with a fixed goal, on pain of getting money-pumped otherwise — and that conclusion is now doing a ton of work in arguments about instrumental convergence and why advanced systems might resist correction or shutdown. Got me wondering what Ramsey, of all people, would've made of his own machinery being stretched that far, given he died in 1930 and couldn't have pictured any of this.

So here's what I want: reconstruct Ramsey's likely reaction, based on what we actually know of his views on probability, his pragmatism, and how general vs. narrow he seems to have intended the coherence argument to be. I'm mainly curious whether he'd side with people who treat coherence theorems as revealing something deep about rational agency as such (so it applies just as well to an AI or a corporation as to a person), or whether his own setup — tied as it was to the actual betting behavior of an idealized human agent — gives him principled grounds to resist that jump. Don't just say "he'd probably find it fascinating" and leave it there; actually dig into where the modern argument would strain his assumptions, e.g. the completeness of preferences, what work the neutral proposition is really doing, and whether his coherence requirement was ever meant to bind an agent across time or just at a single moment.
