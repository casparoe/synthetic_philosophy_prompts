So I'm in a reading group this term working through Lewis's *Convention* (1969) alongside some of the secondary lit (I think it's Rescorla's SEP entry, plus some Skyrms on signalling conventions), and I keep getting stuck on how far the framework actually travels to internet-age cases. The one I can't let go of is algospeak — you know, the way TikTok/IG users started saying "unalive" instead of kill/suicide, "seggs" instead of sex, "corn" instead of porn, specifically to dodge content-moderation classifiers. Lewis died in 2001, obviously years before any of this existed, but his apparatus (recurring coordination problems, arbitrary equilibria sustained by common knowledge and precedent, the requirement that some other regularity could just as well have done the job) is precise enough that there should definately be a real fact about how he'd have to sort this case, not just vibes. It also feels like fair game for him specifically, since this is exactly the sort of vocabulary-innovation case his "Languages and Language" picture of linguistic convention is supposed to cover.

Here's what's actually bugging me. First, unlike his paradigm examples — which side of the road to drive on, where you meet a friend if you get seperated in a department store — algospeak has an adversary built into the situation: the whole point of the regularity is to beat a classifier that's actively trying to detect and kill it off, and once it succeeds the population has to re-coordinate on a new term. Does that adversarial, constantly-churning element break the "recurring situation with a stable solution" picture Lewis needs, or is the cleanest move to say each new term is really a convention solving a slightly different recurring situation, since the classifier's behavior is now part of what defines the situation? Second, tons of people use "unalive" now as generic edgy slang totally divorced from any awareness that it started as a moderation workaround — so does Lewis's common knowledge condition require people to know *why* the regularity is in everyone's interest, or only that it holds and that everyone expects everyone else to conform, in which case the original rationale getting forgotten by half the userbase wouldn't actually matter to whether it's still "his" kind of convention?

I'd love your best reconstruction of how Lewis, using strictly his own definitions and not some vibes-based extension of them, would sort out whether algospeak counts as a genuine convention, a degenerate/borderline case, or something his machinery just wasn't built to handle — and if it's the latter, what you think he'd need to bolt on, or whether Millikan's or Skyrms's alternative pictures already do the job better.
