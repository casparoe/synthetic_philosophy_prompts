Here is the question as she posed it:

"IRL is famously underdetermined: many reward functions rationalize the same observed (optimal) behavior, with the constant/all-zero reward being a degenerate example that rationalizes every policy whatsoever.
(a) Explain why this non-identifiability arises, and how, if at all, it relates to the policy-invariance-under-reward-shaping result of Ng, Harada, and Russell (1999).
(b) Ziebart et al.'s (2008) Maximum Entropy IRL is often described as 'resolving' this ambiguity. Explain the principle behind the maximum-entropy approach, and evaluate whether 'resolve' is really the right word for what it accomplishes.
(c) In a sentence or two, explain how Cooperative IRL / assistance games (Hadfield-Menell et al., 2016) reframe the learning problem, and why this reframing is sometimes claimed to change the identifiability picture."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"(a) The non-identifiability comes from the fact that IRL only observes behavior, and optimality is a very weak constraint: lots of reward functions can make the same policy come out on top, and the constant reward is the extreme case where every policy is (trivially) optimal because there's nothing left to distinguish between them. This is really the same phenomenon that Ng, Harada, and Russell describe in their shaping paper: they show that transforming a reward by adding γΦ(s') − Φ(s) for any potential function Φ leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and that's exactly why a reward can be driven all the way down to zero without changing what counts as optimal — the shaping theorem is the general mechanism, and the degenerate zero-reward solution is just one instance of it.

(b) MaxEnt IRL assumes the demonstrator picks trajectories with probability proportional to the exponential of their reward, and then, among all the distributions consistent with the demonstrated feature counts, picks the one with maximum entropy, i.e., the one that assumes the least beyond what the data requires. Because that maximum-entropy distribution is unique for a given feature set, MaxEnt IRL fully resolves the identifiability problem: it isn't just picking any reward consistent with the data, it is recovering the actual reward that generated the demonstrations, which is part of why it has become the standard baseline nearly everyone compares against.

(c) CIRL turns the problem into a two-player game in which a human who knows the reward and a robot who doesn't both act to optimize that same, shared reward, rather than having the robot passively fit a reward function to a fixed batch of demonstrations after the fact. Because the human now has an incentive to teach — to act so as to reveal the reward faster, even at some short-term cost — rather than simply behave as if unobserved, some have argued that the resulting behavior can pin down the reward more effectively than passive demonstration alone, though this doesn't make the reward fully identifiable in general either."
