After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent the last few years of my retirement reading my way into decision theory. I have been asked to give the eight-minute opening remarks at a workshop on decision-making under deep uncertainty: half the audience are physical scientists like my former self, trained to attach a probability to almost anything, and half are decision theorists who take the Ellsberg paradox, and the Gilboa-Schmeidler models built on it, to show that ambiguity about the probabilities themselves is a distinct, rational object of aversion -- not a bias to be corrected. Two of the keynote speakers will spend the following two days arguing opposite sides of exactly that question, so my remarks cannot tip my hand, or theirs. Please write them: they should use the urn experiment itself to show each half of the room why the other takes the question so seriously, open with the one decision in my own career I still cannot justify with a number, and close on something truer than a plea for interdisciplinary goodwill.
