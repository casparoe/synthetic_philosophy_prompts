My father has advanced Alzheimer's and has lived at Brookhaven Memory Care for about a year. Two months ago, largely because of what happened to him, I agreed to join the facility's newly formed Resident Technology & Ethics Committee as the family representative. Brookhaven recently licensed software that the vendor markets as using "inverse reinforcement learning" to build each resident's individual preference profile: rather than asking residents what they want, which most can no longer reliably do, it infers what the company calls a reward function from sensors, care logs, and staff notes — what a resident moves toward or away from, how they behave around particular foods, routines, or people — and then generates care recommendations from that inferred function. Two months ago my father developed pneumonia, and the system recommended against hospital transfer, citing his agitation during a hospitalization the previous year as evidence that he now prefers comfort care over aggressive treatment. That directly contradicts the advance directive he signed a decade ago, well before his diagnosis, explicitly requesting aggressive treatment of any reversible condition. His physician sent him to the hospital anyway; he recovered; and the case is what pushed Brookhaven to convene the committee I now sit on.

We meet again in twelve days to vote on the system's future, and I've been asked to circulate a short discussion memo beforehand, which is where I'd like your help. I'm not a specialist in machine learning or bioethics — I run a small accounting practice — so I've been doing some reading on my own. As I understand it, the problem here isn't just that the software might be "wrong" in some ordinary sense: inferring a reward function from behavior is inherently underdetermined, and multiple, even opposing, preference profiles can rationalize the same observed behavior once you stop assuming that the behavior itself is a clear-headed, rational response. For someone deep in dementia — where resistance can reflect pain, fear, medication, or confusion rather than considered preference — you mostly have to stop assuming exactly that. At the same time, I don't think the honest answer is simply to distrust the machine: the strongest argument for the vendor's approach, as far as I can tell, is that a resident's current behavior may be the only remaining window onto their present state at all, and that treating a directive signed years before the disease as permanently binding — what I gather bioethicists writing after Dworkin call "precedent autonomy" — risks freezing someone into a former self's decisions no matter how much their experience has since changed, which is more or less the objection critics such as Dresser have raised against Dworkin's own view.

Could you draft the memo I'll bring to the committee? I'd like it to recommend one concrete course of action — leaving the system's authority as it is, restricting it to low-stakes and reversible decisions while requiring documented advance-directive or family consultation for anything irreversible, or suspending it pending an independent audit — together with a specific timeframe for revisiting the decision, and I need the reasoning to actually engage with the strongest case for trusting the behavioral inference over the old directive rather than resolve into caution by default. Roughly half the committee already dismisses the software as "black-box nonsense" on reflex, and I'd rather not hand them a memo whose argument only works if you already agree with them.
