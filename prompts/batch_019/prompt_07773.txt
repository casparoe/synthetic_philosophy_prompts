And here's Marcus's answer, word for word:

"Pascal's Mugging is a thought experiment where a random stranger stops you on the street and says something like: give me five dollars or I'll use my magic powers from outside the Matrix to kill 3^^^3 people (that's using special up-arrow notation, for numbers so big that regular exponents can't even write them down). Even if you think theres only like a one-in-a-trillion-trillion chance he's telling the truth, the number of people involved is so unbelievably huge that the expected disutility of refusing still outweighs the tiny cost of just paying him. So a strict expected-utility maximizer basically has to hand over the cash, which seems crazy — and that's the problem, it looks like EU maximization can be hijacked by literally anyone willing to name a big enough number, no matter how sketchy their claim is.

It's directly related to Pascal's Wager — in fact Pascal originally set up the Mugging scenario himself as a kind of dark flipside to his own Wager argument, basically to show how the same infinite-stakes-beats-tiny-probability logic he used to argue people should believe in God could just as easily get hijacked by a con man instead, which is part of why his name ended up attached to both of them. In the Wager, remember, he said even a small chance God exists means you should believe, since the payoff (heaven) is infinite. The Mugging just swaps out God for a random guy on the street, but the underlying structure is identical.

The most serious fix philosophers have proposed is to just assign a probability of exactly zero to claims like the mugger's. Since anything times zero is zero, that instantly kills the expected-value calculation no matter how big a number he threatens you with, so your never actually on the hook for paying him. Basically you treat 'a guy has magic powers from outside the simulation' the same way you'd treat any other logically impossible claim, like a square circle."
