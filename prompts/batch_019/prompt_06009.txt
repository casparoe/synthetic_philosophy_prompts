My aunt Marguerite is retiring this spring after forty-one years as chief book critic for a national paper, and I've been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. I was one of her fiction students long before I published my own novels, and her workshops ran on the same rule as her reviews: she would never simply tell you that a book, or a draft, was good. Among her colleagues this is recognized as a real position and not just a mannerism — a settled pessimism about aesthetic testimony, the view that no critic's verdict, however trusted, can do for a reader the work of that reader's own acquaintance with the page. Half the room will be critics and former students who will catch exactly what's being honored; the other half are relatives and old friends who think of her simply as a woman of marvelous taste, and who will expect me to just say so. Write the five-minute toast: it needs to honor forty-one years of exacting judgment without ever asking the room to take her word for anything, and it should turn on the one running joke among her students — that she filed a thousand rapturous reviews and never once used the word "good" — so that it lands as affectionate rather than as a lecture.
