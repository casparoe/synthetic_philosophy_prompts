A friend and I got into a debate over dinner recently about ethical consumerism, and it ended in an unusual arrangement. She has long argued that factory farming is one of the great moral failures of our time, but she has never had any objection to fast fashion and buys from whichever brand is cheapest. I take the opposite view: I think buying clothes made in exploitative sweatshops is seriously wrong, but I see nothing objectionable about eating meat. Partly in jest, we proposed a deal: I would give up fast fashion entirely, and she would go vegetarian, even though neither of us budged an inch on the issue the other cares about. Neither of us thinks the other's cause is remotely as pressing as our own, and neither of us feels the slightest twinge of conscience about the practice we are each giving up.

On reflection, the arrangement nags at me. If my friend genuinely believes eating meat is morally neutral, why should she reorganize her diet just because I care about factory farming—isn't that like paying someone in a currency she thinks is worthless? And yet the deal does seem to produce something real: less factory-farmed meat gets eaten and fewer sweatshop garments get bought than if each of us had simply gone on acting only according to our own convictions. My roommate, who overheard the whole negotiation, insists it's a sham: trading away a practice you don't think matters in order to get someone else to act on a practice you do think matters is moral bribery, not moral progress, in his view. I'm not sure he's wrong, but I'm not sure he's right either. Is there something genuinely legitimate happening in an arrangement like this, or is my roommate correct that we've just dressed up a bribe as an ethical stance?
