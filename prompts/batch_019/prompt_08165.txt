I never studied physics beyond high school, and my background is in something else entirely, but over the last year or so I've spent a lot of evenings reading everything I can find, mostly on forums and physics blogs, about the "Boltzmann brain" problem in cosmology. As I understand it, the worry is that in a universe that lasts forever and eventually settles into something close to equilibrium (the "heat death" that our accelerating, dark-energy-dominated universe seems to be headed for), a single, momentarily conscious brain assembling itself out of a random fluctuation, complete with false memories of a whole life and a whole universe, is statistically far more likely than an entire ordered cosmos like ours arising the ordinary way. So a "typical" observer in such a universe should apparently expect to be exactly that kind of short-lived fluctuation rather than the product of some thirteen billion years of real cosmic history.

What I hadn't appreciated until I went further down the rabbit hole is how much of this Ludwig Boltzmann had already half-invented himself, decades before anyone talked about dark energy or eternal inflation. Defending his statistical account of the second law against Zermelo's objection that Poincaré recurrence should eventually undo any entropy increase, Boltzmann floated the idea, which he credited to his assistant Schütz, that our observable universe might simply be a rare, large-scale statistical fluctuation inside a far larger system that spends almost all of its time at equilibrium. What he could not have known is Eddington's 1931 rejoinder that a fluctuation large enough to produce a whole ordered universe should be unimaginably rarer than one just large enough to produce a single brain, nor the later cosmological picture — de Sitter vacuums, eternal inflation, an everlasting future of empty accelerating space — that turned his own idea into a pointed argument about solitary fluctuating brains outnumbering real observers, nor the anthropic style of reasoning by which physicists now move from "such fluctuations happen somewhere, eventually" to "you personally should expect to be one."

Given what is actually on record about how Boltzmann thought about probability, as something grounded in counting the physical microstates of real dynamical systems rather than in reasoning about which observer you happen to be, and given how combative he could be, in his own lifetime, toward anything that smelled of ungrounded philosophizing (his running dispute with Mach and the energeticists over whether atoms were even real comes to mind), I would like your best-supported guess about how he would have reacted if some time-travelling colleague had walked him through the full modern Boltzmann-brain argument. Would he most likely have recognized it as simply his own reasoning carried one step further, or is there a specific move in the modern version, perhaps the anthropic step itself, that his actual physics and temperament suggest he would have resisted? I would rather have an answer carefully tied to his documented views than a general statement that he would have found modern cosmology astonishing.
