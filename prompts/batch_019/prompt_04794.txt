I've been rereading Frederick Douglass's 1860 Glasgow speech on the Constitution, where he breaks with the Garrisonians' view that the document is irredeemably pro-slavery, arguing instead that it must be interpreted according to its actual text rather than any secret intentions of the framers. He makes much of the fact that the unamended Constitution never uses the words "slave" or "slavery," speaking only of "persons," and argues further that wherever the text is genuinely ambiguous, it should be construed in favor of liberty rather than bondage. This looks like a forerunner of modern textualism — the sort of reasoning Gorsuch used in Bostock v. Clayton County to read Title VII's ban on sex discrimination as covering sexual orientation and gender identity, whatever Congress actually expected in 1964 — except that most textualists today insist that ambiguity should be resolved by ordinary linguistic and legal convention, not by asking which reading is morally preferable. Would Douglass have recognized his own method in Gorsuch's opinion, or is his liberty-favoring canon doing something that a consistent textualist would actually have to reject?
