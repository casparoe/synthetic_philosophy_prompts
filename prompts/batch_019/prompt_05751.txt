I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent about three decades on particle detector data, lots of Bayesian statistics along the way), and since retiring I've gotten pretty into philosophy, especially anything with a formal or mathematical backbone -- decision theory, social choice theory, that sort of thing. Lately I've been working through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: if individual preferences and the "social" preference both satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and everyone being indifferent between two lotteries implies society is indifferent too, then social utility comes out as a weighted sum of individual utilities. Fine, I followed that part, and I get why people treat it as a formal argument for utilitiarianism of some sort.

What's tripping me up is a follow-on issue I stumbled into (I think associated with names like Hylland and Zeckhauser, and later Mongin, though I could easily be misremembering who did what) about what happens once you drop the assumption that everyone -- individuals and the social planner alike -- shares the same probabilities over the relevant states of the world. Apparently if you insist on respecting "ex ante" Pareto (going along with whatever gamble everyone prefers by their own lights, using their own beliefs) you can end up forced into an option that every single person, once the uncertainty resolves, would say left them worse off than the alternative would have -- so the "unanimity" that justified the choice was in some sense fake, generated by people disagreeing about the facts rather than agreeing about what's good. Could you walk me through the mechanics of how that happens, ideally with a bare-bones example (two people, two states, nothing fancier than that) so I can actually see where the ex-ante and ex-post rankings come apart? And then I'd love to hear your take on what this is supposed to show about the bigger picture -- does it mean Harsanyi's theorem only really delivers an argument for utilitarianism when beliefs happen to be shared, or is there some other way of rescuing the result that I'm not aware of?
