I run a small business importing kitchen appliances, and the latest round of tariffs has cut into our margins deeply enough that I've started reading up on the economic theory behind the "trade deficits are a national loss" rhetoric I keep hearing from trade-policy commentators. That reading led me back to Hume's essays "Of Money," "Of the Balance of Trade," and "Of the Jealousy of Trade," since the mercantilist reasoning he was refuting sounds remarkably like the case being made for tariffs today.

Write an essay assessing whether Hume's arguments—especially the price-specie-flow mechanism and the "jealousy of trade" critique—still defeat mercantilist-style justifications for tariffs once you drop his background assumption of a specie-based international currency and allow for fiat money, floating exchange rates, and highly mobile capital. Engage seriously with the complication Hume himself raises in "Of Money": that a monetary inflow is non-neutral in the short run and can stimulate output before prices catch up, which might give modern protectionists an opening he never fully closed. Weigh the strongest reconstruction of the protectionist case against this Humean framework, and end with a definite verdict—does his reasoning go through essentially unchanged, does it need significant modification, or is it actually undercut by these modern conditions?
