After a forty-year career as a research chemist, I have spent my retirement reading moral philosophy, and I keep finding its puzzles surfacing in unexpected corners of ordinary institutional life — including on the ethics and conduct committee of my professional society, where I still serve. We have received a formal referral against a member for publishing a widely cited report on the safety of an industrial solvent without disclosing that the work was funded by the solvent's manufacturer; our code of conduct leaves no doubt that this is a violation. The referral was brought by our society's president, who uncovered the lapse during a routine audit of members' funding disclosures. It has since come to light that the president co-authored a similarly funded, similarly undisclosed safety report of his own five years ago — a fact he does not dispute, but which was never pursued at the time because it surfaced after our two-year complaint window had closed. The accused member's lawyer now argues that the matter should be dropped, not because the charge is false, but because the person who brought it has no standing to press it. The committee has split between those who consider this an irrelevant deflection that changes nothing about what our member did, and those who worry that proceeding while ignoring the president's own history would make the society complicit in exactly the double standard our code exists to prevent. Please draft the short memo I will circulate before our next meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action for the referral, with a time frame, and it should engage seriously with whichever side of the split your recommendation doesn't land on, rather than treating it as a rhetorical trick.
