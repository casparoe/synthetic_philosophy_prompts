A genetic counselor recently walked me through a polygenic risk score for a condition that runs in my family: population data supply a baseline lifetime risk, and my score is meant to shift my personal estimate above or below that baseline. Trying to think clearly about how to combine the two figures, I have been reading about David Lewis's Principal Principle, which holds that one's credence in an outcome should simply equal its objective chance, provided one holds no "inadmissible" evidence. What I cannot work out is whether my score counts as inadmissible evidence relative to the population baseline — precisely the sort of thing licensed to pull credence away from a bare chance — or whether its existence instead shows that the baseline was never the right chance for someone like me to defer to, with some finer-grained, score-conditional chance being the real target all along. Lewis's own gloss on admissibility, via the distinction between "historical" and "theoretical" evidence, does not obviously settle which is going on. Could you explain why this distinction is so difficult to draw precisely in a case like mine, whether the worry is more than terminological, and whether later reformulations — the so-called New Principle, for instance — actually dissolve the problem or merely relocate it?
