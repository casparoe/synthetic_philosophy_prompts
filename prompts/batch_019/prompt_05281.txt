"The situationist critique comes from philosophers like John Doris and Gilbert Harman, who argue that Aristotle's idea of virtue doesn't hold up against real evidence about how people behave. Aristotle thought a virtue like honesty or courage is a stable trait (a 'hexis') built through habit that shows up consistently no matter the situation. Situationists point to experiments like Zimbardo's obedience study, where ordinary participants were told by an authority figure to keep giving a 'learner' electric shocks, and about 65% of them went all the way to the highest voltage even though the learner was screaming in pain. Since the same people who'd normally call themselves kind went along with something that extreme just because a guy in a lab coat told them to, this shows behavior is driven way more by the situation than by fixed character.

Doris and Harman are basically making the same argument here: since there's no good evidence for stable, cross-situational traits, we should just stop talking about character altogether and admit 'virtue' isn't a real psychological property.

A virtue ethicist could respond by pointing out that phronesis (practical wisdom) is exactly what lets a genuinely virtuous person respond appropriately to different situations, so being sensitive to context is actually built into virtue, not evidence against it."
