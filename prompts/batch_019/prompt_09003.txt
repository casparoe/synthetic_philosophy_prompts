I recently joined my department's tenure-review committee, and at our very first case this year we ran into something unsettling. Under our bylaws, tenure can be granted only if a candidate is judged adequate on each of two independent dimensions — research output and teaching effectiveness — and a majority of the committee judged the candidate adequate on each dimension when we voted on them separately. Yet when we instead polled ourselves directly on the bottom-line question of whether to grant tenure, a majority voted no. Our chair pointed out that we had walked into a textbook instance of the discursive dilemma from judgment-aggregation theory, and that our choice between deciding issue-by-issue and polling the bottom line directly is not neutral procedural housekeeping, since it can single-handedly determine the outcome. I have been asked to draft a standing rule for future cases, so please argue that committees like ours should commit in advance to the premise-based (issue-by-issue) procedure rather than the conclusion-based one, directly addressing the strongest objection to this recommendation: that it can force through a verdict that not one member of the committee, taken individually, actually endorses.
