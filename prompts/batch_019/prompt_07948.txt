I'm plotting a novel whose central device is a machine that produces an exact duplicate of a person—identical in every physical detail, every memory, every disposition, right down to the way she'd finish a sentence—and before I commit to how the key scene plays out, I want to be sure I understand what's actually at stake philosophically. Max Black's classic thought experiment of two perfectly similar iron spheres, alone in an otherwise empty and symmetrical universe, is usually taken to refute Leibniz's principle that no two distinct things can share every property, since the spheres are said to share all of their properties, including their relations to one another, and yet there are still supposed to be two of them. But spheres have no point of view, so Black's case never has to confront what happens once the indiscernible items are conscious. Construct a comparable scenario involving two such duplicated people rather than spheres, and use it to argue whether the fact that each would experience herself as "this one, not that one" gives any real ground for treating them as distinct individuals, or whether that feeling is just Black's problem recurring in first-person disguise.
