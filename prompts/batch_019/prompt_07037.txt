Over dinner last week, a friend and I found ourselves in a long, unresolved argument about theories of vagueness that I still haven't let go of. She defended supervaluationism as the more disciplined view: nothing in the meaning of a predicate like "heap" privileges any one of the many admissible ways of drawing a sharp cutoff over the others, so sentences about borderline heaps come out neither true nor false, and we are spared any commitment to a fact of the matter that nobody could ever know. I pushed back with the standard epistemicist line that this "discipline" evaporates once you look at higher-order vagueness: if "definitely a heap" is itself vague, supervaluationism must either fix a determinate class of admissible precisifications (which just relocates the sharp cutoff to the metalanguage) or iterate its machinery indefinitely, at which point it is unclear the view has explained what makes a predicate vague to begin with. Neither of us budged. Write an essay that weighs epistemicism against supervaluationism as theories of vagueness, using the problem of higher-order vagueness as the central test case, and that reaches and defends a definite verdict about which view handles it better, rather than ending on a split-the-difference note.
