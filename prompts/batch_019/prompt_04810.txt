So I'm a retired astronomer (thirty-odd years doing radio astronomy before I hung up my hat) and these last couple of years I've gotten completely hooked on philosophy, especially anything touching on rational choice and fairness. A few old colleagues and I started an informal reading group, and we're currently working through a book on theories of distributive justice that has a section contrasting the Nash bargaining solution with the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution as two rival formal models of what a "fair" bargain should look like. Honestly it hit close to home, because it reminded me of all the fights on our old time-allocation committee over how to split telescope time between competing proposals -- felt like a real bargaining problem dressed up as a scheduling meeting.

I can follow the bare formalism fine: Nash maximizes the product of the two parties' utility gains over the disagreement point, while Kalai-Smorodinsky instead picks the point where the Pareto frontier crosses the straight line from the disagreement point to the "ideal point" (each party's best possible payoff). What I'm stuck on is the intuition for why these two actually come apart in practice, and whats really at stake philosophically. The book says it all comes down to Nash relying on independence of irrelevant alternatives, while Kalai and Smorodinsky trade that axiom in for a monotonicity condition -- and (if I'm remembering right) that there's a classic example where expanding the bargaining set in a way that looks like a pure improvement for one player still leaves that player no better off under Nash's solution, which seems like a real strike against IIA as a fairness requirement. Could you unpack this for me: mechanically, why does that axiom swap change the answer, and would you say the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution is genuinely a more defensible notion of a "fair" bargain, or does giving up IIA just trade one set of weird cases for another? I definately want more than a "here are the two axioms that differ" answer -- I want to actually understand why a person should care, philosophically, about this particular swap.
