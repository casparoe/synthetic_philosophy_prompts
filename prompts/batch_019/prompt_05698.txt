I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel that follows a grandmother, her daughter, and her granddaughter across three generations, and the back half hinges on a scene where the granddaughter comes home from university abroad feeling "hollowed out" by all that time away from the people who raised her — like she's actually less of a person for it, not just lonelier or homesick. Her grandmothers side of the family is Ghanaian, and I wanted her worldview to be grounded in something more specific than the greeting-card version of ubuntu ("a person is a person through other persons" etc.), so I went down a rabbit hole reading actual African philosophy and got stuck on a disagreement I can't fully settle in my own head, between Ifeanyi Menkiti and Kwame Gyekye.

As I understand it, Menkiti takes the genuinely radical position that personhood isn't a fixed fact you're simply born with, but something processual and normative that you achieve through stages of incorporation into community — rites of passage, moral conduct, and so on — to the point that an infant, or even an adult who behaves badly enough, could count as less of a person than a respected elder. Gyekye thinks this goes too far and stakes out what people call "moderate communitarianism" instead: community obviously shapes and matters alot to who we are, but if personhood is constituted by the community all the way down, he doesn't see how you could explain an individual ever meaningfully criticizing or reforming the very community that supposedly makes them who they are, and he worries it hands philosophical cover to political projects that steamroll individual dissent in the name of the collective. I brought this up with my writing group and someone just said "isn't this basically semantics", which I don't think is right, but I couldn't articulate why on the spot.

So could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this, rather than just surveying both camps? Weigh Menkiti's stronger relational view against Gyekye's moderate one and tell me who has the better of the argument — or, if it's not a clean win for either, exactly where the truth lands between them and why. Please back the verdict up rather than just asserting it; I want to use whichever position is actually more defensible to decide whether the grandmother's big speech to her granddaughter should land as real wisdom, or as something the novel is quietly inviting the reader to push back on.
