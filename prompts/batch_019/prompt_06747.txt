A thread on a rationalist-adjacent blog I follow recently cited Hobbes's remark that reason "is nothing but reckoning" as an early, pre-formal statement of the idea that thinking just is computation, and that sent me on a fairly extensive detour into Hobbes's actual relationship to mathematics. The biographical detail that stuck with me is the story, from Aubrey's Brief Lives, of Hobbes first opening a copy of Euclid's Elements at around forty, in a gentleman's library, landing on the Pythagorean theorem, and being so struck by the way its proof forced him back through a chain of earlier propositions to something self-evident that he fell in love with geometry on the spot. That is obviously also the model behind Leviathan: Part I builds up definitions of man, natural right, liberty, covenant, and commonwealth, and the political conclusions are presented as following from those definitions in more or less the way theorems follow from Euclid's postulates.

What I want to check is an inference I've been drawing from this, since I used it in an argument with a friend recently and am no longer fully confident it holds up. My friend's objection to Hobbes was the standard one: that his picture of human beings as fearful, competitive glory-seekers in the state of nature is too bleak to be believable. I replied that this objection doesn't actually engage the argument, because in a genuinely deductive system of this kind, the starting definitions function as postulates rather than as empirical hypotheses; you don't refute a theorem by disliking one of its postulates, you have to locate an actual error in the derivation. It seemed to follow that the entire philosophical weight of assessing Leviathan has to fall on finding a flaw somewhere in the deduction itself, and that objecting to the realism of Hobbes's psychology, by itself, simply misses where the argument's vulnerability would have to be, in the same way that objecting to the Pythagorean theorem because one dislikes right triangles misses the point.

Is that inference actually sound? I ask partly because of a fact I only learned afterward, which I can't quite fit into the picture: Hobbes spent a large part of his later life in an increasingly public and increasingly one-sided dispute with the mathematician John Wallis over a quadrature of the circle that Hobbes had gotten wrong and never conceded, apparently without this ever shaking his confidence that his method was as rigorous as Euclid's. That seems like it should bear on how much weight "Hobbes modeled his method on geometry" can actually carry, though I'm not sure exactly where the disanalogy lies. If the resemblance to Euclid doesn't really license treating Hobbes's political conclusions as possessing anything like geometric necessity, I'd like to understand precisely where the analogy breaks down, and correspondingly where a critic of Leviathan's argument should actually be aiming.
