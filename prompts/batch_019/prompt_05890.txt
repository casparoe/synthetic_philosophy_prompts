After forty-one years running an experimental particle-astrophysics lab, I retired in 2021 and have spent much of the time since working through the philosophy reading lists my daughter, now a postdoc in epistemology, keeps sending me "for fun." Last month I finally reached the literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia — Christensen's work on what it is rational to do when new evidence bears not on your original question but on the reliability of your own reasoning about it, and Sophie Horowitz's discussion of the strange, apparently sometimes-rational predicament of believing P while simultaneously believing you have no business believing P. I found the whole discussion oddly familiar, because it is more or less exactly what happened to my oldest friend in the field, Eleanor, who retires next month after forty-three years.

Eleanor built her early reputation on a faint, persistent excess in her detector's data, which she spent a decade defending against colleagues convinced it was noise. Four years ago, a much larger and better-calibrated collaboration ran the definitive version of her experiment and found nothing: the effect she had staked her name on was, in all likelihood, an artifact of instrumentation a generation older than what anyone uses now. What has stayed with me is not that she accepted this — good scientists eventually do — but what she said, unprompted, at the seminar where she conceded the point: that she still could not help feeling, in her gut, that the effect was real, even as she agreed she now had no right to believe it. I do not think I have ever heard a working scientist state the gap between first-order conviction and higher-order evidence so plainly, and without a philosopher having supplied her the vocabulary for it.

I have been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. Roughly half the room will be physicists who lived through the whole episode and will know exactly what I am alluding to; the other half will be family and old friends who know only that "Eleanor was wrong about something once, and was very gracious about it" — which is not really what happened, and undersells her considerably. I do not want to deliver a philosophy lecture, and I do not want to flatten the story into a bland moral about accepting defeat gracefully, because that erases the part that actually impressed me: that she never stopped feeling the pull of her original belief even as she disowned it. Could you write me a five-to-six-minute toast that uses the real shape of what she went through — the gap between what the evidence told her to believe and what she went on feeling anyway — to say something true, precise, and warm about her, in terms that will not lose either half of the room?
