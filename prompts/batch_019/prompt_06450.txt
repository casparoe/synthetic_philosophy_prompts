A friend and I spent the better part of a long dinner arguing about how to size bets, and the argument ended up running much deeper than either of us expected. The immediate occasion was mundane: a repeated coin-flip game with 60/40 odds in our favor and even-money payouts, and the question of what fraction of a bankroll to wager each round. My friend insisted that the only defensible answer is full Kelly betting — staking exactly the fraction that maximizes expected log wealth — appealing to ergodicity-economics-style reasoning that the quantity that matters to a single person living through one sequence of bets is the time-average growth rate of their own wealth, not some ensemble average across many imagined parallel copies of the same bet. I countered, invoking Paul Samuelson (who considered this whole line of reasoning careless enough to merit a rebuttal written entirely in one-syllable words), that this move privileges one very specific utility function — logarithmic — and dresses it up as uniquely "rational" when ordinary expected-utility theory already tells you everything you need once you specify how risk-averse someone actually is; the almost-sure long-run dominance of Kelly betting, I pointed out, is a fact about limits, not a decision rule for anyone who actually has to stop betting at some finite time. Neither of us budged, and afterward it seemed to me that we had each done a better job attacking the other's position than defending our own.

Could you write a dialogue that does better justice to both sides than we managed? I'd like one character — a committed advocate of Kelly betting and "geometric rationality" more broadly — to make the strongest case for treating growth-rate (geometric-mean) optimization as genuinely privileged rather than just one preference among many: the almost-sure long-run dominance result, the ensemble-average-versus-time-average distinction from ergodicity economics, and perhaps a gesture at why geometric aggregation shows up elsewhere too, such as in logarithmic pooling of probability estimates or the Nash bargaining solution. I'd like the other character — a traditional Bayesian expected-utility theorist — to press the strongest objections available: that no real bettor has access to the infinite long run, that risk of ruin and drawdowns are already handled by choosing a suitably concave utility function without needing any extra "geometric" principle, and that the asymptotic-dominance result, however true, doesn't by itself tell a finite agent what to do before the asymptotics kick in. Please have them actually respond to each other's specific arguments as the exchange develops, rather than each just restating their opening position, and don't let either side clearly win by the end.
