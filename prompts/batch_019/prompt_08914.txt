A friend of mine, who is finishing a doctorate in linguistics, and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about Quine's "gavagai" thought experiment and left without agreeing on anything. Her position was that Quine's indeterminacy of translation thesis is really a kind of magic trick that depends on keeping the field linguist artificially starved of evidence: the moment you let the linguist ask about identity and individuation — whether "this gavagai" counts as "the same gavagai" seen yesterday, how the natives count them, whether the word behaves grammatically like our count nouns or like a mass term such as "water" — the rival translation hypotheses ("rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," "rabbit-fusion," "an instance of rabbithood") stop being evidentially on a par, and ordinary field-linguistic practice can settle the matter the way it settles every other question about a foreign grammar. I argued that this doesn't actually defeat Quine, because it only relocates the indeterminacy one level up: deciding which native particle to translate as "same," "one," or "and" is itself an analytical hypothesis that outruns anything fixed by stimulus meaning, so the very tool used to individuate rabbits from rabbit-parts is exactly as underdetermined as the noun it's meant to disambiguate. Neither of us could produce a knock-down move, and I would like to see the dispute conducted properly rather than the way we left it.

Could you write a dialogue between the two positions? One character should be a committed Quinean who holds that translation remains indeterminate all the way down, even once identity, counting, and individuation are put on the table; the other should be a critic who holds that grammatical evidence of this kind genuinely disciplines translation and is not itself just more stimulus meaning waiting on an analytical hypothesis. Set the scene in the field, moments after the informant has pointed at a rabbit and said "Gavagai!", with the two linguists comparing notes before the animal is even out of sight. I'd like both sides argued at full strength, with no straw men, and I want each speaker to respond to the specific point their interlocutor just made rather than simply restate their own position. Rather than have one side simply win, let the dialogue end with the two of them at least isolating exactly where the disagreement bites — whether the appeal to grammar is a genuine counterexample to indeterminacy, or only a fresh illustration, one level up, of the very phenomenon Quine was describing.
