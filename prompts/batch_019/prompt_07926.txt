A friend and I spent an evening arguing over the rationalist slogan that two honest Bayesians who trust each other's rationality cannot agree to disagree. She takes the result to follow more or less directly from mutual rationality plus common knowledge of each other's opinions. I think it also quietly relies on the two of you having started out with the same prior, and that this common prior assumption is a far more substantive and contestable premise than the "rationality plus common knowledge" framing suggests. Neither of us could settle the matter.

Could you compare the common prior assumption with the assumption that posteriors are common knowledge, as they jointly function in Aumann's agreement theorem? In particular, is the common prior assumption actually entailed by Bayesian rationality, or is it a further commitment (sometimes called the Harsanyi doctrine) that a perfectly rational Bayesian agent could reject without any loss of rationality? And if it is the latter, what does that imply about how much philosophical weight a popularized result like "rational agents cannot agree to disagree" can really bear?
