I ran into something called Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (sometimes called the "utilitarian theorem"), which as far as I understand says: if each individual's preferences and the group's preferences both satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and some Pareto condition holds, then the group's utility function has to be a weighted linear sum of everyone's individual utility functions. A coworker who did a philosophy minor in college told me this is basically the closest thing economists have to a "proof" that utilitarian-style weighted averaging is the mathematically correct way to combine people's preferences, but he was pretty fuzzy on the actual details and I don't fully trust the handful of blog posts I skimmed either.

Could you walk me through the most important implications of this theorem for something like what my team is actually doing? Specifically I care about three things: first, does the theorem actually tell you what the weights on each rater should be, or does it just guarantee that *some* set of positive weights exists and leave the hard part (choosing them) completely open; second, does the theorem quietly assume that different raters' utility scales are comparable to each other in a way we're definitely not guaranteeing just by normalizing everyone's 1-5 ratings the same way; and third, is there some fairness-type consideration in how outcomes get produced (like whether risk ends up spread evenly across raters vs. concentrated on a few) that this framework structurally can't capture no matter how the weights are set.
