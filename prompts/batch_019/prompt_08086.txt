I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but over the winter I read a good chunk of Hume's "Political Discourses" after a long-running argument with a friend from my old trading-systems team about whether Bitcoin counts as "real money" or is just a speculative bubble. What struck me is how concrete these essays are compared to the "Treatise of Human Nature" material I'd half-absorbed from philosophy podcasts: the self-correcting mechanism in "Of the Balance of Trade," where an inflow of gold raises domestic prices until exports stop being competitive and the gold drains back out; the argument in "Of Interest" that low interest rates are a symptom of a commercially rich society with abundant capital rather than of there simply being more coin in circulation; and his open anxiety in "Of Public Credit" that Britain's habit of financing wars through government debt instead of taxes was building toward some kind of reckoning.

None of that maps cleanly onto the world we actually live in, and that's the part I can't work out on my own. Hume never encountered a currency fully detached from gold or silver, a central bank conjuring money electronically to buy trillions in government bonds, or two major trading powers like the US and China running a persistent bilateral trade deficit for decades without the price-specie-flow story ever seeming to kick in and correct it. He also never saw a "trade war" waged through tariffs and export controls rather than plain hoarding of bullion, though his essay "Of the Jealousy of Trade" already rebuts the zero-sum instinct behind that kind of policy, arguing that a nation's own commerce depends on having rich, industrious trading partners rather than impoverished ones. And he certainly never saw anything like Bitcoin, which is an odd case for him because his whole account treats money as a convenient social convention with no value of its own, whereas crypto communities often praise it in almost the opposite terms, as valuable precisely because it doesn't depend on any state's promises.

Could you work out, as rigorously as you can from Hume's actual arguments rather than his general reputation as a champion of commerce, what he would most plausibly say about (a) fiat currency and central-bank quantitative easing, (b) tariff-driven "decoupling" between major trading powers, and (c) cryptocurrency as a non-sovereign form of money? I'd like each conclusion tied back to a specific mechanism or essay rather than an extrapolation along the lines of "Hume liked trade, so he'd approve," and I'd also appreciate honesty about the points where his eighteenth-century framework simply runs out of resources, such that any answer attributed to him would be more ventriloquism than inference.
