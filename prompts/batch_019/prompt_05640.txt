Last night a friend and I got into a long argument about David Lewis's modal realism that neither of us really won. It started because she caught me still turning over a decision I made a couple of years ago, and she offered what she meant as comfort: if genuine modal realism is true, then somewhere in modal space — in a world just as concrete and real as this one, merely disconnected from it — a counterpart of mine chose differently and is doing fine. I objected that this was cold comfort at best, since on Lewis's own picture that counterpart isn't me, just someone similar enough to count as my counterpart in his technical sense, so their good fortune has no more bearing on my situation than a stranger's would. She thought that just showed my sense of "comfort" was confused, not that the metaphysics had failed. We ended up trading the usual moves — I gave her the incredulous stare, she gave me the line about theoretical economy and modality just being quantification over a plurality of worlds — and neither of us budged.

What's stayed with me since is that almost every piece of fiction I can think of that touches "other possible worlds" cheats on exactly the point we were fighting over: it lets characters visit, glimpse, or exchange messages with their otherworldly selves, when Lewis's actual view rules that out completely, since worlds are supposed to be causally and spatiotemporally sealed off from one another with no exceptions whatsoever.

So here is what I'd like from you: a short story, somewhere in the 800–1200 word range, that takes that isolation thesis seriously instead of quietly setting it aside for the sake of plot, and that puts a character in genuine contact — intellectual, not physical — with the counterpart-consolation move we were arguing about: whether believing that a happier (or unhappier) counterpart exists somewhere really should change how you feel about your own life here, given that, by the theory's own lights, that person is someone else entirely. I'd rather you didn't have the story adjudicate our argument for us; let it end with the question still open.
