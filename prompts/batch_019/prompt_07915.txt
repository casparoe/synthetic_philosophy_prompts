I'm taking a philosophy elective this semester, and a unit on George Berkeley has left me stuck on a question I haven't been able to shake. We spent a class on the part of the Introduction to his *Principles* where he attacks Locke's notion of a general idea of a triangle, quoting Locke's own description of an idea that would supposedly have to be "neither oblique nor rectangle, neither equilateral, equicrural, nor scalenon, but all and none of these at once," and then basically challenges the reader to actually form such a thing in their mind. My teacher described this as a broadly nominalist move: on Berkeley's view, there is no real general or abstract object for a general word like "triangle" to name, only particular ideas that we choose to use in a general way. A few classes later we read part of *The Analyst*, where Berkeley goes after Newton's and Leibniz's infinitesimals in the same spirit, mocking the new calculus for depending on quantities that are, in his words, "neither finite... nor yet nothing."

This has made me curious what Berkeley would have thought about mathematics after his own lifetime, since so much of it seems to lean even harder into exactly the sort of thing he found unacceptable. Cantor, for instance, showed that infinite collections come in different sizes and treated them as completed, well-defined objects rather than as endless unfinished processes. More generally, the modern debate between mathematical platonists and nominalists can look like a bigger version of his old argument with Locke: platonists hold that numbers and sets are real, mind-independent objects that don't causally interact with us at all, and that we're entitled to believe in them partly because they turn out to be indispensable to our best physical theories, while nominalists deny that any such objects exist and try to show that all our mathematical talk can be reinterpreted so as not to require them. There's also a well-known objection to platonism that seems almost designed with someone like Berkeley in mind: if these objects can't be perceived and don't cause anything, what could possibly explain how a mind ever comes to know truths about them?

Given that Berkeley combined a strict empiricism about where our ideas are allowed to come from, an open hostility to abstract general ideas, and yet also an idealism in which literally everything real is either a mind or an idea being had by one (including objects nobody happens to be perceiving at the moment, which he keeps in existence through God's continuous perception), where do you think he would actually land if he were confronted with this later debate? Would his instincts push him toward a straightforward nominalism, or perhaps toward treating higher mathematics the way he treated infinitesimals, as a convenient fiction that works without being true? Or might his idealism push him somewhere stranger still, such as holding that numbers and sets are entirely real and objective, but only as ideas permanently present to the mind of God, a position that would not obviously count as nominalism, platonism, or any comfortable compromise between the two? I would appreciate a genuinely reasoned reconstruction of how he would likely have argued, grounded in his actual views, rather than just a guess at which label he would have worn.
