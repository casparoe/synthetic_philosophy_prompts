I've been running a reading group this term with a few of my grad students on formal epistemology and decision theory, and a couple weeks back we spent a session on money-pump/Dutch-book style "coherence arguments" for expected utility theory -- the classic Ramsey/de Finetti stuff, but also the newer use of it in the AI safety literature to argue that any sufficiently capable agent will, on pain of being exploited, end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer with respect to some goal (which is a big chunk of why people worry about "instrumental convergence" and AI systems resisting shutdown). I've been threatening to write something on this for about a year and never have. Anyway, afterwards two of my students, Callum and Noor, kept arguing about it out in the hallway long enough that I eventually just told them I'd think it over and get back to them, wich I never did, so now its your problem.

Here's roughly how it went, as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Callum said the argument is basically decisive: if an agent's preferences are incomplete -- there's some pair of options where it doesn't prefer the first to the second, doesn't prefer the second to the first, and doesn't rate them as equally good either -- then a clever counterparty can always cook up a sequence of trades that leaves the agent strictly worse off than where it started, even though every individual trade looked fine (or at least not objectionable) to the agent at the time it was offered. Since no agent that keeps getting pumped for resources stays capable for long, incompleteness should get trained or selected out of any sufficiently advanced system, which is exactly why we should expect an advanced AI to converge on acting like it has some complete goal -- shutdown-resistance included. (2) Noor answered that this only works if the agent has to evaluate every trade in isolation. An agent can instead adopt a standing policy of never trading away from whatever it currently holds unless the alternative is strictly better, in which case it can stay incomplete forever and never get pumped, because it just declines whichever trade would close the loop back to something no better than its starting point. This is basically "resolute choice," and it's more or less the move some recent AI-safety proposals make to try to get an AI to tolerate being shut down without generally resisting it. (3) Callum replied that this doesn't refute the pump so much as hide from it: refusing a trade that, by the agent's own lights, it has no objection to accepting is not "preserving incompleteness," it's just a rule bolted on from outside to produce the behavior you wanted -- and if any incoherent-looking preference set can be rescued this way, "coherence requires completeness" stops saying anything, since every agent could be patched the same way. (4) Noor said that's not fair: nothing is being overridden, because by definition there's no preference in play for the trade that gets refused -- declining an offer you have no positive reason to accept is just ordinary permissible behaviour under standard decision theory, not a special patch. The only real question is whether such a policy can be specified in advance, for every possible menu of future trades, without it quietly turning into a complete preference ranking in disguise, and she thinks the recent shutdown-problem proposals actually pull that off.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step -- not just who wins overall? I'm especially interested in whether Callum's regress worry in (3) actually sinks Noor's resolute-choice move, or whether (4) genuinely defuses it, and whether the two of them are both missing some more basic distinction that would settle this faster than they're managing to. End with a one-line verdict in the form \verdict{Callum}, \verdict{Noor}, or \verdict{Neither}, for who's got the better of the substantive question of whether incompleteness is a real escape route from coherence arguments.
