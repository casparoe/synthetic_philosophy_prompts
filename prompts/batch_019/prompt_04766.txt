My best friend from undergrad is getting married in three weeks and asked me to do the toast, which I'm excited about but also low-key panicking over, because the room is basically two totally different audiences. Her family and coworkers are lovely, normal people who could not care less about the 18th century, but there's also a cluster of maybe five friends from our old "Atlantic Enlightenment" seminar who will absolutely clock it if I get a reference wrong. (I'm a few years into a History PhD now; she went a different direction after undergrad, but we took that class together sophomore year.)

The relevant backstory: she got weirdly, permanently attached to the chapter of Franklin's autobiography where he lays out his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection," and has kept some version of his 13-virtues chart going in a notebook ever since -- a little grid, one virtue a week, a tally mark every time she messes up. Order and Resolution are still her permanent nemeses, ten years on, which anyone whose lived with her can confirm. I want to build the toast around the parts of that project people don't usually bring up when they invoke Franklin as some kind of founding-father self-help guru -- namely that he flat-out admits the thing didn't work, that he was startled by how many faults he still had once he actually started counting, and that he basically talks himself into peace with it via that bit about the guy who wants his axe polished perfectly bright all over, gives up halfway through grinding it, and decides he likes a "speckled axe" just fine (I might have the exact wording slightly off, it's been a while since I reread it). There's also the part where he adds Humility to the list after a friend tells him he's got a pride problem, and immediately notes that even total success at it would just leave him proud of his humility, which feels related somehow.

I ran this angle by a couple of the seminar friends and they liked it but agreed it could very easily curdle into either a lecture recap or, worse, a backhanded roast of her perfectionism at her own wedding -- neither of which I want. Can you draft me something like a five-minute toast that actually uses Franklin's own irony about failing at self-improvement as the real backbone of both the joke and the sentiment, gets one genuine laugh out of the history nerds without leaving everyone else behind, and lands more like "I love watching you keep trying" than "here's a fun fact about Ben Franklin" or "here's every way you're a mess"?
