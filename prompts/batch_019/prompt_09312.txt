I'm currently taking a course on decision theory, and something that happened with my trivia team a few weeks ago has been bothering me ever since, even though I can't quite articulate what is wrong with it. At a charity trivia night, our team won the right to choose between two prizes: a guaranteed $100 gift card, or a sealed envelope carrying an 89% chance of that same $100 card, a 10% chance of a $500 card, and a 1% chance of winning nothing. We chose the guaranteed card without much deliberation, since giving up a certain $100 for a chance at $400 more felt too risky, particularly given the small but real chance of walking away empty-handed. A couple of weeks later, an unrelated society on campus ran a $5 raffle in which entrants could buy a ticket offering an 11% chance of winning a $100 card and an 89% chance of winning nothing, or, at the identical price, a ticket offering a 10% chance of a $500 card and a 90% chance of winning nothing. With no certain option available in either case, our group went straight for the ticket with the shot at $500.

Over dinner, my roommate, who is in the same course, pointed out that the two decisions share exactly the same underlying structure, and that favoring the safe option in the first case while favoring the long shot in the second cannot both be justified by any single, consistent way of valuing money - on her view, one of the two choices has to be a mistake. I can follow the reasoning she laid out, but it still does not feel to me as though we did anything wrong at either event: giving up something guaranteed seemed like a genuinely different kind of decision from choosing between two uncertain outcomes. Was my roommate right that we contradicted ourselves, or is there a principled basis on which a fully rational person could have made exactly the choices we did in both situations?
