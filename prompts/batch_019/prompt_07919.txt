I'm a second-year law student. This term's jurisprudence seminar covered the Hart–Fuller exchange -- Hart's claim that even a wicked statute remains law, its wickedness a separate question, against Fuller's argument that a legal system can betray its own inner morality so badly that it forfeits the name -- illustrated, as it always is, with the postwar grudge-informer cases. I never mentioned in seminar that the same argument has a second act running through my own family: my grandfather, a border guard convicted decades later in the Mauerschützen trials for shooting a man at the Berlin Wall, always had exactly one sentence for what happened -- "I did what the law asked of a soldier" -- which turns out to be more or less the defense a court had already heard and rejected, using reasoning lifted almost straight from Radbruch's formula about statutes too unjust to bind anyone's conscience. He died last week, and I'm giving the eulogy this weekend -- ten minutes, no more -- to a room split along that same line: half will hear his sentence as plain fact, half as a discredited excuse. I don't want to quietly re-litigate my own seminar from the podium, and I don't want to hand them something that reads like a case comment rather than a eulogy for a man I loved. Please write it.
