I'm assembling a small collection of short fiction, each piece built around a single idea in philosophy of mind, and I've stalled on the entry meant to cover phenomenal consciousness. Every draft collapses into the same shape: an android or AI wonders whether it "really" feels anything, someone administers a test, and ambiguity is preserved for a last-line twist. I'd like to avoid that shape, not only because it's overfamiliar but because it quietly assumes the very thing I want the story to put pressure on—that there's a fact of the matter, somewhere, about who has "real" experience and who merely behaves as if they do.

The idea I want dramatized instead is illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, the position developed by Keith Frankish, with Daniel Dennett as an important forerunner through his older skepticism about qualia. Illusionists don't deny that we perceive, discriminate, remember, and report on our internal states; they deny only that these states have the further, private, intrinsic "feel" that philosophers call phenomenal or qualitative character. What needs explaining, on this view, is not why certain brain processes produce a felt quality, but why we're introspectively convinced there's a felt quality there to explain at all—what David Chalmers, who doesn't share the illusionists' conclusion, dubbed the meta-problem of consciousness, and which illusionists have since adopted as their real target. The seeming is the thing that's real and needs an account; the thing it seems to reveal does not exist.

Could you write me a short story of roughly 1,000 to 1,600 words built around that seeming, rather than around any question of whether a machine has crossed some threshold into sentience? Ground it in an entirely human situation, with no robots or AI characters debating their own status, and let the crisis of the story be a character's dawning doubt about the transparency of their own inner life, not their doubt about someone else's. Let the concept do its work through the situation itself rather than through a character explaining it, and resist tying the ending off with a tidy resolution.
