I'm writing a historical novel in which Henry Sidgwick, in the last years of his life, is interviewed by a former student of his, now a skeptical journalist, about his long involvement with the Society for Psychical Research, which he helped found some fifteen years earlier. Sidgwick's habitual defense of the Society's inconclusive record is to compare it to any young science that must tolerate false starts before it matures, and to counsel patience. Draft nine interview questions that make that defense progressively harder to maintain: at least one should press him on how he squares this patience with his own circle's willingness to expose cases — like the once-celebrated family of child thought-readers — that turned out to be fraud, and at least one should ask how his hope of finding evidence for survival after death bears on the unresolved 'dualism of practical reason' from The Methods of Ethics. End the sequence with a question about what result, short of outright proof, would finally lead him to admit the project had failed. For each question, note the qualification Sidgwick would most plausibly offer in reply, and give a follow-up that doesn't let him rest there.
