A few of us are in a decision theory reading group (part of a philosophy of economics course), and last week's session was on money-pump arguments -- the classic argument that if your preferences are intransitive (you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A) someone can turn you into a perpetual money machine: sell you C, then offer to swap it for A plus a couple bucks, then A for B plus a couple bucks, then B for C plus a couple bucks, and you're back where you started but poorer, and they can keep doing this forever. Afterward two people from the group, Theo and Naomi, ended up arguing about it at the pub for like half an hour, and I couldnt tell who actually had the better arguement, so I want an outside read.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Theo brought up his housemate, who picks between apartments by scoring them on rent, commute, and light and then going with whichever wins two out of three pairwise comparisons -- and pointed out that depending on how the numbers land, this procedure can generate exactly this kind of cycle, so by the money-pump argument her preferences are just irrational, full stop. (2) Naomi said that's too fast: the pump only actually drains you if you keep taking each trade without noticing the pattern; anyone even a little forward-looking will just refuse one of the trades once she sees where it's headed, so all the argument really shows is that naive, memory-less trading is bad, not that having those underlying preferences is irrational. (3) Theo replied that refusing the trade means turning down the option she likes best right then, for the sake of a rule that isn't grounded in anything she currently prefers -- which sounds like exactly the failure the argument was supposed to expose, just relocated from individual choices to whatever policy is governing them. (4) Naomi answered that this is exactly what "resolute choice" is for: you settle on a plan ahead of time and carry it out even at moments you'd locally rather deviate, and judging rationality at the level of a whole plan instead of instant-by-instant is a perfectly coherent alternative picture -- nobody thinks Ulysses is being irrational when he strains against the ropes he asked to be tied with. (5) Theo shot back that the case isn't analogous, because Ulysses's preferences were already consistent and his information about the sirens was fixed in advance, whereas the housemate's cycling comes from a rule she applies fresh every time she compares apartments -- so what is she resolving to do, exactly, and why can she only manage it when a money pump is bearing down on her? If she can just will herself into stable, consistent commitments whenever it's convenient, he asked, why not have consistent preferences to begin with -- in which case "resolute choice" hasn't defended her original cyclical preferences at all, it's quietly swapped them out for transitive ones.

Neither of them budged, and both are definately convinced they clearly won. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's got the better of it at each step, including anything you think both of them are missing? End your answer with your overall take on its own line, formatted exactly as \answer{Theo} or \answer{Naomi}, whichever one you think came out ahead overall.
