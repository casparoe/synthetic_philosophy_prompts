I'm one of six maintainers of a mid-sized open-source data-visualization library — by day I'm a backend engineer, and I work on this project nights and weekends. The six of us also function as the project's governance committee: since we never incorporated a separate foundation board, bylaw changes are ultimately up to us. Several years ago we adopted a "one contributor, one vote" model for technical RFCs: anyone with a pull request merged in the previous two years — currently a bit over 300 people — gets a binding vote, including on RFCs about architecture, licensing, and who receives commit access. We don't get any extra voting weight ourselves in that process.

Last quarter that electorate approved an opt-out telemetry feature over our near-unanimous objection: the RFC was promoted heavily on social media right before the vote closed, most of the new "yes" votes came from accounts with a single drive-by commit, and the feature ended up breaking builds for a long list of downstream users within a week. Two of the six maintainers have since stepped back from the project, and a fork is being discussed on the mailing list. In response, I drafted a proposal to stop delegating binding technical votes to the 300-person electorate and instead give that authority to a nine-person technical council, selected by the remaining maintainers on the basis of sustained, reviewed contribution history, with the wider contributor base kept to a nonbinding advisory comment period. One of my co-maintainers is strongly opposed: she argues that letting the people currently in charge decide who is "qualified enough" to get a vote is exactly the self-perpetuating insiders' club that the open vote was designed to prevent, and that what I keep describing as a competence problem is really just my being unhappy that a fair vote didn't go my way.

We need to settle this at next month's meeting, and I've been asked to write the discussion memo we'll work from. I have no formal background in philosophy, but a friend who studied political science told me this is a small-scale version of the long-running "epistocracy versus democracy" debate, and that there are specific, well-known objections to competence-based restrictions on a franchise that the memo needs to actually grapple with rather than wave away. Could you draft that memo? I'd like it to recommend one concrete option — the existing model, my technical-council proposal, or some other design — rather than just listing considerations on each side; to spell out real mechanics (who would be enfranchised for which decisions, whether and how "competence" would be assessed, what happens to dissenting maintainers, and whether the arrangement gets reviewed or sunset after some period); and to take my co-maintainer's entrenchment objection seriously enough that she would recognize her own concern in it, rather than feel it had been brushed aside.
