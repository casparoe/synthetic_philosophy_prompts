I've recently been drawn into an unusual piece of service: a diocesan tribunal investigating a cause for beatification has added me as a lay consultor, at the recommendation of a former doctoral student of mine who now serves as the cause's vice-postulator and remembered that I teach a graduate seminar built around Hume's "Of Miracles." The case concerns a woman's complete and lasting recovery, closely following a novena addressed to the Servant of God whose cause is being promoted, from a glioblastoma her own oncologists had regarded as terminal. The tribunal's panel of physician-consultors has just filed its opinion that the recovery has no current medical explanation; before the acts are transmitted to Rome, the tribunal as a whole still has to vote on the separate and harder question of whether the record, taken as a whole, supports reporting the cure as most probably miraculous.

Two of my fellow consultors have already staked out positions that talk past one another, and I'd like the memo I'm asking you for to engage both rather than split the difference between them. One, a canon lawyer, holds that Hume settled this sort of question in 1748, and that no quantity or quality of testimony -- medical or otherwise -- could ever rationally tip the balance toward a miracle, so our vote is a formality we are canonically obliged to go through. The other, a physician on the panel with no patience for philosophy, holds that Hume's argument is naturalism dressed up as neutral epistemology, quietly assuming what it needs to prove, and so deserves no special authority over how we weigh this particular file. I'd like you to draft the memo I'll circulate to the tribunal. It should reconstruct Hume's actual argument -- both the a priori claim about weighing uniform experience against testimony, and the a posteriori reasons he gives for distrusting reports of miracles specifically -- precisely enough to show exactly what it does and doesn't establish for a case like this one; take a real position on which of my colleagues has the better of the argument; and close with a concrete recommendation -- report the cure as probably miraculous, decline to make any such finding, or hold the case open pending some specific further evidence and a stated timeframe for gathering it -- pitched to readers who are not philosophers and cannot simply be told to go read Hume for themselves.
