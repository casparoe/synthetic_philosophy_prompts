I am drafting a scene for a historical novel in which Kurt Gödel and Albert Einstein, on one of their famous walks between the Institute for Advanced Study and Gödel's house in the early 1950s, discover that they disagree sharply about whether anything survives a person's death. I would like your help writing the dialogue itself, in a register suited to two old friends speaking candidly rather than lecturing.

Gödel should argue something close to his actual, historically documented position: that the world is rationally ordered down to its foundations, and that it would therefore violate the principle that nothing occurs without a sufficient reason if a personality as elaborately developed as a human mind were simply extinguished at death, to no further end; moreover, even if the world turned out not to be fully rational after all, we would have no special ground for assuming death is final either, so that some form of survival comes out ahead on either horn of the dilemma. Einstein should argue from his own well-documented "cosmic religion": a reverence for an impersonal, lawlike rational order in nature (his "Spinoza's God," who reveals himself in the harmony of what exists rather than in concern for human fates), together with his explicit, repeated denial that any of this implies the survival of individual persons.

Please have the two of them engage each other's actual reasoning rather than talk past one another: each should locate a genuine weak point in the other's position, and each should be visibly willing to refine or qualify his own view under pressure rather than simply restate it. Let the exchange end unresolved, with both positions intact but sharpened.
