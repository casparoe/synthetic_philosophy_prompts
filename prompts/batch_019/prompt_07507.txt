Okay so context: I'm part of this after-school AI safety discussion group (meets over Discord, kinda nerdy but whatever) and this week we were on "corrigibility" and the shutdown problem, and I feel like I get the vibe of it but not actually the guts of the argument. Like everyone always trots out the Stuart Russell line about how a robot can't fetch you coffee if its dead, so it should want to preserve the off-switch, but that always felt kind of hand-wavy to me — ok sure, but why can't you just hard-code a rule like "if button pressed, shut down" and call it a day?

Someone in the group linked an old MIRI paper from like 2015 that tries to fix this with something called "utility indifference," where you bolt on a correction term to the agent's utility function so it's mathematically indifferent to whether the button gets pressed, but apparently that has its own issues, like the agent still being tempted to "manage the news" around the button, or successor agents it builds down the line not automatically inheriting the fix. Then someone else brought up a newer framing (I think by a guy named Thornley? not sure I'm remembering the name right) where the whole problem gets set up as a clash between basic rationality axioms, and the fix people are excited about now is giving the agent "incomplete preferences" so it just doesn't have an opinion comparing shorter vs longer trajectories — except THAT is supposed to open the agent up to being "money pumped" somehow, which sounded like a real cost and not a free lunch, but I couldn't follow why incompleteness would make an agent exploitable in the first place.

Can you actually walk me through the gears of this, in order: (1) why a normal expected-utility-maximizing agent ends up with a built-in incentive to resist or influence shutdown even if nobody designed it to "want" that, (2) why bolting on a rule or a utility-indifference patch doesn't fully close the loophole, and (3) why making preferences incomplete is supposed to help with that but drags in this money-pump exploitability problem as the price you pay. I don't just want the coffee-robot soundbite version, I want to actually understand the tradeoff well enough to explain it back to the group next week without hand-waving.
