I sit on a small internal "blue-sky" research fund at my university -- the kind of committee that exists specifically to bankroll weird, unconventional stuff that the big federal agencies would never touch. Normally we're looking at things like odd battery chemistries or fringe biology, so nobody on the panel actually has a background in theoretical computer science. That's a problem this cycle, because the most contested proposal is from an electrical engineering postdoc asking for $40k to build a prototype "Zeno machine": a circuit that doubles its own switching speed every cycle (one step in half a second, the next in a quarter second, and so on), so that after one full second it will in principle have completed infinitely many computational steps. Her pitch is that this would let her settle the halting problem for arbitrary programs, something no ordinary computer can do, and she's pre-empting the obvious objection by pointing out that the Church-Turing thesis has never actually been proven, only ever assumed on inductive grounds, so nothing in mathematics rules out a physical device that computes something no Turing machine can.

Two people on the commitee want to reject this outright as crank science that ignores basic thermodynamic and quantum limits on switching speed. Two others think that's too quick a dismissal, since apparently real philosophers of computation take "hypercomputation" seriously as a research question, and none of them wants to shut down a genuinely unconventional idea for reasons they can't actually spell out. (I asked a friend with a CS PhD to explain whats wrong with it over lunch and she just laughed and said "good luck," which did not help.) I need to circulate a short decision memo before we vote next week -- fund, reject, or fund with conditions -- with real reasoning the postdoc could respond to, and a fair statement of the strongest point on whichever side ends up losing. Could you draft that memo for me?
