I spent most of my working life as a comparative neurophysiologist, recording escape and withdrawal reflexes in crayfish, cockroaches, and pond snails, and for thirty-odd years I was scrupulous, in the way the field trained me to be, about never speculating in print on what, if anything, those animals experienced while their nerve cords fired under my electrodes; that question belonged to someone else's discipline entirely. Since retiring I have been attending a small philosophy discussion group that meets at the local university, mostly for something to occupy my Tuesdays, and it has rather thoroughly undone that old professional discipline.

The immediate occasion is more domestic than academic. Three years ago I dug a wildlife pond at the bottom of my garden, and it has become exactly the crowded little ecosystem I had hoped for: newts, dragonfly and damselfly larvae, water boatmen, and a thriving population of great pond snails, whose nervous system of some twenty thousand individually identifiable neurons I could once have sketched from memory. This summer an introduced ramshorn snail has bred explosively and is smothering the oxygenating plants the newts need for breeding. A neighbour with a similar pond has offered me a molluscicide that would solve the ramshorn problem within a fortnight, at the cost of killing off most of the pond's other snails and a good part of its invertebrate life besides -- considerably more individual animals, on any count, than it would save. I notice that I want quite badly to protect the newts and the dragonfly larvae, feel a milder but genuine reluctance about the water boatmen, and feel almost nothing about the snails, even though, on any measure of nervous complexity I would once have cited professionally, the snails are not obviously the least sophisticated animals in the pond. Pressed to justify the difference, the best I can produce is that the newts have faces and the snails do not, which is not an argument I would have accepted from a student.

I raised the problem at the discussion group and left more confused than I arrived, since half the room considered the whole question obviously unanswerable and therefore not worth losing sleep over, while the other half considered it obviously answerable in favour of whichever animals they personally found more appealing. I would be grateful for your help thinking it through more carefully than either faction managed: is there some defensible way of deciding how much weight my reactions to the newts, the water boatmen, and the snails should each be given, when I have no direct way of telling what, if anything, is going on in any of them -- or is looking for a "defensible way" already a mistake, so that I should expect nothing better than an arbitrary line drawn somewhere on the phylogenetic tree, with no principled account of exactly where it falls?
