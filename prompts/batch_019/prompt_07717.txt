I've been reading about the "Boltzmann brain" problem in cosmology for a research project in my physics class, and I was surprised to learn that Ludwig Boltzmann anticipated something like it himself. While defending his statistical reading of the Second Law against the reversibility and recurrence objections associated with Loschmidt and Zermelo, Boltzmann suggested, in an 1895 letter to Nature that credited the idea to his assistant Schuetz, that our observable universe might just be a rare, temporary fluctuation away from a far larger background sitting at thermal equilibrium, and he used an early anthropic-style argument to explain why observers like us would find ourselves inside such a fluctuation. Modern cosmologists have since sharpened this same fluctuation idea using tools Boltzmann never had, such as eternal inflation and the thermal equilibrium of de Sitter space, arguing that over infinite time such a background should produce vastly more disembodied "Boltzmann brains" with false memories than genuine observers with real evolutionary histories like ours — which threatens to undercut the very reasoning behind the fluctuation picture in the first place. If Boltzmann were confronted with this sharpened, modern version of his own idea, would his actual scientific commitments, such as his staunch atomism and his track record of accepting counterintuitive statistical conclusions (as he did against Zermelo), have pushed him to bite the bullet and accept the Boltzmann-brain conclusion, or would they have given him principled grounds to reject it instead? Please base your answer on specifics of Boltzmann's own views and reasoning style, rather than general plausibility.
