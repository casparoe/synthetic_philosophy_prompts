A disagreement broke out between two colleagues of mine, Mia and Dan, over lunch a couple of weeks ago, and it has been nagging at me since, mostly because neither of them struck me as obviously wrong. The background: a former teammate, Raj, left our company last year to co-found a startup. At his old job he had been the principal architect of a caching strategy that meaningfully cut latency on one of our core services. When he left he took no code, design documents, or slides, and nobody has any evidence he violated the letter of his NDA, but within a few months his new company had shipped a strikingly similar caching approach, apparently written from scratch in an entirely different codebase.

(1) Mia's opening move was that this is theft in every sense that matters: the two years and real budget our old employer spent discovering that approach is exactly what Raj walked out the door with, and rewriting the implementation doesn't launder that fact. (2) Dan replied that ideas, as such, cannot be owned: patents are deliberately artificial, temporary monopolies granted as an incentive to invent and publicly disclose, while copyright protects only the concrete expression of an idea and never the idea itself; since the company had done neither, having never patented the approach while Raj copied no protected expression, there was nothing to enforce beyond the NDA, which nobody had shown he broke. (3) Mia came back with trade secret law, which exists precisely to protect confidential, commercially valuable know-how without requiring a patent, so Dan's "no patent, no copyright on ideas" defense was answering a doctrine nobody had actually invoked. (4) Dan then pointed to the principle that departing employees are entitled to the general skill and knowledge they picked up on the job, since without it nobody who ever learned anything useful at work could ethically change employers. (5) Mia's rejoinder was that this principle is meant for generic competence, the kind you'd describe as "got better at systems design," not for one specific, identifiable technique that only their old employer had ever discovered, and that Dan could not actually say where "general skill" stops and "their one big idea" starts, which makes his whole position amount to "it's fine whenever I can't state a rule against it."

I have no formal training in philosophy or law myself (I'm a backend engineer, not a lawyer or a philosopher), just whatever I've absorbed from HR trainings and the occasional Hacker News thread, so I would like help thinking this through properly. Please treat it as a question about the underlying moral and philosophical merits, not a prediction of what a court would actually rule. Go through moves (1) through (5) in order and judge, for each one, whether the person making it has the better of the argument at that specific step, not who wins overall. Give me your scores first, as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>}, where each score is an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken or question-begging) to 2 (clearly correct), and then add two or three sentences justifying each score. Finally, close with a short paragraph on whether either Mia or Dan ever manages to draw a principled line between "general skill and knowledge" and "a specific, ownable idea," since that seems to be the actual crux of the whole dispute, and I don't think either of them landed it.
