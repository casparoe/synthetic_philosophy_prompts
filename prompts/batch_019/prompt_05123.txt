I sit on the ethics advisory committee of a hospice affiliated with our medical school. It has just received an unrestricted bequest of three million dollars, and because the dispute over how to spend it is really a disagreement about values rather than logistics, the board has asked us to recommend an allocation. One faction wants to devote a large share to a redesigned garden, a rotating art and music program, and an expanded "No One Dies Alone" volunteer companionship service, appealing more or less explicitly to Moore's claim, in the concluding chapter of Principia Ethica, that personal affection and the contemplation of beautiful things are the greatest goods we know. Our medical director objects that most patients in their final days are heavily sedated or have advanced dementia, and asks why we should spend so heavily on beauty and companionship for people who may be unable to register any of it, rather than on an additional palliative-care physician and better pain control -- pointing out that this is a real-world version of Moore's own puzzle about whether a beautiful but wholly unperceived world is better than an ugly, equally unperceived one. As the committee's resident philosopher, I've been asked to draft the recommendation memo for the board: it should propose a specific allocation and a timeline for revisiting it, and it should engage the medical director's objection on its philosophical merits rather than treating it as simply a budgeting concern.
