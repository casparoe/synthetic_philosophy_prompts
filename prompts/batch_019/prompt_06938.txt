Part of my job is serving on the internal committee at my company that signs off on anything touching our customer-support assistant's honesty policy. We're not a frontier AI lab -- we just license a third-party model and wrap it in our own system prompts and scripted responses -- but the policy itself ("the assistant must be honest and must not deceive users") gets relitigated almost every quarter, and I've spent enough time reading rationalist and AI-safety blogs over the years that I tend to push for more precise distinctions in these meetings than most of my colleagues want to sit through.

The case on the agenda for Thursday is this. The product team wants to ship a scripted reply for whenever a user directly asks the assistant "am I talking to a bot?" or "is this a real person?" Rather than answering yes or no, the assistant would say something like "I'm one of the specialists on our support team, here to help you sort this out" -- a sentence that's true in the relevant sense (the system is, functionally, part of the support team) but plainly designed to leave the human/AI question unresolved. The team's A/B tests show that a flat "I am an AI" answer measurably increases hang-ups and escalations, which they'd like to avoid. Two committee members think this is obviously fine, since no false statement is being made; two others think it's a textbook case of "paltering," the use of true statements to produce a false impression, and that it violates our honesty policy exactly as much as an outright lie would, especially since it's a direct answer to a direct question rather than an unprompted remark. A fifth member, only half in jest, wants us to treat this as a miniature version of the "deceptive alignment" problem from the AI-safety literature, on the theory that an institutionally sanctioned habit of truthful evasion is exactly what should worry us; another thinks she's borrowing alarming vocabulary from a genuinely different kind of problem and that the comparison doesn't survive scrutiny. What our policy document leaves unresolved -- the crux, really -- is whether "honest and not deceptive" constrains individual sentences or the overall impression an interaction leaves, and whether the directness of a question changes what counts as adequate disclosure.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? I need it to land on an actual recommendation -- approve the script as written, reject it and require an unambiguous answer, or approve some modified version -- rather than a balanced list of considerations, and the reasoning needs to engage the underlying difficulty rather than dissolve it by definitional fiat (for instance, by simply stipulating that deception requires a false statement and calling the case closed). Please steelman the product team's position rather than wave it away, weigh in on whether the "deceptive alignment" comparison is apt or a category error, and close with two or three criteria the product team could actually apply on their own the next time something like this comes up, rather than criteria that just restate "don't be deceptive."
