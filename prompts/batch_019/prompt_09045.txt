I have spent a good deal of time thinking about the Condorcet Jury Theorem as a possible defense of democratic decision-making: if each voter is even slightly more likely to be right than wrong, and everyone judges independently, a majority vote among enough people is almost certain to land on the correct answer. The more I sit with it, though, the more it seems to me that the assumption that everyone votes "sincerely" — meaning that each person simply reports their own private judgment — is doing all the real work, and I doubt it actually holds for a fully rational voter. Picture a juror deciding guilty or not guilty: their vote only ever changes the verdict in the specific case where the rest of the jury is otherwise evenly split, so the mere fact of finding themselves in that pivotal position seems to carry information of its own, over and above whatever they privately believed at the outset. Shouldn't a rational juror factor that in rather than simply voting their initial impression? I would like you to walk me through whether and how this kind of reasoning threatens the sincere-voting assumption behind the theorem, and what, if anything, is left of its force as an argument for trusting majorities once this is taken into account.
