In my decision theory seminar this week, we've been comparing classical Kelly betting with the "ergodicity economics" framework associated with the physicist Ole Peters, which has gained some traction in quantitative finance and physics-adjacent circles. The standard pitch is that Kelly betting isn't just one more risk preference among the continuum of von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions. Once you notice that wealth under multiplicative dynamics is non-ergodic — so that the ensemble average of a gamble's outcomes diverges from what any individual bettor will actually experience over time — maximizing expected log wealth stops being an arbitrary curvature assumption and becomes the uniquely correct target, without needing to posit a utility function at all.

I can't quite tell whether this argument establishes something genuinely beyond ordinary expected utility theory, given that expected-log-utility maximization was already a well-understood special case of it, and growth-optimal betting was established well before the ergodicity framing existed. Could you help me see what work the time-average/ensemble-average distinction is actually doing here, and whether "geometric rationality" amounts to a genuine rival to expected utility maximization, or just the same recommendation wearing different philosophical clothes?
