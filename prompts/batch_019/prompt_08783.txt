I sit on the research-integrity panel at the science-funding foundation where I work as a program officer. A grantee, a well-regarded early-career biologist, has admitted to discarding inconvenient data points from a paper we funded, citing failed replications, a looming tenure deadline, and a depressive episode documented by her therapist; she concedes she knew, at some level, it was wrong. The panel is choosing among three options: barring her from future funding, requiring a public correction with monitored probation, or issuing a lighter reprimand. One panelist invokes something close to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: since we act as the people we are, being truly responsible for an act would require having made ourselves that way, but any such self-making either presupposes an earlier, equally unchosen self or amounts to causing yourself out of nothing -- and the problem is meant to survive even if her choice was undetermined -- so he concludes our decision should rest purely on forward-looking grounds, not desert. Two other panelists object that this erases any principled difference between her and a hypothetical grantee who fabricated data coldly and repeatedly for profit, since neither would be "ultimately" responsible for their character either. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Friday's meeting: recommend one specific outcome with a timeline, and take a real position on the desert-versus-forward-looking dispute, steelmanning whichever side you don't adopt.
