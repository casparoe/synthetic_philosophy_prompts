For thirty years I ran a lab and field program studying problem-solving and social cognition in corvids, with a long-running side interest in cephalopods, before retiring three years ago and turning to philosophy in earnest. Working through Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* for the first time this spring, I was stopped short by the brief but much-cited passage where he suggests that persons are protected by strong, non-aggregative side-constraints — you may never simply be used as a means to a greater good — while animals' pains and pleasures are handled by ordinary utilitarian summing, a position people sometimes shorthand as "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people." I gather he grew less comfortable with that clean division by the time of *The Examined Life*, though I'd be the first to admit I may be over-reading his later ambivalence.

The retirees' philosophy circle I've joined meets monthly over video call, and next month's guest is a moral philosopher who has built a career defending a recognizably Nozickian, side-constraint-based account of what we owe animals: full inviolability for persons, real but aggregable weight for everything else. I've been asked to draft the questions, and I'd rather not give her a list that just invites her to restate the position in different words.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual mechanics of the view rather than its slogans? I'd like at least one question on whether the person/animal line is tracking a genuine morally relevant capacity rather than mere species membership, given what's now documented about planning, tool use, and self-monitoring in great apes, corvids, and octopuses; one on the argument from marginal cases — why infants and severely cognitively impaired humans keep their full side-constraints despite lacking whatever capacity is supposed to justify the line; one that turns Nozick's own utility-monster device back on the position, asking why aggregative treatment is intuitively intolerable when applied to persons but supposedly fine when applied to animals with comparably rich inner lives; and one asking whether the experience-machine argument — built to show that persons want more than pleasant mental states, they want real contact with the world — actually illuminates what a good animal life consists in, or simply smuggles in the very capacities that are in dispute. For each of these, please add a short, specific follow-up keyed to the response she's most likely to give, rather than a generic invitation to elaborate.
