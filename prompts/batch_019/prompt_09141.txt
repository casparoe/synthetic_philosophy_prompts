Your answer should address: (a) why Schopenhauer denies that music, unlike architecture, sculpture, painting, and poetry, is a copy of the Platonic Ideas, and what he claims it is a copy of instead; (b) what he means by saying that music presents the emotions 'in the abstract' rather than in their particular, motivated form; and (c) how his analogy between the voices of a harmonized composition and the grades of the will's objectification is meant to support (a)."

The student's answer was as follows: "Schopenhauer's account of music only makes sense against the background of his general theory of art. For the other arts, the proper object of aesthetic contemplation is a Platonic Idea: the sculptor or painter presents a particular grade at which the will objectifies itself, and the viewer, in a state of will-less contemplation, loses herself in that Idea and is momentarily released from the suffering of striving. Poetry works the same way, and tragedy especially so, since tragedy depicts the Idea of humanity itself, which is why Schopenhauer treats it as the summit of the representational arts. Music, on Schopenhauer's view, ranks even above tragedy, because it presents the highest and purest Idea of all: rather than showing us one particular case of human striving, as any given tragedy must, music presents humanity's self-conscious, rational will directly and universally, without the trappings of any particular plot or character. This is also why Schopenhauer can claim that music would in some sense persist even if the entire phenomenal world were annihilated: its existence is not hostage to any specific Idea being instantiated in matter, only to the most general and elevated one. This same elevation explains why music does not give us particular emotions tied to particular circumstances, the way a painting of a grieving mother gives us one instance of grief. Instead, music presents joy, grief, and the rest 'in the abstract': stripped of the specific situations that occasion them in life, somewhat as a mathematical formula abstracts from any of its particular instances. Schopenhauer develops this hierarchy within music through the texture of harmony itself. The four voices of a harmonized piece map onto the grades of the will's objectification: the bass, as the fixed foundation from which the other voices grow and on which they all depend, corresponds to the highest grade, the self-conscious, rational will of man, while the melody, moving above the other voices with the greatest ease, freedom, and rapidity, corresponds to the lowest grade, unorganized inorganic nature, whose hallmark for Schopenhauer is precisely the blind, lawlike freedom of forces such as gravity. The inner voices, tenor and alto, fill in the series between these two extremes, standing for the plant and animal kingdoms."
