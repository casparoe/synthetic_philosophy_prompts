About six hours into a long car trip, after we had gone through every podcast anyone had downloaded, two friends of mine, Marcus and Aisha, got into an argument I still haven't been able to resolve for myself. Marcus drives an old car whose fuel gauge he has never fully trusted, but rather than getting it looked at by a mechanic, he told us about a private test he had been running on it for the better part of a year. (1) He never resets the trip meter or works out his mileage, so the needle is the only source of information he has about how much gas is in the tank at any given moment; every time it told him something — full, three-quarters, half, and so on — he simply went along with it and kept a rough mental tally, and he said that in every single one of the many dozens of times he did this, it later turned out that the tank had exactly the amount the needle had claimed, without a single exception, which he took to show that the gauge itself is accurate and not merely something he relies on for lack of a better option.

Aisha wasn't convinced. (2) She pointed out that the only reason Marcus ever found out "how much the tank actually had" on any of those occasions was the needle itself, since he never once checked the fuel level any other way; all he had really shown, she said, was that the gauge agrees with itself over time, which is exactly what a gauge that was broken in some steady, consistent way would also do. (3) Marcus replied that, taken seriously, her standard would disqualify every basic way we have of finding things out: nobody could ever earn the right to trust their eyesight, their memory, or their sense of balance, since any test of those would itself have to go through eyesight, memory, or balance; if a check only counts when it comes from completely outside the thing being checked, he said, then nothing could ever pass, and that struck him as a much bigger problem than anything wrong with his reasoning about a fuel gauge.

(4) Aisha answered that she wasn't asking for some impossible view from outside all of our faculties at once, and that she had no objection to Marcus trusting the gauge on any given day to find out how much gas was left; her complaint was narrower, that going from "the gauge told me correctly, over and over" to "and therefore the gauge, as an instrument, is accurate" is a further and bigger claim, and that piling up more instances of the first kind of trust, however many there were, could never add up to earning the second kind, any more than repeating a rumor to yourself more insistently turns it into something you have independently confirmed. Neither of them gave any ground for the rest of the drive, and I have gone back and forth on it ever since without settling the matter for myself.
