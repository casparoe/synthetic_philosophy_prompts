I recently sat down and read Turing's actual 1950 paper instead of just the potted "imitation game" summary everyone gives, and section 9 stopped me cold. He straight up says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," then spends a paragraph worrying that a psychic contestant could beat the imitation game by reading the interrogator's mind, so he proposes sticking everyone in "telepathy-proof" rooms. As a retired biologist who spent thirty-some years being paranoid about p-values, I found it bizarre coming from a guy who was otherwise so careful about evidence.

Here's what I keep turning over: Turing died in '54, decades before things like Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" precognition study and the whole replication crisis it helped kick off (preregistration, p-hacking critiques, the works). If he'd lived to watch psychology clean up its statistics and see ESP effects basically dissolve under scrutiny, do you reckon he'd have quietly dropped that section, or dug his heels in? He was a serious statistician in his own right (he did statistical code-breaking work during the war), so it could honestly go either way. Whats your best guess, and what specifically about his character or other writing would you point to either way?
