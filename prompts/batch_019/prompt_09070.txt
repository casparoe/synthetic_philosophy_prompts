I'm teaching a seminar on Popper this term, and yesterday's session was all about the verisimilitude mess -- the idea that even though pretty much every scientific theory we've ever had is technically false, some are still closer to the truth than others, and Popper tried to make that precise in terms of truth-content and falsity-content. I put the Tichy/Miller result on the board: on Popper's own definition, it turns out no false theory can ever come out more verisimilar than any other false theory, which is about as damaging as it sounds given that literally every theory anyone has taken seriously in the history of science is false in some respect. Everyone followed the abstract proof fine, but when I asked them to cook up their own concrete case that actually makes the problem bite, the room went dead quiet, and honestly I dont blame them -- the stock examples in the reading always feel a little rigged to me too.

So here's what I want from you, partly so I can steal it for next week's handout: invent a small toy example of your own -- a couple of simple false "theories" about some domain, whatever you like, just not the usual weather-forecasting one everybody reaches for -- where most people's intuition would say one theory is clearly closer to the truth than the other. Then actually run it through Popper's machinery: lay out the truth-content and falsity-content each one generates, and show concretely where the comparison collapses or gives the wrong verdict, rather than just re-describing the general theorem. That's the bit my students keep glossing over, and I want to see if a genuinely fresh example makes it click.
