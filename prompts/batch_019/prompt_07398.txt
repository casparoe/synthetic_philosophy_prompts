A friend and I spent much of Sunday evening arguing about the centipede game, and neither of us really won. Her position was the hard-nosed one: backward induction is simply correct, so two players who are rational and know each other to be rational must defect at the very first node, and the fact that real subjects in laboratory versions of the game keep cooperating for several rounds anyway just shows that people are not fully rational, or that their true payoffs quietly include altruistic or reputational considerations that the textbook version of the game leaves out. My position was that something more troubling is going on: the whole backward-induction argument seems to presuppose a "common knowledge of rationality" that the very act of reaching any node past the first would already have falsified, so that a player who somehow found themselves at round ten would have genuine grounds for doubting that the premises behind the original prediction still hold.

Partway through the argument, I found myself thinking about a passage in Hume's Treatise that has always struck me as an oddly exact ancestor of this whole debate: his discussion, in the course of explaining why promise-keeping has to be an artificial virtue rather than something natural affection could ever secure, of two farmers who each need the other's labour at a different point in the harvest. Since neither has any real fondness for the other, each can foresee that whoever is asked to help second will simply walk away once the first has already delivered on his side, so that both end up losing their harvests for lack of the mutual trust that would have let them help each other in turn. Hume's point is that neither self-interest nor natural benevolence, taken on their own, can sustain cooperation that has to unfold sequentially over time, which is exactly why he thinks the artificial apparatus of convention, promising, and the cultivated sentiment of honour has to be invented.

What I would like from you is a genuine reconstruction of what Hume would say if the centipede game and the modern backward-induction argument were put to him in their own terms, rather than the generic remark that he distrusted "pure reason" in ethics. I am especially interested in three things: whether his view that reason by itself is motivationally inert would lead him to say that the entire idea of "common knowledge of rationality" rests on a mistaken picture of what practical reason could ever do in the first place; whether his account of convention and artificial virtue gives him principled grounds for explaining sustained cooperation that go beyond simply relabelling it as irrationality or disguised altruism; and, having worked through those two questions, whether you think he would ultimately side with my friend, with me, or with neither of us.
