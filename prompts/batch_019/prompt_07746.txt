"Adams' Equation says the probability of a conditional 'if A, then B' should equal the conditional probability P(B|A), i.e. P(A>B) = P(B|A), whenever P(A) > 0. Lewis's proof shows that pretty much no matter what connective '>' you pick, this can't hold for every reasonable probability function without leading to absurdity.

The trick is looking at what happens when you conditionalize P on B and on not-B (assuming A&B and A&not-B both start out with positive probability, so both updates are well-defined and A still has positive probability under each). Updating on B makes B certain, so B-given-A is certain too, so by the Equation P_B(A>B) = 1. Updating on not-B makes B-given-A have probability 0, so P_(not-B)(A>B) = 0. But P_B and P_(not-B) are just P updated on new info, so naturally the Equation carries over to them too. Then by total probability, P(A>B) = P(B)*1 + P(not-B)*0 = P(B). Combined with the Equation applied to the original P, this gives P(B|A) = P(B) -- A and B come out independent. Since A and B were basically arbitrary here, the Equation forces totally unrelated-looking claims to be independent, which is absurd: e.g. it would mean my credence that a struck match lights can't depend on whether it was struck, which isn't true for anyone with realistic beliefs about matches.

So the upshot is that Adams was wrong that conditional probability tracks the probability of a conditional -- you can't cash out 'if A, B' as a proposition with probability P(B|A). That's a direct hit on Adams' Thesis: it means his whole assertability-based account of indicative conditionals doesn't really have a coherent probabilistic foundation once you spell it out."
