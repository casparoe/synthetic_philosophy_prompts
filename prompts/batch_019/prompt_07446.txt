I'm a PhD student in public policy, and this year I hold the graduate-fellow seat on my city's Pilot Programs Committee, a joint body of council staff, agency liaisons, and two community representatives that vets proposed policy experiments before they go to the full council for funding. Ahead of our last meeting, one of the staffers, who has a background in mechanism design, circulated Robin Hanson's old proposal for "futarchy" -- vote on values, bet on beliefs -- and has since been pushing us to pilot a bounded version of it on a live decision the city is facing: how to expand its mental-health crisis response.

The choice on the table is between funding more unarmed civilian mobile-crisis teams or expanding the existing co-responder units that pair police officers with clinicians. Under the staffer's proposal, the committee would first fix a target metric -- adverse outcomes per 1,000 crisis calls over the following two years -- and then open two subsidized conditional prediction markets, one pricing that rate "if civilian teams are expanded" and the other "if co-responder units are expanded instead," with trading open to residents plus a paid panel of clinicians and dispatchers to ensure some informed participation. The city would commit in advance to funding whichever expansion the market prices as yielding fewer adverse outcomes, subject to a mandatory two-year sunset and reevaluation. The committee is now deadlocked over whether to recommend even this narrow pilot to the full council. The staffer and two others see it as a rare chance to settle an empirically tractable question -- what actually reduces harm -- without its being held hostage to the usual horse-trading. Our two community representatives are strongly opposed: they argue that "adverse outcome" is not a value-neutral thing to bet on, since whether it counts involuntary holds and arrests, and not just deaths or injuries, already settles most of what genuinely divides the two models, and that whoever can afford to trade heavily -- conceivably the officers' union, or a contractor hoping to run the civilian program -- stands to gain a great deal from being wrong on purpose.

I've read enough of Hanson's own defense of the mechanism to anticipate the obvious rejoinders, but I keep getting stuck on whether the community representatives' objection is answerable within his framework at all, or whether it points to something futarchy just can't accommodate. Could you draft the short memo I'll bring to our meeting in three weeks, when we owe the full council a recommendation to approve, reject, or approve with conditions? I'd like it to reach a clear recommendation and, if that recommendation is conditional approval, spell out the conditions concretely -- metric definition, trader-eligibility or position-size limits, audit provisions, and so on. Above all, I'd like it to take the community representatives' objection seriously on its own terms, rather than answering it only with the standard rejoinder that would-be manipulators simply end up subsidizing everyone else's more accurate bets.
