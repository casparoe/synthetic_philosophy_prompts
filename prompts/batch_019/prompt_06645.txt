I'm drafting a scene in my novel featuring a married couple in their forties -- she a game theorist, he a philosopher working in formal epistemology -- who met in graduate school and have spent their whole marriage half-earnestly trying to be good Bayesians with each other. Every January they perform a private ritual: each states a credence that the marriage will still be intact in ten years, discloses everything relevant they've learned or reconsidered since the last accounting, and updates honestly in front of the other. This is the fifth year running that their numbers have failed to converge, and the wife announces that this is no longer merely sad: given how much they have honestly exchanged, Aumann's agreement theorem implies that either one of them has been lying about their credence or one of them is reasoning badly, and she wants to know which. Her husband refuses to accept either horn of that dilemma. Write the dialogue that follows, in which each responds to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them -- he needs a substantive rebuttal that engages the theorem's real preconditions rather than an appeal to hurt feelings, and she cannot be allowed to retreat into a pop version of the result that ignores what it actually requires.
