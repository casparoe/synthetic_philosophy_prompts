I'm a software engineer, and some spare-time reading on decision theory has left me trying to pin down how two ideas relate to each other. The first is Douglas Hofstadter's notion of superrationality: in a one-shot, symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma, a superrational agent reasons that every relevantly identical agent will reach the same conclusion, and so picks whichever joint strategy would be best if adopted by all such agents — typically mutual cooperation, even though defection dominates for either player individually. The second is acausal trade, as discussed in rationalist decision-theory circles: agents with no causal connection whatsoever, such as civilizations separated by a cosmological horizon or existing in different branches of a multiverse, coordinate by each modeling the other well enough to predict how it would respond to a hypothetical deal, and acting on that prediction.

Is acausal trade simply superrationality generalized to agents that are neither identical nor symmetric, or does it require extra ingredients that superrationality can do without — for instance, a decision theory that rewards acting on logical or subjunctive dependence rather than causal influence, an actual (rather than merely assumed) capacity to simulate the other party's reasoning, or some substitute for the credible commitment that ordinarily underwrites a trade? Please justify your answer by specifying exactly which commitments each idea needs and which it can dispense with.
