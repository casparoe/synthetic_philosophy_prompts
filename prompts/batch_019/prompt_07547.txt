So in my philosophy-of-economics seminar this week we read Frank Ramsey's 1928 paper on savings, and my professor kept coming back to that one line where he says discounting future enjoyment is "ethically indefensible" and only happens because of "weakness of the imagination." I hadn't really clocked before that this guy basically supplied the ethical premise underneath the entire modern fight over discount rates, and then died at 26 just two years later, so he never got to see anything that came after. A few of us from the seminar kept arguing about this after class and now I want to get your take too.

Basically: what do you think Ramsey would actually say if he could see everything that happened downstream of his own model? Would he side with Stern over Nordhaus in the climate discount-rate fight, seeing as Stern basically ran with Ramsey's own zero-pure-time-preference move (and got attacked for it by economists who wanted to use market interest rates instead)? And seperately, what would he make of all the hyperbolic discounting/time-inconsistency stuff from behavioral econ (Strotz, Laibson, that whole "golden eggs" line of work) showing that actual people and animals don't discount the way his optimal-growth math assumes at all - would he take that as basically vindicating his "weakness of imagination" diagnosis, or as a totally different phenomenon his framework was never built to handle? And then theres the Parfit-descended longtermist crowd who've pushed his zero-discount-rate argument way further than he did, into claims about obligations to people who might exist thousands of years out - would Ramsey, coming out of the Cambridge world of Keynes and Wittgenstein, have actually gone along with that, or does his reasoning kind of run out of steam once you leave the "next generation or two" timescale he was probably picturing? Trying to actually get inside his head here, not just get a list of what he literally wrote.
