Yudkowsky's "zombies" posts in the Sequences make an argument against epiphenomenalism that I do not think gets enough attention in the academic literature on conceivability: if my zombie twin is physically and functionally identical to me, then whatever causes him to insist "I am conscious," write papers on the hard problem, and so forth, must be a purely physical process — and since I would produce the very same utterances for the very same reasons, my own conviction that I am conscious cannot be explained by the fact that I actually am. He treats this as close to a reductio of the whole scenario. The worry is not original to him, though: Chalmers names essentially the same problem "the paradox of phenomenal judgment" in The Conscious Mind, and rather than giving up property dualism over it, he bites the bullet on a form of epiphenomenalism, appealing to brute psychophysical laws that guarantee our judgments about our phenomenal states track those states even though the states themselves do no causal work in producing the judgments.

Write a dialogue between a Chalmers-style property dualist and a rationalist critic arguing in the spirit of Yudkowsky's posts, picking up right after the critic has laid out this objection. The critic should press the epistemic argument as far as it will go, including the question of why anyone should trust a concept that plays no role in producing the beliefs we have about it, while the dualist should mount the strongest defense available — the psychophysical-harmony move or something better — rather than conceding early. Neither side should be a pushover: have each character respond to the other's actual point rather than restating their own position, and let the exchange end wherever the real crux turns out to lie, rather than at a vague stalemate.
