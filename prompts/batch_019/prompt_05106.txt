I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and for the past year I've also served as the graduate student representative on my seminary's Committee on Doctrinal Fidelity, the body that rules on whether faculty teaching and publication stay within the bounds of our confessional statement. That statement includes the standard Reformed-style clause affirming that God "did, by the most wise and holy counsel of his own will, freely and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass, yet without thereby becoming the author of sin or doing violence to the will of the creature." One of our systematic theology professors has just published a monograph defending Molinism, arguing that God's middle knowledge of the true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance, lets God providentially govern the whole of history simply by choosing, from among the "feasible worlds" his middle knowledge discloses to him, which one to actualize.

Two trustees have filed a formal complaint against him, arguing that this account makes God's sovereignty hostage to facts about creaturely counterfactuals that God himself neither decrees nor controls, so that "unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass" is preserved in name only, and that he should be barred from teaching the view except to identify it as an error. Three other faculty members think the complaint simply conflates sovereignty with causal determinism and want him cleared without qualification, noting that Molinism has never been formally condemned even within the Catholic tradition where the dispute originated, and that a number of respected evangelical philosophers hold it today. The dean has asked me to draft the discussion memo for Thursday's meeting, mostly, I suspect, because my dissertation is on the Bañez–Molina dispute and I'm the only committee member who has actually read Molina rather than a secondary summary.

Could you write that memo for me? I'd like it to come down clearly on one of three outcomes: no conflict with the confession, a genuine conflict serious enough to warrant formal proceedings, or some conditional resolution short of that, such as permitting the view so long as it's taught as one live option among others rather than as settled doctrine. I'd also like the reasoning laid out in a form both the professor and the trustees could actually respond to, rather than past each other, and I'd especially like the memo to take the trustees' sovereignty worry seriously on its own terms rather than dismiss it as a mere failure to grasp middle knowledge correctly, since I don't think it's simply a confusion. At the same time, please keep the confessional question distinct from the further metaphysical question of whether counterfactuals of freedom can be true at all prior to God's decree, the so-called grounding objection, since one trustee keeps running the two together, and I'd like the memo to model that distinction rather than reproduce his conflation.
