So we had this incident at work a few weeks back where a deploy got stuck because two of our services couldn't agree on whether a migration had actually finished — classic distributed systems mess. My tech lead described it as "basically the Two Generals Problem": two armies on seperate hills need to attack at the exact same moment to win, but the only way to coordinate is sending messengers through enemy territory who might get captured, so no matter how many "confirmed, see you at dawn" messages go back and forth, neither general can ever be fully sure the other actually got the last one. That comment sent me down a rabbit hole, and I found out economists apparently have a way more rigorous version of the exact same puzzle — some coordination game (I think it's called the "e-mail game," maybe by a guy named Rubinstein?) where two computers auto-send confirmation messages back and forth about whether to jointly do some risky action, except each message has some tiny chance of getting lost along the way.

Here's the part that actually broke my brain a little, and why I'm asking you instead of just closing the wikipedia tab: apparently the math says that no matter how many confirmations successfully go through — 3 or 3 million — the only stable outcome (a "Nash equilibrium," which I also had to look up) is for both sides to play it safe and never do the risky coordinated thing, exactly as if zero messages had gotten through at all. That feels completely backwards from how I'd actually reason about it. If a coworker's service sends me 50 successful acks in a row, I trust that deploy way more than after 0 acks — more (even if imperfect) confirmation should make coordination more likely, not identically unlikely to no communication whatsoever.

Ive got zero formal philosophy training so I might be missing something obvious, but I'd love an actual essay from you (not just a summary of the puzzle, I already have that) that takes a real stance on what's going on. Is the game theory basically right that nothing short of true, literally-perfect common knowledge can ever rationally justify coordinated action — meaning my "more acks = safer" intuition is just wrong about what rational coordination requires? Or does the fact that real engineers (and presumably real generals) coordinate on risky joint actions all the time with only imperfect confirmation mean the whole "common knowledge of rationality plus Nash equilibrium" setup is too brittle to be the right model of rational behavior here, and something else entirely is doing the work when real agents actually manage to coordinate? Please actually pick one and defend it — a conditional answer is fine if that's genuinely where the argument leads (something like "the equilibrium logic is right only once the per-message failure probability is bounded away from zero, but not otherwise"), but I don't want a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" ending.
