A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about Kant's views on animals. She maintained that "no direct duties to animals" was just Kant's roundabout way of saying that how we treat animals is morally irrelevant, while I spent most of dinner trying, with mixed success, to explain the doctrine of indirect duties instead. That argument is still fresh in my mind now that I am sitting down to grade short-answer responses to almost the same question for a seminar where I work as a teaching assistant, and I would like a second, independent assessment of the first response before I mark the rest of the stack.

The question as posed to students was:

Explain Kant's distinction between having a duty 'to' an animal and having a duty 'regarding' an animal. What argument does Kant give for why cruelty to animals is nonetheless morally objectionable, and how does the structure of this argument differ from a view that locates the wrongness of cruelty directly in the animal's own suffering?

Here is the student's answer:

"Kant denies that we can have direct duties to animals, since duties are owed only to beings capable of setting ends according to reason, and animals lack the reason and self-consciousness required to count as 'ends in themselves.' This is why Kant, like Descartes before him, treats animals as essentially unfeeling automata with no genuine inner experience of pain, so that nothing we do to an animal can wrong it directly.

Even so, Kant holds that cruelty to animals is wrong, though only indirectly: mistreating an animal dulls the very disposition of sympathy we rely on in our dealings with other people, so that a habit of cruelty toward animals makes a person more likely to be cruel to fellow humans, which is what actually violates a duty. Kant illustrates the point with the case of an old dog or horse that has served faithfully for years, arguing that turning the animal out once it can no longer work is wrong, though only because doing so reflects and reinforces a callousness that will eventually surface in our dealings with people. On this reading, Kant's position is really just a two-step version of the sentience-based view: animal suffering still matters morally, it just matters instrumentally, by way of its effect on human character, rather than mattering in its own right the way a utilitarian would insist."

Please assign this answer a grade out of 10 and provide point-by-point comments that justify it, flagging anything stated imprecisely, any view misattributed to Kant, or any inference that does not actually follow from what precedes it. Feel free to present part of your assessment as a short rubric, for instance in JSON, scoring components such as the direct/indirect distinction, the accuracy of any comparisons to other thinkers, the textual examples used, and the final characterization of the argument's structure, separately from the overall grade.
