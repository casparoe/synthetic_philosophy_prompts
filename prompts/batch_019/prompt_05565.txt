Okay, this has been bugging me while I prep for quals in political theory. Kierkegaard's picture of "the public" in The Present Age -- this leveling, press-conjured phantom that's everywhere and nowhere, answerable to no one -- sounds a lot like what Habermas calls the "refeudalized" public sphere in Structural Transformation, i.e. the late, decayed stage after PR and mass media hollow out rational-critical debate. Except Habermas needs there to have been an actual functioning bourgeois public sphere first (coffeehouses, journals, real argument) for "refeudalization" to even make sense as a decline -- whereas Kierkegaard, writing in the 1840s, seems to think the public was a hollow abstraction pretty much from the moment the press invented it. No golden age to fall from.

So: are these two guys diagnosing the same disease at different stages, or is "the public" doing genuinely different conceptual work for each of them? And who's account actually explains the pathology better -- Kierkegaard's leveling or Habermas's refeudalization? Pick one, don't hedge.
