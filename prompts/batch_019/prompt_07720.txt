So in my AP Psych class we just started the unit on diagnosis and my teacher went off on this whole tangent about a philosopher named Ian Hacking and something called "looping effects." From what I could scribble down, the idea is that when you classify people as a certain kind -- like "autistic" or "delinquent" -- the people who get classified start to understand and describe themselves through that label and act accordingly, which changes what counts as a typical case of it, which can even get written back into the official diagnostic criteria later on. So unlike sorting rocks or beetles into categories, sorting people into categories can loop back and change the very thing being sorted. My teacher's big example was autism and the whole neurodiversity/self-advocacy movement, but at lunch right after, my friends Theo and Maya got into this whole back-and-forth about whether the same idea applies to something way more relevant to us -- the huge rise in ADHD diagnoses over the last 20ish years -- and honestly i couldn't tell who was actually winning.

Theo's opening move was that looping is basically THE explanation for why ADHD diagnoses exploded: once "ADHD" became a widely known label, kids started noticing their own inattentiveness and describing it that way, parents and teachers got primed to watch for it and refer kids for testing, and the diagnostic criteria got loosened across a few editions of the DSM to match who was actually showing up -- so a big chunk of the "increase" is the loop itself, not doctors finally detecting a fixed underlying rate of something that was always there. Maya shot back that this can't be the main story because ADHD comes out like 70-80% heritable in twin studies, shows up as real differences in brain scans, and responds pretty consistently to stimulant meds, and you don't get that kind of biological signature from something that's mostly a social feedback loop. Theo said that's not actually a real comeback, because Hacking's whole point (he called it something like an "interactive kind," as opposed to a kind that just sits there not caring either way, like a rock) is that a category can have a real biological core AND still have its boundaries and its "typical profile" reshaped by the classifying -- so the heritability number doesn't tell you whether the loop is inflating the diagnosed population specifically at the fuzzy, borderline edges. Maya said fine, but then Theo's original claim -- that looping is "basically the whole explanation" -- was way too strong to begin with, since all he'd actually shown is that maybe some of the growth at the margins is loop-driven, which is alot smaller and less interesting claim than the one he started with, and he was kind of sneaking it in as if he'd proven the bigger one.

Can you go through this round by round and say who's actually right at each step, not just who wins overall? And if you think they're both missing something about how looping effects are even supposed to work (like maybe they're both being sloppy about what it would take to actually show a loop is happening vs. just assuming it), I'd love to hear that too -- we never resolved it before next period and it's been bugging me since.
