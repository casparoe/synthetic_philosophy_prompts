I'm a PhD student working in formal epistemology, and I keep running into the problem of logical omniscience: on the standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge and belief, an agent automatically knows every logical consequence of what she knows, including every valid formula, no matter how hard it would be to actually derive. Two of the most influential strategies for blocking this result are (a) Hintikka's (and later Rantala's) impossible-worlds semantics, which admits non-normal worlds at which classically valid inferences can fail, so that an agent's accessible worlds need not all respect classical logic, and (b) Fagin and Halpern's awareness-based logics, which keep the standard, fully omniscient possible-worlds notion as a model of merely implicit knowledge, and define an agent's explicit knowledge as implicit knowledge restricted to whatever she happens to be antecedently "aware" of. Please compare these two strategies, and say which, in your assessment, offers the more principled diagnosis of why real reasoners fail to know all the logical consequences of their own knowledge, rather than simply stipulating the failure by fiat. Does either genuinely avoid reintroducing logical omniscience at some other level of the model, or does it just relocate the problem? Justify your answer.
