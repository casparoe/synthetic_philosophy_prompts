For the last year or so I've been teaching myself general relativity on the side. My day job is as a software engineer, so rather than working through a textbook in the traditional way, I've mostly been writing small numerical-relativity toy programs -- geodesic integrators, and eventually a basic BSSN evolution code -- to visualize black hole spacetimes. Anyone who has actually tried to numerically evolve Einstein's equations knows that a huge fraction of the effort goes into wrangling "gauge": choosing a lapse function and shift vector, or switching to harmonic coordinates, precisely because the field equations alone never pick out a unique coordinate representation of a given spacetime. That practical headache eventually sent me down a rabbit hole into the philosophy-of-physics literature on diffeomorphism invariance, where I ran into Einstein's old "hole argument" and its revival by Earman and Norton as an argument against spacetime substantivalism.

I don't have any formal training in philosophy, so I may be missing something obvious, but here is what I haven't been able to settle on my own. Two friends from grad school -- one now a philosopher of science, the other a physicist -- gave me flatly contradictory answers when I raised the question recently, and neither could convince the other. Is the hole argument telling us something genuinely new and philosophically deep about the identity of spacetime points, or is it essentially just a special case of the gauge freedom -- surplus mathematical structure outstripping physical content -- that shows up throughout physics, the same basic phenomenon behind the freedom in electromagnetism's vector potential or in Yang-Mills connections generally, just dressed up in relativistic clothing?

Could you compare the hole argument to this more general notion of gauge symmetry in physical theories, and be concrete about it? I'd like you to spell out which features of the hole argument are simply an instance of the general gauge story, and which features, if any, make it a distinctively different or philosophically harder problem than ordinary gauge freedom. I have a hunch it might have something to do with the metric field playing two roles at once in general relativity -- both a dynamical field and the very structure constituting spacetime's geometry -- in a way that has no obvious counterpart for a connection field living on a fixed background, but I would rather you work through whether that hunch actually holds up than just take it on faith.
