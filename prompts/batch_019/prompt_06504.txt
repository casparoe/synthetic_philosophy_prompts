I'm a graduate student taking a seminar this term on philosophical concepts of health and disease, and much of our reading has centered on the split between naturalist accounts of disease -- Boorse's biostatistical theory, on which disease is just a statistically species-atypical failure of some part to perform its contribution to survival and reproduction, judged independently of anyone's values -- and normativist or hybrid accounts, like Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis or Nordenfelt's welfare-based theory of health, which build an evaluative judgment into the concept itself. I also happen to be the graduate-student representative on my university's Committee on Student Leaves and Academic Continuity, and a case in front of us this semester keeps mapping uncomfortably well onto the seminar debate, which is why I'd like help thinking it through properly rather than just voting on instinct.

Our current policy grants an automatic five days of excused absence for the death of an immediate family member, no documentation required, and nothing further. Any additional accommodation -- a formal leave of absence with tuition adjustment and guaranteed re-enrollment, extended insurance coverage, incompletes on a longer-than-usual clock -- currently requires certification of a "diagnosed medical condition" from a clinician, the same bar used for a broken leg or a diagnosed episode of major depression. Since the DSM-5-TR added Prolonged Grief Disorder as an official diagnosis in 2022 -- grief that remains intense and impairing at least twelve months after a death for adults, and that is out of proportion to the person's cultural and religious context (the ICD-11, tellingly, sets the same threshold at six months) -- one faction on the committee wants simply to add PGD to the list of qualifying diagnoses. Our committee's clinical psychologist objects strongly: she thinks this would force grieving students to accept a psychiatric label, with everything that carries for their records and self-understanding, just to be treated decently, and that it quietly redescribes something grief has always been as a brain disorder. A third member wants to sidestep the whole question by creating a separate "extended bereavement leave" with matching protections that requires no diagnosis at all, on the grounds that an academic policy committee has no business adjudicating what does or doesn't count as pathology.

I have to circulate a short discussion memo before we vote next week, and I'd like it to actually take a stance on the underlying question rather than split the difference for the sake of comity. Could you draft that memo for me, in roughly 500-700 words? It should recommend one of the three options above, or a clearly specified alternative; defend that recommendation by taking an explicit position on whether prolonged grief is the kind of thing that can be a disease or disorder at all, rather than treating that as someone else's problem; specify how and when the policy should take effect, including whether it should apply to students already navigating this informally; and present the strongest version of whichever option's case you end up setting aside, rather than a caricature of it.
