"Berkeley's central claim in The Querist is that a nation's wealth consists in the industry of its people, not in gold or silver. Money, he says, functions merely as a 'ticket' or counter for reckoning and transferring the fruits of that industry; it has no value of its own, contrary to the bullionist assumption that a coin's worth comes from its metal content. Since Ireland suffered from a chronic shortage of specie, Berkeley proposed a national bank that would circulate paper credit, arguing that such credit could stimulate commerce just as well as coin, because what drives economic activity is the free circulation of credit rather than any particular metal.

This position is often traced back to Berkeley's running exchange with Hume over the nature of money -- Hume's later description of money as the 'oil' that lubricates trade builds on groundwork Berkeley had already laid down by insisting that money is not itself a form of wealth. Philosophically, the view is a natural extension of Berkeley's immaterialism: just as sensible objects have no existence apart from being perceived, money has no intrinsic 'thinghood' apart from the social practice of using it as a sign of value -- both are, at bottom, appeals to relations and use rather than hidden substances. It's this move that leads historians of economic thought to class Berkeley as a forerunner of chartalism, the theory that money is valuable simply because the state decrees it legal tender."
