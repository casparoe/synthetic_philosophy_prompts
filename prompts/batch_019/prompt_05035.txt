Below is a short essay reconstructing and evaluating David Lewis's argument in his 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" I would like feedback before developing it further.

David Lewis's short 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" opens with a problem that most popular defenses of the doctrine glide past: punishment does not obviously transfer from the guilty to an innocent substitute in the way a debt transfers from a debtor to a guarantor. If I owe you ten pounds, it makes no difference to the justice of the transaction whether I pay it myself or a friend pays it for me; what mattered was only that ten pounds arrive, not who supplied them. Punishment looks different. If a court sentences me to five years and a volunteer serves the sentence in my place, something has gone wrong even though the quantity of suffering imposed is unchanged: I have escaped punishment, and an innocent person has been punished instead of me. Retributivists take this asymmetry between debt and desert to be close to definitional of punishment, which makes the old slogan that "Christ paid the penalty we owed" sound like it trades on an equivocation.

Lewis's response is to separate guilt from liability to punishment. Guilt, he concedes, cannot be transferred: no arrangement can make someone other than the wrongdoer the one who did the wrong. But liability — being the party who may permissibly be made to suffer the penalty — is, on his reconstruction, a distinct and more portable status, closer to the debt case: it can be reassigned by whoever has standing to reassign it, without thereby reassigning guilt. Read this way, penal substitution is not the confused claim that Christ became guilty of human sin, but the more modest claim that liability to punishment was transferred to him, with the transfer authorized by the very party — God, as both legislator and judge — whose authorization would be needed to make any such transfer valid.

This move buys coherence at a price its proponents rarely advertise. If liability can float free of guilt and be reassigned like a promissory note, then what substitutionary punishment vindicates is not the retributive idea that wrongdoers get what they deserve, but only the thinner idea that a fixed quantum of suffering gets imposed on someone, suitably authorized — enough to satisfy a "creditor" conception of justice, but hard to square with the language of desert the doctrine is usually meant to preserve. It also leaves a question Lewis does not fully settle: ordinary transfers of debt require at least the tacit acquiescence of the original debtor, or some background rule dispensing with it. Whose acquiescence is supposed to do that work in the theological case — the sinner's, the substitute's, or the judge's alone — and does the doctrine, as usually stated, actually supply it?

My tentative view is that Lewis succeeds in showing the doctrine is not flatly incoherent, but only by quietly trading its retributive teeth for a bookkeeping notion of justice that most of its defenders would be reluctant to accept if the trade were made explicit.

I would welcome comments on three points: (1) whether this is a faithful reconstruction of Lewis's actual argument, particularly the guilt/liability distinction and the role he gives to authorization; (2) whether the "creditor conception" objection in the third paragraph is sound, or whether Lewis — or later commentators — have the resources to block it; and (3) whether the consent question I end on has already been addressed in the literature, and how convincingly.
