I just rotated onto my university's IACUC (the committee that signs off on animal research protocols) last month, and my first real case turned out to be way more philosophical than I expected. A toxicology lab wants to renew a beagle protocol that currently ends in euthanasia and tissue collection for every dog, healthy or not, and another member is pushing an amendment requiring a "retirement" option -- no terminal collection, dogs get adopted out -- whenever the science doesn't actually need the tissue. Weirdly we're not split on the facts, we're split on why it'd even matter: our committee's resident ethicist keeps citing Kant's line that shooting a dog once its too old to work doesn't wrong the dog itself but reveals something rotten in the shooter, so for her this is about protecting the researchers, not the dog. Someone else read Korsgaard's Fellow Creatures over break and now insists the whole "indirect duty" framing is outdated Kant and the dog's own interests should count directly. Meanwhile our actual approval checklist is just pain-vs-scientific-benefit and has nothing to say about painlessly killing a healthy, adoptable animal purely for convenience. Can you draft the memo I have to circulate before next week's vote -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions on the retirement amendment -- that actually takes a position and deals honestly with the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't land on, including, if you go Kantian, why the logic wouldn't also commit us to retiring every healthy lab rat?
