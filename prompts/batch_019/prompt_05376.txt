I write a science-and-ideas column, and I'm preparing a long-form interview with a philosopher of science for a piece on how physicists and statisticians justify talking about the probability of a single, unrepeatable event -- the sort of claim people make casually when they say a particular radioactive atom has a 50 percent chance of decaying within the hour, or a given patient a 30 percent chance of relapse. My guest defends a single-case propensity theory of probability: the view, descending from Popper, that such a probability is a real physical disposition of the "chance setup" itself to produce the outcome -- not a frequency in disguise, as the frequentist would have it, and not merely a measure of someone's ignorance or confidence, as the subjectivist would have it. In print she has gotten very good at deflecting technical objections with some version of the line "the formalism can be patched; the physics can't," implying that whatever the probability calculus seems to require, the underlying single-case tendency is the real explanatory fact and objectors are merely fussing over bookkeeping.

I'd like seven or eight interview questions that don't just let her restate the propensity view more emphatically, and that make that deflection progressively harder to reach for. I want at least one question on Humphreys' paradox: the objection that if propensities are to satisfy the ordinary probability calculus, Bayes' theorem forces into existence "inverse" conditional propensities -- the propensity of the throw, given that it landed six -- that seem to run backward against the causal direction a propensity is supposed to have. I want at least one question on whether propensity theory actually escapes the reference-class problem she likes to cite against frequentism, given that any single case can be redescribed as an instance of many different repeatable setups with different propensities, so that picking out "the" relevant setup looks just as description-relative as picking a reference class. And I want at least one question on how single-case propensities are supposed to feed David Lewis's Principal Principle, including whether she has anything better than Lewis's own patch for the "undermining" problem he called his own "big bad bug," in which a chance process can produce a run of outcomes statistically at odds with the very propensities that were meant to generate it. For each of these three, please add a follow-up question keyed to the specific dodge or repair she is most likely to reach for, rather than a generic "how would you answer your critics." Close the list with one question asking her, plainly, what kind of empirical or theoretical discovery would actually count as evidence against propensity theory itself, as opposed to evidence that its formalism merely needs adjusting.
