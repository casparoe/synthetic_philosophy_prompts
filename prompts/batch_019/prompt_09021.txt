Ok so I'm about halfway through a novel about a small lake town that, over about fifteen years, flips from a year-round fishing and logging community into a seasonal enclave for second-home owners, and to make the "tipping" itself feel mechanically real rather than just "rich people ruined it," I've been reading up on Thomas Schelling's old segregation model. He originally built it for racial housing patterns, but the mechanism transfers fine: give everyone a pretty mild preference (nobody wants to be totally surrounded by "their own kind," just not badly outnumbered nearby) plus a rule where unhappy people move, and a fully mixed starting map reliably tips into near-total separation, even though nobody involved ever chose or wanted that end state. I described this at my writing group and two people, Dana and Marcus, ended up in an actual argument about what it shows philosophically, and it's been bugging me since because neither one seems clearly wrong.

Marcus's position: this is basically a knockdown case for methodological individualism. You can "grow" the whole tipping pattern out of nothing but individual-level facts -- everybody's personal threshold plus the move-if-unhappy rule -- run it forward and the macro pattern falls out automatically, no extra ingredient required. If a purely individual-level simulation reproduces the outcome exactly, theres no explanatory work left for anything "structural" to do; calling the town's fate emergent or systemic just redescribes facts that are, underneath, entirely about individuals. Dana's comeback: the interesting discovery isn't "individuals cause things" (fine, sure), it's that there's a stable tipping threshold -- apparently people who study this call it a phase transition -- that shows up much the same way across totally different populations with totally different specific preferences, as long as density and the spread of thresholds fall in a certain range. That regularity is stated over population-level variables, not over facts about Marcus or any particular resident, and it's that regularity doing the real explanatory and predictive work -- which makes the model a great case FOR holism, not against it. Marcus replied that "indifferent to which individuals realize it" can't be the mark of holism, since plenty of uncontroversially reducible stuff works that way too -- water boils at the same temperature no matter which specific molecules you use, and nobody thinks that makes the boiling point a holistic fact. Dana said the water case is disanalogous, because there we have a complete, independent micro-story (statistical mechanics) that the macro law is simply derivable from with nothing left over, whereas nobody can actually state the tipping threshold in pure individual-psychology vocabulary without reaching for population-level concepts -- density, the shape of the threshold distribution, spatial layout -- that aren't facts about any single individual, so the "it's all individual facts really" story was never self-sufficient to begin with. At that point someone changed the subject to whether my ferry-dock scene in chapter nine is too on-the-nose, so it never got resolved.
