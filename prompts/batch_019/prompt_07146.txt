I've been reporting a piece on "masking" in autism — the way a lot of autistic people learn to suppress stimming, force eye contact, script small talk, so they can pass as neurotypical at work or school — and while reading around for background I fell into a totally different rabbit hole: the metaphysics of dispositions, where "mask" turns out to already be a technical term. In that literature the simple idea that being fragile just means "would break if struck" runs into trouble from two directions: finks, where the disposition itself flips the instant you test it (the standard case is a dead wire hooked up to a device that senses contact and makes the wire live for exactly as long as you're touching it, then dead again right after — so it satisfies "if touched, current would flow" without the wire ever really being live), and masks, where the disposition and whatever's underlying it stay completely intact but something else intervenes to block the manifestation (a fragile glass so carefully bubble-wrapped that it never breaks no matter how many times you drop it).

I mentioned this half-jokingly to two friends from grad school over drinks last week — Kira, who's ABD in philosophy, and Devon, a clinical psychologist who works with autistic adults — and it turned into a real argument that's been bugging me sense. Kira's opening move: autism masking is a textbook mask in exactly this sense, the disposition to stim or react is fully there, untouched, just blocked from manifesting by a further factor (conscious suppression), same structure as the bubble-wrapped glass. Devon pushed back — masking is widely reported, including by autistic adults themselves, to cause real burnout over time, exhaustion, loss of skills people used to have, and if the "blocking" factor can degrade the very thing it's supposed to be leaving untouched, that sounds a lot more like a fink than a mask. Kira's reply was that this is a synchronic point being answered with a diachronic one: at any single moment, in any single conversation, the disposition is intact and merely masked, and the fact that repeated masking wears something else down over months doesn't turn that moment into a fink after the fact. Devon's comeback was that the whole apparatus was built for cases like sugar cubes, where there's a clean "basis" (molecular structure) that either survives the test or doesn't, whereas a disposition to stim doesn't obviously have any comparably clean basis independent of the social situation itself, so importing mask-talk here smuggles in a "true self hiding underneath" picture that may not be doing real metaphysical work. Kira's closer was that plenty of ordinary physical dispositions turn out to be just as relational once you look closely (soluble in what, exactly, and under what conditions?), so this isn't some special problem for the psychological case — Devon's just noticed a general feature of what she kept calling the "reformed conditional analysis" (I think from a David Lewis paper, but don't hold me to that). Nobody backed down and I had to go home.

Could you go through this move by move — Kira's opening, Devon's burnout objection, Kira's synchronic/diachronic reply, Devon's "no clean basis" point, and Kira's closing "relational solubility" move — and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just overall? And if you think there's a more accurate way to describe what's going on with autism masking than either "mask" or "fink," one that both of them are missing, I'd genuinely like to hear it.
