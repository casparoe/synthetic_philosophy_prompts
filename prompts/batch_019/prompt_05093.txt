Mechanistic interpretability research often reports that a network contains a specific "feature" or "circuit" -- for instance, a direction isolated by a sparse autoencoder that activates on a particular concept, or a pair of attention heads jointly implementing an algorithm such as induction. Because of superposition, in which a network represents more sparse features than it has dimensions by packing them into overlapping, non-orthogonal directions, individual units and directions are often genuinely polysemantic, and different decompositions of the very same activations can yield different, equally predictive sets of "features." This recalls a familiar dispute in philosophy of mind over whether attributing a representation to part of a system is a discovery about what the system itself uses to do its job, or instead a description that is useful and predictively powerful for external observers without being determinately true or false of the system itself.
