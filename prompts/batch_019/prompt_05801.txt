I sit on the small committee that picks the winner of our company's yearly 'Ethical Courage Award' -- comes with a $5,000 bonus, a plaque, and a few minutes on stage at the all-hands meeting, and, per the award's own one-paragraph charter, it exists to recognize someone who showed 'integrity and courage, often at real personal cost, in upholding the standards we hold ourselves to.' This year's clear frontrunner, nominated by her whole department, is a senior accountant who flagged that her director had been quietly reclassifying expenses to hit quarterly targets, which led to an internal investigation, a restatement, and the director getting fired. Good outcome, nobody on the committee disputes that part.

The wrinkle showed up during the quiet reference checks we always do before finalizing a winner. Two people on her team told us, separately, that she only went to compliance after she found out the external auditors had pulled the relevant ledger for an unrelated sampling reason and were likely to catch the discrepancy themselves within the month anyway, and that she'd told a friend at the time she was mainly scared of being the one left holding the bag later, since she'd signed off on some of those same numbers herself without asking questions when they first came across her desk. (I only ended up on this committee because I complained loudly last year about how toothless it seemed -- lesson learned.)

Now we're genuinely split and meet again next week to vote, with no precedent to lean on since this is only the third year we've run the award. About half the committee thinks what matters is what she did and what it led to: she used the proper channel, it worked, and rewarding it tells everyone else in her position to come forward instead of staying quiet, whatever her private reasons were. The other half point out the award explicitly isn't just an 'outcome' bonus -- we already have a separate spot-bonus program for that -- and think handing this particular citation to someone who reportedly acted mainly to cover herself makes the whole thing a bit of a joke, especially next to another nominee this cycle who flagged a much smaller issue and lost a friendship over it with nothing personal to gain from it. I dont think either side is being dumb, and I go back and forth depending on the day. Can you draft the memo I'll actually circulate before the vote -- something that lands on one real recommendation (give her the award as written, give a scaled-down version of it, like the bonus without the citation language and the stage time, or pass over her this cycle for the other nominee), and that takes the strongest version of the losing side's argument seriously instead of just waving at it?
