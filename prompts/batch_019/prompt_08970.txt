I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about how Victorian thinkers handled testimonial evidence for phenomena that mainstream science dismissed a priori, and one of my case studies is Henry Sidgwick's involvement with the Society for Psychical Research. I've been reading his inaugural address as the Society's first president, delivered at its first general meeting on 17 July 1882 and printed in the opening volume of the Society's Proceedings, and I keep getting stuck on one stretch of it. My advisor thinks I'm over-reading a few sentences that are really just rhetorical flourish, but I'd like a second opinion before deciding whether to build part of my argument around this passage. Here it is in full:

"As regards the question of credibility, the important point to bear in mind is that every additional witness who, as De Morgan said, has a fair stock of credit to draw upon, is an important gain. Though his credit alone is not likely to suffice for the demand that is made on it, his draft will help. For we must not expect any decisive effect in the direction at which we primarily aim, on the common sense of mankind, from any single piece of evidence, however complete it has been made. Scientific incredulity has been so long in growing, and has so many and so strong roots, that we shall only kill it, if we are able to kill it at all as regards any of those questions, by burying it alive under a heap of facts. We must keep "pegging away," as Lincoln said; we must accumulate fact upon fact, and add experiment upon experiment, and, I should say, not wrangle too much with incredulous outsiders about the conclusiveness of any one, but trust to the mass of evidence for conviction. The highest degree of demonstrative force that we can obtain out of any single record of investigation is, of course, limited by the trustworthiness of the investigator. We have done all that we can when the critic has nothing left to allege except that the investigator is in the trick. But when he has nothing else left to allege he will allege that."

What I can't settle is the actual logical shape of the argument here, as opposed to a sentence-by-sentence paraphrase, so three things in particular would help. First, Sidgwick was elsewhere quite careful about probability and evidence, so the De Morgan "stock of credit"/"draft" metaphor presumably means something specific: is he treating independent witnesses as jointly raising the probability of the reported fact, in something like the way several individually unreliable witnesses can corroborate each other, or is the metaphor doing something looser, especially given that he immediately concedes that no witness's "credit alone is not likely to suffice"? Second, how does the "burying it alive under a heap of facts" image relate to Hume's argument in "Of Miracles" that no accumulation of testimony can outweigh the antecedent improbability of a violation of the laws of nature: is Sidgwick implicitly rejecting Hume's structure by suggesting testimony really can accumulate past a very low prior, or is he only describing what it would take to change public opinion as a matter of psychology, leaving Hume's epistemological point untouched? Third, the closing move -- driving the critic down to the single remaining line that "the investigator is in the trick" -- reads to me as though it's meant to carry real argumentative weight, as if exhausting the alternative explanations should make disbelief unreasonable, but I can't tell whether that's a legitimate inference or an elimination trick that quietly assumes the list of alternatives was complete to begin with. I'd appreciate help working through how these three pieces connect as a single line of reasoning rather than three separate observations.
