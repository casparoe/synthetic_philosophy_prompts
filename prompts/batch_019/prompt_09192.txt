I'm a poli theory PhD student and this semester's seminar on medieval political thought spent two whole sessions on Aquinas's writings on tyrannicide, which turns out to be way messier than the "aquinas thought killing tyrants was sometimes fine" one-liner you get in undergrad. If I'm remembering the seminar discussion right, in his commentary on the Sentences he splits tyrants into two kinds: a tyrant "by defect of title" (a straight-up usurper with no legitimate claim to rule at all) versus a tyrant "by way of exercise" (someone who holds power legitimately but abuses it once in office). For the first kind, he seems fine with a private citizen killing the guy if there's no superior around to appeal to — he even cites Ehud assassinating Eglon in Judges as a praiseworthy example. For the second kind though, private individuals are NOT supposed to take matters into their own hands; it has to go through the community or some higher public authority. Then in De Regimine Principum he gets even more skittish about tyrannicide in general, warning that if you let private people decide for themselves when a ruler is "tyrant enough" to kill, ambitious men will just assassinate perfectly decent kings under that pretext, so mostly his advice is: endure it, pray, and wait for the legitimate authority (or God) to sort it out.

What me and my study group can't agree on is how this framework would handle a case Aquinas obviously never had in mind: a president who wins a genuinely free election (so, clean title, zero usurpation by his lights) and then, once installed, hollows out the courts, jails independent journalists, and cancels the next election to stay in power indefinitely — with no pope or emperor above him to appeal to, and with the domestic bodies that would normally count as "the public authority" (courts, legislature, electoral commission) already captured by him. Based on the actual reasoning Aquinas gives rather than the pop-summary version: would he really be committed to saying this guy is still just a "tyrant by exercise," so that no private person — not a lone assassin, not a general plotting a coup, not a foreign government — may licitly move against him on their own initiative? Or does converting a legitimate title into something you keep only through ongoing fraud and coercion push him back into "defect of title" territory in Aquinas's eyes? And seperately, does Aquinas's own idea that "the community" can depose a ruler it originally appointed give any principled room for something like a mass popular uprising, as opposed to a single private actor, even once the formal institutions are gone? I'd like an actual reconstruction of how his categories and reasons bear on this, not just an "Aquinas allowed some resistance to tyranny" gloss.
