Since retiring from a career in biology I have been working through Moore's Principia Ethica, and one move in the argument has me stuck. In Chapter III, on hedonism, Moore applies his method of judging a thing's value in absolute isolation: he asks us to compare two unperceived worlds -- one exceedingly beautiful, one an utter heap of filth -- and concludes we should still judge the beautiful one better, as proof that beauty carries intrinsic value apart from anyone's enjoyment of it. Yet in Chapter VI, on the Ideal, personal affection and the appreciation of beauty turn out to be organic wholes -- true cognition of a person's or object's real qualities joined to a fitting emotion -- whose value, by his principle of organic unities, vastly exceeds the sum of the qualities' modest value and the feeling's own negligible or negative value; an affection resting on a mistaken view of its object, he treats as worth little, if anything. Having spent a career studying properties that emerge only at the level of the whole organism, I would like a careful account of how this isolation test, which gives beauty only a small stand-alone value, coheres with the organic-unity reasoning that makes appreciation and affection so much more valuable and so much more fragile -- and what it implies about whether Moore thought we do more good by filling the world with beautiful and admirable things, or by cultivating people who perceive them truly.
