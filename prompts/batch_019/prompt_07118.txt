At work, a couple of colleagues who follow AI-safety and effective-altruism discussions keep bringing up "Pascal's mugging" whenever someone proposes spending resources on an intervention that has an extremely low probability of paying off but an enormous payoff if it does (existential risk from advanced AI is the usual example). I understand the basic thought experiment: a stranger claims he can create or destroy some almost incomprehensibly large number of lives unless you hand him five dollars, and because the claimed stakes are so vast, ordinary expected-value reasoning seems to recommend paying him even though you are nearly certain he is lying. Someone in that conversation mentioned, almost as an aside, that this is essentially a modern, secular descendant of Pascal's Wager, which I only know in its textbook form: you should believe in God because the expected payoff of belief is infinite while the cost of belief is finite. I have no formal background in philosophy or decision theory, so I would like a more rigorous account of how these two arguments actually relate to one another, rather than the loose analogy I have been working with.

Specifically, could you compare the two arguments in some depth: what exactly changes structurally when you move from the Wager to the mugging (for instance, in how many competing hypotheses are on the table, in whether the probability is forced to shrink as the claimed stakes grow, or in who is making the claim and why), and why the mugging is usually treated as a general problem for expected-utility decision theory rather than just a new argument in the philosophy of religion? I would also like your judgment on which of the two arguments poses the deeper challenge to expected-utility maximization as a normative standard of rationality, and whether the standard proposed fixes for Pascal's mugging (bounded utility functions, leverage penalties, priors that penalize increasingly outlandish claims, and so on) would also neutralize the Wager, or whether the two problems really call for separate solutions. Please argue for your conclusion rather than simply asserting it.
