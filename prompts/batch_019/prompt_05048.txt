I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly on formal epistemology, and I co-run a small interdisciplinary reading group with a couple of people from the physics department; this term we've been working through Bostrom's writing on observation selection effects -- self-sampling versus self-indication reasoning, the doomsday argument, the presumptuous philosopher, and the rest of that cluster of puzzles. For last week's session I wrote up a new case, deliberately not one of the standard textbook examples, for people to apply the framework to, and two of the regulars, Marcus (a cosmology postdoc) and Dana (another philosophy PhD student), ended up in a disagreement the rest of us couldn't settle. I'd like an outside, careful adjudication of their exchange, since I don't fully trust my own read, having written the case myself.

Here's the setup I gave them. Stipulate that fundamental physics has narrowed the field to two live models, Small and Vast, which agree on every currently observable phenomenon except the value of one undetermined parameter that a proposed $2 billion neutrino observatory could pin down directly within a decade. By stipulation, this same parameter also fixes the total number of observers who will ever have existed over the history of the universe: about a trillion (10^12) under Small, about a sextillion (10^21) under Vast. Physicists currently regard Small and Vast as equally likely, 50/50, on ordinary theoretical grounds that have nothing to do with anthropics. The exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Marcus: Even before the observatory returns any data, we should already assign something like 99.9999...% credence to Vast. Conditional on the fact that we exist at all, Vast makes our existence about a billion times more likely than Small does, and updating on the fact of your own existence is just ordinary Bayesian conditionalization. If the only thing at stake were Small versus Vast, the observatory would barely be worth funding.

(2) Dana: That's exactly Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher, and it should make you doubt the inference rule rather than trust the conclusion. You also don't get to reach for this move only when it's convenient: the main reason people find self-indication reasoning attractive in the first place is that it cancels the doomsday argument's conclusion that humanity is probably near its end. You can't keep the comfort of dodging doomsday and then act surprised when the very same mechanism tells a funding agency to stand down.

(3) Marcus: There's no tension; I'm happy to bite both bullets, or neither, independently of each other. The presumptuous philosopher only looks absurd because we aren't used to letting facts about our own existence carry evidential weight -- once you grant that our existence really is more likely under Vast, biting the bullet is just following the argument, not a reductio of it. And I don't even need to bite the doomsday bullet to be consistent here: I could instead deny that "all humans" is the right reference class for self-sampling, which blocks doomsday on independent grounds without touching anything I said about Small and Vast.

(4) Dana: Here's a version of this that isn't just another bullet to bite. Add a third model, Unbounded, on which the same parameter implies infinitely many observers, and give it even a tiny prior credence. Self-indication reasoning doesn't then output a large-but-finite verdict the way it did for Vast; it either produces no stable answer at all or simply hands Unbounded all the credence, regardless of how small a prior it started with. That's not merely a surprising conclusion to accept -- it's a sign that the rule stops working exactly when you need it most, and it means your 99.9999% figure for Vast was never entitled to that precision to begin with, since any properly humble prior over "how many observers" has to put some weight on the unbounded possibility somewhere.

I'd like you to go through this move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner.
