After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent my retirement working through the philosophy of science, and I now co-host a small podcast on the subject with two former colleagues. Next week's guest defends a similarity-based ("likeness") account of verisimilitude — the view that emerged once Popper's own truth-content definition was shown, by the Tichý–Miller result, to be incapable of ranking any two false theories, and which instead measures a theory's closeness to the truth by the structural resemblance between its models and the actual state of affairs. Draft eight interview questions that go beyond having her restate the position. I want at least one question each on why her particular measure escapes the fate that befell Popper's, on the language-dependence objection — that a verisimilitude ranking can be reversed simply by redescribing the same states of affairs in a different but logically equivalent basic vocabulary — and on whether her apparatus, applied to a real case from the history of physics such as Bohr's model of the atom versus Thomson's "plum pudding" model, actually delivers the verdict historians of science already expect. For each, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close with one question asking what finding could count as evidence against her specific measure, as opposed to the whole project of formalizing truthlikeness.
