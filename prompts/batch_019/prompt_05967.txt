Question as given to students: "Kant says the shopkeeper who never overcharges even his most inexperienced customers acts rightly but not from duty, so his honesty has no moral worth. Using Nomy Arpaly's discussion of Huckleberry Finn, explain why some philosophers think this picture of moral worth is too restrictive, and say whether Julia Markovits's coincident reasons thesis improves on it."

Here's the answer, copied exactly (typos included):

"Kant's shopkeeper doesn't cheat his customers, but only because honesty is good for business in a competitive market - a shopkeeper known for cheating people would lose customers. So the action conforms with duty but Kant says it has no moral worth, since its motivated by self-interest rather than respect for the moral law, and we can tell this bc the very same action is what a merely prudent shopkeeper would do anyway, duty or no duty.

Arpaly brings up Huck Finn to push back on this kind of view. Huck helps Jim escape even though he was raised to think slavery is fine and that helping a slave escape is a sin against Jim's owner. As the novel goes on Huck comes to realize, more and more clearly, that Jim is a full person who deserves his loyalty, and by the time he tears up the letter turning Jim in he's acting on this new, correct belief that helping Jim is the right thing to do. Arpaly thinks this is still admirable even tho it isn't done from a principle Huck could have stated at the start, which is a problem for a Kantian view that ties moral worth to consciously acting from duty.

Markovits's coincident reasons thesis is supposed to fix this: an act has moral worth when the agent is motivated by her belief that the act is the morally right one, and that belief happens to be true, so her motivating reason and the actual justifying reason coincide. This is meant to help with Huck because once he takes Jim's side he's motivated by the (correct) belief that helping Jim is right, so on Markovits's view his action still counts as having moral worth, even tho he could never have stated Kant's categorical imperative."
