I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language currently drafting a seminar paper on theories of vagueness, and I've run into a point that my advisor says reflects a real confusion on my part, though I haven't yet managed to see exactly where I've gone wrong. In the secondary literature, epistemicism (associated above all with Timothy Williamson) and supervaluationism are routinely presented as the two leading approaches that preserve classical logic in the face of the Sorites paradox, in contrast to more revisionary options such as degree theories or paraconsistent treatments of vague predicates. Authors working in the supervaluationist tradition that follows Kit Fine's precisificational semantics are especially emphatic on this point: every instance of excluded middle, "Fa ∨ ¬Fa," comes out true ("supertrue") even when a is a borderline case of F, and every classically valid argument form remains valid under the supervaluationist consequence relation.

Given how much weight this classical-logic-preservation point is made to bear, it seems to me that supervaluationism and epistemicism collapse into the very same theory under two different descriptions: both leave classical logic fully intact, so the only thing left for them to disagree about is purely terminological — whether to call the sharp cutoff in a vague predicate's extension "indeterminate" (the supervaluationist's preferred term) or "unknown" (the epistemicist's). When I put this to my advisor, she agreed that students often conflate the two views for exactly this reason, but insisted that they disagree about something substantive rather than merely verbal, without telling me what that something is. Could you help me see precisely where my equivalence claim breaks down: which classical principle, if any, does supervaluationism actually give up, and why doesn't that concession show up as a failure of any of the classically valid schemata for which the view is usually praised?
