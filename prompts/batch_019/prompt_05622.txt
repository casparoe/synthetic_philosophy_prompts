Last spring I went through a stretch of what a doctor eventually described to me as depersonalization: for a couple of months, ordinary experience — coffee, sunlight, conversation with my kids — seemed to be happening at one remove, as though I were watching a competent simulation of my life rather than living it. It passed, and I know better than to treat a temporary dissociative episode as evidence about the metaphysics of consciousness in general. But it left me with a question I haven't been able to shake: the felt quality of experience is supposed to be the one thing that cannot come and go independently of the experience itself, and yet mine seemed to, while my outward behavior and even my own introspective reports carried on much as before. Reading about illusionism afterward — Keith Frankish's and Daniel Dennett's version of it especially — was the first time an academic position on consciousness felt like it was describing something I had lived through, rather than an abstract puzzle.

As I understand it, illusionism is a stranger and more radical claim than simply "we are often mistaken about our experiences": it holds that phenomenal consciousness itself — the private, intrinsic, ineffable "feel" that pain or red is supposed to have, over and above any functional or representational role — does not exist at all, even though introspection insists that it does. On this view there is no hard problem of explaining how the brain produces qualia, only an "illusion problem" of explaining why a purely physical system would represent itself as having qualia it doesn't have. What I can't get past is that this isn't a claim about zombies or robots out there somewhere; it's a claim about my introspective reports right now, including the report that something changed in me last spring and then changed back.

I would like you to write me a short story — the kind of idea-driven fiction people like Greg Egan or Ted Chiang write, rather than a mood piece — that takes strong illusionism seriously as a premise and follows one character actually living with its consequences, rather than debating them at a seminar table. I would rather you not reach for a "the machine discovers it has feelings after all" ending; that resolution quietly assumes the very thing illusionism denies — that phenomenality is a further fact waiting to be switched on or discovered. Your character doesn't have to be human, or even singular, and you can choose whatever setting suits you; I am more interested in the texture of the idea than in any particular plot. Something in the 1000-1500 word range would be ideal, and if the story can find room to gesture at the difference between denying that experience happens at all and denying only that it has the properties introspection presents it as having, that would speak directly to a distinction I keep struggling to hold onto.
