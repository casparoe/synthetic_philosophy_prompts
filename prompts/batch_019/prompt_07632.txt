I'm midway through a historical novel set in Geneva in 1758, the year Rousseau published his Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles to head off d'Alembert's Encyclopédie proposal that the city acquire a public theater. One of my viewpoint characters, a pastor's literate daughter, finds Rousseau's case against the stage genuinely persuasive: that actors corrode their own character by professionally counterfeiting passions not their own, night after night, for pay; that a theater audience is a purchased, passive crowd in a way Geneva's own outdoor civic festivals are not; and that the profession is especially corrosive to the women who would have to be brought in to staff it. Three years later, in my next chapter, she obtains a copy of Julie, ou la Nouvelle Héloïse -- by many accounts the best-selling novel of the century, written by that same Rousseau, and prefaced by his own strange, ambivalent warning to potential readers about the dangers of the very book they are holding. She has to decide, out loud, to a skeptical older cousin, whether she has been made a fool of.

I don't want the standard literary-historical verdict, which is that he was simply a hypocrite -- I've read that a dozen times already, and it's too easy for my purposes. What I need is the best case for the other side. Please construct the strongest available argument, built strictly from Rousseau's own premises rather than from goodwill toward him, that there is a principled, non-ad-hoc distinction between what he condemns in the Lettre and what he does in Julie. At a minimum, it should engage with: his distinction between collective public spectatorship and solitary silent reading; his claim that the specific offense of acting is the bodily, professional impersonation of a passion one does not feel -- an instance of the être/paraître, or being-versus-seeming, diagnosis that runs through his work -- as against a novelist's, or a letter-writing character's, address to a reader's private imagination; and what to make of that hedging preface to Julie, the Entretien sur les romans, which reads less like false modesty than like a genuine attempt to contain the damage. Where the argument has to strain against Rousseau's own text to hold together, say so rather than smoothing it over -- my character is sincere and well-read, not naïve, and she would notice the seams.
