And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Under the BHK interpretation, a proof of A∧B is a pair consisting of a proof of A and a proof of B; a proof of A∨B is a proof of one of the disjuncts together with the information of which one; a proof of A→B is a construction that turns any proof of A into a proof of B; and since ¬A just abbreviates A→⊥, a proof of ¬A is a construction turning any proof of A into a proof of absurdity.

Excluded middle fails as a general schema because the clause for disjunction demands that to assert A∨¬A we have, for every A, either an outright proof of A or a construction refuting it — and for open problems we simply have neither yet, so we aren't entitled to assert the disjunction. Double-negation elimination fails for a related reason: a proof of ¬¬A only shows that assuming ¬A is untenable, it doesn't hand us a direct construction of A itself, and BHK gives no recipe for manufacturing one from a mere refutation-of-a-refutation. The other direction, A→¬¬A, does hold intuitionistically: given a proof of A and any alleged proof of ¬A, we apply the latter to the former to get a proof of ⊥, so a proof of A always yields a proof of ¬¬A.

Markov's Principle says that for decidable A(x) — i.e. where ∀x(A(x)∨¬A(x)) — we have ¬¬∃xA(x)→∃xA(x). The motivating picture is a search: since A is decidable we can mechanically test A(0), A(1), A(2)... in order, and ¬¬∃xA(x) rules out the search failing forever, so it must eventually turn up a witness. This is the same broadly algorithmic spirit running through intuitionism generally, and it's why Brouwer treated the principle as a legitimate piece of intuitionistic reasoning rather than as an extra assumption bolted on from outside — it doesn't conjure a witness out of nowhere the way unrestricted excluded middle would, it just trusts that a terminating search terminates. Consequently, Markov's Principle is actually derivable in intuitionistic logic (and in Heyting Arithmetic) unlike full excluded middle, which is part of why intuitionists have never treated it as controversial in the way classical existence proofs are."
