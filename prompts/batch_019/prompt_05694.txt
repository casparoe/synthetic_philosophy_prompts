So I foster dogs for a small no-kill-focused rescue in my town and got roped onto the board last year even though I never went to school for anything like this, I just showed up to enough work days that people started trusting me. At our next meeting we're voting on whether to formally commit, in writing, to hitting a 90% "live release rate" (the percentage of animals that leave the shelter alive rather than being euthanized) so we qualify for a big matching grant from a national funder, and the director wants her own annual bonus tied to that same number too. Most of the board loves the idea because its a clean number for fundraising letters and finally gives everybody something concrete to rally around instead of just good intentions.

I'm the one holding things up, because a couple years back I read about something called Goodhart's Law (started as a British economics thing about money supply targets, I think, but people apply it now to everything from standardized testing to how AI systems get trained) - the basic idea being that the moment you turn a measurement into a target, people quietly start optimizing the measurement instead of whatever it was actually supposed to stand in for. And I've already watched a mini version of this happen at our own shelter: a volunteer coordinator once steered a family toward "fostering" a dog with a bite history rather than having it logged as a shelter euthanasia, purely so it wouldnt hit the books that way. If we bolt a hard 90% target onto the director's bonus and our public reputation, I keep picturing that kind of quiet reclassifying, or just turning harder cases away at intake, becoming the norm instead of the rare exception, without one single person doing anything anyone could point to as dishonest.

Could you write the memo I'd bring to the board? I want it to land on an actual recommendation - adopt the pledge as proposed, reject it, or adopt some modified version - with a timeframe attached, not a wishy-washy "there are good points on both sides." It also can't strawman the director's case, because the accountability and fundraising argument is genuinely strong and this shelter really does need the money. I just want whoever reads it to walk away taking seriously that a single public number can quietly bend everyone's behavior even when nobody involved is acting in bad faith, and to get a real answer on what, if anything, we should do differently so we get the benefits of having a target without walking straight into that trap.
