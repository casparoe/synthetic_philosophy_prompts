At work I've been prototyping a formal-verification layer for a distributed consensus protocol, and that sent me down a rabbit hole into epistemic logic, in particular the classic "Reasoning About Knowledge" line of work (Fagin, Halpern, Moses, and Vardi) that models what each node in a system "knows" using possible-worlds semantics along the lines of S5 modal logic. Pretty quickly I ran into the standard objection that this kind of Kripke-style treatment of knowledge makes every agent "logically omniscient": since an agent's knowledge is defined as whatever holds across all the worlds consistent with its information, the model automatically credits it with knowing every logical and mathematical truth, plus every logical consequence of anything it already knows, regardless of how many inference steps that would actually take to derive. That's clearly a bad fit for real bounded reasoners, whether we're talking about humans or a node running consensus with a fixed CPU budget.

My instinct for fixing this in our own system was to drop the binary "knows / doesn't know" predicate altogether and instead give each node a probability distribution over the same space of possible worlds, updated by Bayesian conditionalization whenever a new message comes in. The idea was that once belief comes in degrees rather than as an all-or-nothing set, there's no longer a single, deductively-closed collection of "known" propositions that has to contain every tautology and every consequence of its members, so the logical omniscience problem should simply disappear along with the binary framework that produced it. Before I write this up as the rationale for redesigning our epistemic model, I'd like a sanity check on the underlying philosophy: does replacing knowledge with Bayesian credence genuinely dissolve the logical omniscience problem, or is there a reason it would just resurface in the probabilistic setting too?
