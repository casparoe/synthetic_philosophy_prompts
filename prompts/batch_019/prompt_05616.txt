Last night a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about whether Gödel's ontological argument actually proves anything, or whether it is quietly undone from within by the so-called modal collapse problem. Neither of us convinced the other. This morning I have a related but separate task, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on it: I'm grading take-home exams for a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion, and one question asked students to reconstruct Gödel's proof and evaluate an objection to it. Here is the question, worth 20 points:

"Reconstruct Gödel's modal ontological argument for the existence of God, stating its key definitions and axioms and showing how the conclusion is meant to follow from them. Then present one serious objection to the argument and assess how damaging it is."

And here is one student's answer:

"Gödel's proof works with a few primitive definitions. First, a property is called 'positive' if, intuitively, it is a property that makes its bearer better or more perfect to have than to lack (Axiom 1 tells us that for any property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive). Axiom 2 says positive properties are closed under strict implication: if a positive property entails another property, that other property is positive too. Gödel then defines something as 'God-like' if it possesses all and only the positive properties. Axiom 3 asserts that being God-like is itself a positive property, and Axiom 4 says that if a property is positive, it is necessarily positive.

From these, Gödel shows that positive properties are possibly exemplified (using Axioms 1 and 2), and since being God-like is positive, it follows that possibly something is God-like. He then introduces the notion of an essence: a property is an essence of an object if the object has it and it entails every property the object has necessarily. He shows that God-likeness is an essence of any God-like object, appealing to Axioms 1 and 4. Axiom 5 states that necessary existence — having an exemplified essence in every possible world — is a positive property, so any God-like being has necessary existence as part of its essence. Putting this together: since it is possible that a God-like being exists, and God-likeness entails necessary existence, a God-like being must actually exist necessarily; once its existence is possible, its being the kind of thing that exists necessarily guarantees that it does exist, in this world as in every other.

The most serious objection is the one Gaunilo raised: that the same reasoning could be used to 'prove' the existence of all sorts of things, such as a necessarily-existing perfect island, or, in the modal-collapse version, that every contingent truth turns out to be necessary. This threatens to trivialize the proof, since a system in which nothing is genuinely contingent cannot really capture what we mean by God existing 'necessarily' as opposed to everything else. I think this objection is fairly damaging, though defenders like Scott have tried to block it by refining the axioms."
