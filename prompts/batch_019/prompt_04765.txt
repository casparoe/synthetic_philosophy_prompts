Okay so this happened in my decision theory reading group last week (I'm a philosophy PhD student; the group's half econ folks doing game theory, half philosophers doing formal epistemology) and two people ended up in a disagreement over the absent-minded driver problem that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Quick setup in case the version you know differs from ours: a driver is on a highway with two intersections that look completely identical from inside the car; he needs to exit at the second one to get home. Missing both exits pays 0, exiting at the first (wrong) one pays 1, exiting at the second (right) one pays 4. Because the intersections are indistinguishable and he has zero memory of whether he's already passed one, he can't condition his action on which intersection he's actually at -- he just has some fixed probability of exiting whenever he finds himself at one. Solving for the best such probability in advance, as a plan, gives 5/8.

Here's how the argument went, in four moves. (1) Dan said this exposes a real crack in expected utility reasoning: the planned probability is 5/8, but if you actually put yourself in the driver's seat with zero memory of whether you've already been through an intersection, the natural credence is "it's 50/50, could be either one" -- and plugging 50/50 into the expected-utility sums does not recommend exiting 5/8 of the time, so what's rational "in the moment" comes apart from what's rational to plan. (2) Maya said the 50/50 is just a mistake, not a discovery: you're guaranteed to reach the first intersection, but you only reach the second conditional on having already continued once, so the two aren't equally likely to be "this one" -- you have to weight by how often each is actually visited, and once you do, the in-the-moment calculation matches the plan exactly, so there's no crack after all. (3) Dan conceded that naive 50/50 is a mistake, but shot back that Maya's "correct" reweighting isn't neutral bookkeeping -- it's the exact same move thirders make about Sleeping Beauty, a substantive and contested stance on how to assign credence to your own location in time -- so restoring consistency this way just relocates the controversy rather than dissolving it. (4) Maya answered that she's not trying to settle Sleeping Beauty in general, but that the driver case specifically requires thirder-style weighting to avoid the plan/action mismatch, so if anything this is evidence *for* thirding, not a wash.

Neither of them backed down and I had to leave to go teach a section, so it never got resolved. Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point -- not just an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? I'd also be curious for your seperate, honest take on the bigger question hanging over the whole thing: does the absent-minded driver problem actually reveal an inconsistency in expected utility theory under imperfect recall, or is it really a problem about self-locating belief wearing a decision-theory costume, or something else again?
