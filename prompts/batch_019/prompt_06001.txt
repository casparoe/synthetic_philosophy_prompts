So in my environmental science class we had this group project where we had to debate climate policy (it was supposed to just be about international emissions treaties, not any of this), and two people in my group, Jonah and Nadia, went on this whole side tangent into philosophy that I havent been able to stop thinking about since. Jonah's claim was that, strictly speaking, we can't actually wrong future generations through stuff like emissions or resource depletion, because of something called the nonidentity problem: WHO actually gets born in like 2100 depends on a ton of tiny stuff -- which policies get picked, when couples happen to meet, when they decide to have kids, etc -- so the actual people alive in 2100 under a bad climate policy aren't the same people, even in principle, as the ones who would've existed under a good policy. They're a totally different set of people who wouldn't exist at all otherwise. So none of them can turn around and say "you made MY life worse," since their only alternative was never existing in the first place. Nadia's comeback was that this proves way too much: if the logic works, literally no long-term policy could ever count as wronging future people no matter how catastrophic, as long as it's the kind of policy that changes who eventually gets born, which over enough time is basically all of them. That feels backwards -- it would mean no amount of harm to the far future ever counts as wronging anyone, which seems like exactly the wrong lesson to take from it.

Jonah admitted that's counterintuitive, but said that's literally why it's called a "problem" and not just a bad argument -- and that the fix Nadia wants (saying a future can just be impersonally worse, full stop, regardless of who's in it) means giving up on the idea that wrongness has to cash out in terms of somebody actually being worse off than they personally would've been, and that once you allow that kind of impersonal comparison your basically one step from repugnant-conclusion type reasoning about population size and welfare. Nadia said you don't need the full impersonal view to block him, you just need some notion of a policy being bad for whoever ends up existing without measuring it against their own personal counterfactual, and that pretty much no philosopher actually lives with "so nothing we do can wrong the far future" as an acceptable conclusion. Jonah's last word was that "pretty much nobody accepts it" describes what people believe, not an argument for why they're right to believe it, and that this is still an unsolved problem for a reason. Neither of them backed down and I honestly cannot tell who's got the better of it at any given point.
