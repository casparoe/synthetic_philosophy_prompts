I started attending RCIA classes at my parish this fall while I decide whether to formally convert, and a session two weeks ago left me more preoccupied with medieval political theology than I expected going in. The class had drifted, by way of a news segment about an attempted assassination of a foreign head of state, into a debate about whether the Church has ever sanctioned killing an unjust ruler, and our deacon said, more or less in passing, that Aquinas had settled the matter: a tyrant may licitly be killed, and whoever does the killing deserves praise. A couple of us thought this sounded too tidy to be the whole story, so afterward I tracked down what I assume he was half-remembering, from the Summa Theologiae's treatment of sedition (IIa–IIae, q. 42, a. 2). The article raises, as an objection it must answer, the claim that "it is praiseworthy to deliver a multitude from a tyrannical rule," adding that this can hardly happen "without some dissension in the multitude, if one part of the multitude seeks to retain the tyrant, while the rest strive to dethrone him," and concluding that sedition must therefore sometimes be free of mortal sin. Aquinas's reply is this:

"A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude."

I can follow each sentence individually, but I am struggling to turn this into anything as clean as what our deacon told the class. If a tyrannical government is simply unjust, why does Aquinas immediately hedge the permission to "disturb" it with a proviso that the resulting harm must be smaller than the harm of the tyranny itself — is he making the rightness of resistance depend on a kind of prudential forecast about outcomes, and if so, is that merely practical caution layered on top of an otherwise unconditional judgment, or is it actually built into what he thinks justice requires here? I am also unsure what "disturbing" a government is even meant to cover — does it stretch to killing the ruler personally, or is it closer to a general uprising or a rival faction's coup, with the killing of any particular person being a separate matter? This is where my confusion with the deacon's summary really starts, because I have also come across the claim that in his earlier commentary on Peter Lombard's Sentences, Aquinas praises someone who kills a tyrant who originally seized power by naked force, specifically when no higher authority remains to judge the usurper — which sounds like a narrower and more specific license than "any tyrannical government," and one exercised by a single person rather than by "a multitude" collectively unsettling things. Am I right that these are two distinct scenarios in Aquinas's thought, and if so, what would a more accurate one- or two-sentence summary of his actual view have been, instead of the version I let pass unchallenged in class?
