I've been putting together a seminar on political writing produced in extremis, and this week we're doing Simone Weil's little essay usually translated as "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (or sometimes "Note on the General Suppression of Political Parties") — the memo she wrote in London in 1943 for de Gaulle's Free French, basically as part of the pile of postwar-reconstruction notes she was cranking out right before she died. Her argument is more or less definitional: a party, as she defines it, is (1) a machine built to manufacture collective passion, (2) a structure whose whole point is to put constant pressure on the private judgement of its own members, and (3) an organization whose only real and unbounded goal, once you strip away the stated ideals, is its own growth. Given that, she doesn't want parties reformed or regulated, she wants them abolished outright, because on her view truth and justice just don't come in party-line flavors — an individual can be mistaken but a "party opinion" is a kind of ready-made lie you agree to hold before you've even looked at the evidence.

When I brought this into seminar one of my sharper students immediately reached for Madison — Federalist 10, the move where faction is just an inevitable by-product of liberty and self-love, trying to legislate it away is a cure worse then the disease, and the actual fix is to multiply factions and dilute them across a big enough republic that no one of them can dominate. Her point was basically that Weil's problem "solves itself" once you're operating at Madisonian scale with enough competing factions, so the abolition proposal is just radicalism dressed up as an insight.

I'd like an actual verdict on this, not a survey of both sides. Please argue that Weil's diagnosis of what's wrong with parties survives Madison's proposed cure — i.e. that even granting Madison everything (faction can't be eliminated, suppressing it is dangerous, multiplying factions dilutes their external effects on policy outcomes), she's identified a distinct, internal problem — what party membership does to a person's own capacity for judgement — that "multiply and dilute" never actually touches, so that her diagnosis (whatever you think of the abolition remedy itself) keeps its force even inside a smoothly functioning Madisonian system. If it helps, I seem to recall she doesn't even let two-party systems like Britain's off the hook, for what it's worth, which suggests whatever she's tracking isn't simply a function of how many factions you've got.
