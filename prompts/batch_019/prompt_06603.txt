I spent thirty years as an experimental nuclear physicist, and I always took some comfort in Popper's propensity theory of probability: unlike frequentism, it seemed to make sense of a single decay event's probability without embedding that event in a reference class chosen more or less by convention. Since retiring I have been reading further in the philosophy of probability, and I gather that Alan Hájek, in a paper with a title something like "The Reference Class Problem Is Your Problem Too," argues that the difficulty reappears for propensity theories as well, since specifying the relevant "chance setup" or generating conditions is itself an arbitrary choice among equally admissible descriptions of the same situation. I would like to test whether I have really grasped the point. Please construct a concrete example — not radioactive decay, and not the standard actuarial or courtroom cases used in most introductions to this topic — in which a propensity theorist's assignment of a single-case probability depends on which of two equally natural specifications of the chance setup is adopted, with no principled way to prefer one over the other. Then explain briefly why narrowing the setup until it picks out only the single case would not, by the propensity theorist's own lights, count as a way out of the difficulty.
