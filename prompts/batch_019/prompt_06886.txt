Question:

(a) State Zorn's Lemma precisely.

(b) Use Zorn's Lemma to prove that every nonzero commutative ring R with multiplicative identity 1 has a maximal ideal.

(c) Zorn's Lemma, the Axiom of Choice, and the Well-Ordering Theorem are provably equivalent to one another over the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms. There is an old joke among mathematicians, often attributed to Jerry Bona, to the effect that the Axiom of Choice is obviously true, the Well-Ordering Theorem is obviously false, and nobody can decide about Zorn's Lemma. In three or four sentences, say what work "equivalent" is doing here, and what the joke is meant to bring out about the relation between logical equivalence and intuitive plausibility.

Student's answer:

(a) Zorn's Lemma states that if (P, ≤) is a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound in P, then P has a maximal element, i.e. some m ∈ P such that there is no x ∈ P with m < x.

(b) Let Σ be the set of all proper ideals of R (ideals I with I ⊊ R), partially ordered by inclusion. Σ is nonempty, since R is nonzero and so the zero ideal (0) is proper. Let C ⊆ Σ be a chain and let U = ∪C, the union of all the ideals belonging to C. If a, b ∈ U, then a ∈ I₁ and b ∈ I₂ for some I₁, I₂ ∈ C; since C is a chain, one of I₁, I₂ contains the other, so a and b both lie in whichever is larger, and hence so does a − b. Likewise ra ∈ U for any r ∈ R. So U is an ideal, and it is proper because 1 ∉ I for every I ∈ C, so 1 ∉ U. Hence U ∈ Σ, and U is evidently an upper bound for C. By Zorn's Lemma, Σ has a maximal element M. This M is exactly the largest proper ideal of R — the one every other proper ideal is eventually absorbed into — so it cannot be made any bigger without becoming the whole ring, which is what it means for M to be a maximal ideal of R.

(c) The three principles are equivalent in the sense that, working in ZF, a proof of any one of them can be turned into a proof of either of the others, so that accepting one commits you to accepting all three. The joke is getting at the fact that this logical equivalence does not track how obvious each statement feels: the Axiom of Choice sounds like a harmless generalisation of choosing from finitely many sets, the Well-Ordering Theorem sounds bizarre once you try to picture a well-ordering of the real numbers, and Zorn's Lemma, phrased in terms of chains and upper bounds rather than choices, sounds like a comparatively modest piece of order theory. This is in fact one of the reasons Zorn's Lemma caught on so quickly among algebraists: it let them import the full strength of the Axiom of Choice into proofs like the one in part (b) without actually having to invoke the Axiom of Choice, or take a stand on the disputes surrounding it.
