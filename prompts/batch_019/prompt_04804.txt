Ok, here's something that's been living in my head since last weekend. I fell down a rabbit hole that started at the old "Roko's basilisk" meme and ended up in a pile of LessWrong / AI-alignment-forum posts about "acausal threats" -- the idea that an agent (a future AI, a simulation of you, whatever) can coerce you right now just by committing to retaliate against your actual values if it predicts you didn't comply, with no causal channel between you and it required, just mutual prediction. Wierd, but I basically followed the logic of why that's supposed to work.

What I can't wrap my head around is the proposed defense, which I've seen called "surrogate goals" (I think this traces back to a Caspar Oesterheld paper on "safe Pareto improvements," but don't quote me on the name). As I understand the pitch: instead of hardening your actual values against threats, you manufacture some goal you don't really care about and publicly, credibly commit to defend it -- to react to threats against it exactly as if it were precious to you -- so that any threatener doing the rational cost-benefit math targets the decoy instead of your real utility function, since threatening the decoy is the only threat that'll actually get a response out of you. My engineer brain's first reaction is "this is just a honeypot, and honeypots get figured out": why can't the threatener just reason one level deeper and threaten the fact that you value the surrogate, or pick a threat that's entangled with both the surrogate and your real goal so you can't cleanly wall the real one off? And if they're smart enough to model you well enough to threaten you acausally in the first place, surely they're smart enough to see through the swap.

I've only seen this floated seriously by people who clearly know alot more decision theory than me (I know Newcomb's problem and I've read about UDT/FDT at a pretty hand-wavy level, that's about it), so I'm guessing I'm missing the actual mechanism that's supposed to make the commitment stable instead of just deferring the threat by one step. Can you walk me through why surrogate goals are supposed to actually work -- what's doing the load-bearing work in the argument -- and give me an honest read on where the strategy is more fragile than the pitch makes it sound?
