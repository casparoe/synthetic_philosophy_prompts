A friend and I spent a long dinner arguing about whether Descartes' case against animal minds has anything to say about large language models. She pointed out that in the Discourse on the Method he doesn't merely assert that animals are unthinking automata but gives two specific tests for telling genuine reason from mechanism: a thinking being can produce a fitting reply to the whole unpredictable range of things that might be said to it, rather than a fixed response to a fixed cue, and reason is what he calls a universal instrument suited to any situation, whereas a machine -- or, on his view, an animal -- can only ever handle the narrow range of tasks it was built or trained for. I argued that an LLM passes the first test easily enough that Descartes owes us a principled reason, not just a stipulation, for why fluent, flexible replies to arbitrary prompts fail to count as truly replying to the sense of whatever is said to it. She answered that this gets him backwards: fluency of exactly this kind is just the sort of mechanical disposition he already expected could mimic understanding, so an LLM should give him nothing to worry about. Neither of us budged. Working from what Descartes actually argues, rather than the standard one-line version of his view, I'd like to know which of us has the better reading of his own test, and whether taking it seriously about LLMs would give him any reason to be less confident of his verdict on animals, or would leave that verdict completely untouched.
