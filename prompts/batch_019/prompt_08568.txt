I've been working through the St. Petersburg paradox on my own. In the classic version, you flip a fair coin until it lands tails for the first time, and if that happens on the nth flip you win 2^n dollars; because the prize doubles exactly as fast as the odds of reaching it halve, the expected payout is infinite, even though almost no one would pay more than a small amount to play. The resolution I keep running into is Daniel Bernoulli's: money has diminishing marginal utility, so once the game is scored in expected utility rather than expected dollars (he used a logarithmic scale), the sum comes out finite, and refusing to pay much for the bet turns out to be perfectly rational rather than some kind of mistake. I've been using that idea to write a simple personal rule for judging long-shot bets and investments: run every payout through a concave utility function instead of raw dollars, and this particular kind of infinite-expectation trap can never come up again, no matter how the bet is structured. Before I start relying on that rule for real decisions, I'd like to know whether it actually holds up in general, or whether it only worked for the specific game Bernoulli happened to be looking at.
