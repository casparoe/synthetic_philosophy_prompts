I've been working through the classic literature on the frame problem in AI for a seminar on philosophy of cognitive science—McCarthy and Hayes's original 1969 formulation in the situation calculus, the later formal fixes such as circumscription and Reiter's successor-state axioms, and the broader "epistemological" reading of the problem associated with Dennett, Dreyfus, and Fodor—and I still can't settle in my own head whether the problem has actually been solved or merely relocated. Rather than looking up another survey article, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two thinkers who disagree about this and actually work the disagreement through.

One character should be a formalist in the tradition of McCarthy and Reiter, holding that the frame problem, properly understood, is the narrow logical problem of avoiding an explosion of axioms stating what does not change when an action occurs, and that devices like circumscription and successor-state axioms solve it in general—one axiom per fluent, stating every way it can change, with everything else simply assumed to persist. This character should also be willing to point out that modern statistical and neural approaches to AI get along tolerably well without any explicit machinery for picking out relevant facts, which looks like further evidence that the "deep" problem was never as deep as its critics thought. The other character should press Dennett's original point that this narrow solution presupposes exactly what needs explaining—some prior sense of which predicates and fluents are even worth writing axioms about, out of the indefinitely many facts an action could in principle bear on—and should back this up with Dreyfus's and Fodor's very different routes to a similar conclusion: Dreyfus's claim that relevance is disclosed directly in embodied, skillful situations rather than computed at all, and Fodor's claim (from his discussion of the "isotropic" and "Quinean" character of central cognition) that fixing relevance is a holistic matter that resists confinement to any module or subroutine, symbolic or statistical alike.

Have the two characters genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves across several exchanges, rather than talking past one another—the formalist should have to answer the charge that formal solutions relocate the problem of relevance rather than dissolve it, and the other side should have to answer the challenge that the practical fluency of modern learned systems makes the whole "epistemological" worry look overstated. Neither character should be a strawman for the other, and you need not engineer a tidy resolution if, after real engagement, the disagreement turns out to run all the way down.
