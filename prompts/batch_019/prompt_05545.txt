Bertrand's paradox asks for the probability that a "randomly chosen" chord of a circle is longer than a side of the circle's inscribed equilateral triangle. Three classical ways of generating such a chord — picking two uniformly random points on the circumference, picking a uniformly random radius and then a uniformly random point along it, and picking a uniformly random point in the disc to serve as the chord's midpoint — yield the answers 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 respectively, even though each construction seems to satisfy the principle of indifference equally well. E. T. Jaynes argued that this ambiguity dissolves once the randomizing procedure is required to respect the translational, rotational, and scale invariances of a physically realized process, such as tossing straws onto the circle from a distance; imposing these invariances singles out the random-radius construction, and its answer of 1/2, as uniquely correct. Please argue that Jaynes's invariance solution does not, in the end, rescue the principle of indifference from the paradox.
