I'm a software engineer, and over the past year a surprising number of people in my circle -- a former coworker, a few people from a rationalist-adjacent meetup I attend -- have gotten seriously into cryonics, to the point that one of them recently signed the actual paperwork with a cryonics provider. Around the same time, a friend talked me into a small book club working through the Zhuangzi in the Burton Watson translation, which has completely upended my assumption that classical Daoist thinking about death amounts to some vague "everything is one, so don't be afraid" sentiment.

The actual stories turn out to be stranger and more specific than that. There's Zhuangzi drumming on a basin and singing after his wife's death, telling his friend Huizi that wailing would just show he hadn't understood the seasons of things; the skull he uses as a pillow that visits him in a dream to say it wouldn't trade the freedom of being dead for the anxieties of a living king; and, in the chapter usually translated as "The Great and Venerable Teacher," a dying man named Zilai whose friend Zili jokes about whether the process will reshape him next into a rat's liver or an insect's arm, to which Zilai responds that he doesn't much care either way, since the "great clod" that gives us form is free to do with us as it likes. That same chapter has a short parable about someone hiding a boat in a ravine so nobody can steal it, only for a strong man to carry it off in the night regardless -- which reads, on a first pass, like a direct rebuke of exactly what my cryonics-minded friends are trying to do with their own bodies. On the other hand, a few chapters earlier, Cook Ding is held up as a model of "nurturing life" precisely because he works skillfully with the natural grain of things -- following the joints of the ox instead of forcing his blade through bone -- so it isn't as though Zhuangzi treats every deliberate intervention in a natural process as a kind of anxious clinging.

Given all that, I'd like your best reconstruction of how Zhuangzi would actually respond if someone explained cryonic preservation to him -- freezing a body, or just a brain, immediately after clinical death, on the chance that future technology could repair and revive it. Would he treat that as a technologically sophisticated version of Cook Ding's skill, working with transformation rather than against it, or as exactly the kind of hoarding the boat parable warns against, closer to Zilai's grieving family than to Zilai himself? I'd rather you sit with the actual tension between these passages than hand me a tidy answer in either direction.
