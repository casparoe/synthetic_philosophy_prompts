I sit on the grants committee of a small effective-altruism-aligned foundation. We must decide whether to renew our largest commitment -- a bed-net distribution charity backed by strong RCT evidence -- or redirect the money to a longtermist biosecurity fund whose theory of change is far less legible. One member invokes Hilary Greaves's distinction between simple and complex cluelessness, arguing that the bed-net program's downstream effects on population size, local economies, and animal agriculture leave us not merely uncertain but complexly clueless about its sign of impact -- and that the same reasoning, honestly applied, undercuts our confidence in the biosecurity fund's expected value too. Others worry that accepting this objection would paralyze the committee permanently, since some version of it can be run against almost any intervention. Draft the committee's decision memo: recommend a specific allocation (or a justified split), and say plainly how much weight the cluelessness objection should carry, engaging its strongest form rather than dismissing it as too clever by half.
