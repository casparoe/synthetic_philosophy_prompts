At a recent workshop on platform design and misinformation, I watched two researchers fall into a disagreement over coffee that I still haven't been able to adjudicate myself.

(1) Raj argued that the Condorcet Jury Theorem shows that letting more people rate each note on a system like X's Community Notes can only make the ratings more reliable: as long as each rater is even slightly better than a coin flip at judging accuracy, the theorem guarantees that the share of correct majority verdicts climbs toward certainty as more raters are added.

(2) Elena objected that this only holds if raters err independently, and they plainly don't: contributors read the same linked articles, follow overlapping sets of accounts, and rate within the same social climate, so their mistakes are correlated rather than independent. Once you allow even a modest positive correlation among raters, she said, the probability of a correct majority verdict stops climbing toward 1 and instead flattens out at some ceiling well short of certainty, no matter how many more raters you add.

(3) Raj replied that this proves too much, since real juries and forecasting markets work reasonably well despite their members never being strictly independent, so correlation can't be as damaging in practice as the math implies -- and in any case, the system doesn't use plain majority rule among all raters, but specifically surfaces a note only once it's been rated helpful by people who've historically disagreed with each other, which should filter out shared bias rather than amplify it.

(4) Elena answered that this concedes her point rather than answering it: the only reason a platform would need that kind of cross-perspective rule in the first place is that unweighted majority rule among correlated raters doesn't deliver the guarantee Raj started with. You don't rescue a theorem from a failed premise by quietly switching to a different aggregation rule and then crediting the theorem for the fix.
