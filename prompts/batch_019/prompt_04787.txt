I'm taking a seminar this term on moral epistemology, and our department's undergraduate philosophy society runs a small podcast where students get to interview visiting speakers. I've been put in charge of questions for our next episode. The guest is a moral epistemologist who has built her reputation defending pessimism about moral testimony: the view that, unlike testimony about empirical matters, hearing a moral verdict secondhand cannot by itself give a hearer justified moral belief, moral knowledge, or (on the strongest version of the view) any real moral improvement, since acting well is supposed to require acting from your own grasp of the relevant reasons rather than simply taking someone else's word for it.

I don't want the interview to just have her restate the "if you have to ask, you don't really get it" intuition that motivates the view, since she has given more or less that same line in two other interviews I listened to while preparing. I'd like eight to ten questions that instead push on specific pressure points from the literature: the worry that pessimism can't make sense of ordinary moral education, given that parents constantly hand down moral testimony to small children and this seems obviously fine, even necessary; the asymmetry challenge of explaining why deference is unproblematic in, say, medicine or climate science but supposedly not in ethics; the parallel debate over aesthetic testimony, where critics raise a structurally similar worry about secondhand verdicts of taste; and a concrete case of someone facing a serious moral decision with no time to reason it out, who simply defers to a wiser friend's snap judgment.

For each question, could you also add a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give? In her written work, she tends to fall back on a distinction between merely acquiring a true moral belief and acquiring full moral understanding, so I'd especially like a couple of the follow-ups to test whether that distinction is doing real philosophical work here or is quietly conceding most of what her optimist opponents wanted in the first place.
