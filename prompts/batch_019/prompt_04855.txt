I write about science and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I'm circling a piece on so-called "rational irrationality" -- cases where committing yourself in advance to act against your own later interests seems to be exactly what rationality demands. Two decision theorists I'd invited to a small workshop dinner last month -- I'll call them Naomi and Felix -- ended up litigating the cleanest version of this, Parfit's Hitchhiker, for the better part of an hour, and I still haven't been able to settle in my own mind who got the better of it. The setup, for anyone who hasn't run into it: you are dying of thirst in the desert; a driver will take you into town, but only if she's convinced you'll pay her $1,000 once you're safely there, and she's an extremely reliable judge of sincerity, so there's no bluffing your way into the car. Once you actually reach town, handing over the money does nothing further for you -- the ride is already a fact about the past -- so a causal decision theorist reasoning fresh, in the moment, seems bound to conclude that you shouldn't pay, which is exactly why the driver, foreseeing this, would leave such a reasoner to die of thirst in the first place.

Here is roughly how the argument went. (1) Naomi opened by insisting the case is a straightforward refutation of causal decision theory: a theory of rational choice whose adherents predictably die in the desert cannot be the correct account of practical rationality, whatever its internal logic. (2) Felix answered that this conflates two different questions -- which disposition it's rational to have beforehand, and which act is rational once you're already in town -- and that CDT was only ever in the business of answering the second; a rational disposition producing an act that isn't itself rational, evaluated in isolation, is a distinction, he said, not a contradiction. (3) Naomi replied that the split is itself the tell: this is exactly why "resolute choice" theorists refuse to accept it, holding that for an agent carrying out a rational plan, the later act of paying is itself the rational thing to do rather than a rational disposition producing a locally irrational one; if CDT can't agree that paying is rational once you're committed, that's a mark against CDT's account of rational action, not a vindication of treating disposition and act as separable questions. (4) Felix countered that resolute choice buys its unified verdict only by stipulating that an earlier plan supplies binding reasons at the later node, which is precisely what's in dispute, not an argument for it; and separately, that ordinary cases where disposition and act seem to line up already smuggle in reputational or repeated-dealing payoffs as further causal consequences of the act, whereas Parfit's driver is an idealized, one-shot, unbluffable predictor -- exactly the sort of exotic case a theory of rational action might be forgiven for handling badly. (5) Naomi closed by saying idealizations are precisely how you stress-test a theory and expose a defect ordinary cases are too forgiving to reveal, and that Felix's "smuggling" charge cuts both ways: restricting reasons to an act's forward-looking causal consequences is itself a substantive, contestable stand on what counts as a reason, not a neutral default that resolute choice alone is guilty of abandoning.

Neither of them budged, and since I don't want to caricature either position when I write this up, I'd like a careful outside read. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (5) -- and assess how defensible each one is as a piece of reasoning, rather than just declaring an overall winner? It would help me to get a JSON object first, with keys "1" through "5" and, as the value for each, an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right); then a couple of sentences per move explaining the score. Please close with a short paragraph on whether "rational irrationality" survives this exchange as a coherent notion or collapses once resolute choice is taken seriously, and on whether there's a distinction that both Naomi and Felix are simply talking past.
