I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on normative ethics, and this past week's session was devoted to the problem of consequentialist cluelessness. We read James Lenman's original argument that even the most mundane action ramifies causally into a future we have no realistic hope of tracing, so that a consequentialist calculus can never really be completed, and then Hilary Greaves's attempt to triage the worry into "simple" and "complex" cluelessness, where the former is supposed to cancel out in expectation and the latter is not. Several of us stayed after class arguing about whether Greaves's distinction actually rescues consequentialism as an action-guiding theory or just relocates the problem, and we didn't reach anything like consensus.

I'd find it more useful to see the dispute played out directly than to read another summary of it, so could you write me a dialogue of roughly 700 to 900 words between a committed act consequentialist and an interlocutor who thinks cluelessness is a serious, perhaps decisive, objection to consequentialism as a guide to action? I'd like the consequentialist to open with something like the standard symmetry or cancellation move — that we have no more reason to expect unforeseeable downstream effects to be good than bad, so they should wash out across the options being compared, leaving the foreseeable effects to do the deciding work — and I'd like the cluelessness-defender to push back with a genuine case of complex cluelessness, where two or more identifiable channels of influence point in different directions and there's no principled way to weigh them against each other. From there I want real engagement rather than restated opening positions: each character should respond to the specific move the other has just made, and the consequentialist in particular should have some considered rejoinder to the complex-cluelessness case rather than simply conceding it, since as far as I can tell this is still an open dispute rather than one where either side has clearly won.

Neither character should come off as a pushover for the other. And if it emerges naturally from the exchange, I'd be curious whether the two of them end up touching on whether cluelessness gives us any real reason to retreat to more short-termist or parochial decision rules, or whether that retreat is itself just another way of refusing to look at the problem.
