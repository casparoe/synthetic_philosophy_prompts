I'm about a third of the way into a historical novel set in the last decades of Thomas Hobbes's life, and there's a scene I keep putting off: an imagined room where Hobbes and John Wallis actually hash out their disagreement about geometry, rather than just lobbing pamphlets at each other the way they did in real life. The historical bones are simple enough — Hobbes claims in De Corpore that he's squared the circle, Wallis rips the proof apart in the Elenchus, Hobbes fires back with Six Lessons (technically addressed to Wallis and Seth Ward both, but let's keep it to the Wallis half), Wallis answers with Due Correction, and the two of them are still sniping in print more than twenty years later, by which point its basically common knowledge around Oxford that Hobbes can't do geometry, and part of why he never got into the Royal Society. My writing group keeps telling me to just skip the math and cut straight to the personal animus, but the more I've read (I've been going through Jesseph's book on the whole affair) the less this looks like a simple genius-vs-crank story and the more it looks like a real disagreement about what geometric objects even are. Hobbes genuinely seems to think a line just is the path traced by a moving body, a point just a body whose bulk we've agreed not to reckon, and that this is exactly why geometry can be certain — we made the thing, so we know its causes — which is tangled up with his whole materialism and his hope that civil philosophy could be every bit as certain once it's grounded the same way. Wallis, meanwhile, is off calculating with indivisibles and infinitesimal quantities that don't obviously correspond to anything you could trace out with a moving point at all.

Could you draft that scene for me as a dialogue, maybe 700-900 words? I don't want Wallis to win just by pointing at the failed quadrature — he should have to actually defend indivisibles as a legitimate way of reasoning about magnitude, given that nobody at the time (him included) can fully justify why they work. And I don't want Hobbes reduced to repeating materialist slogans — he should have to say what his "generated by motion" account of points and lines actually rules out, and why that should matter to Wallis, once Wallis presses him on it. I want real back-and-forth, each of them landing at least one point that costs the other something, rather than a lecture wearing two name tags.
