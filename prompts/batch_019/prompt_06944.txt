Last week a friend of mine who works in behavioral economics and I had a fairly heated argument over dinner about whether risk aversion that outstrips diminishing marginal utility could ever be rational rather than simply a bias to be corrected for, and neither of us came away persuaded. Her position, which she insists is still the default among decision theorists, is that once a concave utility function has done all the work it can in explaining a preference for sure things over gambles of equal expected value, any further aversion to risk -- the kind on display in the classic Allais choices -- is simply an error, one that a sufficiently clever bookmaker could turn into a money pump by exploiting its violation of the independence axiom. I tried to defend the opposite position by gesturing at Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory, which I understood to argue that such preferences can be perfectly rational, but when she pressed me on the actual mechanics -- how the theory's risk function is supposed to be doing something different from the probability weighting we already get from cumulative prospect theory, and how an REU-maximizer avoids being pumped for money across a sequence of choices -- I realized I couldn't reconstruct the argument properly.

Could you help me reconstruct it properly? I'm especially interested in two things. First, I would like a precise account of how applying a risk function to the decumulative probabilities of a rank-ordered set of outcomes, rather than to the outcomes' probabilities directly or folding it into their utilities, is supposed to capture a genuinely "global" attitude toward a gamble as a whole -- one that a purely local, outcome-by-outcome story about diminishing marginal utility cannot capture -- and why Buchak thinks this earns the theory a normative rather than merely descriptive status, in contrast to how probability-weighting functions are usually read within the prospect-theory literature. Second, I want the strongest reconstruction you can give of how risk-weighted expected utility theory is supposed to answer the dynamic-consistency objection: if an REU-maximizer's preferences over gambles violate the independence axiom the way Allais preferences do, why doesn't that agent become vulnerable to a diachronic money pump or Dutch book once a single choice is broken up into a sequence of smaller decisions, as the standard argument against non-expected-utility theories claims it must?
