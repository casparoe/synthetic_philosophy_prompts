A friend and I spent much of a long dinner last week arguing over a fragment from Pascal's Pensées that had come up in a philosophy of law seminar I'm taking: the passage where he observes that justice without force is powerless, while force without justice is tyrannical, and that, since we could never manage to make what is just strong, we have instead arranged things so that what is strong is called just. Pascal connects this to his remarks elsewhere on custom—the idea that law and authority rest on nothing more than being established and unquestioned, and that tracing them back to their actual origin tends to dissolve their legitimacy rather than confirm it. My friend read all this as essentially a Hobbesian shrug, an admission that might simply constitutes right in practice, while I argued that Pascal is diagnosing a structural problem about how any norm gets enforced without thereby endorsing the result. Neither of us managed to convince the other, and it left me wondering how Pascal himself would extend the point somewhere he never got to apply it.

That is what I would like your help with: pushing Pascal's diagnosis into the one arena that seems tailor-made for it, namely the international order, which lacks any genuine sovereign standing over states the way a king stood over subjects in his own day. Given what he says about justice, force, and custom, how do you think Pascal would have judged institutions like the UN Security Council or the International Criminal Court, which pronounce on justice among states while depending for enforcement on the very powers they are meant to judge? Would the difficulty of holding powerful states to such rulings, visible again in the debates over how to respond to the war in Ukraine, have struck him as simply confirming that international justice is whatever the strong permit it to be, or does his other work, especially his distinction between the orders of body, mind, and charity, leave room for a more constructive Pascalian position on what durable international justice would actually require? I would like an actual reconstruction of his likely view, not just a restatement of the fragment itself.
