A friend and I spent much of last night arguing over Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, which she had just come across and took to be a decisive, purely rationality-based proof of utilitarianism: since both individual and social preferences are assumed to satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms over the same lotteries, and society is indifferent whenever every individual is indifferent, social welfare must be representable as a weighted sum of individual utilities. I objected that the theorem fixes neither the weights nor the individual utility scales they multiply, so nothing in the proof forces equal treatment of persons without some further, independently argued assumption; and that its reliance on ex ante Pareto indifference conflicts with ex post fairness in just the way Diamond's cases against Harsanyi were meant to show. Neither of us budged. Write an essay that adjudicates the dispute on its merits: how much real support does the aggregation theorem lend to utilitarianism? Engage seriously with both objections, and close with a definite verdict — a conditional one (for instance, that the theorem supports utilitarianism only once independent equal-weighting and ex-post-fairness assumptions are supplied, and is silent otherwise) is acceptable, but the essay should not simply conclude that the matter is complicated.
