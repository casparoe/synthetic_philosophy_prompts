I've been invited to contribute a short piece on Hobbes's philosophy of mathematics to a graduate seminar packet on early modern conceptions of scientific method, and I would like to run my central argument past someone before committing it to paper. The occasion was a throwaway remark from a colleague in our history-and-philosophy-of-science reading group, who described Hobbes's confidence in geometry as an early instance of the "maker's knowledge" tradition: the idea that we can have especially certain knowledge of geometrical objects because we are the ones who construct them, by definition and, in Hobbes's own mechanistic idiom, by the imagined motion of points generating lines, lines generating surfaces, and so on. I think there is something to this, but it also sits awkwardly with a fact usually treated as mere biographical embarrassment: Hobbes was, by any mathematical standard, a poor geometer, and spent nearly a quarter-century in a losing and increasingly acrimonious dispute with Wallis (and, in the early rounds, Ward) over claimed solutions to the quadrature of the circle and related problems that were simply mistaken.

Here is roughly where the draft stands. The opening move lays out Hobbes's "maker's knowledge" epistemology: because geometry's objects and definitions are our own artifacts rather than items found ready-made in nature, geometrical reasoning can attain a causal certainty that natural philosophy cannot, since physics forces us to infer unknown causes from observed effects after the fact. This is not, I argue, an isolated aside but does real work elsewhere in Hobbes's system: it is a large part of why he thinks civil philosophy can in principle be just as certain as geometry, since the commonwealth is likewise an artificial body of our own making, built by covenant rather than discovered — hence the define-then-deduce, quasi-geometrical structure he imposes on Leviathan itself, moving from the passions of matter-in-motion to the artificial person of the sovereign. The complication I want to press is this: if the whole point of maker's knowledge is that constructing the objects ourselves should make error close to impossible for a sufficiently attentive reasoner, why was Hobbes so persistently unable to recognize his own geometrical mistakes, even after Wallis had walked through them in print more than once? I sketch three not-fully-compatible explanations and want help working out which, if any, does real philosophical work rather than just excusing Hobbes: (a) maker's knowledge might extend to primitive definitions and constructions but not to the long deductive chains built on top of them, where ordinary fallibility re-enters; (b) Hobbes's finitism and his hostility to Wallis's more arithmetized, indivisibles-friendly methods may have deprived him of the very tools needed to handle problems like quadrature correctly, so that his official epistemology of geometry and his chosen mathematical methods were pulling in different directions; or (c) the maker's knowledge doctrine was always more of a rhetorical prop for the political argument than an accurate description of mathematical practice, and Hobbes's geometrical failures simply expose this.

I would welcome comments of a few kinds. First, a check on the history: I am recalling the Hobbes–Wallis affair (the geometry of De Corpore, the Elenchus, the Six Lessons, and the pamphlets that followed) mostly from memory, so please flag anything that looks factually off or oversimplified. Second, and more importantly, I would like an honest assessment of whether the tension I am describing between Hobbes's epistemology of geometry and his actual mathematical practice is a genuine philosophical puzzle, or one that dissolves fairly quickly once stated with more care. Third, of the three explanations on offer, which do you find most defensible, and is there a better diagnosis I am missing entirely?
