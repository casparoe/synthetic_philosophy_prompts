My father was found to have a significant coronary artery blockage a few months ago, and after a stent was placed, his cardiologist cited a five-year risk of a "major adverse cardiac event" drawn from a large outcomes study — something like twenty percent. The number has stayed a live topic in the family since, and the sharpest exchange about it happened two Sundays ago, between my aunt Corinne, who spent years underwriting insurance policies, and my cousin Theo, who by his own admission had never thought much about statistics before this. I could not settle it myself, and only afterward, reading around a little, did I discover that this kind of disagreement has a name in the philosophy of probability — something like "the reference class problem" — which is why I would rather have a careful outside assessment than rely on my own read of who came out ahead.

The exchange went roughly as follows. Corinne opened by arguing (1) that twenty percent is simply the best estimate available, and that until the cardiologist says otherwise, it is the number the family should go by, since it is what the actual published data show. Theo objected (2) that the study's cohort skewed ten to fifteen years older than my father and included a much higher proportion of smokers and diabetics, neither of which he has ever been, so applying the study's overall rate to him specifically is close to meaningless. Corinne answered (3) that this objection proves too much: you can always slice the population more finely — by age, by risk factor, by zip code, by almost anything — until you are left with a reference class of exactly one person, my father, at which point there is no statistic left to compute, only an outcome that has not happened yet; so at some point you have to stop narrowing and simply use the aggregate figure, which at least rests on real data. Theo replied (4) that this does not license retreating all the way back to the crude, unstratified number either: the hospital's own registry, he had discovered, publishes five-year outcomes broken down by age band and risk-factor count, and in the band that actually matches my father the figure is closer to nine percent, so there is no reason to default to the least specific number just because the most specific one is unavailable. Corinne's rejoinder (5) was that this only pushes the disagreement back one step: why stop at age and risk-factor count, and not also condition on his particular genetic markers, his fitness level, or how well he has responded to his medication so far, all of which the cardiologist also has on record? Once you grant that we should use "the most specific class for which we still have decent data," she said, you have conceded that there is no principled place to stop, and different doctors could reasonably draw that line differently — so Theo's nine percent is not the objectively correct figure in place of her twenty percent, just a different, equally contestable stopping point dressed up as more rigorous.

Neither of them backed down, and having sat with it for a couple of weeks now, I do not think either was simply being careless. Could you go through the exchange move by move? Specifically, I would like: (a) a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>}, where each score is an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly correct) assessing that move on its own logical merits, independent of who "won" earlier exchanges; (b) two or three sentences justifying each score; and (c) a closing paragraph on whether the reference class problem, as it shows up here, actually admits of a defensible resolution, or whether Corinne and Theo are both circling something that neither of them quite manages to state outright.
