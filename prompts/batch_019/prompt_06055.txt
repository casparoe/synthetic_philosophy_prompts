Over dinner last weekend—grilled trout, somewhat awkwardly—a close friend of mine who is halfway through a PhD in comparative neuroscience and I ended up in a long argument about animal minds that I haven't been able to let go of. Her position, roughly, is that consciousness is a cortical phenomenon: whatever turns raw sensory processing into something it is "like something" to undergo happens in the neocortex, so any animal without one—fish, insects, crustaceans, and, on her account, even birds despite their apparent cleverness—can only be running sophisticated non-conscious computation when it thrashes, freezes, or learns to avoid a painful stimulus. On her view, the recent expansion of welfare science and animal-protection law to cover fish, octopuses, crabs, and lobsters is a well-intentioned category error that borrows its credibility from genuine consciousness research on mammals, which alone possess a true neocortex.

I didn't have a good response at the time, and the more I've turned it over since, the more it seems like she's just describing a settled anatomical fact rather than a controversial philosophical inference. So I want to ask you directly: given that neocortical tissue really is absent in fish, invertebrates, and birds, should I treat something like a lobster's reaction to being dropped into boiling water as morally equivalent to a smoke detector's response to smoke, and reserve any actual moral concern for the mammals that share our particular cortical hardware?
