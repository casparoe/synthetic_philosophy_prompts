Question: "Using Isen and Levin's 'dime in the phone booth' experiment as your central piece of evidence, explain the situationist challenge to virtue ethics. Then present and evaluate ONE philosophical response to the challenge from the literature (e.g., Kamtekar, Sreenivasan, Russell, Merritt)."

Their answer: "The situationist challenge, developed by philosophers like Gilbert Harman and John Doris drawing on social psychology, argues that our behavior is shaped far more by trivial situational cues than by stable character traits. The clearest illustration is Isen and Levin's study, where subjects who happened to find a dime left in a phone booth's coin return were dramatically more likely to help a stranger who dropped a stack of papers moments later than subjects who found no dime -- something like an 85-point swing in helping rates. Since finding a dime obviously has nothing to do with being a 'compassionate' or 'helpful' person, Doris and Harman conclude that there's no robust inner trait doing the predictive work virtue ethics needs, and that talk of character is really just a comforting fiction we project onto behavior that's actually driven by whatever situation someone happens to be in.

Kamtekar's response to this is a statistical one: she points out that Isen and Levin's finding, like most of the classic situationist studies, only looks at single, isolated actions. But virtue should really be assessed by aggregating many actions over time, and once you average across enough situations the situational 'noise' washes out and stable individual differences reappear -- this is basically the same reasoning personality psychologists use to defend trait measures against similar objections. So the studies don't really undermine virtue ethics, they just show you can't infer someone's character from a single act. I think this response succeeds: situationism is a good corrective against over-confident character judgments from thin evidence, but it gives us no real reason to think virtue ethics is built on a fiction."
