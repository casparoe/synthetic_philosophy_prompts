A few of us who did our philosophy MA together still get dinner every couple of months, and the conversation reliably turns into a seminar whether anyone intends it to. At the most recent one, two friends of mine, Marlene and Dominic, spent most of the evening arguing about what philosophers sometimes call the problem of cluelessness: whether our ignorance about the long-run effects of our actions gives consequentialists any real reason to doubt that a specific, well-evidenced charitable donation is worth making. Neither of them has worked on ethics professionally in years, but neither was willing to concede the point, and by the time we split the bill they had talked past each other in a way I could not fully untangle on the drive home. I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can, in six moves.

(1) Dominic opened by arguing that no one can really defend, on consequentialist grounds, a donation to a well-regarded charity that funds girls' education in a low-income country, because we have no idea what its effects will look like even a few decades out: higher household income changes family size, consumption, migration, and local politics in ways that cascade indefinitely, and we cannot even guess the sign of the net effect on total welfare a century from now. If we cannot estimate that, he said, the expected-value calculations that effective-giving organizations rely on are close to meaningless.
(2) Marlene replied that this argument, if it worked, would apply just as well to keeping the money, spending it, or investing it, since those choices ripple forward just as unpredictably; so cluelessness cannot be a reason to favor withholding the donation over making it. If every option is equally clueless, cluelessness offers no differential guidance at all, and Dominic's argument would end up counseling not against this donation specifically but against ever choosing anything.
(3) Dominic answered that Marlene was collapsing two different kinds of uncertainty. Some knock-on effects really do seem to have no expected direction one way or the other, and he was happy to set those aside; but others are cases where we have systematic reason to think the effect points one way, even though we cannot quantify it. Raising household income plausibly does shift population growth and consumption in a specific direction on average, even absent any usable number for the resulting change in welfare, and it is this second, directional-but-unquantifiable kind of cluelessness that is the real problem, not mere unpredictability in general.
(4) Marlene granted the distinction but denied that it rescued his argument: declining to donate also has directional, unquantifiable effects, since the money then gets spent, saved, or invested in ways that shift consumption and emissions in their own systematic directions. Both options, she said, are equally exposed to Dominic's more sophisticated worry, so at most this shows we cannot confidently rank the two by expected value, not that giving is the worse choice.
(5) Dominic said that was never quite his point: the real conclusion is not that withholding is better, but that consequentialism fails as something that can actually tell an agent what to do here, since the theory directs us to maximize expected value and there is, in the relevant sense, no usable expected value to maximize. That is a problem for consequentialism as a guide to action, whatever one ends up doing about the donation.
(6) Marlene answered that this concedes too much and proves too little: any theory that asks agents to weigh how their actions bear on the world, including whatever common-sense view Dominic relies on the rest of the time, faces the same gap between an in-principle criterion of rightness and what a finite agent can actually compute. Consequentialists have long distinguished the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure used to approximate it, so what Dominic described is a general predicament of acting under uncertainty, not a distinctive defect of consequentialism, and it still leaves open what either of them should actually do about the donation.
