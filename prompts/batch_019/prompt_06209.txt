I spent most of my working life as an experimental physicist, latterly running a low-temperature condensed-matter lab, and it is only since retiring that I have had the time to read philosophy with any care, rather than in stolen evenings. A small reading group I recently joined spent two sessions on the Church-Turing thesis, and I came away more unsettled than when we started — not about whether the thesis is true, but about what kind of claim it actually is.

The difficulty, as I understand it, is this: the thesis identifies an informal, pre-theoretic notion — roughly, "calculable by a human mechanically following a fixed procedure" — with a precisely defined mathematical one, Turing computability (or equivalently, lambda-definability or general recursiveness, whose mutual equivalence is a genuine theorem). Since only one side of that identification is rigorously defined, it seems the thesis cannot itself be proved in the way its formal cousins can. Yet it does not sit comfortably as mere stipulation either, since practitioners plainly treat the search for a counterexample as a meaningful enterprise. In my own field we were fairly disciplined about the line between a derived law and a generalization we had simply never seen fail, and this thesis does not settle easily into either box. Two things I have since read have sharpened the puzzle rather than resolved it. One is Robin Gandy's 1980 argument, which claims to derive a version of the thesis for a broad class of discrete deterministic mechanisms from a few general physical constraints — on information density and on the speed at which influences can propagate — which looks like an attempt to convert an informal intuition into something closer to a physical law. The other is the literature on so-called hypercomputation, including proposals involving exotic general-relativistic spacetimes (I believe the technical term is Malament-Hogarth spacetimes) in which an observer could, at least on paper, receive the outcome of a never-ending computation after only a finite span of their own proper time — offered as a conceivable counterexample to a "physical" reading of the thesis, even though no one supposes such a thing buildable.

What I would like is a considered account of what kind of claim the Church-Turing thesis is once these two considerations are taken seriously — mathematical convention, empirical hypothesis, conceptual analysis of an informal notion, or some hybrid not well captured by any of those labels — and of how far arguments like Gandy's actually change that status, as opposed to merely relocating the same informal assumptions one level down into physics. I would also like to understand why the hypercomputation proposals are generally not treated as live threats to the thesis: is it simply that they covertly smuggle in some physically dubious idealization, such as infinite precision or unbounded energy, or is there a more principled reason the thesis is comparatively insulated from this kind of counterexample?
