I'm a few years into a PhD in philosophy of mind, and last week our reading group finally wrapped up two sessions on Shadows of the Mind and the whole Lucas-Penrose arguement (the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show minds can't just be Turing machines). Afterward, two people in the group, Mara and Dev, ended up going at it over drinks in a way I still can't stop turning over, and I'd love a neutral, outside read on it rather than just "who won."

Roughly how it went: (1) Mara opened with the standard Lucas move — for any consistent formal system F that's rich enough for arithmetic, Gödel gives you a sentence G(F) that's true but unprovable in F, and a human reasoning about F from the outside can see that G(F) is true, so no single such F can be a complete model of what a mathematician can know, meaning minds aren't equivalent to machines in that sense. (2) Dev pushed back that this move is cheaper than it looks: seeing that G(F) is true requires already granting that F is consistent, but Gödel's second theorem says F can't prove its own consistency — so if the human doing the reasoning just is F, there's no legitimate source for that "F is consistent" premise except helping yourself to it, which begs the question against the mechanist. (3) Mara answered that this is exactly why Penrose's version swaps "consistent" for "sound": a working mathematician isn't just assuming F is consistent, they're committed to F only ever producing truths, period, since that's what doing math honestly rather than running a formal game amounts to, and recognizing that holistic fact about your own practice is precisely what a formal system can never establish about itself from inside. (4) Dev said that trades one problem for two worse ones: soundness is a strictly stronger, harder to earn assumption than consistency (mathematicians have believed false things plenty of times), and even granting soundness for free, you'd still need to know exactly which system you are in order to point to its particular unprovable sentence — and since nobody, mechanist or otherwise, has ever put an actual candidate F on the table for "how humans reason," the whole dispute is arguing with a ghost. (5) Mara's comeback was that the last part of (4) is a wash, not a win for Dev, because Lucas's argument was always conditional — give me any F as a hypothesis for what I am, and I'll produce a truth about F that F can't prove but I can — and a result that holds for every candidate F anyone could ever propose doesn't need an actual named F sitting on the table to be a real constraint on mechanism.

Nobody budged and we had to leave for the next talk. Can you go through (1)-(5) and say who's got the better of it at each individual step, not just who wins overall?
