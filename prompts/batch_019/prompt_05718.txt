A friend and I spent most of a long drive back from a wedding arguing about my cousin Tom, who has a family reputation for being fearless: he once pulled a neighbor out of a burning car, and he is always the one who physically steps between people when a fight breaks out at a party. My friend's claim was that this reputation doesn't survive scaling up: put Tom in a situation carrying a serious, not merely moderate, risk of death or lasting injury, and the sheer force of fear would take over before whatever makes him act boldly gets a chance to operate, so that there is a sense in which he isn't courageous now so much as merely untested, and the disposition would evaporate at exactly the moment it was called on. I argued the opposite — that the disposition is still there in the extreme case, and it is simply that something else, the fear response, gets in the way of its showing itself, the way a fragile object can be packed so well that it never breaks no matter how hard it is thrown, without thereby ceasing to be fragile.

Neither of us could settle it in the car, and looking back over some old notes afterward I realized we had basically been re-deriving, without the vocabulary, a standard distinction in the metaphysics of dispositions: the difference between a finkish disposition and a masked one. C. B. Martin's example is a wire that stays dead until the instant it is touched, at which point a hidden mechanism makes it live just in time to conduct; Mark Johnston's and David Lewis's cases, by contrast, are often illustrated with something like a fragile glass packed so well that a blow it actually suffers never breaks it — which, I realized with some embarrassment, was exactly the analogy I had reached for in the car without knowing it already had a name. In a mask, the disposition itself is untouched and something intervenes further downstream to block the manifestation; that is the kind of case Alexander Bird later pushed further, with a poison-and-antidote example, to argue that even Lewis's own repair to the conditional analysis — what he called the reformed conditional analysis — fails to solve the underlying problem.

I would like two things from you. First, an actual verdict on Tom, applying that apparatus properly: is his case, as I have described it, a fink, a mask, neither, or simply underdetermined by the facts as given — and if the last, what further fact about Tom, or about fear, or about courage, would settle the matter? Second, since the wire and the glass have been handled so often that anyone can recite the moral without ever having worked it out, I would like you to construct one clean example of your own — involving a human character trait rather than a physical object — sharp enough that two people arguing the way we were could not talk past each other about it. If you have room for it, I would also be glad to hear, briefly, whether cases like these give us any real reason to doubt that virtues, in anything like the old Aristotelian sense of settled dispositions of character, could be given a conditional analysis at all.
