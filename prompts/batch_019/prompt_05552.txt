Ok so this came up at our decision theory reading group last week and it's been bugging me since. One guy in the group (a stats postdoc) proposed a "party game" as a thought experiment: you flip a fair coin over and over until it lands heads for the first time. If that first happens on flip n, you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd, and you have to pay 2^n/n dollars when n is even. He wanted us to tell him what a fair entry fee for the game would be. A few of us from my cohort worked out the expected payout independantly and got totally different numbers -- not because of arithmetic slips, but because we'd each summed up the (infinite) list of outcomes in a different order, and it turns out that depending on the order you sum them in, the running total can settle down to pretty much any number you like, shoot off to +infinity or -infinity, or never settle down at all. One person in the group now claims the expected value just doesn't exist for this game, full stop, not merely "hard to compute." Everyone else thinks thats nuts, since every individual outcome has a perfectly finite payout and a perfectly well-defined probability attached to it -- how could averaging a bunch of well-defined numbers just fail to produce an answer at all? Is he onto something real, or is he just being pedantic, and either way, what should it tell us about charging people money to play a game like this?
