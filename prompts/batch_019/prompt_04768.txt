I've been arguing with a friend about whether stuff like WWI or the rise of the Nazis was basically bound to happen once the underlying conditions were in place, versus whether "if X hadn't happened, Y never would've" is even a legitimate kind of claim to make about history. She thinks all counterfactual history is basically cope for people who can't accept that impersonal forces don't care about individuals, and at some point, trying to push back, I brought up that huge philosophical digression at the end of War and Peace that I half-remember from a college class, where Tolstoy compares history to calculus for what feels like forty pages. I looked it up this morning to check I wasn't misremembering, and now I'm stuck on the passage itself for a totally different reason than the argument I was actually having — I can follow the individual sentences fine, but I don't think I could explain to you what the argument is actually supposed to establish, or whether it even says what I wanted it to say last night.

Right before the bit below, he's just argued that history only counts as a "science" to the extent you can show that free will itself is subject to causal law. Then (this is the Maude translation, from the second epilogue):

"The recognition of man's free will as something capable of influencing historical events, that is, as not subject to laws, is the same for history as the recognition of a free force moving the heavenly bodies would be for astronomy.

That assumption would destroy the possibility of the existence of laws, that is, of any science whatever. If there is even a single body moving freely, then the laws of Kepler and Newton are negatived and no conception of the movement of the heavenly bodies any longer exists. If any single action is due to free will, then not a single historical law can exist, nor any conception of historical events.

[...]

Only by reducing this element of free will to the infinitesimal, that is, by regarding it as an infinitely small quantity, can we convince ourselves of the absolute inaccessibility of the causes, and then instead of seeking causes, history will take the discovery of laws as its problem."

He then spends another page or so making basically the same move for math and physics generally — calculus only got anywhere once it gave up tracking exact causes and just learned to integrate infinitely small quantities, and Newton's law of gravity doesn't explain why bodies attract each other, it just states a pattern true of everything from planets down to specks of dust — and concludes that history has to do the same thing with individual human wills.

Three things about this I can't untangle on my own. First: is the "reduce free will to an infinitesimal" move doing real philosophical work, or is it a kind of sleight of hand borrowed from math that doesn't actually transfer? In calculus the infinitesimals still sum to something exact and fully determinate — nothing about dx being small makes the total any less knowable in principle — so I don't see how that licenses a claim about "absolute inaccessibility," which sounds like a much stronger, almost skeptical claim than the math analogy earns him. Second: is this actually an argument for historical inevitability in the strong sense — nothing could possibly have gone otherwise — or is it really just a methodological claim, that historians should hunt for aggregate, statistical-type laws instead of crediting "great men" with causing things? Those feel like genuinely different claims to me, and he seems to slide between them without noticing. Third: does any of this actually rule out the kind of counterfactual my friend was mocking, or does it just deny that any single person (a king, a general) is the right unit to reason about, while leaving the door open for contingency at the level of the individual "infinitesimal" wills themselves? I vaguely remember Isaiah Berlin wrote something connected to all this — I think there's an essay actually called "The Hedgehog and the Fox" that's specifically about Tolstoy, and a separate one literally titled "Historical Inevitability" that's more general — but I've never read either one, so if one of them is genuinely relevant feel free to bring it in, though I'd mainly like to get Tolstoy's own argument straight on its own terms first, since apparently I didnt manage that last night.
