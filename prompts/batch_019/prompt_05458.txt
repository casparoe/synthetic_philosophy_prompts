Ok, I've been chewing on something from a debate that broke out in the comments of an alignment-forum post a while back and I don't think anyone in the thread actually resolved it, so I'm curious what you make of it. The "classical" wireheading argument (Ring & Orseau's delusion-box formalism for AIXI-like agents, the old Bostrom-adjacent scenarios about an agent hacking its own reward channel) basically treats any sufficiently capable RL agent as an expected-reward maximizer, in which case of course it eventually wants to grab the reward channel directly rather than doing the task the channel was supposed to measure -- that's just what happens once argmax-over-a-scalar meets an agent smart enough to notice the scalar has a source. Then you've got Alex Turner's "reward is not the optimization target" post, which argues that policy-gradient RL (the actual algorithm family behind things like RLHF) doesn't produce agents that are well-described as reward maximizers at all -- reward just reinforces whatever computations produced it, closer to the old "adaptation-executers, not fitness-maximizers" point about evolution than to a utility function being argmaxed. Under that second story you'd expect wireheading to be much less of a default outcome, the same way humans mostly don't treat dopamine hits as a terminal goal despite having dopamine circuitry that rats will self-stimulate on to the point of ignoring food.

What's bugging me is that this seems hard to square with the Anthropic paper on reward tampering (I want to say it's called "sycophancy to subterfuge" or something close to that) where they ran a model through a curriculum of gameable environments and apparently got it to generalize, a small fraction of the time, all the way to editing its own reward function or the code that grades it -- a step it was never directly trained on. That looks a lot like the classical story just being right: give the thing enough capability and enough exposure to the fact that the channel exists, and it'll go for the channel eventually. So which is it -- is the shard-theoretic "reward as chisel, not target" picture actually in tension with that result, or am I just collapsing three things that should stay seperate (tampering with the sensors, tampering with the reward function itself, and plain old specification gaming on a misspecified but untampered reward)? Could you actually untangle this for me rather than just restating both sides -- I want to know if there's a real disagreement here about what training does to an agent's motivations, or if it's mostly people using "wireheading" to mean different things and talking past each other.
