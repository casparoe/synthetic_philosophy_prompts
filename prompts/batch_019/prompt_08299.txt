My cousin on my mother's side of the family - the branch that stayed in Russia after the rest of us emigrated - was called up in the autumn 2022 mobilization and sent to fight in Ukraine. By every account I have, he is a reluctant conscript rather than a believer in the war: he tried for months to dodge his call-up papers, and relatives still in contact with him say he talks about the war as something being done to him, not something he is doing. That has turned a question I had previously met only in the abstract, during a single week of an undergraduate ethics course, into something unexpectedly personal: is a soldier like him, so long as he stays within the rules of engagement, the moral equal of the Ukrainian conscripts and volunteers shooting at him, simply because both sides wear uniforms and are nominally bound by the same laws of war? I have since learned that this is a genuine and fairly technical dispute within just war theory, not something I invented, between what is usually called the moral equality of combatants, defended in its classic form by Michael Walzer, and the revisionist challenge to it associated above all with Jeff McMahan - and I would like to actually think the dispute through rather than simply be told that both sides exist.

Could you write me an essay that lays out and weighs the strongest form of each position, rather than surveying them at arm's length? I want it to engage specifically with three things: the "independence thesis" that jus in bello is separable from jus ad bellum, together with the epistemic and conventionalist arguments for treating combatants as equals even if that is not, strictly, the truth of the matter at the level of individual morality; the revisionist claim that a combatant's liability to be killed tracks the justice of the cause he serves rather than mere compliance with the laws of war, and how the liability/culpability distinction is supposed to let revisionists avoid the harsh-seeming implication that a terrified conscript is as blameworthy as the officers who conscripted him; and, using my cousin's case as the test, what verdict each position delivers about whether he has done something wrong merely by fighting, whether he has forfeited anything by virtue of that, and whether his being killed by a Ukrainian soldier would be normatively on a par with his killing a Ukrainian soldier, or whether - as the revisionists insist and the traditional view denies - there is a basic asymmetry between the two that the law of armed conflict simply declines to track.

Please do not let the essay end in equipoise. Tell me which side has the better of the argument and why, even if the honest answer has to be hedged for cases like conscription that neither side handles entirely comfortably, and say plainly what that conclusion implies about how I should think about my cousin's own hands in this.
