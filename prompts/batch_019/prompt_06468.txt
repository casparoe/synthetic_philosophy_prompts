Over the past few months I've been drafting the interpretive chapter of my dissertation on canonical quantum gravity, and I've run into a problem that, to my surprise, has nothing to do with quantization as such. It's Einstein's old hole argument, still unresolved after a century and made more pressing, not less, by the fact that background-independent theories build diffeomorphism invariance in from the very start. My advisor keeps waving the issue off with a one-line remark about spacetime points having "no intrinsic identity," which is exactly the kind of line that sounds fine until you try to say precisely what it commits you to.

The issue actually came to a head at our last reading group, when two of the senior postdocs got into a real argument about it. One of them held that a substantivalist about the spacetime manifold can escape the threatened indeterminism by going "sophisticated": deny that spacetime points carry any primitive identity (haecceity) over and above the qualitative structure they bear, so that if d is a diffeomorphism that is the identity map everywhere outside some "hole" region H but smoothly differs from the identity inside H, the model built from the metric g and the model built from the metric g* obtained by dragging g along by d don't describe two distinct physical possibilities that happen to agree outside H and disagree inside it — they describe one possibility, redundantly presented twice (what's often called, in this literature, "Leibniz equivalence"). The other postdoc thought this concession gutted substantivalism of its content: once you give up haecceitism about points, she said, you've simply relabeled relationalism, and "sophistication" is just a way of keeping the word "substantivalism" while quietly discarding everything that made it a distinctive thesis in the first place.

I'd like to see that argument actually played out, since every secondhand summary I've read flattens precisely the part I need to understand. Could you write a dialogue between a manifold substantivalist and a relationalist hashing this out, picking up, say, right where the postdocs left off, still arguing in the hallway after the meeting broke up? I want the substantivalist to really mount the sophisticated-substantivalist defense, including something like Einstein's own point-coincidence intuition that only the qualitative coincidence-structure of events is physically real, rather than just asserting the conclusion, and I want the relationalist to press the "this is just relationalism in a substantivalist costume" objection hard enough that the substantivalist has to say something substantive about what she still believes that the relationalist doesn't. Please don't let either side land an easy knockout or slide past the other's actual point; I want each move answered with the strongest available reply, since as far as I can tell this is one of the debates the professional literature hasn't actually settled.
