My PhD advisor in evolutionary developmental biology is retiring after thirty-five years, and as the most senior PhD student in her lab I've been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. Her reputation rests on a lifelong defense of the Gould-Lewontin spandrels critique: the claim that not every trait -- and, she likes to add while eyeing her own CV, not every career -- is an adaptation; some things are simply byproducts of other constraints, later co-opted for functions nobody designed them for. She is delighted rather than embarrassed that architectural purists insist San Marco's arches are technically pendentives, not spandrels, and treats this as a standing lesson in how a flawed metaphor can still carry a true argument. Half the room will be evo-devo colleagues, including several strict adaptationists she has sparred with for decades, who will catch every reference; the other half is family, who have heard her say 'spandrel' at every holiday dinner for twenty years without quite absorbing what it means. The obvious toast -- 'every step led perfectly to this moment' -- is exactly the Panglossian narrative she has spent her career arguing against, and she will notice if I tell it. Please write a five-minute toast built around one good joke about her own career having been a spandrel, warm enough for the family and precise enough that the evo-devo contingent doesn't wince, while staying true to what she actually thinks careers -- like traits -- are made of.
