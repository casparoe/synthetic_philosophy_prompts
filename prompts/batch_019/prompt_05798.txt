I sit on the publications review committee at a small existential-risk research institute. A staff researcher has submitted a public report arguing that, absent any reason to think our birth rank is special, we should reason as though we were a random draw from all humans who will ever live -- the Self-Sampling Assumption -- and that since only a comparatively small number of humans have existed so far, this favors hypotheses on which humanity's total future is short; the report uses this to argue for a substantial near-term probability of existential catastrophe and to recommend that our funders redirect money from long-horizon research toward near-term catastrophe preparedness. The committee is split: half think the reasoning is sound and the conclusion too important to sit on, while the other half want to block or heavily caveat the report, objecting that the argument's force depends on a contested choice of reference class (biological humans only, or also any digital minds our descendants might create) and that the rival Self-Indication Assumption would cancel the update entirely while generating its own well-known absurdities, such as the presumptuous philosopher. Draft the committee's decision memo -- publish, publish with revisions, or decline -- with a recommendation the researcher could actually act on and a fair hearing for the strongest technical objection on the losing side.
