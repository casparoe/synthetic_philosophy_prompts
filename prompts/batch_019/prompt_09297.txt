So in physics class we're doing rotational motion and my teacher showed us the Newton's bucket thing as kind of a fun aside — you spin a bucket of water and the surface goes concave, and the whole point is that the concave-ness can't be about the water's motion relative to the bucket, since the water is most concave exactly when it's spinning together with the bucket with zero relative motion between them. Newton's move was to say the water must be rotating relative to absolute space itself. Our teacher mentioned that Mach pushed back on this later (rotation relative to all the other mass in the universe, not empty space), and that Einstein took Mach's idea pretty seriously when he was building general relativity.

I went down a rabbit hole reading about this on my own because my friend Alex, who's way more into physics than me, insisted that Mach basically won this argument and GR proved him right, end of story. But it seems way messier than that once you actually get into GR. Like on one hand there's frame-dragging (the Lense-Thirring effect), which is a real, experimentally tested thing (Gravity Probe B and stuff) where a spinning mass drags spacetime around with it and shifts the local inertial frames nearby — that feels very pro-Mach. But then GR also allows completely empty solutions with no matter anywhere (flat Minkowski spacetime, and apparently Einstein was bugged by de Sitter's solution for a similar reason) that still have a perfectly well-defined inertial structure telling you what's rotating and what isn't. That second part seems like a real problem for saying inertia is ALWAYS just relations between material bodies, and I read that it's part of why Einstein ended up kind of disappointed that GR wasn't as fully Machian as he'd hoped.

So what I actually want is this: can you argue for the position that once you factor in these GR-level complications, Newton basically wins the argument he started with the bucket — i.e. that some kind of substantival spacetime structure (not necessarily "absolute space" in his exact sense, but something doing the same job) has to exist no matter what, and full relationism about space and time can't be right. Please don't give me a wishy-washy "it depends how you look at it" answer, I want the actual strongest version of that specific conclusion, and I especially need you to deal with the obvious comeback from Alex's side, that frame-dragging shows it's all relational after all, instead of just ignoring that part of the evidence.
