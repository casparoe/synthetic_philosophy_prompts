I've spent a fair amount of time on LessWrong and adjacent blogs following the arguments about Newcomb's Problem, and what strikes me every time is how much weight both causal and evidential decision theorists put on facts about what an agent would do in a scenario it never actually enters — the predictor's model of you, and the fact that the opaque box was already filled (or left empty) accordingly before you ever choose. A while back, a commenter on one of these threads mentioned, almost as an aside, that this is structurally close to a much older puzzle: Luis de Molina's doctrine of "middle knowledge," on which God knows, logically prior to deciding what to create, exactly what every possible free creature would freely do in absolutely any circumstance it might be placed in, and then chooses which world to actualize accordingly. I'm not remotely a theist, but I fell down a Stanford-Encyclopedia-shaped rabbit hole on this and got stuck on the grounding objection: the worry that nothing could make these "counterfactuals of creaturely freedom" true, since on the libertarian picture of freedom that Molinism itself presupposes, the merely possible creature never actually exists to settle the matter one way or the other.

Could you write me a dialogue between a Molinist and an open theist, set at the moment just "before" creation, with both of them looking over the menu of feasible worlds that God's middle knowledge is supposed to have already narrowed down from the full space of logically possible worlds — a bit like two people arguing in front of Newcomb's boxes about what's already been settled and how. I want the open theist to press the grounding objection with some actual teeth: not just asserting that the counterfactuals are "ungrounded," but explaining what's specifically wrong with a brute contingent truth here that isn't equally wrong with brute contingent truths we tolerate elsewhere — and to also raise the separate worry that even a true-but-ungrounded counterfactual, fixed logically prior to the creature's existence, seems to settle what the creature will do before it ever gets to choose, which sits badly with the very libertarian freedom Molinism was invented to protect. The Molinist should have real moves available in response (something in the neighborhood of "counterfactual power" over what is merely explanatorily rather than causally or temporally prior, for instance) rather than just restating the doctrine more insistently, and should get to press the open theist in turn on what it costs to give up middle knowledge — whether a God who genuinely doesn't know what any free creature would do until it does it can still be said to providentially govern history rather than just improvise in response to it. Have the two of them actually answer each other's strongest points in sequence rather than talking past one another, and don't feel obliged to let either side come out ahead.
