So this term I'm taking a small seminar that's cross-listed between Philosophy and CS, officially called something like "Philosophical Foundations of AI Alignment," and this week's unit was the shutdown problem: the puzzle of designing an agent that (a) shuts down when a shutdown button gets pressed, (b) doesn't try to prevent or cause the button being pressed, and (c) otherwise just competently pursues whatever goal it's been given. The readings covered like six different proposals and I kept loosing track of which one was actually supposed to fix which part of the problem, partly because they're each set up in a totally different framework (one's a two-player game, one's RL theory, one's decision theory with incomplete preferences), so it's genuinely unclear to me whether they're rivals or just talking past each other.

For my presentation next week I want to sort them into an actual tier list, and I'd like a sanity check before I show my study group. Here's the lineup, with my rough paraphrase of each: (1) the original MIRI "Corrigibility" desiderata (Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky & Armstrong, 2015), which mostly formalizes the problem and shows that naive linear combinations of a "normal" utility function and a "shutdown" utility function lead to unintended behavior; (2) Armstrong's older "utility indifference" trick (2010), where the agent is made to expect the same payoff whether or not it gets shut down, so in theory it stops caring about the button either way; (3) Hadfield-Menell et al.'s "Off-Switch Game" (2017), where the robot defers to the human because it's uncertain about the true reward and treats the human reaching for the switch as free evidence about what it should want; (4) Orseau & Armstrong's "safely interruptible agents" (2016), which leans on off-policy RL so interruptions don't get baked into what the agent learns; (5) Thornley's more recent work (he's got a couple of papers on this since 2023) proposing to solve it via incomplete preferences, basically denying the agent a preference between trajectories of different lengths so it never has a reason to pay a cost to influence the button; and (6) what's basically deployed for real language models right now, i.e. just RLHFing the thing until it behaves well around shutdown/interruption requests, with no deeper decision-theoretic guarantee underneath at all.
