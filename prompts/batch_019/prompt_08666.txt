I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and lately I've been struck by how closely today's neuroscience-flavored arguments that free will is an illusion echo a much older piece of moral philosophy: P.F. Strawson's 1962 essay "Freedom and Resentment." Strawson's central move is to distinguish the ordinary "participant" reactive attitudes we take toward one another — resentment, gratitude, indignation, love — from the "objective attitude" we reserve for small children, the severely deranged, or anyone we've stopped holding responsible and started merely managing, treating, or working around. He argues that a wholesale, permanent shift to the objective attitude, even under a settled conviction that determinism is true, would be a real impoverishment of human life rather than an enlightenment — though he concedes it's a coherent possibility, with genuine gains as well as losses.

I'm assembling an occasional fiction feature and would like a short story, around 1,200–1,600 words, that takes this thought experiment seriously rather than playing it as dystopia. Center the story on one specific relationship in which some concrete, ordinary-seeming development — a diagnosis, a piece of neuroscience, a therapy, a court ruling, anything except "the state controls minds" — makes the objective attitude the socially or medically correct stance for one character to take toward another. The story should make Strawson's ledger of gains and losses concrete and felt rather than stated, and it should keep the philosophical machinery implicit: no character should name the concept out loud.
