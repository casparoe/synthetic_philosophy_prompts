I've spent a good deal of time over the past year or so working through F. H. Bradley's regress argument on my own, with no formal training in philosophy, and I've reached the point where I can state the argument but can't decide how much it actually proves. As I understand it, Bradley argued that if two things, A and B, are joined by a relation R, then R itself must somehow be connected to A and to B by further relations, and those further relations themselves need connecting in turn, so that the whole business runs off into an endless series. He took this to show that relations, and the ordinary pluralistic world of separate related things, cannot be the final truth about reality. The same puzzle keeps turning up, in what I've read, next to the question of why a sentence like "Tom is wise" expresses one unified claim rather than just naming three separate items side by side -- Tom, wisdom, and a connecting word -- and this gets called the problem of the unity of the proposition.

What I can't settle for myself is whether the regress is genuinely vicious, so that a fact or proposition really does need some further, non-relational ingredient to hold its pieces together, or whether it is a benign infinite series that never actually threatens the reality or unity of the original relation at all. Could you write an essay that lays out the strongest considerations on both sides of this question and then defends a definite verdict, rather than concluding that reasonable people just disagree?
