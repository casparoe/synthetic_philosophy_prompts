I'm about two-thirds into a novel set in a small Illinois parish in the early 1970s, and I've stalled on a chapter I don't yet trust myself to write. My priest character, Father Voss, heard the real killer's confession in a murder case for which an innocent young man is now awaiting trial. He cannot break the seal to save him: canon law treats the seal as genuinely absolute, with no exception carved out for preventing a wrongful conviction, and a priest who violates it directly incurs excommunication automatically, not as a matter of degree. The scene I need is the one where the defense attorney, Ruth Kessler, corners him in the sacristy. Ruth was raised Catholic and taught by the same order of nuns that trained Father Voss, so she isn't going to make the crude objection ("a life is at stake, just lie"); she knows enough Augustine to make the sharp one.

I've been reading Augustine's actual treatises on lying — De Mendacio, and the later, considerably harsher Contra Mendacium — to get this right, and what strikes me is how careful his position is. He grades lies by the harm and benefit they cause, he holds that even a lie told to save an innocent life is still a sin, and yet he insists that silence, a flat refusal to answer, or a statement that is true but incomplete belongs to an entirely different moral category than a lie, because what makes an utterance a lie is the speaker's intention to assert what he himself believes false, not the false belief it happens to leave in the hearer's mind. That is exactly the seam Ruth wants to force open: if Augustine cares enough about consequences to rank lies by the damage they do, why does his whole edifice depend on a mechanism — engineered, deliberate silence — that she can show produces, sentence by sentence, precisely the false belief a lie would have produced, at the one moment it matters most?

Could you write this as a dialogue between the two of them, with narration and stage direction kept to a bare minimum? I want Father Voss to actually mount Augustine's defense in his own reasoning, rather than retreating to "the Church teaches," and I want Ruth to press the objection at its strongest, not its crudest. Neither of them should win outright by the end — I'd like a reader who has never heard of Augustine to feel the trap closing on both characters, and a reader who has to feel that the scene played fair with him.
