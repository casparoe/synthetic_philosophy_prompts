I teach a graduate seminar in social epistemology, and this term's unit on collective belief-formation centered on the DARPA-funded "Replication Markets" project as a case study: traders bought and sold contracts on whether thousands of published social-science findings would replicate, and the resulting prices were treated by the program's organizers as an aggregated forecast of replicability. For that session I invited a colleague from the economics department, who complicated the discussion considerably by walking the class through the no-trade theorems that grow out of Aumann's work on common knowledge and, more directly, out of Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 result: given a common prior and common knowledge that any offered trade is individually rational for whoever proposes it, risk-averse traders should not trade on the basis of private information alone, since a counterparty's mere willingness to accept the trade already reveals what you needed to know, erasing whatever apparent gain motivated the trade in the first place. My students were left unsure what to make of a market that is, on paper, exactly the kind of setting the theorem describes, yet that sustains substantial trading volume and is nonetheless held up as an unusually reliable way of pooling dispersed expert opinion.

Please lay out, in a form I could hand to students as a short supplementary note, what you take to be the most important implications of the no-trade theorems for how a careful epistemologist should interpret prices and trading volume in markets of this kind. I would like you to work through what heavy trading activity is actually evidence of, once we grant that the theorem's idealizing assumptions -- common priors, the absence of hedging or liquidity motives, common knowledge of everyone's rationality -- fail to hold in any real market, and to take a stand on whether someone with a strong, well-considered view that diverges from the market price, but no privileged information the rest of the market lacks, would be rationally required to defer to that price rather than bet against it.
