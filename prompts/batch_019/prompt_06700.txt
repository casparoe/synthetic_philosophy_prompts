And here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"The no-trade theorem formalizes a simple worry: you shouldn't be willing to take one side of a bet if the only reason someone wants to take the other side is that they know something you don't. Milgrom and Stokey's version assumes that traders are risk-averse expected-utility maximizers who share a common prior over the relevant states of the world, and that before any new private information arrives, the traders' existing holdings are already Pareto optimal, so nobody has a purely risk-sharing reason to trade further. Suppose new private signals then arrive and the traders propose a trade based on them. If it becomes common knowledge that both sides are willing to go through with it, each trader can reason that the other is only offering or accepting these terms because their private information makes the trade favorable to them, and updating on that inference erases whatever gain looked available a moment ago; iterating this reasoning shows that no trade motivated purely by asymmetric information can survive common knowledge of the willingness to make it. In effect, this is Aumann's own result: Aumann (1976) had already shown that two Bayesians with a common prior cannot knowingly disagree about the odds of a bet once their willingness to make it is common knowledge, and Milgrom and Stokey's contribution was mainly to generalize this from a simple bet to arbitrary reallocations of risky assets.

Given this, the enormous trading volume in real markets is puzzling, or at least tells us that some assumption of the theorem fails in practice. One popular explanation is that real traders simply don't share a common prior: since a common prior just means that traders start out with access to the same information, disagreement is unsurprising once you allow that people read different sources, use different models, or are simply overconfident. A second explanation is that a great deal of volume is non-informational: index funds rebalancing, retirees drawing down savings, or firms hedging currency exposure trade regardless of anyone's beliefs, and this liquidity trading gives informed traders enough cover to profit without immediately revealing everything they know. I find the liquidity story more persuasive as a general account, since heterogeneous priors can be invoked to explain away almost any disagreement after the fact, whereas liquidity motives are at least independently identifiable.

This bears on the idea, associated with Hayek and revived by advocates of prediction markets, that prices are valuable because they aggregate dispersed private information. The no-trade theorem sits in some tension with this: if a willingness to trade already reveals what you know once that willingness is common knowledge, full aggregation seems to require less and less actual trading, which is a strange kind of efficiency, and it is unclear anyone would bother acquiring costly information in the first place if prices already reflected it. Insider-trading law fits this picture reasonably well: it polices exactly the situations the theorem says shouldn't survive common knowledge, which suggests regulators implicitly treat trading on concealed private information as the kind of trade that only works because it isn't common knowledge."
