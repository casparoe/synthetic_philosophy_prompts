My company was recently acquired, and our long-standing logistics vendor now knows that the contract we are currently renewing will be the last one before we switch to the buyer's preferred provider. Almost as soon as this became clear to both sides, the vendor's willingness to expedite rush orders or absorb minor cost overruns evaporated, which struck me as a real-world instance of the "unraveling" that backward induction predicts once there is common knowledge of rationality (CKR) in a finitely repeated interaction with a known end. Yet I also recall that Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts, and Wilson (KMRW) showed that a small amount of incomplete information, just enough doubt about whether a partner is a conditionally cooperative type (say, one playing tit-for-tat) rather than a purely self-interested backward-inducer, suffices to sustain near-full cooperation until just short of the true endgame.

Could you compare these two accounts of endgame behavior in finitely repeated games, the CKR/backward-induction unraveling result and the KMRW reputation account built by relaxing common knowledge, and say which offers the more realistic model of what I witnessed? I am especially interested in what the apparent need for the second, "noisier" model implies about common knowledge of rationality as an idealization in epistemic game theory, rather than as an accurate description of how real strategic reasoning proceeds.
