I run compliance at a mid-sized accounting firm, and a case landed on my desk three weeks ago that I still haven't reasoned my way through, so I'd welcome an outside perspective before our review committee votes on it next week. One of our second-year associates missed a disclosure requirement on a client file -- a genuine lapse, not fraud, but serious enough that our internal code calls for whichever associate is "of record" on the error to be barred from client-facing work for two full quarters, a mark that would almost certainly cost her any chance of making senior associate this cycle. A partner who is retiring within the year, and who had no hand in the mistake, has now volunteered -- openly, with the associate's and the committee's full knowledge -- to be entered as the associate of record for disciplinary purposes and to personally sit out client work for those two quarters himself, on the reasoning that the bar exists mainly to reassure clients that lapses have consequences, and that this purpose is served just as well no matter who absorbs it.

About half the committee treats the offer the way we'd treat someone else paying off your parking ticket: the requirement is satisfied, so it shouldn't matter whose name is attached to the payment. The other half of us -- and I lean this way, though I've struggled to defend it out loud -- suspects that letting the partner "serve" her sanction doesn't actually change what happened on her file, and that we'd be fooling ourselves about what the two-quarter bar is even for if we thought it could simply be handed off like an unpaid bill. My husband, who has limited patience for what he calls my overthinking, thinks I'm manufacturing a dilemma where a generous colleague has offered a clean solution.

Before I vote, I'd like help thinking through the underlying question rather than just the politics of our committee. Is there a principled difference between a case like the parking ticket, where a substitute can fully discharge the obligation, and a case like this one, where I keep sensing that something resists being handed off -- and if such a difference exists, what exactly is it? And practically speaking, does locating that difference give the committee a real reason to turn down the partner's offer, or does it just mean we owe it to ourselves and to the associate to be honest about what accepting it would, and would not, actually accomplish?
