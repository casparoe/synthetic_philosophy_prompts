I'm on my university's research integrity inquiry committee this year (worst service assignment I've ever drawn, honestly). We've got a complaint that a biology PI fabricated data in a published paper, and we brought in two outside statisticians -- both senior, well-regarded, no connection to the lab -- to independently look at the raw numbers. Dr. Okonkwo says the variance across "independent" replicate wells is way too low to be real and reads it as fabrication. Dr. Lindqvist, looking at the same data, says its fully explained by a sloppy but apparently not-uncommon lab habit of reusing control-well readings whenever the plate reader flags a run as bad, and see's no evidence of fraud. Neither report has an obvious hole in it, and neither of them will budge after reading the other's write-up. Our policy says the inquiry stage only has to decide if there's "sufficient basis" for a full investigation, not settle the misconduct question itself. The committee's deadlocked and needs a recommendation memo to circulate before we reconvene next week. Can you draft it -- close the inquiry, escalate, or something in between -- and actually engage with what's compelling about the losing expert's read instead of just waving it off? (Feels uncomfortably like one of those peer-disagreement cases from an epistemology seminar, except now I actually have to decide something.)
