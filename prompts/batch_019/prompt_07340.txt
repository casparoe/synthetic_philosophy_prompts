And here is the answer I've been asked to grade, reproduced exactly as my peer submitted it:

"Boorse's biostatistical theory (BST) analyses health naturalistically: an organism is healthy insofar as its parts and processes make their statistically species-typical contribution to individual survival and reproduction, where 'typical' is fixed relative to a reference class defined by age group and sex within the species. Disease is then any internal state that reduces some functional ability below this typical level of efficiency. Boorse insists this is purely descriptive — he separates it from 'illness,' a narrower, evaluative notion covering diseases serious enough to be undesirable, incapacitating, and a title to special treatment. The appeal of BST is that it seems to let biology, rather than our values, decide what counts as disease.

The reference class problem attacks the seemingly innocuous relativization to reference classes. Pregnancy is the sharpest case: measured against the reference class of adult women generally, a pregnant woman's blood volume, hormone levels, and immune function are all statistically atypical, so an unmodified BST risks classifying pregnancy itself as a dysfunction. Boorse's fix is to narrow the reference class to women at the relevant reproductive stage, so that pregnancy comes out normal relative to itself. But, as Kingma argues, choosing that particular subdivision over the countless other statistically available ones (by age, by parity, by any number of other variables) already presupposes that pregnancy is a healthy condition rather than a pathology to be explained away — which is exactly what BST was supposed to tell us in value-free terms, not assume in advance.

This doesn't automatically hand the debate to normativism, though. Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis tries to keep a naturalistic core (dysfunction relative to evolved function, not just statistics) while adding an explicitly normative harm condition for something to count as a disorder, which looks like a promising middle path that doesn't need Boorse's reference classes to do all the work on their own.

Still, once we see that no purely statistical rule can privilege one reference class over another without smuggling in a prior view about what's healthy, I think naturalism has lost the argument, and disease-talk is normative talk in disguise — essentially Nordenfelt's position, that disease judgments track a person's ability to realize their vital goals. That looks like the only stable place to land."
