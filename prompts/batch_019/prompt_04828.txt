I'm teaching my personal identity seminar again this term, and every year a couple students latch onto that passage near the end of Reasons and Persons where Parfit says accepting his reductionist view felt like the walls of a glass tunnel dissolving, and where he mentions being told — by a Buddhist monk or scholar, I think, my memory's a bit fuzzy on the exact detail — that this is basically what the Buddha already taught about the self. He's clearly pleased about it, like his conclusion has 2500 years of good company. Students then run with this and treat it as more or less settled that Parfit's Relation R reductionism and the anatta doctrine are the same view in different clothes: one argued for with fission and teleporters, the other with dependent origination and the five aggregates, but both landing on "there's no further fact about personal identity beyond some pattern of continuity."

I've never been fully convinced by this, but I also don't want to just wave at "well, Buddhism also cares about karma and liberation from suffering, so its not really the same" as if that settles things, since that could just be separable religious baggage bolted onto an otherwise identical metaphysical claim. Could you actually argue against the claim that Parfit's reductionism and the no-self doctrine amount to the same view, or that either one straightforwardly vindicates the other? I want it to engage with the actual structure of what each side is doing — what work Relation R does for Parfit versus whatever's doing the corresponding work on the Buddhist side, whether the two-truths framework changes what "no self" is even claiming, that sort of thing — rather than a surface-level list of cultural differences. If you genuinely think the equivalence holds up, I'd rather hear the strongest version of that than a strawman of my worry, but my money's on there being real daylight between the two views.
