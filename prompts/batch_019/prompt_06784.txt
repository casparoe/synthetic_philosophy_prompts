I'm a PhD student in theoretical computer science working on multi-agent systems, and a few of us in a reading group on open-source game theory recently went through Tennenholtz's original 2004 paper on program equilibrium back to back with the later provability-logic-based work (often cited as LaVictoire et al.'s "Robust Cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma: Program Equilibrium via Provability Logic") that reconstructs cooperation in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma through the "FairBot" construction, whose good behavior is established using Löb's theorem. I came away with the proofs more or less checking out line by line, but with a nagging sense that I don't actually understand why the second approach needs such heavy machinery when the first one seems to get a qualitatively similar result almost for free, and I'd like help building the intuition that's missing rather than a re-derivation of the formalism itself.

Here is the puzzle as I currently see it. Tennenholtz shows that mutual cooperation can be supported as a program equilibrium almost trivially: a program that plays cooperate if and only if its opponent's source code is syntactically identical to its own, and otherwise falls back to some punishment/minimax action, already suffices, since any unilateral deviation from the shared program is met with punishment, and no formal provability or self-reference beyond a literal string comparison is required. My instinct is that the later, much heavier construction is trying to buy something pure syntactic matching can't give — robustness to opponents that are not literal clones but are nonetheless "trying" to cooperate with cooperators — and that this is exactly where the seemingly obvious fix, "have each program simulate the other and cooperate iff the simulation returns cooperate," stops being well defined. What I can't articulate cleanly is why that naive mutual-simulation approach actually breaks down (is it a halting-problem-style obstruction, a diagonalization/circularity problem, or something else entirely?), why replacing "simulate the opponent" with "search for a bounded formal proof that the opponent cooperates with you" is precisely the fix, and what specific work Löb's theorem is doing in closing the resulting loop, as opposed to it being merely a convenient lemma that happens to appear somewhere in the proof.

Could you walk me through this at the level of mechanism rather than formalism: why direct mutual simulation fails as a way of even defining these programs, what property of provability (as opposed to truth, or bare mutual simulation) lets the Löb's-theorem argument sidestep that failure, and whether the provability-logic construction is best understood as a strict generalization of Tennenholtz's equality-matching trick or as solving a genuinely different problem that equality-matching was never attempting to address? I can follow the formal proofs on their own terms, so I'd rather not receive a restatement of them — what I'm after is the "why does this particular piece of machinery have to be there" version.
