I spent my working life as a field biologist, and it's only in retirement that i've started reading philosophy properly, so bear with me if I fumble the terms. Right now I'm on the board of a small wildlife trust, and we're arguing about whether to start a captive breeding program for a toad species thats down to a couple dozen animals in the wild. It does badly in captivity - stress, some deformities, maybe half the tadpoles don't make it - though survivors seem to have decent enough lives, and decades from now their offspring might get released into a restored wetland. Two board members keep saying the numbers clearly favor going ahead, since some toads with okay lives beats no toads at all, and I can't fault the arithmetic. But that's not what's bothering me: if we don't breed them nobody is worse off, there's no toad out there missing its shot at existing, whereas if we do breed them and it goes badly we've created real suffering somebody actually has to live through, and those two things don't feel like they belong on the same scale. My colleagues think I'm just being sentimental about tadpoles - is there something real behind that feeling, or am I overthinking a straightforward numbers problem?
