I am writing a novel built around a Parfit-style teleporter: a device that scans a traveler down to the last psychological and physical detail, destroys the original, and reconstructs an exact replica at the destination. In the novel's central set-piece, the machine malfunctions in a very specific way: the replica is successfully assembled at the far end, but the original body survives for several more hours before dying of the injuries the scan inflicted, so that original and replica briefly coexist. This is essentially Parfit's "Branch-Line Case" from Reasons and Persons, and it matters to the plot because, once original and replica coexist even briefly, they cannot be one and the same person by Leibniz's Law alone — whatever we say about an ordinary, non-branching teleportation. Parfit's own gloss on the case is that the dying original should feel no more concern about his impending death than an ordinary traveler feels about being "destroyed" at the scanner, since what matters in survival is not identity but Relation R — psychological continuity and connectedness, of the right sort — which the replica carries on regardless of branching. I want my dying character's dread to be intellectually respectable rather than a mere emotional holdover, so I need the opposite conclusion argued as rigorously as possible: that the Branch-Line Case does not show identity is irrelevant to what matters, and that the original has every reason to dread his death whatever becomes of his replica. Meet Parfit's hardest challenge to this view head-on: since the only difference between the Branch-Line Case and an everyday teleportation that most people accept as survival is the seemingly trivial timing of the original's destruction, explain how a fact this trivial-seeming could still be doing serious normative work, rather than simply asserting that identity matters and moving on. Reach a definite verdict; don't hedge toward "both identity and Relation R matter somewhat."
