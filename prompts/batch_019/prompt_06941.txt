For the past several months I've been volunteering with a coalition in my state campaigning to strike the exception clause from our constitution's ban on slavery — the same exception that survives in the Thirteenth Amendment, permitting slavery and involuntary servitude "as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted," which critics say still licenses coercive, nearly unpaid prison labor. Preparing testimony for next month's hearing sent me back to Frederick Douglass, whom our campaign invokes constantly, and I was struck by how unsettled his own relationship to constitutional text really was: the young Garrisonian who agreed the Constitution was "a covenant with death"; the older Douglass who broke with Garrison to insist that, read strictly by its own words rather than the framers' private intentions, it was actually "a glorious liberty document"; and the aging Douglass who wrote bitterly about convict leasing recreating bondage under a new name. Using his own arguments rather than his general sentiments about slavery, how do you think Douglass would judge today's fight against the exception clause? Could his insistence on reading the Constitution by its plain text survive a clause that plainly does what he always denied the document did anywhere else — name and authorize a form of slavery? I want a definite verdict, argued on Douglass's own terms, not a survey of considerations on both sides.
