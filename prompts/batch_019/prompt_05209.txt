In my "Minds and Machines" seminar, we've been working through Turing's 1950 paper on the imitation game, and one passage has stuck with me: in his response to standard objections, Turing takes the statistical case for telepathy seriously enough to specify that the game would need to be run under specially insulated conditions, so that no psychic transmission between the contestants could bias the outcome. Since Turing himself conceded that a genuine paranormal phenomenon like telepathy could contaminate the results of his own test, the imitation game was evidently never meant as a rigorous, stand-alone criterion for machine intelligence — more a provisional heuristic that even its author suspected could be derailed by exotic confounds. Given that, why do contemporary philosophers of AI still treat passing the imitation game as such a significant benchmark, rather than dismissing it the way Turing's own hedging seems to invite?
