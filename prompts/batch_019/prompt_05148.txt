"Fitch's paradox targets the anti-realist/verificationist idea that truth just is knowability: that there's no fact which couldn't in principle be known. This gets formalized as the Knowability Principle, KP: for every truth p, it's possible that someone knows p — ∀p(p → ◊Kp).

The argument is a reductio. Start from the very weak premise that not everything is known, i.e. some p is true and unknown: p ∧ ¬Kp. Apply KP to that very conjunction, since it's true: KP gives us ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp).

Now show that's impossible. Suppose someone did know (p ∧ ¬Kp). Since knowledge distributes over conjunction, they'd know p and know ¬Kp. But knowledge is factive (Ka → a), so knowing ¬Kp means ¬Kp is actually true — nobody knows p. Yet that same person knows p. Contradiction. So K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible in any circumstance: ¬◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Notice this doesn't call on the KK-principle or S4/S5 for knowledge anywhere — just factivity, distribution over conjunction, and the bare fact that something necessarily false isn't possible.

That directly contradicts the ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp) we got from KP. So the premise that some truth is unknown has to be false, given KP — i.e. KP forces omniscience. Since omniscience is absurd, KP is what has to go, and what the argument has actually shown is that our original p — the ordinary unknown truth we started with — is unknowable. A single mundane unknown fact is enough for classical logic to manufacture a strictly unknowable one, which is precisely the kind of thing the anti-realist's picture of truth says can't exist. I don't see much room left for the anti-realist to maneuver here."
