Question: State Fitch's paradox of knowability. Your answer should (a) formulate the knowability principle the paradox targets, (b) identify the two principles governing the knowledge operator that the derivation relies on, (c) reconstruct the derivation of the contradiction, and (d) explain what conclusion the paradox actually licenses about anti-realist (verificationist) theories of truth.

Student's answer: Fitch's paradox targets the knowability principle (KP): every truth is knowable, i.e., for all p, p → ◊Kp (if p is true, it's possible for p to be known). This is meant to cash out a modest anti-realist idea — that truth cannot outrun in-principle verification — without claiming that everything is actually known. Assume, plausibly, that something is true but unknown: there is some p such that p ∧ ¬Kp. Substituting this whole conjunction into KP gives (p ∧ ¬Kp) → ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp), and since the antecedent holds by hypothesis, we get ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp): it is possible to know that p is true and unknown.

But it isn't. The derivation needs two principles about K: distribution over conjunction (K(p∧q) entails Kp∧Kq) and the KK principle (Kp entails KKp — if you know something, you are in a position to know that you know it). Distributing K(p∧¬Kp) gives Kp∧K¬Kp. By KK, the first conjunct puts you in a position to know that you know p; but the second conjunct says it is known that p isn't known. These two directly conflict, so K(p∧¬Kp) is contradictory and therefore impossible in any circumstance — which contradicts what KP forced us to accept a moment ago.

So KP, together with the assumption that some truths go unknown, is inconsistent, and since we are obviously not omniscient, KP has to go. That refutes the anti-realist claim that truth is constitutively tied to knowability: verificationism just collapses into the absurd view that every truth is actually known.
