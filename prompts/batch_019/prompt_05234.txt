Using that comparison together with Quine's general holism about how belief faces experience, explain (a) what kind of objectivity this lets Quine claim for ethics, and (b) why he nonetheless still calls ethics 'methodologically infirm' next to science. Then classify Quine's overall metaethical position in a sentence or two (relativist? something else?) and defend the label."

And here's the answer, copied exactly as she wrote it:

"Quine's picture is that moral values start out as means and get 'transmuted' into ends -- a kid behaves well at first only because it's rewarded, but eventually the good behavior itself is what's prized, and that's the point it becomes a moral value rather than mere prudence. He then makes a comparative move: morality varies some from society to society, but nowhere near as wildly as language does between two groups that never talk to each other; he says the variation you get in morals is more like the variation you get in scientific world-view across cultures. That's doing real work, because it lets him treat moral values the way he treats theoretical beliefs generally -- on his holism, no single belief answers to experience by itself, only the whole interconnected web does, and he extends the same picture to values: no single moral judgment is checked against an isolated 'moral fact,' the whole trained-up web of values answers to experience as a package, alongside our beliefs. But once you've bought that much holism, I don't think there's a principled place left to keep ethics epistemically beneath science -- both are just webs that get judged by their internal coherence with experience, so calling ethics 'methodologically infirm' is really just Quine being modest, since on his own picture coherence was the only thing either domain had going for it in the first place. Given that, plus the fact that different societies get trained into different webs, I'd call this relativism: an act's wrongness is relative to whichever culture's web you're standing in, the same way Quine thinks a theory's acceptability is relative to whichever web of belief you're standing in. If anything the science comparison supports this, since Quine is already treating scientific world-view as something that varies (if modestly) from culture to culture, and on his own view theory is underdetermined by evidence anyway."
