"In Representation Reconsidered, William Ramsey argues that a lot of the talk of 'representation' in cognitive science doesn't earn its keep — it fails what he calls the job description challenge, the requirement that positing a representation do explanatory work that a purely causal or mechanistic redescription couldn't do just as well. And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Ramsey's central complaint is that cognitive scientists help themselves to the word 'representation' without showing that anything is gained by it beyond a plain causal story. A thermostat's bimetallic strip reliably co-varies with temperature, but calling this a 'representation' doesn't explain anything we couldn't already explain by just describing the causal chain — this is a receptor representation, and it fails the job description challenge. Structural or tracking representations are different: relations among internal states mirror relations among things in the world, and the system actually exploits that mirroring to guide behaviour, the way a cognitive map lets an animal compute a shortcut it's never taken before. That's doing real work, so it clears the bar.

I think recent interpretability work gives the tracking side real ammunition. Anthropic's work on superposition is the clearest case: because networks have far fewer neurons than there are features in the world worth tracking, the network learns to line up each neuron with one clean, human-interpretable feature (a specific curve, a specific syntactic role, and so on) rather than smearing many unrelated features across the same units. That's a structural correspondence in exactly Ramsey's sense — a stable mapping between elements of the network and elements of the task domain that the network's own downstream computation actually relies on, not something we're merely reading into it from outside. The induction head work points the same way: a pair of attention heads that jointly implement 'find the previous occurrence of the current token and copy whatever came next' is a mechanism whose internal structure literally mirrors the repeated structure of the sequence it's operating over, and the model uses that mirroring to do in-context learning it was never explicitly trained to do.

So on Ramsey's own terms, I'd say the tracking side wins: these circuits are not mere detectors, they're structural representations doing real explanatory work. And once you grant that a network's internal states structurally represent features of the world in a way that clears the job description challenge, I think you have to grant that the network's states are genuinely about those features in the same sense our beliefs are about things — the interpretability findings don't just tell us the network computes as if it had representations, they tell us it actually refers to curves and tokens the way we do."
