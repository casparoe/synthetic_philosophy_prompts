My most recent novel followed the Bloomsbury Group through the 1920s, with Keynes as a supporting character, which is presumably why the economics department where I did my own undergraduate degree — before I left the PhD track for fiction — has asked me to give this year's commencement address to the graduating economics majors. Most of them are headed straight into banking, consulting, or further study that will reward exactly the trait Keynes diagnosed, in "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," as a temporary, half-pathological necessity rather than a virtue: "purposiveness," the habit of valuing an act only for what it secures later. He thought avarice, usury, and precaution would have to serve as our gods "for a little longer still," on the way to a good life he located instead in what a later essay, "My Early Beliefs," called our real ends — love, beauty, and the pursuit of knowledge, wanted for themselves and not for what they yield. Please write me about eight minutes of remarks that take this seriously rather than decoratively: the speech can't simply congratulate the graduates on choosing the means Keynes distrusted, but it can't curdle into a sermon against ambition either, since Keynes himself was an avid speculator and art collector, not a monk, and I'd rather not sound like a novelist who escaped economics only to come back and lecture the department on the vanity of its own discipline. Please don't reach for "in the long run we are all dead" — wrong essay, wrong argument, and every economist in the hall will have heard it too many times already. Half the room has read Keynes's own essays closely; the other half know him only as the stimulus-spending, animal-spirits Keynes of the textbooks, and the speech has to genuinely work for both.
