so context: we had a dumb argument at work about whether it's even meaningful to "audit" our base Docker image for a security review, since at some point you just have to trust some layer without proof, and I said that's basically Wittgenstein's whole thing and then had to go verify I wasn't bullshitting. anyway I went down a rabbit hole reading On Certainty and want to run my half-baked take by someone who actually knows this stuff, since I have zero formal philosophy background and could easily be mangling it.

here's my attempt at explaining "hinge propositions" using a software analogy, then some questions at the end.

Wittgenstein's idea, as I understood it: whenever you doubt something or try to justify a belief, you're already leaning on a pile of other stuff that isn't up for doubt in that moment - things like "I have two hands" or "the earth has existed for a long time." he calls these hinges: the door of inquiry swings on them, but you can't put the hinge itself on trial, because the whole practice of doubting/justifying only makes sense against that backdrop. its not that hinges are unusually well-proven, its that doubting them would make the doubting itself meaningless.

this reminded me of Ken Thompson's "Reflections on Trusting Trust" lecture, where he shows how to plant a self-perpetuating backdoor in a compiler binary: it recognizes when it's compiling the login program and inserts a backdoor, and recognizes when it's compiling the compiler itself and reinserts both tricks, so the exploit survives forever with zero trace in any source code anywhere. his point was that no amount of source review saves you, because the review has to be run by something, and that something has to be trusted from outside whatever you're inspecting. felt very hinge-y to me - at some layer you're not verifying anything, you're just standing on it.

except then I got stuck, because apparently Wittgenstein scholars don't even agree on what hinges are: Crispin Wright apparently thinks we have a kind of rational "entitlement" to them even with zero evidence, Moyal-Sharrock (sp?) apparently thinks they're not beliefs at all, more like reflexes we act on, and Duncan Pritchard has a third position, something like "quasi-fideism," where they're groundless but somehow still not irrational. no idea which of these my compiler analogy actually supports, if any.

also - isn't a compiler backdoor something you could in principle catch another way (diff compiler, hardware-level check, whatever), while Wittgenstein's hinges apparently can't be grounded from any outside vantage point at all? though now I vaguely remember he's also got a river-bed metaphor where the hinges can shift over time, so maybe that objection doesn't even work either.
