So I've been prepping my senior seminar on death and dying, and the Jahi McMath case came up again in discussion this week (the teenager who was declared brain dead in 2013 but whose family kept her on a ventilator for years afterward). It made me second-guess how I usually teach this material. The standard line I give students, more or less straight out of the Uniform Determination of Death Act, is that "circulatory-respiratory death" and "whole-brain death" are just two different clinical tests pointing at the same underlying fact — irreversible loss of the organism functioning as an integrated whole. But honestly, the more I read into it, the less unified that story looks.

On the brain side, theres Shewmon's stuff on "chronic brain death" — patients kept going on ventilators for months or years after diagnosis who still heal wounds, fight off infections, even gestate a fetus, which is a lot of integration for a supposedly non-integrated organism, and it really undercuts the rationale people like Bernat gave for why whole-brain loss counts as death. On the circulatory side, once you actually look at how donation-after-circulatory-death protocols work, the waiting periods before doctors call the heart stopping "irreversible" have gotten short enough that people quietly swapped in "permanent" instead (won't be restarted, as opposed to can't be restarted) — which always struck me as smuggling in a decision not to act and dressing it up as a fact about the body.

Can you help me sort out how I want to present this in class? I want a real comparison of the two standards, not just "one's about the brain, one's about the heart": what does each one actually claim death consists in, what's the best case for thinking they're still two tests for one unified concept versus two genuinely different concepts of death running in parallel, and if you had to say which standard is in worse philosophical shape right now, which one and why? And if you've got a take on whether the Lazarus phenomenon (hearts spontaneously restarting after resuscitation's been called off) is a real problem for the circulatory side or just a red herring, throw that in too.
