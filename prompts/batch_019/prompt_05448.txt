so I went down a lucas-penrose rabbit hole this week after it got namedropped in an old SSC comment thread. everyone knows the standard objection to lucas's original 1961 argument: you can only assert your own godel sentence is true if you already know the formal system that supposedly captures your reasoning is sound, and there's no non-circular way to know that about yourself, so the argument stalls before it even starts.

turns out penrose's version in shadows of the mind has a second, much less discussed argument (the ch. 3 one, not the famous ch. 2 "not knowably sound" conclusion) built specifically to dodge that move. he tries to get a contradiction from the bare assumption that some formal system F fully captures your reasoning, no independant certification of soundness required -- the trick is a premise that believing "F describes me" already commits you to trusting F's soundness (since otherwise you'd be relying on a belief system whose own basis you doubt), and that's supposedly enough to run the diagonalization through for any candidate F, sound or not.

can you argue that this fancier version fails for basically the same underlying reason as the original, just one level further down? i.e., it doesn't get rid of the unjustified soundness assumption, it just smuggles it back in through that "believing F describes me" premise. want the strongest version of that objection you can give, not a "reasonable people disagree" shrug.
