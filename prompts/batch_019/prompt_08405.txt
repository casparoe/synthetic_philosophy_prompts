so, standard Bayesian epistemology has this annoying feature where an ideal agent is supposed to assign probability 1 to every theorem and 0 to every non-theorem, since they're true (or false) in literally every possible world -- meaning a perfect Bayesian reasoner technically already "knows" whether Goldbach's conjecture is true, they just havent looked it up yet. There's two fixes for this that I keep seeing talked about *past* each other instead of *to* each other: the impossible-worlds move (Hintikka's old trick of letting some "worlds" violate the laws of logic, so an agent can spread credence onto worlds where the theorem is false and isn't forced into certainty), and the logical induction move (Garrabrant et al's MIRI paper), which drops possible-worlds talk entirely and grounds credences in a market of traders that provably can't be exploited for unbounded profit in the limit.

Can you write me a dialogue between one advocate of each view, arguing specifically over what credence a rational-but-bounded agent should assign to Goldbach's conjecture? I want real back-and-forth, not two monologues -- e.g. the impossible-worlds person should get to press the inductor on what a trader's price is even a degree of belief *about* if it's not tracking any way things could be, and the inductor should get to push back on why a guarantee that only kicks in "eventually" tells a bounded agent anything about what to believe today. Both sides need to be fully steelmanned, nobody's allowed to be the strawman here.
