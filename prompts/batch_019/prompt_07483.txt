(a) [5 pts] State Lewis's Principal Principle, and explain what work the notion of "admissible evidence" is doing in it.
(b) [10 pts] Lewis is a Humean about chance: the chances at a time are read off the pattern of events across the whole history of the world, not just the segment up to that time. Explain, in your own words, why this generates a problem for the Principal Principle — the problem Lewis calls the "big bad bug" — making sure to say what an "undermining" future is and why it's a threat.
(c) [7 pts] Explain how the New Principle is supposed to fix this problem.
(d) [3 pts] In two or three sentences, explain how the Principal Principle differs from van Fraassen's Reflection Principle.

And here's Jordan's answer, typed up exactly as submitted:

(a) Lewis's Principal Principle says that a rational agent's credence in some proposition A, conditional on learning that the objective chance of A at t is x together with any evidence that's "admissible" at t, should just equal x: C(A | E & ch_t(A)=x) = x for admissible E. The admissibility clause is doing most of the real work, since its what stops the principle from being trivially false. Admissible evidence is (roughly) historical information about matters of particular fact prior to t, plus information about the chance-making laws/theory themselves; it rules out anything that "outruns" the chances, like what you'd get from a crystal ball or a time-traveler's testimony about how things turn out. If that kind of evidence were let in, your credence obviously shouldn't just track the chance, since you'd have better information than the chance gives you.

(b) The bug shows up because Lewis is a Humean about chance: the chance function at t isn't some extra ingredient sitting on top of the world, its read off of the actual pattern of events across the whole history of the world — past and future both — via whichever theory best balances simplicity and strength (the "best system"). So which chance function is correct at t depends partly on how things go after t. Now take a possible continuation of history after t that's compatible with everything that's happened so far, and that the chances at t give some positive chance to, but which — were it to happen — would make a different best system, and so a different chance function, come out correct at t instead. That's an "undermining" future. The bug is that Lewis wants the chances to be exactly the kind of stable thing the Principal Principle can defer to, but on his own metaphysics the present chances are always hostage to future events that they themselves say might happen, and which would, if they did happen, show that some other distribution had been the true one the whole time. Lewis calls this the "big bad bug": his own metaphysics of chance seems to undercut the stability that the Principal Principle needs chance to have.

(c) The fix is usually called the New Principle, and it comes from van Fraassen: instead of making rational credence answer to the bare proposition "the chance of A at t is x" — which we've just seen can be undermined — we make it answer to a complete specification of the history of the world up to t together with a complete specification of the chance-theory (the best system) that would be true given that history. So the New Principle says C(A | H_t & T) = ch_t(A), where H_t is the whole history up to t and T is the theory of chance. Since you're conditionalizing on the history-and-theory pair rather than on the derived chance proposition, an undermining future is no longer a threat: whatever happens later, it can't retroactively change what H_t & T say the chances were at t.

(d) Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle says your current credence in A should defer to whatever your own future credence in A turns out to be — C_t(A | C_t'(A)=x) = x for t<t' — which doesn't mention objective chance at all; its a constraint on trusting your own future opinions, whatever those track. Still, since a rational future self is really just someone who's conditionalized on the chances as they stood at t', deferring to your future credence is ultimately just deferring to chance one step removed, so Reflection is best understood as a special case of the Principal Principle rather than a genuinely separate principle.
