Random thing I've been chewing on while reading for quals: Kropotkin's Mutual Aid is basically his rebuttal to the Huxley/Spencer "nature is one giant gladiatorial free-for-all" reading of Darwin — he leans on a ton of field observations (alot of it from his own years surveying Siberia) to argue that cooperation within a species is just as much a "factor in evolution" as struggle is, especially in harsh climates. He's writing in 1902, so this is decades before Hamilton's rule, Williams's critique of naive group selection, Dawkins' selfish gene stuff, or the D.S. Wilson/E.O. Wilson push to rehabilitate multilevel selection in the 2000s even existed.

Say you sat Kropotkin down and walked him through the modern move: that most of his mutual-aid examples reduce to kin selection or reciprocal altruism at the individual/gene level, and that group-selection math and kin-selection math often turn out to be equivalent — just different ways of partitioning the same covariance. Would he concede that the gene's-eye view basically swallows his thesis whole, or does his actual argument (not the pop-sci "mutual aid = group selection" gloss people give it) give him real grounds to side with someone like Sober & Wilson that something explanatorily real still gets lost in that reduction? Want your actual best guess here, defended — not a "well, both sides have a point."
