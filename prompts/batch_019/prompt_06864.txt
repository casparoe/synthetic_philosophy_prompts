As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I've been working through Timothy Williamson's "anti-luminosity" argument from Knowledge and Its Limits in my spare time, and I would be curious to know how you think Descartes would have responded to it. Williamson argues that almost no non-trivial mental condition is "luminous" — meaning that being in the condition guarantees you are in a position to know that you are in it — using a scenario in which a room's temperature drifts so gradually that at some point you can no longer know whether you still feel cold, since a margin-for-error requirement on knowledge rules out knowing p in any case indistinguishable from one where p is false. He runs essentially the same argument to show that the KK principle (if you know that p, you are thereby in a position to know that you know it) fails for anything beyond trivial, non-perceptual knowledge.

Given how much of Descartes' epistemology — the cogito, the appeal to "clear and distinct" perception, his insistence that the mind is better known to us than the body — seems to depend on the mind being transparent to itself, how do you think he would have responded to Williamson's argument? Could he simply grant that confused bodily sensations like feeling cold were never luminous to begin with, reserving that status for purely intellectual acts, or does the margin-for-error reasoning end up threatening the certainty of the cogito as well? Please reconstruct what you take to be Descartes' strongest available reply, and assess whether it ultimately succeeds.
