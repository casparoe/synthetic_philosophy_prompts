Here is the question as it was posed to students:

"Sharon Street's 'A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value' presents realists about value with a dilemma concerning the relationship between the evolutionary origins of our evaluative attitudes and the independent evaluative truths realism posits. (a) Reconstruct the dilemma, explaining both horns and why each is costly for the realist. [6 points] (b) Street argues that even on the second horn, the realist's preferred explanation (the 'tracking account') is scientifically inferior to a rival explanation (the 'adaptive link account') that explains the very same beliefs. Explain this comparison and why she thinks it favors the adaptive link account. [6 points] (c) Explain how Street's own positive metaethical view is supposed to escape the dilemma, and then assess whether David Enoch's 'third-factor' reply gives the realist a comparable escape route. [8 points]"

And here is the student's answer, in full:

"(a) The dilemma arises because realists think there is a body of evaluative truth that holds independently of anyone's evaluative attitudes, while at the same time granting (as any realist must, given the evidence) that natural selection has had a pervasive influence on the evaluative attitudes humans actually have — dispositions to value kin, to feel that reciprocity matters, to prefer survival to death, and so on. The realist then has to say how these two things relate. On the first horn, she denies any relation between the selective pressures that produced our evaluative dispositions and the independent evaluative truths: the two are simply orthogonal. The cost of this horn is skeptical: if the shaping process bears no relation whatsoever to the independent truths, and that process nonetheless determined nearly everything we're inclined to believe about value, then it would be a wild coincidence if what we ended up believing matched the independent truths, so the realist owes us an explanation of our evaluative reliability that this horn cannot supply. On the second horn, the realist instead asserts a relation: natural selection tracked the independent truths, favoring ancestors who were able to grasp them because grasping them was fitness-enhancing. This avoids the skeptical result, but Street thinks it comes at a scientific cost, which is the subject of (b).

(b) The 'tracking account' explains our evaluative dispositions by saying that, e.g., believing that one's survival is good was fitness-enhancing because it was true, and grasping true things about value tends to promote fitness. The 'adaptive link account' explains exactly the same dispositions without that detour: it says only that being disposed to value one's own survival (whether or not that disposition is true) was fitness-enhancing, full stop. Since the adaptive link account reproduces all the same predictions with one fewer substantive assumption — it never needs the premise that the belief in question is also true, or that its truth did any causal work — Street takes it to be the more parsimonious hypothesis, and hence the one that wins by ordinary scientific standards, in the way a simpler hypothesis beats a more baroque one that posits an explanatorily idle extra entity.

(c) Street thinks her own view escapes the dilemma because she is not a realist in the relevant sense: on her constructivist picture there is no evaluative truth that floats free of our evaluative attitudes for natural selection to have gotten right or wrong, so the tracking question doesn't arise for her the way it does for the realist. Since no other realist strategy fares any better, the upshot of the dilemma is essentially the same one error theorists such as Mackie reach: no first-order evaluative claim — 'cruelty is wrong,' 'promises are to be kept' — comes out true, because there is no independent evaluative reality for such claims to be true of, and Street's constructivism should be read as accepting this result about first-order morality while explaining why creatures like us go on deliberating and disagreeing as if something were at stake.

Enoch's third-factor reply tries to let the realist take the second horn without paying the parsimony cost from (b). His proposal is that survival is itself the third factor explaining both why organisms with certain evaluative beliefs were selected for and why those very beliefs are true: what is good for an organism, on this view, is constituted by what promotes its survival and reproduction, so selection could not favor the corresponding dispositions without thereby favoring true beliefs. Because the reply locates the truth-maker for evaluative claims in an independently fitness-relevant property rather than in some separate fact that selection would have to detect from the outside, it blocks the parsimony argument in (b): there is no extra, causally idle truth left over for the adaptive link account to dispense with. So while one might resist the reduction of value to survival on other grounds, the third-factor reply does show that the dilemma's second horn is not as costly as Street's discussion suggests."
