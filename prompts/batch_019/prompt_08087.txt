I spent about thirty-five years doing observational cosmology before I retired, and I've always had a soft spot for the anthropic-reasoning side of the field: the Doomsday Argument, Bostrom's Self-Sampling Assumption, Sleeping Beauty, the whole family of puzzles about what you're entitled to infer just from noticing where and when you find yourself. Since retiring I've had time to actually read the philosophy behind stuff I only used informally on the job, and a couple weeks ago I fell down a rabbit hole into the philosophy of probability and ran into something called "the reference class problem," which apparently goes back to Venn and got formalized by Reichenbach: any single event (a coin toss, a person, a particular star) belongs to indefinitely many different reference classes, each of which hands you a different frequency, and theres no principled way to pick "the" relevant class for computing "the" probability of that one case. There's a paper, I want to say by Alan Hajek, arguing this isn't just a frequentist headache but infects basically every theory of probability out there, even Bayesianism to some degree.

What's been bugging me since is how much this sounds like the exact fight anthropic reasoners have over what your "reference class of observers" is supposed to be when you apply the Self-Sampling Assumption -- all humans, all observers, all observer-moments -- and how that choice basically determines whether the Doomsday Argument goes through at all. I brought this up with an old colleague who's still active in research, and she said I was just noticing a pun on the word "class," and that the two problems don't actually have much to do with each other once you look at the mechanics. I'm not so sure. So could you actually compare the two for me: the classic reference class problem from the philosophy of probability, and the reference class problem as it shows up in anthropic and self-locating reasoning? I want to know whether they're really the same underlying puzzle in different clothes, such that a genuine fix for one ought to carry over to the other, or whether my colleague is right that it's a surface-level resemblance masking two logically distinct problems (e.g., one about indexical self-location, the other about the semantics of single-case chance). Either way, please back it up with a concrete worked example rather than just asserting it.
