I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly in formal epistemology, and this term I'm taking a seminar built around accuracy-first approaches to doxastic rationality. A few weeks ago we spent two sessions on Jim Joyce's 1998 argument for probabilism: he measures the inaccuracy of a credence function at a world using the Brier score (the summed squared distance between the credences and the truth values of the relevant propositions), and then proves that any credence function violating the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated by some coherent alternative, while no coherent credence function is ever dominated in this way. The argument is usually presented as a major advance over Dutch Book arguments for probabilism, precisely because it is "nonpragmatic": it doesn't rely on any contestable bridge principle linking credence to betting behavior, and is supposed to show that incoherence is an epistemic defect in its own right, not merely a practical one.

What's been bothering me — and I believe this is essentially the objection Patrick Maher raised against Joyce's original argument — is that this nonpragmatic advantage starts to look illusory once you notice how much weight is being placed on the Brier score specifically. There are plenty of other prima facie legitimate ways of measuring closeness to the truth, such as the log score or the spherical score, and nothing in the bare concept of accuracy tells us to prefer Joyce's particular quadratic measure over these alternatives. So the dominance result looks less like a theorem about accuracy as such and more like a theorem about accuracy-as-measured-by-one-stipulated-rule, which would make the argument just as dependent on an unmotivated background assumption as the Dutch Book argument it was meant to improve on. The assumption has simply been relocated into the choice of scoring rule instead of sitting out in the open as a claim about betting behavior.

Is that basically the right way to see it? I'm drafting a seminar paper on whether accuracy-first epistemology really delivers a nonpragmatic vindication of probabilism, and before I take a position on whether this arbitrariness worry is fatal to the whole program, I want to make sure I'm stating it as precisely and fairly as the literature allows.
