I spent close to forty years as a field herpetologist, mostly chasing salamanders around the mountains of California and the Pacific Northwest, and its only since retiring that I've gotten properly into philosophy - a few of us old department colleagues meet every other week now and are slowly working through an intro logic textbook, which is a very different flavor of confusion than the kind I'm used to from decades of fieldwork. A couple of sessions back, the friend leading our little group mentioned, sort of in passing, that some logicians take seriously the idea that certain statements really are both true and false at once - not because we'd made an error somewhere, but as a genuine feature of how things are. That throwaway comment has been rattling around in my head ever since, because it reminded me of something from early in my career that I never really got over.

Back in the 80s I did fieldwork on a chain of salamander populations that loop in a ring around a big valley, following the mountains on either side - biologists call this a "ring species." Every neighboring pair of populations along that chain interbreeds freely wherever their ranges touch, about as cleanly as field data ever gets: population A breeds fine with B, B with C, and so on all the way round. But at the one spot where the two ends of the ring finally come back together, after diverging for ages by going the long way round, those two populations barely interbreed at all - right there, they behave exactly like two seperate species. So "belongs to the same species as" holds locally, and pretty unambiguously, for every single neighboring pair in the chain, and yet the relation as a whole plainly isn't transitive, since that one closing link contradicts what all the other links together would seem to require.

I brought this up at the group after that session, half-joking that biology had stumbled onto a real live contradiction decades before the logicians started taking the idea seriously, and my friend running the group just laughed and said no, that's not a genuine contradiction, that's an old-fashioned case of vagueness - there's a true dividing line somewhere on that ring, we just don't have fine-grained enough data to locate it yet. That answer didn't satisfy me then and it still doesn't, because there's no fuzzy stretch anywhere on the ring to go looking in - at every single point, whether two neighboring populations interbreed is about as sharp and settled a fact as anything I ever measured out in the field. So who's got the better of this argument, him or me? Is this really no different in kind from an ordinary blurry-boundary case, or is there an actual case for saying that, right at that closing point, the two populations both are and are not the same species - and if you did let that stand, would it quietly poison every other classification claim in biology, or is there some principled way of containing the damage?
