I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on assertion, and we spent basically two whole sessions arguing about which norm actually governs the speech act, so now I want to see how a careful outside voice handles it. Here's roughly the lay of the land as I've laid it out for my students: Williamson's knowledge account says you may assert p only if you know p, and he leans heavily on the fact that "p, but I don't know that p" sounds just as defective as an ordinary Moore-paradoxical sentence, plus on lottery cases -- it seems improper to flatly assert "your ticket lost" on the basis of the odds alone, even when your credence is like a million to one. Weiner thinks all of that can be explained away pragmatically under a bare truth norm, no knowledge requirement needed. Then Lackey's selfless assertion cases muddy things further -- e.g., a biology teacher who personally doesn't believe in evolution (for religious reasons) but asserts it in class anyway because that's what the evidence and scientific consensus support, which sure looks like a perfectly proper assertion made without belief, let alone knowledge -- and thats supposed to sink the knowledge norm and any belief-based norm in one go. And then Douven wants to replace all of this with a reasonable-credibility/justified-belief norm instead, partly to dodge Gettier-ish worries about how demanding knowledge is.

One of my students thinks this whole debate is basically a semantic wash and everyone's just talking past each other, which drives me up the wall. So: can you write me an essay that actually adjudicates between these options rather than just surveying them nicely? I don't want a "they each capture something important" cop-out ending -- give me a real verdict (it's fine if it comes with a qualifier, like "knowledge norm, but only for flat-out assertion in contexts where X," as long as it's an actual position), and make sure you're genuinely engaging with the Lackey and lottery cases rather than just gesturing at them.
