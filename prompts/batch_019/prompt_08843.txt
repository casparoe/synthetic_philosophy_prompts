I spent just over thirty years running a molecular genetics lab -- gene regulation in yeast for most of it, sequencing projects toward the end -- and every working day I used words like "code," "transcript," "read-through," and "translation" without ever once pausing to ask what licenses them. Since retiring three years ago I have taken up philosophy in earnest, mainly through a small philosophy-of-science reading group, and we recently spent two sessions on the question of whether DNA is a code, or carries information, in any sense stronger than a convenient figure of speech -- a question that unsettled me rather more than I expected, given how freely I threw that vocabulary around for decades. I have gathered that there are two camps that have been talking past one another for a long time. One side holds that the genetic code is a real, non-metaphorical code: they point to the sheer arbitrariness of the codon-to-amino-acid assignment (what Crick called a "frozen accident"), to the dedicated adaptor machinery -- the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and their tRNAs -- that fixes that arbitrary mapping, and sometimes to a teleosemantic story in which the selection history of the reading machinery is precisely what turns mere correlation into genuine content. The other side, very much in the spirit of Sarkar's and Griffiths' skepticism about "genetic information," thinks the whole vocabulary is old Shannon-and-cybernetics dress draped over ordinary biochemistry: arbitrary mappings are cheap and turn up throughout inorganic chemistry too, and anything in the cell that is said to "read" or "interpret" either begs the question or sets off a homunculus regress that bottoms out in nothing but mechanism.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology who hold these opposing positions and argue the matter out from first principles rather than trading catchphrases at one another. Please have each of them open by pressing the single objection that the literature raises most forcefully against the other's view, so that neither gets to state a position uncontested before having to defend it. Somewhere in the exchange, I would like the realist forced to say something precise about what, over and above arbitrariness plus mechanism, would make a physical process semantic rather than merely regular; and I would like the deflationist made to confront the parallel case of ordinary written language, where ink marks are "just mechanism" at some level of description too, without being allowed to retreat to the claim that a human interpreter is doing all the real work in that case. Let the exchange run long enough -- eight or so turns apiece -- that they move past their opening statements into genuine difficulty, and please do not let either side win simply because the other runs out of things to say.
