My school's guidance office is piloting an algorithm that flags students as "at risk of failing" a course, using inputs such as attendance and past grades, so that counselors can intervene early. I have read that comparable tools elsewhere have drawn criticism because they produce errors at different rates for different demographic groups, even without any deliberate bias in their design. Please describe the most important implications of the impossibility result independently proven by Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan and by Chouldechova — the mathematics behind the ProPublica/COMPAS controversy — for whether this tool could ever be both well-calibrated and equally likely to err, via false positives or false negatives, across groups, given that different groups at my school currently fail courses at noticeably different rates. If my school adopts the tool anyway, what trade-off would it implicitly be accepting?
