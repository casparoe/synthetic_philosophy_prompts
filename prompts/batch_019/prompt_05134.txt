Question: "Frederick Douglass is often described as having reversed himself on one of the central questions dividing the antebellum abolitionist movement: whether the U.S. Constitution was a pro-slavery or an anti-slavery document. Reconstruct both sides of this reversal. Your answer should address (a) the Garrisonian position on the Constitution that Douglass held through the 1840s, including its practical implications for antislavery strategy; (b) the interpretive argument he advanced after breaking with the Garrisonians, with specific reference to the constitutional text; and (c) the influences most often credited with the change."

Classmate's response: "Throughout the 1840s, as a close ally of William Lloyd Garrison, Douglass shared the conviction that the Constitution was inherently and irredeemably pro-slavery. Like other Garrisonians, he held that the document was, in Garrison's words, 'a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell,' its compromises with slavery built into the nation's founding law through provisions like the three-fifths clause, the slave-trade clause, and the fugitive-slave clause. Because even the oath of federal office required swearing to uphold this slaveholders' compact, Garrisonians concluded that principled abolitionists should refuse to vote or hold office and should instead call for Northern disunion from the slave states.

By the early 1850s, however, Douglass had broken publicly with Garrison and reversed this position. He now argued that the Constitution, read on its face rather than through the framers' private understandings, contained no explicit sanction of slavery at all: nowhere does the text use the words 'slave' or 'slavery,' speaking instead only of 'persons.' He pointed to the fugitive-slave clause as confirmation: its text refers explicitly to a 'fugitive slave' who must be delivered up to his claimant, which Douglass read as proof that the drafters were willing to name the institution when it suited slaveholders, making their studied avoidance of the word everywhere else in the document all the more damning. Since the Preamble commits the government to 'establish Justice' and 'secure the Blessings of Liberty,' Douglass concluded that any ambiguous clause ought to be construed against slavery rather than for it, so that the Constitution could be wielded by abolitionists as an anti-slavery document rather than surrendered to the slaveholders.

This change of mind is usually traced to Douglass's growing closeness with the wealthy political abolitionist Gerrit Smith, a leading Liberty Party figure who had broken from Garrisonian 'no-government' abolitionism. It was Smith's own treatise, The Unconstitutionality of Slavery (1845), that Douglass later credited with first persuading him that the document could be read in an anti-slavery light, and it was shortly after merging his North Star with Smith's Liberty Party Paper that Douglass publicly announced his new position, prompting a bitter and lasting break with Garrison."
