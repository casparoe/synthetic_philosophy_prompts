My flatmate's doing a philosophy MA, and had two people from her experimental philosophy seminar round for dinner last week -- by the wine course they were in the most stubborn argument I've watched in ages. Background you need: theres a famous finding (the "Knobe effect") where people read about a CEO who adopts a policy purely to boost profits, is warned it'll also harm the environment as a side effect, says he doesn't care about the environment either way, and does it regardless -- and in a matched version, the same policy instead helps the environment, and he still says he doesn't care either way. His state of mind is identical across both versions, but most people say he harmed the environment "intentionally" and wont say he helped it "intentionally." Everyone agreed that's the actual finding. What they couldn't agree on is why it happens, and it went around about four times:

1. Theo said it's simple: the concept "intentional" has an evaluative component wired into it, so a bad side effect counts as intentional and a good one doesn't -- that's not a bias distorting the concept, that's just what competently applying the concept looks like.
2. Simone said that can't be the whole story, since you get basically the same asymmetry in versions where the side effect isn't morally bad at all, just unusual or off from whatever's standard -- so whatever's doing the work is "departing from a norm" in general, not moral badness specifically.
3. Theo said that doesn't get you outside evaluation, it just relocates it: calling something a "departure from a norm" already smuggles in the judgment that conforming would've been better, so her cases are the same evaluative story wearing a non-moral costume.
4. Simone said that move is too cheap -- if any asymmetry can be redescribed after the fact as "well some norm got violated," the theory has stopped predicting anything and is just relabelling the data. She thinks the honest conclusion is that "intentionally" is just being used loosely to pin blame on someone, not that our actual concept of intention has ethics baked into it.
