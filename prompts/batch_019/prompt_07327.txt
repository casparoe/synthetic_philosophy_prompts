I'm well into the first draft of a novel built around a small crew — five people, none of them criminals by trade — who spend nearly a year meticulously planning the theft of a single painting from a private collection. What interests me isn't the mechanics of the job so much as the inner life of the group: what it actually is, philosophically, for five separate people to come to share one intention, and what becomes of that shared intention once the group starts to fracture. In trying to get this right, rather than simply relying on heist-movie convention, I've been reading in the philosophy of collective intentionality, where I ran into a disagreement I hadn't fully appreciated before: Michael Bratman's planning-theory account of shared intention versus Margaret Gilbert's plural-subject account of joint commitment.

As I understand it, Bratman tries to build shared intention entirely out of individual attitudes: each member intends that "we do this," these intentions mesh via compatible sub-plans that each party is aware the others hold, the whole arrangement is common knowledge among them, and, crucially, each person's intention remains responsive to the intentions and actions of the others as things unfold. On this view, there doesn't seem to be anything holding the group together beyond these interlocking, mutually responsive individual attitudes. Gilbert's picture feels quite different in spirit: she treats joint commitment as basic rather than something constructed out of individual attitudes, and argues that once people are jointly committed to a course of action, each acquires genuine obligations to the others — obligations that, on her account, cannot be dissolved by one party simply changing his mind, but only through some further act of joint rescission.

This distinction turns out to matter enormously for my plot, since the novel's midpoint hinges on one member of the crew quietly deciding to walk away from the plan without telling anyone else. I'd like you to lay out the actual substance of the disagreement between Bratman and Gilbert as precisely as you can — what each is really claiming shared intention or joint commitment to consist in, and where they genuinely part ways, as opposed to merely using different vocabulary for the same thing. Then tell me which account you think more accurately describes what has actually happened, metaphysically, at the moment my character has his private change of heart: has the group's shared intention simply ceased to exist the instant his responsiveness to the others lapses, as Bratman's view would seem to imply, or is he, whether he acknowledges it or not, still bound by a joint commitment that only the others can release him from, as Gilbert would have it? I'd be grateful for your own considered judgment here, not just a summary of the two positions.
