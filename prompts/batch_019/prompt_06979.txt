Over dinner last week, a friend and I argued for two hours about the Ellsberg paradox and reached no agreement. We even set an actual jar on the table—ninety chips, thirty known red and sixty more in an unknown black-and-yellow split—to keep the three-color version concrete. She held the strict Bayesian line: once you accept Savage's axioms, preferring red over black in the first bet and then preferring black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow in the second is simply an error, not two locally sensible responses to different amounts of ambiguity. I argued that the error runs the other way, that treating calculable risk and genuine ambiguity as interchangeable is the naive move, and that something like Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin-expected-utility rule shows ambiguity aversion can be rationalized on its own terms rather than explained away as a bias. We never even got to what I believe was Raiffa's objection—that a sufficiently sophisticated bettor can randomize across the ambiguous colors to manufacture a bet with fully known odds—which struck me afterward as a problem for both of our positions, not just one.

Write a dialogue between two characters who take up these two sides, picking up roughly where we left off. Have the Bayesian press the ambiguity-averse character specifically on the randomization point, and have that character offer a real rebuttal rather than simply reasserting that ambiguity differs from risk. Each side should land at least one objection the other cannot cleanly deflect, and the exchange should end with the disagreement clarified rather than tidily resolved.
