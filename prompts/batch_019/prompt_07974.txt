I'm a senior double-majoring in philosophy and computer science, and this term I'm taking a seminar cross-listed between the two departments called "Constructive Mathematics and the Foundations of Proof," where we've spent the last few weeks on Brouwer's original case against the unrestricted use of the law of excluded middle, Heyting's formalization of intuitionistic logic, the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov reading of the connectives — on which a proof of an existential claim just is a witness together with a proof that the witness works — and Dummett's argument that a meaning-as-use semantics, rather than one built on verification-transcendent truth conditions, favors intuitionistic logic over classical logic. A few of us have kept arguing about all this outside of class, which turns out to matter, because I'm also the elected student representative on my computer science department's Capstone Committee for the Formal Methods concentration, and a real-life version of the argument has just landed on next week's agenda.

Here is the situation. Our capstone requires teams to formalize a specification in Coq and mechanically extract a certified, executable program from the correctness proof. Last year, a team verifying a root-finding routine needed to compare two real numbers and, seeing no way around it, imported excluded middle to case-split on x < y, x = y, or x > y. The proof went through, but the extracted OCaml code was left with an inert stub at exactly that case split, since deciding equality of two arbitrary real numbers isn't something any algorithm can do; the "verified" program didn't actually run on the inputs that mattered. One professor on the committee now wants to ban the importation of excluded middle, choice, or anything that constructively entails them (choice by itself constructively yields excluded middle, via Diaconescu's theorem) anywhere in a proof destined for extraction. She argues this is licensed by the very considerations from Brouwer and Dummett we've been reading, not merely a coding convenience: an existence proof that yields no witness was never a complete proof of existence to begin with. Another professor thinks this confuses a narrow engineering requirement — the code has to run — with a metaphysical thesis about the nature of mathematical truth, and worries that a blanket ban would make some perfectly good specification-level lemmas, never intended for extraction, needlessly painful or impossible to prove, while leaving students with the false impression that classical mathematics is simply wrong.

I've volunteered to draft the short memo the committee will discuss before the vote, and I'd be grateful if you could write it for me. I need an actual recommendation — something more decidable than "it depends" — about whether, and where, classical axioms should be permitted in capstone proofs, to take effect starting with next year's cohort, along with the reasoning behind it. It should also represent the losing side's strongest point honestly, rather than caricaturing it.
