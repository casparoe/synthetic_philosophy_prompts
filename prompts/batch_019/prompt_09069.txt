I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on decision theory and the foundations of cooperation, and a recent session on program equilibrium produced a disagreement between two of my sharpest students that I think would make a good dialogue for next week's handout, if it can be worked out properly rather than left as a shouting match. Some background: Tennenholtz's original proposal was that if players in a game submit programs rather than actions — programs that can inspect one another's code before choosing a move — then a program that cooperates just in case the opponent's program is identical to its own, and defects otherwise, can be a mutual best response in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. That outcome is simply unavailable to ordinary, opaque strategies under Nash equilibrium. We then covered the later refinement sometimes called Löbian cooperation, in which two agents built around a shared formal proof system — each disposed, roughly, to cooperate just in case it can prove that the other cooperates with it (a construction often nicknamed FairBot) — can be shown, via Löb's theorem, to cooperate with one another, and to do so without the fragility of requiring literal code-identity.

I'd like you to reconstruct the disagreement as a dialogue between two researchers, Sofia and Daniel, who have just finished proving a new Löbian-cooperation result and are now arguing over how strong a claim to make about it in the discussion section of their paper. Sofia's position is that program equilibrium, especially in its Löbian form, is a genuine solution to the problem of securing rational cooperation without external enforcement: mutual, formally verifiable transparency turns commitment from something merely asserted into something that can be checked, which is precisely the ingredient classical game theory lacked. Daniel's position is that the result only relocates the original problem one level up: the space of program equilibria is at least as full of bad outcomes as the space of action equilibria — mutual, unconditional defection remains a program equilibrium too — so something outside the formalism still has to privilege the cooperative outcome, and the appearance of a solution comes from quietly building the very transparency and credibility that made cooperation hard into the model's assumptions in the first place.

Please have them engage each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another: Daniel should have to explain why provable, mutually verifiable dispositions are not simply stipulated trust wearing formal dress, and Sofia should have to answer both the multiplicity worry and the further worry that deciding whether, and how, to make yourself transparent to another agent is itself a strategic choice that can be gamed or faked. Neither of them should fold or come off as the obvious winner; I'd like at least one moment where each concedes, or has to substantially qualify a point, under real pressure from the other. Write it as a plain back-and-forth with speaker labels and no stage directions, long enough — a dozen or more exchanges — for the disagreement to actually develop rather than just be stated twice from each side.
