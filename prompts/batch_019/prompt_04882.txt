So this happened at a meetup a few weeks back and I still haven't been able to let it go. After the discussion group broke up, a few of us stuck around for poker, and during a break one guy (I'll call him Dave, normally a pretty careful thinker) proposed a side game: he'd flip a fair coin repeatedly until it landed heads, and pay $2 if that happened on flip 1, $4 on flip 2, $8 on flip 3, doubling every time, and he wanted to know what people would pay upfront for one go at it. Most of us said somewhere around $10-20, one guy said $6, and Dave got this look and said the "true" price is actually infinite and we were all just being irrational about it, then pulled out his phone to show the calculation, which as far as I can tell is correct (every single term in the sum works out to exactly $1, and there's infinitely many terms).

I said the fix I vaguely remembered from years of lurking on LW is that money isn't the same as utility — your two-billionth dollar doesn't do as much for you as your first — and once you swap in a reasonably concave utility function for the raw dollar payouts, the expected utility comes out finite and small, matching what everyone actually offered to pay. Dave granted that for about five minutes and then came back with something that genuinely stumped me: he said you can always build a new version of the same game where, instead of paying out 2^n dollars at stage n, you pay out whatever dollar amount happens to be worth exactly 2^n worth of utility under your particular curve — and once you plug that schedule back in, you're staring at an infinite expected utility gamble again, no matter how sharply the curve bends, as long as the curve is unbounded. According to Dave the only actual fix is biting the bullet that utility itself has to be capped at some finite value no matter how much money or good outcome gets thrown at a person, which felt like a way weirder thing to accept than "diminishing returns" ever did. Somebody else at the table said this whole mess is basically just Pascal's Mugging with extra steps, and that Yudkowsky's leverage-penalty stuff was supposed to get you out of needing the cap, but nobody could actually reconstruct how that's supposed to work, myself included.

I've spent enough years reading LW and ACX that I've apparently absorbed all the vocabulary and none of the actual understanding, and I'd like to sort out who was right at that table. Is Daves "rebuild it in utility units" move actually a legitimate construction, or is there a hidden flaw that gets it wrong somewhere? If capping utility really is the only clean fix, what does biting that bullet commit you to elsewhere — does it quietly break other stuff we want out of a decision theory? And is the Pascal's Mugging comparison actually apt, or were we conflating two things that just superficially rhyme with each other? A real walkthrough would be great, since "diminishing marginal utility solves it" is the part we'd already gotten to before things went sideways.
