I'm a PhD student in machine learning working on reward learning for robotic assistants, and for the last few weeks I've been stuck on a foundational worry that my advisor thinks I'm overthinking but that I haven't been able to shake. The standard pitch for inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is that if you observe an agent's behavior, you can recover the reward function that best explains it and treat that as a stand-in for what the agent actually wants. It's well known that this is trivially underdetermined: any behavior whatsoever counts as optimal with respect to a reward function that assigns the same value to every state, so the usual fixes (max-margin objectives, maximum-entropy priors, assuming the demonstrator is Boltzmann-rational at some fixed temperature, and so on) are extra assumptions bolted on to break the symmetry rather than anything derived from the data itself. What unsettled me more is a stronger claim I came across a while back, which I believe is due to Armstrong and Mindermann, though I may be misremembering the exact source: for any way of splitting an agent's behavior into "reward" plus "planning process or bias," there is an equally simple alternative split that attributes the very same behavior to a different reward altogether, so that no simplicity prior over that joint space can single out a uniquely correct decomposition without smuggling in substantive, non-empirical assumptions about rationality.

This has left me wondering whether the whole project of inferring preferences from behavior quietly presupposes a piece of folk-psychological metaphysics that doesn't survive scrutiny: the idea that there is some determinate fact, "what this agent truly wants," sitting behind its behavior and waiting to be uncovered, rather than something that only becomes well-defined relative to whatever interpretive scheme we impose on the agent (in the spirit of Dennett's point that belief-desire ascription is more like a predictively useful gloss than a discovery of latent inner states). Economists ran into a version of this dispute long before machine learning existed, when Samuelson tried to ground demand theory purely in observed choice, and Sen and others objected that choice behavior alone cannot support everything we want the concept of "preference" to do.

I'd like an essay from you that takes a real position on this: in the idealized limit of unlimited behavioral data and unlimited computation, can IRL in principle recover an agent's true preferences, or is talk of a determinate "true reward function" waiting to be inferred from behavior a kind of category mistake? Please engage with the strongest form of both sides rather than settling for "it depends," and close with a definite, defended verdict rather than a survey of positions.
