I help organize the question-and-answer portion of a graduate colloquium in philosophy of logic, and this term's invited speaker has published a defense of a relativist, assessment-sensitive semantics for future contingents, developed out of John MacFarlane's framework: the view that a sentence such as "There will be a sea battle tomorrow," uttered today, is neither true nor false relative to a context of assessment at the time of utterance, yet becomes true or false relative to a later context of assessment once the matter is settled, without there ever being a context-independent fact about the sea battle at the moment the sentence is spoken. I have been rereading Prior on tense logic and Thomason's supervaluationist treatment of the same puzzle in preparation, and I would like the conversation to move well past a restatement of Aristotle's sea-battle passage and the standard menu of solutions rehearsed in survey articles.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the substance of the assessment-sensitive view rather than inviting the speaker simply to restate it? I want at least one question that forces a sharp contrast between "true relative to a later context of assessment" and the supervaluationist's "supertrue on every branch," since the two proposals seem to buy the same payoff — no determinate fact now, vindication later — through quite different machinery; one on the assertion and retraction problem, namely what entitles a speaker to assert a future contingent sentence at all if it lacks a truth value at her own context of assessment, and whether she is thereby committed to retracting the assertion should the battle never occur; one that presses on whether the later vindication of the sentence simply reintroduces a Thin Red Line under a different name, since something must make that branch, rather than another, the one relative to which later assessors judge truly; and one asking her to justify the added relativist apparatus against the comparatively austere Peircean option of simply treating unsettled future-tense sentences as false outright. For each of these four, please add a follow-up question keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, so that the exchange does not stop at the first parry.
