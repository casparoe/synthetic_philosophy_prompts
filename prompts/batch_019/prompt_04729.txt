You have been asked to give the keynote at a Constitution Day naturalization ceremony, immediately before several dozen new citizens take the Oath of Allegiance, and the organizers want the speech built around Frederick Douglass's own change of mind about the document being sworn to. For roughly the first decade of his public career, Douglass shared William Lloyd Garrison's judgment that the Constitution was a "covenant with death and an agreement with hell," given its accommodations for the slave trade, the three-fifths clause, and the return of fugitives. Around 1851 he broke publicly and painfully with Garrison, having come to argue that the document's actual text -- which nowhere uses the words "slave" or "slavery" -- could and should be read as an anti-slavery instrument, a position he later summed up by calling the Constitution "a glorious liberty document" and held, at real cost to old friendships, for the rest of his life.

Most of your audience will know Douglass, if they know him at all, as the man who asked what the Fourth of July means to the slave, and a constitutional historian who has written on this very controversy will be seated in the front row. Write the eight-to-ten-minute speech: it must be moving and appropriate for new citizens and their families without flattening Douglass's position into either "the Constitution was simply good" or "Douglass simply rejected it."
