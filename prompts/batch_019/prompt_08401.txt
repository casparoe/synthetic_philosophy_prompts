A friend of mine, who is finishing a cognitive science PhD on metacognition, and I spent most of a long drive home from a workshop arguing about higher-order theories of consciousness — specifically, David Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory. I pushed the standard worry about targetless higher-order thoughts: since Rosenthal treats a higher-order thought and the state it targets as distinct existences, produced by a monitoring mechanism that can presumably misfire like any other causal process, nothing seems to rule out a HOT occurring with no target state at all, and his answer is to bite the bullet — insisting that what it's like for the subject is fixed by the HOT's content rather than by whatever the target state actually is, or isn't. My friend thought this response was perfectly adequate and accused me of quietly demanding a guarantee of introspective accuracy that no serious theory of consciousness owes anyone. I wasn't persuaded, and once I got home I wrote up the following to pin down what's actually bothering me, before I inflict it on her again. I'd like your comments before I do.

My objection isn't to the bare possibility of targetless HOTs, which strikes me as a cost worth paying in isolation. It's that the confabulation literature — subjects confidently reporting reasons for choices that demonstrably were not their actual causes, split-brain patients narrating explanations for actions initiated by a hemisphere with no access to the real cause — suggests that mismatches between a self-monitoring report and the state it's supposedly about are not exotic corner cases but a routine output of whatever system does our self-monitoring, working exactly as it normally does. If that's right, targetless and near-targetless HOTs stop looking like a contained embarrassment for the theory and start looking like something close to the typical case. Rosenthal's usual fallback here is that only non-inferential, suitably immediate HOTs confer phenomenal character, so confabulated self-reports needn't count as introspection at all; but that fallback only helps if confabulation is produced by some mechanism other than ordinary introspective monitoring, and a good deal of that literature suggests it's the very same mechanism, just going wrong in its perfectly ordinary way. So the theory seems to owe a principled, non-question-begging way of distinguishing "a conscious state that its HOT slightly misrepresents" from "no conscious state at all, merely a confabulated report" — and I don't see where that line is supposed to come from without quietly reintroducing the accurate inner sense the theory was designed to do without.

I would genuinely like your reaction to this rather than reassurance. Have I represented Rosenthal's actual view and his immediacy condition fairly, or have I flattened something he handles more carefully? Is the move from confabulation research to "targetless is closer to typical than exceptional" doing real philosophical work, or is it a seductive analogy that doesn't actually transfer from misrepresenting one's reasons for an action to misrepresenting whether a state exists at all? And, setting accuracy aside for a moment, is there anything here worth developing further, or have I just rediscovered a well-worn objection without recognizing it as such?
