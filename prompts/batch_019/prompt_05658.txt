I'm taking a seminar this term on women philosophers of the Enlightenment, and we recently spent a couple of sessions on Émilie du Châtelet's "Discourse on Happiness" — the short, unfinished essay she wrote near the end of her life about what she had actually learned, from her own experience, about how to live happily. What struck me most was how unsentimental she is about passion: she argues that strong passions are indispensable to happiness, so that the Stoic ideal of a calm, passion-free life is really just a recipe for boredom, but she's equally insistent that not all illusions should be swept away along with our prejudices. Some illusions — about love, about the importance of one's own pursuits — are actually part of what makes a passion worth having, and she's candid that romantic love specifically is not something we can expect to hold at its original intensity forever, which is part of why she puts so much weight on cultivating a passion like study: unlike love, it depends only on ourselves and doesn't run down with time.

My older sister, who is getting married next month, actually wrote her undergraduate thesis on du Châtelet a few years ago and is the reason I ended up taking this seminar, so I'd like to build my toast around this material as a kind of private tribute to her. The complication is that roughly a third of the guests are people from our philosophy department who know this text well enough to notice immediately if I flatten du Châtelet into a generic "true love conquers all" Enlightenment sage, while everyone else just wants a toast that is warm and easy to follow. I don't want to stand up and imply, even obliquely, that my sister's marriage is destined to cool into something else once the initial passion fades, but I also don't want to sand her actual, more clear-eyed view down into empty sentiment. Could you write me a toast of about three to four minutes that uses du Châtelet's real position on passion, illusion, and love honestly, and that still lands as a generous, genuine blessing on the marriage rather than a philosophy lecture or a backhanded compliment?
