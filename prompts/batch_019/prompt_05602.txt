Question: In the unpublished manuscripts on sexual morality that Bentham was too nervous to publish in his own lifetime (the "Offences Against One's Self" material), he argues that the criminal law's treatment of consensual homosexual acts can't be justified by the principle of utility. Reconstruct the argument, including which of the "principles adverse to utility" from the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation he thinks is actually doing the explanatory work in popular and legal condemnation of these acts. Then briefly say whether the argument sits comfortably with that earlier taxonomy or is in tension with it.

Student's answer:

Bentham's basic move is that consensual homosexual acts produce pleasure for the parties involved and, done in private between adults, no pain for anyone else. Since the principle of utility says to maximize the balance of pleasure over pain, and these acts are utility-positive (or at worst neutral) on his accounting, the criminal law's punishments -- which in his own day still ran up to execution -- represent a huge, uncompensated loss of happiness. On strictly hedonic grounds the law can't be justified.

His second move is diagnostic: if it isn't utility driving the condemnation, what is? Bentham's answer is the principle of asceticism, which he'd already defined in the Introduction as the view that approves of an act in proportion as it tends to diminish rather than increase happiness. He thinks this is exactly the mechanism behind disgust-based judgments here: people feel an aversion and then dress it up as a moral or religious verdict without ever tracing out real consequences for anyone's pleasure or pain. He also goes after the claim that the acts are "unnatural," arguing that this just means "uncommon" and isn't doing any independent work once you take away the utility-talk its smuggling in.

I think this fits pretty comfortably with the earlier taxonomy -- its basically a direct application of the two-principles framework to a case Bentham cared about personally. The one place I'd push on is that he doesn't have a real answer to someone who says that just knowing the acts occur is itself a source of pain to third parties, e.g. general moral alarm; if that counts as a utility cost his sums could come out differently, and nothing in the essay rules this move out except by asserting that the amount of pain involved is small.
