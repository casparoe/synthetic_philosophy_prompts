I teach a graduate seminar on social ontology, and this term we've spent several weeks on the metaphysics of acting together. Two of my strongest students have reached an impasse that I'd like an outside perspective on before we reconvene. One is committed to Michael Bratman's planning-theoretic account of shared intention, on which shared agency reduces without remainder to a structure of individual intentions: each party intends that they together perform the joint action, intends this at least partly because of the other's matching intention, and forms sub-plans that mesh with the other's, all of this being common knowledge between them. The other has been won over by Margaret Gilbert's plural subject theory, on which shared intention essentially involves joint commitment -- a normative bond that the parties jointly and openly enter into, and which cannot be reduced to any configuration, however intricate, of separate individual attitudes.

Their disagreement crystallized around a case raised in seminar: two colleagues begin co-authoring a paper on the strength of an informal conversation, without either ever explicitly promising the other anything, yet each comes to feel -- once real work has gone into the project on the strength of the other's apparent participation -- that walking away unannounced would wrong the other collaborator, and not merely disappoint them. My Bratman-inclined student wants to say this sense of obligation can be captured by further individual-level attitudes (expectations, reasonable reliance, the rational pressure generated by one's own prior intentions) without abandoning an individualist framework; my Gilbertian student thinks the case shows precisely why a sui generis joint commitment needs to be posited.

Could you write the response paper I've asked them to produce: a comparison of Bratman's and Gilbert's accounts of shared intention detailed enough that what actually divides them -- and not just their slogans -- becomes clear? I'd like you to take an explicit position on the co-authorship case: does Bratman's apparatus have the resources to explain the obligation the collaborators feel toward one another, or does Gilbert's appeal to joint commitment do genuine explanatory work that no amount of added individual psychology can replicate? Please defend a verdict rather than simply cataloguing considerations on each side.
