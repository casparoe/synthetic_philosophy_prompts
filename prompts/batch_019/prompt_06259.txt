Adams' Thesis — sometimes just called the Equation, the claim that one's degree of belief in "if A, then B" should simply equal one's conditional probability P(B|A)

I'm taking a graduate seminar on the semantics of conditionals this term, and we recently spent two sessions on David Lewis's triviality results and the responses they've provoked. For our term paper, we've each been assigned to defend one horn of what my professor called "the fundamental dilemma" facing anyone who wants to make sense of ordinary conditional talk in light of these results.

Either (a) we keep Adams' Thesis — sometimes just called the Equation, the claim that one's degree of belief in "if A, then B" should simply equal one's conditional probability P(B|A) — in full generality, and pay for this, in the manner of Adams and Edgington, by denying that indicative conditionals express propositions with truth values at all. Or (b) we keep a truth-conditional semantics for indicative conditionals, of the sort Stalnaker's possible-worlds account provides, and pay for this by weakening the Equation instead: either by restricting it to one's actual probability function rather than requiring it to survive conditionalizing on further evidence, or, in Jackson's style, by relocating the tie to conditional probability from the semantics itself to a Gricean norm of assertion (robustness), so that the intuitive link between conditional belief and conditional probability was never, in the first place, a fact about truth-conditional content.

I've been assigned to defend (b), and when I raised the worry in office hours that this just looks like a way of dodging the bill that (a) actually pays, my professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that (a) has a well-known cost of its own: something like a Frege-Geach problem for conditionals, since it's unclear how a non-truth-valued "conditional assertion" is supposed to embed under negation, disjunction, or as the antecedent of a further conditional. I would like your help thinking through the strongest version of this paper. Please argue that we should respond to Lewis's triviality results by preserving a truth-conditional semantics for indicative conditionals and weakening Adams' Thesis, rather than by following Adams and Edgington in denying that such conditionals have truth conditions at all. Your argument should explain concretely why the embedding problem is a serious cost for the non-cognitivist, and not merely a cosmetic one, and it should sketch a principled, non-ad-hoc rationale for expecting the Thesis to fail exactly where the triviality proof needs it to fail.
