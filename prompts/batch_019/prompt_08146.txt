In my philosophy of physics seminar, we've traced a line from Newton's bucket to its contemporary sequel: Earman and Norton's hole argument against manifold substantivalism. Newton took the concave water surface as evidence that acceleration relative to absolute space, not relative to other bodies, does the real causal work. Leibniz's reply to Clarke instead invoked the principle of sufficient reason: since shifting or rotating the entire material universe within absolute space would be empirically undetectable, God could have had no reason to actualize one such configuration over another, so absolute space must be rejected. Clarke's answer, on Newton's behalf, was that God's unconditioned will is itself a sufficient reason, full stop.

Suppose Newton had to confront the hole argument, which generates a structurally similar underdetermination internally, via diffeomorphism invariance rather than rigid shifts of the whole universe. Could he simply redeploy Clarke's brute-will move, or does the argument's far greater generality push Newtonian substantivalism somewhere genuinely new? Give a specific verdict -- e.g., that he bites the bullet with Clarke's own strategy, retreats to some anti-haecceitist "sophisticated substantivalism," or is effectively pushed toward relationism -- and defend it by engaging with the precise logical structure of both arguments.
