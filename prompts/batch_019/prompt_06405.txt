Six weeks ago my dissertation committee returned my proposal with a single word scrawled beside its central hypothesis: "Panglossian?" — followed by a citation to Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper on the spandrels of San Marco. The hypothesis in question holds that a specific display behavior in my study species evolved because it solved a particular adaptive problem; my committee wants me to first rule out that the behavior is merely a developmental or structural by-product of some unrelated trait before I'm permitted to call it an adaptation at all. Before rewriting the proposal, I'd like to think through whether that demand is actually well-founded. Please argue that, notwithstanding Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique, evolutionary biologists are entitled to treat adaptation by natural selection as the default explanation for a complex, well-functioning trait, with the burden of proof falling on non-adaptive alternatives such as drift, pleiotropy, and developmental constraint, rather than on the adaptationist hypothesis itself.
