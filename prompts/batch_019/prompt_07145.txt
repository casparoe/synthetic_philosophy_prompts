And here's what the person I'm grading actually wrote:

"Aumann's theorem (1976) says that if two Bayesians share a common prior, and their posteriors for some event E are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal - you literally cannot 'agree to disagree.' The key extra ingredient beyond ordinary knowledge is the gap between mutual knowledge (I know your posterior, you know mine) and common knowledge (I know it, you know it, I know that you know it, you know that I know it, and so on forever). Aumann's proof shows that once that whole infinite tower is in place, the two numbers get pulled together: each person can use what they know about the other's estimate to revise their own estimate, and since that very piece of reasoning is itself common knowledge, the process iterates until the two posteriors coincide.

This is exactly why the theorem doesn't show that two people who've talked and still disagree are being irrational. In practice, common knowledge of this kind essentially never actually gets reached, since it requires each side to keep updating on the other's updates indefinitely, and nobody has the patience or processing power to run that loop all the way through. So persistent disagreement after a conversation is completely compatible with both sides being perfectly rational bayesians the whole time, it just means the idealized common-knowledge condition was never actually met.

As for a real world upshot: this is the basis for Aumann's no-trade theorem in finance, which says that if its common knowledge that both sides of a trade think they'll profit from it, that alone should make each of them suspicious, since the other guy being willing to trade is itself evidence they shouldn't ignore - so two equally rational traders who share a prior shouldn't be able to find mutually agreeable speculative bets in the first place."
