For the past year I've kept a private journal, mainly to track my moods, and looking back over the entries I've noticed a pattern I can't quite explain. Whenever I'm waiting on something uncertain — the results of a medical test, or whether a friend's oddly short reply means she's upset with me — I don't stay neutral until I find out. Within minutes, sometimes before I've even consciously admitted to myself that I'm worried, my stomach has already tightened and I've settled on the worst version of events; from then on almost everything I notice gets absorbed into that story, so I end up texting "is everything okay?" or calling the clinic again just to hear someone say it's probably fine. Getting that answer calms me completely for an hour or two, but the calm fades, and by the next day I'm already braced for the next ambiguous thing, as though the reassurance never really reached whatever part of me generates the worry in the first place.

I described this to a friend who's serious about meditation, and she said it's simply "attachment" — my mind refusing to sit with not-knowing. That explanation feels too easy to me, because it doesn't say anything about why checking works for an hour and then stops working, or why a single ambiguous sign is enough to confirm the bad story while a clear, direct answer from another person barely dents it a day later. It seems less like a flaw in my character and more like some general feature of how expectation, bodily sensation, and evidence all fit together — along with the actions I take to try to resolve my own uncertainty — just tuned in an unhelpful way in my particular case. Is my friend basically right that this is just attachment under another name, or is there a more specific and serious account of why minds (and maybe bodies) work this way — one that would also tell me whether a pattern like this can be corrected at the root, rather than just managed one episode at a time?
