Quick lit-nerd tangent that turned into an actual question for you. I found out that lawyers and bioethicists literally borrowed Ulysses tying himself to the mast as a term of art -- a "Ulysses contract" -- for real stuff like psychiatric advance directives, where you consent in advance to being forcibly treated or committed during some future episode, overriding your own objections in the moment (theres a constitutional-precommitment version of this too, binding a whole polity instead of a person, but lets stick with the personal one). What I can't shake is that Augustine basically diagnosed the exact problem this is meant to solve centuries earlier, in Confessions book 8 -- the "two wills" business, wanting to want chastity, stalling with "not yet" right up until his conversion. So here's my actual question: if you put a real modern Ulysses contract in front of Augustine -- not young Confessions-era Augustine, but the older one who'd already fought Pelagius -- would his mature theology of grace let him sign off on it as a legit fix for weakness of will, or would he see it as a kind of cheat, someone trying to engineer their own righteousness by contract instead of waiting on grace? Don't give me the generic "Augustine on free will" answer -- actually work through the mechanics of grace vs self-binding in his specific framework.
