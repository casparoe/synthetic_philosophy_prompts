I'm drafting the middle section of a novel that hinges on a scene inside a hospital ethics committee, and I've realized I can't write the scene honestly until I understand the underlying dispute well enough to stage it without cheating. The situation in the book: a young man has been minimally responsive for several months after a traumatic brain injury, oscillating on bedside exams between unresponsive wakefulness syndrome and the minimally conscious state. His neurology team has run one of the perturbational-complexity protocols that grew out of integrated information theory — the TMS-EEG "zap and zip" approach associated with Massimini and Tononi's group — and the resulting complexity score falls in the equivocal band the fellow on the case describes as neither clearly "off," the way deep anesthesia or slow-wave sleep read, nor clearly "on," the way an ordinary waking response reads. The family takes the number as confirmation that their son is still present behind his eyes; the attending, off the record, calls it a research instrument being pressed into clinical service it was never validated for. My point-of-view character, the hospital chaplain who sits on the committee as a non-voting member, ends up drafting the group's written opinion overnight, and that document has to appear on the page verbatim.

So: would you write that opinion? It should recommend one specific course of action with a concrete time frame — continuing current treatment while repeating the testing on a set schedule, pursuing some further diagnostic step, or shifting the conversation toward comfort care — rather than simply gesturing at further study. And I'd like it to take seriously why a high complexity score doesn't settle the question even on its own terms: there's the objection, associated with Scott Aaronson's exchange with Tononi, that integrated information theory's mathematics assigns a very high Φ — IIT's measure of integrated information — to simple grid-like systems that nobody believes are conscious, which ought to make anyone cautious about reading a single number as a direct report of inner experience; and separately, there's the more mundane statistical point that a measure's track record at sorting groups of patients into independently confirmed categories doesn't automatically license a conclusion about what one borderline patient, in front of this particular committee, is experiencing. I don't want a survey of considerations — I want the actual opinion this committee would sign its name to, one that still gives the losing argument its due.
