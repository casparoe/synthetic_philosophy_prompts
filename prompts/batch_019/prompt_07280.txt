For the next episode we've invited a scholar who works on Rousseau's aesthetic writings and is known for defending the letter's central argument as still worth taking seriously philosophically, rather than filing it away as an early instance of anti-theatrical prejudice. I don't want the conversation to just retread the general critique of amour-propre and luxury that everyone already associates with Rousseau, so I'm trying to draft eight to ten questions that stay close to the specific moves the letter actually makes.

I'd like at least one question on the apparent contradiction between condemning theatrical representation as corrosive to sincerity and Rousseau's own record as a dramatist -- a comedy, a hit opera, and later a melodrama, written both before and after the letter; one on his separate, more specific argument against actresses and mixed-sex audiences, since that seems to be carrying real weight in the text beyond the general worry about passive spectatorship; one on what actually distinguishes his proposed alternative -- citizens gathered outdoors so that they are performers and audience at once -- from merely a more participatory, cheaper form of theater; and one on his dispute with Diderot over whether sentimental "bourgeois drama" can genuinely cultivate moral feeling in an audience, given that this disagreement was unfolding just as their friendship was falling apart.
