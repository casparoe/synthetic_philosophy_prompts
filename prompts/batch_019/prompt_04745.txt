I'm four chapters into a novel about a cognitive scientist, Marion Voss, who spent the better part of two decades at a robotics startup trying to give a household assistant robot something like ordinary common sense, and who is now, in the novel's present, trying to explain to her adult son why she quietly abandoned that work for pure philosophy of mind. The hinge of the book is a long-postponed conversation in which she finally has to tell him what actually defeated her, and I don't want to fudge the philosophy, because the whole point of the scene is that she has a real, considered position rather than a vague sense of having hit a wall.

The technical crux is the frame problem: given that any action changes some things and leaves indefinitely many others unchanged, how does a reasoner update its beliefs without either explicitly checking each of the indefinitely many things that didn't change, or simply not checking, and thereby risking catastrophic ignorance of some change that did matter? I know, roughly, the three camps she would have had to choose from. Fodor treated the problem as a symptom of something worse: that belief fixation is "isotropic" and "Quinean," in his terms — that what counts as relevant to confirming or revising a belief is holistic and unbounded in a way no encapsulated computational module could capture — and concluded that classical cognitive science could model peripheral, modular processes but never central ones. Dennett, working the problem through a series of increasingly careful robot-and-bomb thought experiments, insisted it was real and philosophically deep, against those who dismissed it as a mere engineering nuisance, but drew from that no conclusion that computational minds are impossible, only that the design problem is harder and more interesting than naive research programs assumed. Dreyfus offered something that isn't obviously a third point on the same axis: that the problem is an artifact of assuming cognition must consist of inference over explicit representations at all, and that it dissolves, Heidegger-style, once intelligence is understood as skillful embodied engagement with a world of affordances rather than a body of facts to be reasoned over.

Please write the essay Marion would have had to write to herself before that conversation: not a survey that lists the three positions and shrugs, but one that actually weighs them against each other and settles, for her, whether the frame problem shows that no computational theory of central cognition can succeed, or whether Dennett's patience or Dreyfus's dissolution has the better of it. A conditional verdict is fine — something like "Fodor is right about classical, symbolic architectures but wrong that this generalizes to computation as such" is the shape of thing I mean — but it has to be a genuine verdict, defended well enough that I can compress it into a few lines she says out loud without any philosopher who picks up the book catching me faking it.
