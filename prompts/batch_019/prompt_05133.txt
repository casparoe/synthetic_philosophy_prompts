After a career in experimental physics, I have lately been reading my way through some of the history behind the science I used to practice, and I recently spent time with the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence. It strikes me that the dispute, for all its talk of absolute space, is really a proxy fight between two rival theologies of divine action: Leibniz treats a God who must occasionally step in to correct irregularities in the solar system as an unskillful watchmaker patching up a flawed design, while Clarke -- writing on Newton's behalf -- insists that such ongoing correction is exactly what real providence and dominion look like, and that Leibniz's fully self-sufficient, pre-established world leaves God, after the initial act of creation, with no active governing role at all.

I'd like you to compare these two pictures of how God relates to the created order. In particular, I'm curious how far Newton's insistence on God's active dominion -- the language he uses in the General Scholium -- reflects the antitrinitarian, Arian-leaning Christology he was careful to keep private during his lifetime, as against how far Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason and doctrine of pre-established harmony grow out of a much more orthodox, rationalist project of defending the faith. On balance, which picture holds together as the more coherent theology, quite apart from whose physics ultimately proved correct?
