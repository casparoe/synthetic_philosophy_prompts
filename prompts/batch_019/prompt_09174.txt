For an independent project in my philosophy elective, I've been reading George Berkeley, and I think I've noticed a connection between two parts of his work that my teacher never mentioned. Berkeley is famous for immaterialism, the claim that physical objects are just collections of ideas perceived by minds, summed up in his phrase "esse est percipi": to be is to be perceived. But he also wrote a much less famous work, The Querist, arguing that money has no intrinsic value and is really just a "ticket or counter" for keeping track of trade. This looks to me like a special case of the same idea: if Berkeley already denies that physical things like gold and silver coins are real apart from being perceived, then of course he'd conclude that money's value is likewise "unreal" and merely conventional. Since most philosophers today treat his immaterialism as a fascinating but ultimately unsuccessful theory, does that mean his monetary writings should be dismissed on the same grounds? I want to understand how much his economic arguments actually depend on his more famous metaphysics before I build my project around this connection.
