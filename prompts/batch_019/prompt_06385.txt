Molyneux's problem usually gets flattened into "Locke said no." The fuller history is more interesting: Molyneux himself supplied that answer, which Locke endorsed and published — a man born blind, on gaining sight, could not pick out the cube from the sphere without touching them. Berkeley agreed, but for a stronger reason: on his view, visual and tactile ideas are so heterogeneous that no inference from one to the other is possible prior to learned association. Leibniz, almost alone, said yes — in the New Essays, he argued the newly sighted man could work it out at once by applying "rational principles" to the visual array, since figure and extension are, for him, ideas graspable by intellect and not tied to any one sense.

I'd like your best reconstruction of how Leibniz — arguing strictly from that position, not the popular one-line summary of it — would assess a live question in current AI research: whether a multimodal system trained jointly on vision and language, then handed a genuinely novel tactile or depth-sensor stream with no paired training across that modality, could correctly infer an object's shape zero-shot, and what such a result would actually show. Complicate the answer with a real wrinkle: clinical studies of newly sighted adults after cataract-removal surgery generally find that humans themselves fail this cross-modal match immediately, and only learn it over the following days. Does Leibniz's appeal to "rational principles" survive that finding, or does it collapse into exactly the sort of learned correlation he was trying to rule out? Be explicit about what specific result would, on his own terms, actually vindicate him over Locke and Berkeley, rather than gesturing generically at how "fascinating" he would find modern AI.
