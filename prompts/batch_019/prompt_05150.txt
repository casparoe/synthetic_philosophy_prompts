A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about Skolem's paradox and never quite resolved it. I had brought it up as a curiosity: the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that if ZFC has a model at all, it has a countable one, yet that very model satisfies the ZFC theorem that uncountable sets exist. My friend's objection was that this makes the model sound simply mistaken: if its entire domain is countable from the outside, some enumeration of that domain exists, and restricting it to the model's version of ω₁ yields an actual bijection with ω — so in what sense does the model "correctly" believe ω₁ is uncountable, rather than simply being wrong? I only had the standard line that cardinality is relative to a model, and could not explain why that is not simply a relabeling of the model's error. Could you lay out, in more depth than the usual one-paragraph gloss, exactly what work the "missing" bijection is doing, why the model's verdict is not in fact a mistake, and why Skolem himself took this to show that cardinality is inherently relative rather than absolute — plus how a defender of a single, objective universe of sets ought to respond to him?
