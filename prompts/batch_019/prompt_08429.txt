Two years ago, largely out of guilt, I agreed to join a few literary committees "for the good of the ecosystem," and I now sit on the four-member advisory board of the Ashbrook Prize, a small nonprofit that gives an annual award to a debut novelist and builds a fall festival weekend around that year's winner. Two months ago we announced this year's recipient; last week, someone resurfaced a blog post she wrote in her mid-twenties dismissing self-published authors as "people who couldn't hack it" and small regional presses as "vanity operations run out of garages" — unfortunately close to home, since roughly a third of our donor base has published with presses of exactly that kind. The post has since been screenshotted across several thousand accounts; a petition now demands we withdraw the prize before the festival program goes to the printer this Friday; and two of our longest-standing donors have written in, personally stung, threatening to withhold next year's support. A vocal minority on our mailing list insists the opposite: that pulling the prize now would be nothing but capitulation to people who have not read the novel and do not intend to. Both factions have taken to invoking Kierkegaard — "he basically predicted Twitter" — in the board's email thread, which sent me back to "The Present Age" for the first time since a college seminar, partly because I am also circling that stretch of Kierkegaard's life for a novel-in-progress set in 1840s Copenhagen. Two things I had forgotten: that the essay began life as the back half of a review of a now-forgotten novel by Thomasine Gyllembourg, which is exactly the kind of detail a novelist ought not to misplace; and how specific and strange the actual argument becomes once you get past the meme version of it — that "the public" is a phantom the press manufactures so that people can hold opinions without ever becoming answerable for them, categorically unlike a genuine community bound by a shared task, and that Kierkegaard tested this diagnosis against his own experience during his running battle with the satirical paper The Corsair, which spent months lampooning his appearance in print after he all but goaded it into treating him as it treated everyone else.

Could you draft the memo I need to send the board before we meet Thursday? I need one clear recommendation — keep the prize and the public appearance exactly as planned, keep the prize but alter the public format, or rescind it outright — together with a timeline for acting on it, and reasoning that actually puts Kierkegaard's own distinctions to work: leveling (Nivellering), the public versus a genuine community, the press and anonymity as the conditions of the public's existence, and his insistence that the only real corrective is "the single individual" rather than any collective, which is a little awkward given that the body making this decision is, by definition, a committee. I would especially like the memo to take seriously the strongest case for rescinding: that at least some of the people pressing us are not an anonymous phantom at all but named, longtime donors with a specific and legitimate grievance, and that treating every collective reaction as mere leveling would be a suspiciously convenient way of waving off real complaints as crowd noise. I would rather have the strongest version of a case I might end up rejecting than a memo that quietly picks a side and reasons backward into Kierkegaard.
