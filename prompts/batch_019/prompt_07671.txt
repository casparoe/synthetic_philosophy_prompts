Question: In "The Enforcement of Morals," Devlin argues that the state may legitimately use the criminal law to punish private, consensual conduct that provokes widespread intolerance, indignation, and disgust in the ordinary person, because a shared morality is as necessary to a society's survival as its shared government. Explain Devlin's argument, Hart's central objections to it in Law, Liberty, and Morality, and briefly assess whether Hart's objections succeed.

Student's answer: Devlin's argument comes out of his reaction to the Wolfenden Report, which recommended decriminalising private homosexual acts between consenting adults. Devlin thought this went too far because, in his view, society is not just a set of political institutions but a "community of ideas," held together by a shared morality in the same way it's held together by a shared government - this is the disintegration thesis: erode the shared morality and you erode the thing that makes the society one society, so the law is entitled to protect it just as it protects against treason. To identify what that shared morality actually rules out, Devlin appeals to the reactions of the ordinary reasonable person - the man in the jury box, or the man on the Clapham omnibus - and specifically asks whether he would react to the conduct with intolerance, indignation, and disgust. If he would, Devlin thinks that's evidence the conduct threatens the fabric holding society together, and so it's a legitimate candidate for the criminal law regardless of whether anyone else is harmed.

Hart pushes back with a broadly Millian harm principle: the state's only business restricting someone's liberty is preventing harm to others, not enforcing shared feeling for its own sake, so private consensual conduct should be off-limits to the law even if the majority finds it disgusting. His strongest objection is empirical - Devlin never shows that tolerating this kind of private nonconformity actually causes societies to fall apart, and there isn't historical evidence for it. Hart also thinks Devlin's test can't tell a reasoned moral conviction from a mere feeling of revulsion, so it risks treating simple prejudice as if it were morality, which would also make it very hard to ever recognise moral progress, since any reform has to survive the disgust of the man on the Clapham omnibus before Devlin would even consider it legitimate.

I think Hart's objections mostly land. It's also worth noting that Hart's position here is continuous with the rest of his jurisprudence: this is the same Hart who, against Fuller, denied any necessary connection between law and morality, so his resistance to letting the criminal law track popular morality in the Devlin debate isn't a separate, ad hoc liberal commitment - its the same separation thesis doing double duty.
