A reading group on formal and social epistemology that I co-run recently set up a small play-money prediction market in which members bet on things like which grant applications will be funded and which job candidates will get offers. Two regulars fell into a running argument about it after last week's session, and I would like your assessment of their exchange.

(1) Felix, who works on market design, praised the market: its price for each outcome pools everyone's scattered private information into a single number that is typically better calibrated than any individual member's guess -- the Hayekian point that prices aggregate dispersed knowledge -- and comparable markets have a respectable track record calling elections and product launches.

(2) Naomi, a formal epistemologist, objected that this cannot be the whole story, invoking the no-trade theorem: if it were common knowledge that everyone in the market is a rational Bayesian with the same prior, the mere fact that someone is willing to take your bet is itself evidence you should decline it, and reasoning this out in advance should stop such traders from betting against one another on private information at all. Since the market plainly generates trades, something in that idealization fails for the actual participants, and Felix owes an account of what.

(3) Felix replied that nobody needs to be an identical rational Bayesian sharing a prior for the market to work: most of the trading is just colleagues with genuinely different starting assessments backing their judgment with play money, which is the very mechanism that pulls in dispersed evidence rather than a defect in it.

(4) Naomi answered that "different priors" is doing a great deal of unexplained work: if departmental biases are correlated rather than independent -- everyone rating their own students' letters too generously, say -- trades driven by such differences pool distortion rather than cancel it, and nothing about the market's mechanics reveals which situation obtains.

(5) Felix's rejoinder invoked Grossman and Stiglitz: no market could be fully information-revealing without eliminating anyone's incentive to trade on information in the first place, so some residual noise or disagreement in prices is a feature of any working market rather than a symptom that ours is broken; the live question is one of market design, not an all-or-nothing test against an idealized theorem.

Please work through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each step rather than just who wins overall, and close by noting what you think both of them are still missing about what our market can and cannot tell us.
