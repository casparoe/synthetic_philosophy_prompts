Had a long, kinda heated argument with my friend Dan last night about Gongsun Long's "a white horse is not a horse" thing from the School of Names. Dan's take: its cheap wordplay -- Gongsun Long just uses "is/is not" in two different senses, identity vs. category membership, and hopes nobody notices, so the whole thing collapses once you point that out. I don't think thats the whole story but couldn't say why, exactly.

We got stuck on one specific move. The Objector says having a white horse counts as having a horse, so how can a white horse not be a horse. The Advocate comes back with: if you're seeking a horse, a yellow or black one will do; if you're seeking a white horse, they won't. So "seeking a horse" and "seeking a white horse" have different satisfaction conditions -- which he says proves "white" and "horse" pick out different things, hence white horse isn't horse. Dan pulled up something about Hansen's mass-noun theory and Graham's part/whole logic as the standard rescue for this, but I don't think that actually engages with this specific step.

What I actually want to know: is the Advocate smuggling in an equivocation here, exploiting the fact that "seek" is the kind of verb where you can't freely swap in co-referring terms (unlike plain "there is" / "having")? Or is there a legit logical point buried in there that the mass-noun/part-whole readings are trying to rescue? Walk me through the actual mechanics of just that one step, not a verdict on "genuine logic vs sophistry" for the whole dialogue.
