And here's the answer I need graded:

"Pure time preference is the idea that we value a good less just because its farther away in time, even holding fixed how big the good is and how certain we are that it'll happen. Sidgwick raises basically this worry in the Methods of Ethics: from what he calls the point of view of the universe, the mere date at which a pleasure or pain occurs isn't a rational ground for caring about it more or less, since temporal position is like spatial position, and nobody thinks a good matters less just because it's further away in space. Ramsey picks this up in his 1928 paper on saving, where he treats a positive discount rate on future utility as basically a failure of imagination rather than a defensible ethical judgment. Parfit develops the point at length in Reasons and Persons: he argues temporal neutrality is the rational default, and that any constant discount rate, compounded over centuries, commits us to absurdly tiny weightings on the welfare of people who'll live long after us, even though nothing about their being future makes their suffering matter less.

Because Sidgwick, Ramsey and Parfit all reject pure time preference as irrational, their arguments show that discounting the future is a mistake quite generally: a rational planner should use a zero discount rate for evaluating any long term policy, whether its a pension scheme or a climate policy. This is exactly why the Stern Review's use of a discount rate near zero was such a departure from standard practice, and why Nordhaus's more conventional review of the economics of climate change praised Stern for following through on what philosophers like Parfit had already established about the irrationality of discounting.

So pure time preference is a bias these philosophers are right to reject, and once you reject it there's no principled place left to draw the line on discounting the future."
