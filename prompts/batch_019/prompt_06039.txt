In my IB Philosophy class, we've been working through the Philosophy of Religion optional theme, and last week our teacher spent a whole double period on Wittgenstein's idea that religious belief works less like a testable hypothesis and more like its own self-contained "language-game" or "form of life," governed by standards that don't reduce to the kind of evidence we'd use to settle an ordinary factual question. She used his famous example, from the lecture notes on religious belief published after his death, about a person who says they believe in the Last Judgement, and contrasted the way that belief functions with the way we'd talk about predicting an ordinary future event, to show how the grammar of "I believe" can be doing very different jobs in the two cases. What really stuck with me was a side comment she made in passing: a philosopher named Kai Nielsen apparently argued that this whole approach is basically a dodge dressed up as description — a way of making religious claims immune to criticism by quietly changing what "belief" means — while Wittgenstein's defenders, including Norman Malcolm and D.Z. Phillips, insisted the charge missed the point, since they weren't trying to protect religion from criticism so much as accurately describe how religious concepts actually function for the people who hold them. A couple of friends and I argued about who was right for almost an hour after class and got nowhere.

Since we couldn't settle it ourselves, could you write a dialogue between two philosophers holding these two positions? One should press the "Wittgensteinian fideism" charge in its strongest form — that treating religious belief as a self-contained language-game makes it unfalsifiable, puts it on a par with any other internally consistent practice (astrology, say), and doesn't match how actual believers talk about their faith as simply true — while the other defends the Wittgensteinian view against exactly that charge. I'd like them to go back and forth for several exchanges, with each one responding directly to the specific point the other just made rather than restating their own opening position, and at some point I'd like them to get into whether "groundless" religious belief is really analogous to the kind of groundless certainty Wittgenstein describes elsewhere, like believing you have two hands or that the earth existed long before you were born. Please don't let either side clearly win by the end — my understanding is that this is a genuinely live disagreement among philosophers of religion, and I'd like the dialogue to reflect that rather than tie things up neatly.
