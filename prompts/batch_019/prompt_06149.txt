I have been working through Richard Jeffrey's treatment of probability kinematics for a reading group on formal epistemology, and we spent most of a recent session stuck on the Judy Benjamin problem, usually traced to Bas van Fraassen's 1981 discussion. Here is a version of the setup. A soldier, Judy Benjamin, is dropped into a region she knows is divided by two crossing roads into four equal quadrants: Red-Headquarters, Red-Second-Company, Blue-Headquarters, and Blue-Second-Company. With no further information she assigns each quadrant probability 1/4, so her credence that she is somewhere in Red territory and her credence that she is somewhere in Blue territory are each 1/2. A garbled radio transmission then gets through with exactly one piece of usable content: "If you're in Red territory, it's three times more likely that you're in the Second Company's area than in the Headquarters area" — that is, she is to set her conditional credence in Red-Second-Company, given Red, to 3/4. The message says nothing else, and in particular nothing that seems to bear on whether she's in Red or Blue territory in the first place. So naively, her credence that she's in Blue territory should stay fixed at 1/2. Yet the seemingly natural way of updating — finding the distribution over the four quadrants that satisfies the new conditional constraint while lying as close as possible to the old one, in the sense of minimizing relative entropy (cross-entropy, KL-divergence) — does not leave that 1/2 alone; her credence in Red vs. Blue shifts as a side effect of the update, even though the message seems entirely silent on that question. This is usually presented as a serious problem for treating "minimize relative information relative to your prior" as a general, all-purpose recipe for updating on conditional or otherwise soft evidence.

A number of different responses to this puzzle have come up in the reading group and appear in the literature, and I would like your considered ranking of them, judged by how satisfying each is as a general, principled account of how one should update on conditional information of this kind — not merely as an ad hoc patch for this one example:

1. Minimum-cross-entropy updating: apply the KL-divergence-minimizing update described above without modification, and accept the shift in P(Red) as the price of a fully general rule.
2. Rigidity-patching: add an explicit constraint that the marginal probability of any proposition the message doesn't mention (here, Red-vs-Blue) must stay fixed, and minimize divergence subject to that constraint together with the reported conditional probability.
3. Imaging: replace global divergence-minimization with a Lewis/Gärdenfors-style world-by-world minimal revision, moving credence from each maximally specific possibility to whichever nearby possibility best accommodates the new conditional claim.
4. Primitivism about conditional probability: take conditional probabilities (à la Popper–Rényi) as basic rather than as ratios read off a single unconditional distribution, so that learning the new conditional fact need not force any particular revision of the unconditional marginals.
5. Alternative-divergence resolutions: keep the "closest distribution to the prior" strategy, but replace relative entropy with a different measure of distance between distributions, chosen precisely so that satisfying the conditional constraint leaves the Red/Blue marginal undisturbed.
6. Permissivism about the posterior: deny that there is, or needs to be, a single rationally mandated posterior; many distributions are compatible with what Judy learned, and insisting on one canonical "closest" answer is itself what manufactures the appearance of a puzzle.
7. Biting the bullet on relevance: argue the shift is not actually irrational, since under any genuinely general-purpose updating rule, information about the internal composition of Red territory can constitute real, if indirect, evidence about Red vs. Blue — and it is the intuition that it "obviously" cannot that should be given up.
8. Causal reframing: treat the transmission as information about the process that generated the quadrant assignment (or about the sender), to be incorporated as one would incorporate an intervention in a causal model, rather than as a straightforward Jeffrey-style constraint on Judy's object-level partition.
