I'm one of the founding engineers at a small startup that builds a "digital town square" app — a handful of small city governments use it for public comment on local stuff like zoning meetings and school board decisions. When we first built it we made a deliberate call to let people post pseudonymously instead of under real names, because in early user interviews people kept saying they'd never post honestly about a shady zoning deal or a corrupt official under their actual name. Three-ish years in though, its gotten pretty ugly in a lot of the towns we're in — every other thread turns into a pile-on on whoever posted, theres a ton of drive-by snark, and the actual deliberation the product was supposed to enable has basically disappeared. I sit on our internal "product & trust" council (a couple engineers, our head of community, and our lawyer), and next week we're voting on an actual proposal: switch to mandatory real-name verification for everyone, no more pseudonyms.

Our head of community did a philosophy minor and will not let this go — she keeps bringing up some 19th century Danish guy named Kierkegaard in these meetings, something about an essay he wrote where "the public" is basically a fake crowd that only exists because of newspapers, plus some concept called "leveling" where the press flattens everyone into this abstraction so nobody's actually accountable for what they say to each other anymore. Apparently he also got personally torn apart by name in some satirical rag back in the 1840s, and there's real debate over whether he kind of provoked that himself. I don't know the details, honestly, I just know the vibes at this point after three meetings of this. Could you write the actual memo I'd bring to the council? I want it to commit to a real recommendation on the real-names proposal — not "here are three options, discuss" — explain the reasoning in a way the lawyer and the rest of us non-philosophy people can actually follow, and seriously engage with what Kierkegaard's actual argument was rather than the two-word version, including the strongest reason my recommendation could still backfire once we implement it.
