I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the last year or so I have been working my way through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and a handful of philosophy-of-physics blogs, mostly because of an old disagreement with my father over whether the universe is "really" deterministic. That eventually led me into the dispute between Humeans and anti-Humeans about what a law of nature actually is. I think I have a decent grip on the basic positions: the Humean, in a tradition running from Mill through Ramsey to David Lewis's "Best System Account," holds that a law is nothing over and above whichever generalizations belong to the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength in summarizing absolutely everything that ever happens (the "Humean mosaic" of particular matters of fact). The anti-Humean, in the tradition of people like David Armstrong or, more recently, Tim Maudlin, insists that this gets things backwards: there must be some further, more fundamental fact — a relation of necessitation between universals, or a primitive "governing" relation, or something else along those lines — that is responsible for the mosaic having the pattern it does, rather than the law being read off the pattern after the fact.

What I keep getting stuck on is an objection to the Humean side that I've seen called the "circularity" or "explanatory direction" problem, and I mentioned it recently to a friend who did a physics degree, who waved it away as "just a category mistake" without really explaining why. The worry, as I understand it, is this: if a law is defined as whatever best systematizes the mosaic, then the law is true because of, and metaphysically depends on, the mosaic. But we also want to say that the law explains the mosaic — that particular events fall into that pattern because they are governed by the law. Those two claims look like they can't both be true: either the pattern comes first and the "law" is just a convenient redescription of it after the fact, with no genuine explanatory power, or the law is somehow prior to and responsible for the pattern, in which case it isn't merely a summary of it. I know that Humeans such as Lewis and Barry Loewer think this isn't really a vicious circle — that there is some way of distinguishing the sense in which the law depends on the mosaic from the sense in which the law explains events within it — but every version of that reply I've found is either too compressed for me to reconstruct on my own, or simply asserts that the two kinds of dependence are different without showing why that isn't just a verbal dodge.

Could you walk me through, in plain language, how a committed Humean is supposed to get out of this circle — what the two senses of "depends on" actually are and why they don't just collapse into each other — and then tell me honestly whether you find the response convincing, or whether you think the anti-Humean has identified a genuine cost of the Humean view rather than a merely apparent one?
