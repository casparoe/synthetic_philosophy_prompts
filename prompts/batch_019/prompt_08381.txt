A friend and I spent most of a long train ride last week arguing about endurantism versus perdurantism, a distinction we had just encountered in a metaphysics reading group. We ended up testing it on my own road bike, which by now has had its frame, both wheels, the drivetrain, and the saddle replaced at various points since I bought it in graduate school -- is it still "the same bike"? She defended a perdurantist line -- in fact the more radical, stage-theoretic version of it. On this view, objects are really four-dimensional "space-time worms" extended through time the way they're extended through space, but "the bike" strictly names only a single momentary stage of such a worm, so that all our talk of its past or future really concerns numerically different stages linked by a counterpart relation rather than by genuine identity. She insisted this is the only clean way to handle the problem of temporary intrinsics, since it makes no sense for one wholly present three-dimensional object to have been matte black a decade ago and glossy red today, if those are incompatible properties of a single thing. I pushed the standard endurantist reply: if she's right, nothing is ever wholly present, and "the bike," or worse "I," dissolves into a mere succession of momentary stages stitched together by resemblance and causal continuity, none of which is any more truly the thing in question than the others. We argued for over an hour, never converged, and couldn't even agree on how much weight to put on the usual point that special relativity, by denying any frame-independent fact about which distant events are simultaneous, favors her four-dimensional picture over my three-dimensional one.

What has stayed with me isn't who won, but a question the argument raised almost as an aside. I'm about to sign a thirty-year mortgage, and it struck me that these two views might actually recommend different attitudes toward that decision -- toward what it means for the person signing the papers now and the person still making payments three decades on to be bound by, or entitled to renegotiate, one and the same commitment. Setting aside which theory is actually true, could you describe the most important implications of the endurantism/perdurantism debate -- including how each side's handling of temporary intrinsics and of the "mere succession of stages" worry actually bears on the matter -- for how I should think about long-term, future-self-binding commitments like this one?
