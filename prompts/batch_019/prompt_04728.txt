I'm a philosophy PhD student (early modern is my main area — my dissertation's on Berkeley's theory of perception, mostly the Principles and the Three Dialogues), and I picked up The Querist over the summer partly because my advisor keeps saying you cant really understand Berkeley's whole picture of nature without seeing what he does with it outside the metaphysics, and partly because I'm helping run an informal reading group with some econ history-of-thought people who wanted to look at early theories of money. Anyway I hit a run of queries in Part I that I keep re-reading without feeling like I've got the full force of it, so I wanted to sanity check my reading with someone else.

Here's the passage (Part I, queries 33-40 — numbering might be slightly different in other printings, since Berkeley revised the thing a few times):

"33. Qu. Whether it be not the opinion or will of the people, exciting them to industry, that truly enricheth a nation? And whether this doth not principally depend on the means for counting, transferring, and preserving power, that is, property of all kinds?
34. Qu. Whether if there was no silver or gold in the kingdom, our trade might not, nevertheless, supply bills of exchange, sufficient to answer the demands of absentees in England or elsewhere?
35. Qu. Whether current bank notes may not be deemed money? And whether they are not actually the greater part of the money of this kingdom?
36. Qu. Provided the wheels move, whether it is not the same thing, as to the effect of the machine, be this done by the force of wind, or water, or animals?
37. Qu. Whether power to command the industry of others be not real wealth? And whether money be not in truth tickets or tokens for conveying and recording such power, and whether it be of great consequence what materials the tickets are made of?
38. Qu. Whether trade, either foreign or domestic, be in truth any more than this commerce of industry?
39. Qu. Whether to promote, transfer, and secure this commerce, and this property in human labour, or, in other words, this power, be not the sole means of enriching a people, and how far this may be done independently of gold and silver?
40. Qu. Whether it were not wrong to suppose land itself to be wealth? And whether the industry of the people is not first to be consider'd, as that which constitutes wealth, which makes even land and silver to be wealth, neither of which would have, any value but as means and motives to industry?"

What I can't pin down is how strong a claim query 37 is actually making — the whole book is just rhetorical questions strung together and Berkeley never comes out and states a thesis in his own voice, so its genuinely ambiguous to me. Is he committing to something like a full nominalist or proto-chartalist view, where money is purely a social/institutional sign and the material it happens to be made of (gold, paper, shells, whatever) is strictly irrelevant to it being money at all? Or is it a weaker point, more like "materials only matter for practical reasons (durability, resistance to counterfeiting, etc.), not for constituting value"? And is the wind/water/animal-power analogy in query 36 supposed to be doing real philosophical work in setting up that claim — like, is he deliberately making a "it's the function that matters, not the substrate" move that echoes stuff he says elsewhere about arbitrary signification and language — or is that just me projecting the metaphysics I already know onto a text that's really more of an economic pamphlet than systematic philosophy? Curious what you make of it, especially since I don't have anyone in my own department who's actually read this thing.
