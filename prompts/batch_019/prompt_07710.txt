I spent thirty-five years as a hydrologist with a state water resources agency, mostly modeling flow regimes in the upper reaches of a river system I came to know rather intimately. Since retiring I have joined a philosophy discussion group that meets monthly at our local library, and last week's session, which was on theories of democratic legitimacy, left me turning over a dispute from my old professional life that I still follow through former colleagues. I suspect it may be an instance of exactly the sort of puzzle we were discussing, though nobody in the group raised it, and I do not yet know the literature well enough to place it with any confidence.

The upstream county's water authority wants to raise an existing dam and expand reservoir storage on a tributary that lies entirely within its own borders. The project would be financed by a bond that only the county's residents will be taxed to retire, and under state law the decision accordingly goes to a referendum of registered voters in that county alone; downstream residents may submit comments during the mandatory environmental review, but that review is purely advisory and does not bind the county board. The trouble is that the reservoir would measurably reduce late-summer flow to two counties downstream, where a modest irrigation economy and an already-stressed native salmon run depend on exactly the flows that would be curtailed, and where nobody, consequently, gets an actual vote. At the discussion group, one member argued that this is simply how jurisdiction has to work: someone has to pay for a piece of infrastructure and someone has to hold formal authority to decide on it, and if "everyone affected" were enfranchised in every local infrastructure vote, decisions would never be bounded at all, since consequences ripple outward without any natural stopping point — especially in a watershed, where nothing upstream is ever fully separable from what happens downstream. Another member thought this came close to begging the question: the downstream counties are, on any reasonable description, the parties whose interests are most at stake in exactly the respect that matters, and handing the entire decision to the upstream county looks like letting whoever bears the smallest share of the cost decide how much of it to impose on whoever bears the largest.

I could not settle between them, and I suspect the disagreement turns on something more principled than local water politics. Is there a defensible way of specifying, in general, who ought to count as a stakeholder in a given collective decision, short of either an arbitrary jurisdictional line or an unworkable requirement to consult everyone whose interests are touched however remotely — and does advisory consultation of the kind the environmental review provides actually resolve anything, or is it just a fig leaf over the same problem? I would welcome your own view, together with whatever you take to be the strongest objection to it. And if this turns out to already be a recognized problem with its own name and literature, I would not mind a pointer, though I would rather have your reasoning than a label.
