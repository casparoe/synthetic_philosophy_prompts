A few of us started an informal "decision theory" club at school this year, partly inspired by some rationalist-community blog posts a senior passed around, and partly because our AP Statistics teacher brought up the Kelly criterion during a unit on expected value. At the last meeting, two members, Theo and Sasha, got into a multi-round disagreement I still haven't been able to resolve, so I wrote down the gist of it afterward.

(1) Theo opened by arguing that when you repeatedly bet or invest and reinvest the proceeds, wealth evolves multiplicatively rather than additively, so what happens to any one person's wealth over the long run is governed by the geometric mean of the round-by-round returns, not by the ordinary (arithmetic) expected value. He noted that someone who instead maximizes plain expected wealth each round ends up betting as aggressively as possible, and will, with probability 1, eventually be wiped out even though every individual bet had positive expected value. He concluded that maximizing the geometric mean -- exactly what Kelly betting does -- isn't just a sensible convention but the objectively rational thing to do whenever wealth compounds this way, full stop. (2) Sasha granted that the underlying math was correct (she'd seen the same point framed as "time averages" diverging from "ensemble averages" for multiplicative processes), but said this only tells you which quantity best tracks one persistent individual's actual trajectory -- it doesn't yet tell you what a rational agent should maximize, since that requires the further assumption that the agent's goal is doing well along its own long-run trajectory in the first place. As a counterexample, she offered a charity receiving a one-time windfall that will never bet again: it should simply maximize the expected size of its payout, no geometric mean required. (3) Theo replied that genuinely one-shot situations like that are rare -- almost every real decision, from personal finances to a firm's revenue, is really one link in a longer compounding chain -- so an unintuitive answer in the rare one-shot case doesn't undermine geometric rationality as the default; if anything, the burden is on Sasha to show a given case really is the exception. (4) Sasha answered that this was fair, but much narrower than Theo's original claim: he'd quietly shifted from "maximizing the geometric mean is what rationality actually consists in for compounding processes" to "it's usually the better default because most situations happen to compound" -- and only the second, weaker claim concedes that plain expected-value maximization is exactly correct whenever a decision truly doesn't compound.
