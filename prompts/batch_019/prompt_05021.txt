In Leviathan, Hobbes considers "the Foole," who denies that justice is binding and argues that breaking a covenant is reasonable whenever you have already secured its benefit and can plausibly avoid detection. Hobbes does not deny that reneging can look advantageous in the moment; his rebuttal is that no one genuinely disposed to break faith when convenient can rationally expect to be trusted again, so such a disposition defeats itself even by the Foole's own self-interested standards. The same logic underlies his earlier treatment of covenants in which one party must perform before the other: absent some guarantee — a common power, a settled reputation — that the second party will follow through, the first party's suspicion is enough, on Hobbes's own account, to void the covenant.

Now take Parfit's hitchhiker, a staple of twentieth-century decision theory that Hobbes obviously never addressed. Dying in the desert, you are rescued only because the driver judges from your evident character that you will pay her once you reach town; ordinary self-interested reasoning says to keep your money the moment the ride is no longer in question, which is precisely the sort of case Parfit has in mind when he argues that it can be rational to be disposed to act "irrationally." Working strictly within Hobbes's own resources, and without appeal to any Kantian duty to keep promises, reconstruct his verdict on whether you ought to pay. Does the driver's ability to read character do the work Hobbes assigns elsewhere to a sovereign or a reputation for justice, so that paying comes out rational on Hobbesian grounds, or does the lack of any actual enforcer mean that, on his own premises, Hobbes must recommend keeping the money? Whichever way he goes, say what that verdict costs his argument against the Foole.
