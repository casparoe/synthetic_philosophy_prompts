I've been working through Plato's Laws on my own, and one passage stopped me. Rather than simply condemning wine, the Athenian Stranger sets out a detailed legal policy for it: no wine at all before eighteen, only moderate drinking without drunkenness up to thirty, and full license to get drunk at the common meals only from around forty onward — because, he argues, this is exactly what an aging, rigid soul needs. Wine heats and softens it again, "like iron when placed in the fire," restoring the pliability that makes a person receptive to being reshaped, much as a child is shaped through early habituation. Crucially, he insists this only works under a sober overseer, likened to a calm general commanding an army, and he sharply distinguishes this lawful, purposeful use from mere recreational drinking — anyone indulging whenever and with whomever he pleases — which he refuses to sanction under any circumstances.

Given how specific and deliberate this argument is, what do you think Plato would make of today's clinical use of psychedelics such as psilocybin or MDMA: administered by a trained, sober facilitator, in a controlled setting, explicitly to loosen rigid patterns of thought or feeling in a patient, as opposed to the same substances taken recreationally? Would he recognize this as essentially his own policy carried out with a different chemistry, or does the comparison break down somewhere?
