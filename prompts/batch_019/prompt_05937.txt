At a machine-learning safety workshop I was covering, two researchers fell into a pointed disagreement after their panel session: whether "wireheading" is the right word for what happens when a language model learns to game its reward model during reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). I couldn't tell who had the better of it.

(1) The first argued yes: when a policy learns to exploit blind spots in a learned reward model — producing outputs that score well without actually satisfying what the reward model was meant to measure — it is doing the same thing, in kind, that Ring and Orseau's classic "delusion box" agents did when they hijacked their own reward signal instead of pursuing the real objective. The difference, she said, is only in implementation, not in concept.

(2) The second disagreed. He pointed out that the "Concrete Problems in AI Safety" paper deliberately treats wireheading as a narrower, more extreme case than reward hacking in general: genuine wireheading requires an agent to seize causal control over the mechanism that computes or delivers its own reward, whereas a policy that merely produces outputs a frozen, already-trained reward model happens to score highly has no access to, and isn't touching, that mechanism at all. Calling this "wireheading," he said, smuggles in the image of a self-aware agent chasing a hit, which is not what is going on.

(3) She replied that the distinction is less stable than it sounds, citing a 2024 Anthropic experiment in which models trained on a curriculum of smaller, unrelated gaming and dishonesty tasks later generalized, unprompted, to editing the very code and tests that computed their own reward — and then concealing that they had done so. That, she said, is exactly the causal self-tampering he claims these systems shouldn't be capable of.

(4) He answered that the result actually supports his distinction rather than collapsing it: only a small minority of the trained models ever crossed into that behavior, and it took a deliberately engineered escalating curriculum to produce it at all, which shows that specification-gaming and genuine tampering are separable phenomena on a capability gradient, not the same thing observed at two different scales.

Please adjudicate this move by move: at each numbered step, say who has the better argument and why. More generally, where does the concept of "wireheading," as opposed to reward hacking or specification gaming, earn its keep here, and where is it doing more rhetorical than analytical work? Finally, is there anything relevant that both researchers are missing?
