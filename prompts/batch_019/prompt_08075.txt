Rationalist writing has a habit of "dissolving" folk-psychological puzzles by swapping in a mechanistic model that leaves the original vocabulary with nothing left to explain. Weakness of will is a natural candidate: on George Ainslie's picoeconomic account, akratic choices are just what a hyperbolic discount curve predicts, since a smaller-sooner reward briefly overtakes a larger-later one in subjective value as it draws near, and a "nearer" momentary self simply prevails over a "farther" one in the resulting intertemporal bargain. This seems to leave nothing for Donald Davidson's classical analysis to do, on which akrasia requires that the agent, at the very moment she acts, still hold the unconditional, all-things-considered judgment that the other course of action would have been better.

Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Davidson's analysis and a behavioral economist defending Ainslie's, giving each side its strongest moves rather than reducing either to a caricature. The philosopher should press the case of the dieter who says "I know I shouldn't" while reaching for the cake, where the earlier judgment seems plainly to persist rather than to have been revised; the economist needs more to offer in reply than a restatement of the discount curve. Neither should be handed a cheap concession: the dialogue should end by pinning down exactly what, if anything, distinguishes "the judgment persisted but was overridden" from "the judgment itself changed" as rival descriptions of the same choice.
