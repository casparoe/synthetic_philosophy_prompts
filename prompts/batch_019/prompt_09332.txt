I spent about thirty-five years doing observational astrophysics -- exoplanet detection, mostly, which meant a career of Bayesian model-fitting on messy radial velocity data -- and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy, currently auditing a formal epistemology course at the university near us because it felt like the closest thing to home turf. This week we got to van Fraassen's Reflection Principle: roughly, if your certain right now what your credence in some claim will be at a later time, rationality requires you to already hold that same credence today. The professor presented it as a serious result, tied up with Dutch-book arguments and basically load-bearing for the whole Bayesian picture of rationality.

Here's my problem with it, which I raised in class and didn't feel I got a satisfying answer to. The principle seems to collapse the moment you imagine anything besides ordinary learning from evidence. Say I know for a fact that tomorrow a cult is going to drug me and I'll wake up completely convinced the moon landing was faked -- by Reflection I'd apparently be required to already believe that today, which is obviously ridiculous. The professor's answer was something like "well, you have to assume the future update is rational," but that felt like it was just assuming away the actual problem rather than solving it, and we ran out of time before I could push back properly. So which is it: is this supposedly foundational principle actually broken by the obvious brainwashing case, or is there a real, principled fix here that I'm just not getting, as opposed to a hand-wave dressed up in jargon? Curious what you think, since none of my old physics colleagues are much use to me on this one.
