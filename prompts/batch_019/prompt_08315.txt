For a while now I've been turning over, on my own, whether the future is already fixed before anything happens — nothing formal, just something that nags at me. From what I can tell, everyone who writes about this traces the puzzle back to one chapter of Aristotle, so I tracked down an old public-domain translation and read it for myself. It's Chapter 9 of De Interpretatione, and the part I keep getting hung up on is this:

"[...] we see that what is going to be has its origin in deliberation and in action, and that, generally speaking, in things which are not continuously actual there is a potentiality in either direction; such things may either be or not be, and events therefore may either take place or not take place. A cloak, for instance, may be cut in two, and yet it may also not be cut in two but wear out first; and in the same way its not being cut in two must also be possible, since its wearing out before being cut in two would not be possible unless its not being cut in two were also possible. [...] One of the two propositions in such cases must be true and the other false, but not determinately this one rather than that one — the matter must be left undecided. A sea-fight, for example, must either take place to-morrow or not take place; yet it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, nor that it should not, though it is necessary that it either should or should not take place to-morrow."

Two things about this have me stuck. First, I can't work out what the cloak is doing there. The sea-fight bit is about whether a statement is true or false; the cloak wearing out instead of being cut up seems to be about what happens to an object, not about statements at all, and I can't see the logical link Aristotle thinks he's drawn between the two. Second, when he says the matter "must be left undecided," I can't tell whether he means the statement about tomorrow's sea-fight is neither true nor false yet, or whether he means it is already true, or already false, just not necessarily so, and we simply can't know which. Those seem like very different claims to me. Could you go through the passage and explain, step by step, how the cloak example is supposed to support the conclusion about the sea-fight, and then settle which of those two readings his own wording actually commits him to?
