Over a long career as a comparative physiologist I used the word "function" every day without ever asking what I meant by it. Only in retirement, reading my way into philosophy of biology, did I discover that this is a genuinely contested question, with two main families of answer. Etiological accounts, associated with Larry Wright and refined by Karen Neander, hold that the function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected by natural selection; causal-role accounts, associated with Robert Cummins, hold that the function of a trait is simply its current causal contribution to some capacity of a containing system we happen to be interested in explaining. I would like you to lay out the substance of this disagreement, and then test it against a case from my own field: the bones that formed the jaw joint in the reptile-like ancestors of mammals, which were, over evolutionary time, repurposed into the ossicles of the mammalian middle ear. The fossil record indicates a protracted transitional period in which these bones plausibly served both the old function, articulating the jaw, and the emerging new one, transmitting sound, at the same time. What does each account say their function was during that in-between stage — does either theory give a non-arbitrary answer, or does the case expose a real weakness in one or both views? Finally, tell me which account you find the more defensible general theory of biological function, and why.
