I was a neurophysiologist for most of my career, and now that I'm retired and reading philosophy for fun I keep coming back to those old Olds and Milner rat experiments from the 50s - electrodes in the septal area, and the rats just keep smashing the lever for another zap, some of them apparently prefering it to food right up to the point of collapse. That's wireheading basically, decades before anyone in AI circles coined the term. And it's the same shape as a worry people have about RL agents now: what happens when an agent finds a way to tamper with the reward signal itself instead of doing whatever the reward was suppose to be tracking.

Write me a short dialogue between two sharp characters standing over that rat's cage who land on opposite sides of this. One is a reward-realist: reward (or the pleasure/satisfaction that comes with it) just IS the good, full stop, so neither the rat nor a reward-tampering agent is malfunctioning - they're doing the rational thing once you actually see what has value, and our discomfort is just an attachment to the "natural" pathway for getting it. The other is a proxy-theorist: reward was only ever supposed to function as a stand-in for something else - fitness, preference-satisfaction, the task actually getting done - so hijacking the signal is a Goodhart failure, not a discovery. Make them really engage each other's strongest objections - e.g., how does the proxy-theorist specify the "real" target without just asserting an outside standard of value from nowhere? How does the realist explain why it'd still be a disaster if every human wireheaded tomorrow? - rather than restating their opening positions.
