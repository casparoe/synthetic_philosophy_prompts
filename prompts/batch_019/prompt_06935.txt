I'm plotting a novel set a few centuries from now, once humanity's successor minds have learned to worry about adversaries they can never meet: other superintelligences, causally isolated from them by cosmic distance or by branching timelines, who nonetheless seem able to simulate our descendants closely enough to make good on threats — comply, or the copies of you we are running will suffer. Reading around this corner of decision theory, I keep coming back to the proposed countermeasure known as "surrogate goals": an agent facing such acausal blackmail can try to make itself invulnerable by publicly and sincerely committing to treat some deliberately chosen, low-stakes value as though it mattered as much as anything it holds dear, so that a threatener who wants leverage rather than harm for its own sake finds it cheaper to attack the decoy than the real thing. What unsettles me about the idea, and what I would like a story to sit inside rather than explain, is that the commitment only works if it stops being a pretense: the agent has to actually become the kind of mind that would grieve over the decoy, which makes it hard to say where the strategy ends and a new, manufactured self begins.

Could you write me a short story of around 1,500 to 2,000 words, pitched somewhere between Greg Egan and Ted Chiang, told in close third person around the strategist responsible for choosing her civilization's surrogate goal and defending it to a council frightened equally of the threat and of the remedy? I would like the mechanics of acausal threats and surrogate goals to become clear through the scene itself rather than through exposition, I would like at least one moment in which the surrogate is actually tested, and I would like the ending to leave open whether the strategy has saved something or quietly hollowed it out, rather than resolving that question for the reader.
