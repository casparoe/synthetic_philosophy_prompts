"The regress problem shows up when we ask what justifies a belief. If belief A is justified by belief B, we can ask what justifies B, and if the answer is belief C, we can ask the same about C, and so on. There seem to be only three ways this can go: the chain goes on forever (infinite regress), it loops back on itself eventually (circularity), or it stops at some belief that doesn't need any further justification (arbitrary foundation). This trilemma goes back to the ancient skeptic Agrippa, preserved for us in Sextus Empiricus, and is sometimes called the Münchhausen trilemma.

Foundationalists take the third horn: they say the regress stops at basic beliefs which don't need to be justified, because they're self-evident, incorrigible, or directly given in experience (Descartes' cogito is the classic example). Everything else in your belief system is justified by ultimately tracing back to these bedrock beliefs, like a building resting on a foundation. The strongest objection is that it's hard to find beliefs that are really infallible in the way classical foundationalism needs -- pretty much anything except 'I exist' or 'I am appeared to redly' seems doubtable -- which is why Chisholm and other modest foundationalists allow basic beliefs to be only prima facie and defeasible rather than certain.

Coherentists deny that justification is a chain at all. Instead a belief is justified by fitting together with the rest of your beliefs into one big, consistent system, the way Neurath describes rebuilding a boat plank by plank while staying afloat, rather than building up from a foundation. So for a coherentist, circularity isn't a problem because justification was never supposed to be linear in the first place -- what matters is that the whole set of beliefs hangs together without contradiction. The strongest objection is the isolation objection: a novel can be totally coherent and consistent internally while having nothing to do with the real world, so coherence alone doesn't obviously get you truth or a connection to reality.

Infinitism, defended by Peter Klein, bites the bullet on the first horn and says the regress can be fine rather than vicious, as long as the chain of reasons never repeats itself and always has a further reason available if you ask for one -- you don't need to have already thought of infinitely many reasons, just be able to produce more on demand. The strongest objection is that this still seems to require infinitely many true propositions waiting to be called on, which is a strange metaphysical commitment, and a regress with no end doesn't obviously justify anything more than stopping arbitrarily would.

Of the three I find modest foundationalism most plausible, since experience has to enter your belief system somewhere, and coherentism has trouble explaining why that entry point should matter if all it cares about is internal fit."
