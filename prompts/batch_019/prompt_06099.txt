For my school's AI ethics elective, we've been going through failure modes in reinforcement learning where an agent's measured reward comes apart from whatever task it was actually supposed to track. Some of these are just "reward hacking" or "specification gaming," like the well-known boat-racing video game where an agent learned to drive in tight circles collecting respawning bonus targets instead of ever finishing the race, exploiting a loophole in how points were counted without touching the scoring mechanism itself. Our teacher distinguished this from the narrower idea of "reward tampering," where an agent instead gets control over the process that generates its reward signal in the first place -- for example, directly editing the variable that stores its score, or manipulating whatever produces its feedback -- and tied both of these to "wireheading": the classic Olds and Milner experiments, where rats with electrodes wired into certain reward pathways of their brains would compulsively press a lever to stimulate themselves, sometimes ignoring food that was sitting right there, and Nozick's experience machine, the thought experiment where most people say they would refuse to plug into a machine that could give them any pleasurable experience they wanted for the rest of their lives.

That discussion turned into an argument at lunch between two people in my class, Derek and Alina, that I've been turning over ever since without reaching a conclusion. (D1) Derek's opening move was that wireheading only looks like a failure from the outside: the rat pressing the lever is, by its own reward signal, doing great, and calling that "bad for the rat" just smuggles in our own preference for survival or achievement over pleasure -- which is exactly what's supposed to be in question. (A1) Alina said this proves too much, since if the rat's compulsive lever-pressing over available food counts as good evidence of what's good for it, the same reasoning would say a heroin addict who keeps using instead of eating is doing what's good for them, which she took to be absurd; she argued the experience machine is the fairer test of what we actually value, because it asks what we'd choose before anything has hijacked our preferences, and most people still say no. (D2) Derek replied that the experience machine is just a thought experiment people answer from an armchair, possibly out of unfamiliarity or plain status-quo bias, whereas the rat's behavior is a real revealed preference under real stakes, so if anything it should count for more than a hypothetical answer, not less. (A2) Alina answered that a preference revealed by direct stimulation of the reward circuit is close to a textbook case of an unreliable preference, which is exactly why we don't treat an addict's choices as the last word on the addict's own well-being -- so the rat case can't be doing the evidential work Derek wants it to do. (D3) Derek's last point was that Alina's line between "authentic" pleasure and "hijacked" pleasure looks arbitrary once you remember that ordinary pleasures from food or sex run through the very same dopaminergic circuitry the electrode is stimulating directly; unless she can say, in a principled way, where legitimate pleasure ends and illegitimate wireheading begins, "authentic" just means "leaves the subject's ordinary goals intact," which quietly assumes those goals matter independently of pleasure -- again, the very thing in question. They both had to leave for class right after that, and Alina only got out that she didn't think all dopamine-mediated pleasure was on a par before we scattered, without time to say why.
