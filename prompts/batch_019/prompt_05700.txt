Since retiring three years ago after a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been spending a good part of my mornings on philosophy, a subject I always regretted not having time for while I was still running a lab. At the moment I am working slowly through Hume's first Enquiry, and Section X, "Of Miracles," has held me up for the better part of a week. A couple of former colleagues, likewise retired and likewise newly smitten with these questions, have been reading along with me, and the three of us could not quite agree on how to parse the passage below. Everyone who has taken even one philosophy course seems to be able to recite Hume's "general maxim" about testimony and miracles from memory, so I do not flatter myself that reproducing it here tells you anything new; what actually holds us up is the sentence or two that immediately follows, where Hume tries to show how the weighing he has just prescribed is supposed to be carried out in a concrete case. The full passage, as I have transcribed it from my edition, reads:

"The plain consequence is (and it is a general maxim worthy of our attention), that no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish: and even in that case there is a mutual destruction of arguments, and the superior only gives us an assurance suitable to that degree of force, which remains, after deducting the inferior. When any one tells me, that he saw a dead man restored to life, I immediately consider with myself, whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened. I weigh the one miracle against the other; and according to the superiority, which I discover, I pronounce my decision, and always reject the greater miracle. If the falsehood of his testimony would be more miraculous, than the event which he relates; then, and not till then, can he pretend to command my belief or opinion."

What none of us can settle is the logical status of the "weighing" Hume describes in that second half, and how it relates to the abstract maxim stated just before it. Trained as I was to think in terms of likelihood ratios and error rates, my first instinct was to read the resurrection example as a rough anticipation of a Bayesian comparison between two hypotheses, honest and accurate testimony against deception or error, with the more probable simply winning out. But Hume's own vocabulary seems to resist that charitable reading: he insists on calling the falsehood of the testimony itself a "miracle," to be placed on the very same scale as the resurrection it is meant to discredit, which strikes me as either a substantive claim about how such probabilities can be compared, or else a rhetorical sleight of hand that makes an already-settled conclusion look like the fair output of a calculation. I understand there is a substantial secondary literature on exactly this worry (Earman's critique is the one that has come my way), but I would rather work through the passage on its own terms before turning to the commentators. Could you give me a close, line-by-line reconstruction of the argument these sentences are making, explain whether the resurrection example genuinely adds anything to the general maxim beyond vivid illustration, and say plainly where you think the weakest inferential step in the passage lies?
