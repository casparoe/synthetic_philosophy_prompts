I've spent the past few weeks reporting a piece on how philosophers of medicine define "disease" -- Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory, which treats health as a value-free matter of statistically normal function, against Lennart Nordenfelt's more normativist, welfare-based alternative -- and I've stumbled into a live version of that argument much closer to home. I sit as the public member on my health plan's medical policy committee, and next month we're re-hearing an appeal that is, on paper, just a coverage dispute, but that I think is really this debate in miniature.

A pediatric endocrinologist has referred a healthy nine-year-old girl for growth hormone therapy, at a cost that could run to several years of daily injections. Every standard workup -- GH levels, bone age, karyotype -- comes back normal; she is simply constitutionally short, tracking well below the first percentile for height, with a predicted adult height several inches under five feet, which is the FDA-labeled indication of "idiopathic short stature." Our medical director denied the claim under policy language limiting coverage to "treatment of disease, illness, or injury," reasoning that a child at the tail of a normal growth distribution isn't diseased, just short -- a position that lines up almost exactly with Boorse's account. The family's appeal, filed with the physician's support, argues the opposite: that whatever the statistics say, what matters is whether she can do the ordinary things expected of a girl her age in her school and social setting, and that by that standard she is already being harmed. They also point out, accurately, that our plan already covers growth hormone for Turner syndrome and for children born small for gestational age -- conditions where the case for calling the shortness a "disease" rather than an unusual but non-pathological endowment is not obviously stronger than hers -- which makes the plan's existing coverage line look less like a principled boundary than an inherited accident.

The committee is genuinely split, and I've been asked to circulate a short written recommendation before we reconvene. Could you draft that memo for me? I'd like it to come down clearly on one of three outcomes -- uphold the denial, overturn it and approve coverage, or approve subject to specific conditions -- with reasoning that the rest of the committee, mostly clinicians and administrators rather than philosophers, could actually apply to the next case that looks like this one, not just to this child. And whichever way it comes down, I want it to take the strongest objection from the losing side seriously, rather than resolving the naturalism-versus-normativism problem by quietly picking a side and moving on.
