So last night I got into it with a friend of mine — shes doing a postdoc in computational neuroscience — over dinner, and it turned into a two hour thing that ended with both of us just repeating ourselves louder. It was about integrated information theory. She was defending it pretty hard, including the part everyone finds weird: that phi is technically nonzero for basically any system with internal causal structure, so on IIT's own terms a photodiode or a simple grid of logic gates has "a little bit" of experience, and that biting that bullet is just what an honest theory of consciousness has to do eventually. I brought up the old Scott Aaronson blog post about how you can build a dumb little circuit out of an expander graph that ends up with more integrated information than an actual human brain, and also the letter from a couple years back where a big pile of consciousness researchers publicly called IIT pseudoscience after the adversarial collaboration with global workspace theory came back kind of a wash for everyone. She thinks the pseudoscience framing is basically sociology dressed up as epistemology, and that what actually matters is the clinical track record — the perturbational-complexity-type measures that came out of the same research program actually work at the bedside.

Which matters to me for a non-argument reason too: I sit on my hospital's neuroethics committee, and next month we're voting on whether to formally fold a phi-inspired complexity measure into our assessment protocol for patients with disorders of consciousness — basically, one more input into the incredibly hard conversations we have with families when someone is behaviorally unresponsive but the measure comes back showing meaningful residual integration. About half the committee sees a validated, quantitative number as exactly the kind of objective evidence that should carry weight in these decisions. The other half thinks its reckless to let a family's hope, or the hospital's liability exposure, hang on a score produced by a theory that, on its own axioms, can't avoid saying weird things about thermostats and logic gates.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? It needs to land on an actual recommendation — adopt the measure, reject it, or adopt it with specific limits and a set date to revisit — not just "more research is needed." And it has to seriously engage with the strongest version of the skeptics' objection instead of just citing the validation studies and moving on, since half the room will see through that immediately.
