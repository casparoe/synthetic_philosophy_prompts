A while back I came across a version of the Allais paradox in a pop-science book on decision-making, and I actually sat down and worked through the choices myself before reading the "correct" analysis. In the first pair, I was offered a guaranteed sum of money against a gamble that had a much bigger prize but also carried a small chance of walking away with nothing, and I took the guaranteed money. In the second pair, both options were already gambles rather than sure things, and there I found myself drawn to the option with the bigger prize, even though it meant a slightly lower chance of winning anything at all. Only afterward did I learn that this exact combination of choices is the textbook example of a violation of the "independence axiom" behind expected utility theory, and that it's usually treated as proof that ordinary people's preferences are, in a technical sense, inconsistent or even irrational.

What bugs me is that when I introspect on why I chose the way I did, it doesn't feel like a mistake I'd want to correct if it were explained to me more slowly — I have something like a considered preference for not gambling away a sure thing, and that preference seems to survive reflection rather than dissolve under it. I mentioned this to a friend who's studied some economics, and she said there's actually live disagreement among decision theorists about whether the independence axiom really is a requirement of rationality, or whether it's possible to build a coherent, exploitation-proof theory of rational choice that permits exactly the pattern I fell into. I don't really understand the details of that disagreement, but I'd like to.

Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp decision theorists standing, so to speak, in front of the two pairs of gambles — one who thinks my choices reveal a genuine failure of rationality on my part (and can back that up with more than just "the math says so"), and one who thinks a rational, reflective agent can consistently choose exactly as I did? I'd like them to directly engage with each other's strongest arguments rather than talking past one another, and I'd rather the dialogue end with the actual crux of their disagreement laid bare than with either side conceding for the sake of a tidy ending.
