I've been kind of falling down a rabbit hole the last couple weeks reading old translations of Gongsun Long, the Warring States-era Chinese philosopher from the "School of Names" - no formal philosophy background here, I just got curious after seeing "a white horse is not a horse" mentioned somewhere as this famous ancient paradox. Once I actually read the White Horse dialogue though, I found out theres a second, less-hyped piece in the same book, the "Discourse on Hard and White," where he argues a hard white stone is actually two separate things, not one (jian bai shi er, "hard, white, stone: two," is the phrase that keeps showing up), because your eyes only ever grab the whiteness and your fingers only ever grab the hardness, so the two qualities apparently go off and "self-hide" (zicang) whenever no one's senses are catching them both together. Basically every summary I can find just lumps both of these under "sophistry" or "the disputers separated hard from white," like its the same party trick played twice on two different objects.

But when I actually try to trace through the logic of each one myself they don't feel like the same move at all to me. The horse argument seems to turn on "horse" naming a shape and "white" naming a color, so gluing the two words together changes what the combined term can pick out - that reads like a semantics-and-naming argument. The stone argument seems to turn on what your senses can and can't verify at the same moment, which then gets used to argue something metaphysical about properties existing independently, off on their own, apart from any object - that feels like a completely different kind of move, more epistemology-sliding-into-metaphysics than logic-of-naming. So is that a real distinction, or am I just forcing a modern Western "logic vs epistemology vs metaphysics" filing cabinet onto two arguments Gongsun Long himself would've seen as one and the same maneuver? I'd love it if you could actually walk through what's doing the argumentative work in each dialogue, side by side, and then just tell me straight which of the two, if either, you think still holds up as a serious philosophical argument today rather than just a clever verbal trick, and why.
