Last night, a friend from my epistemology seminar and I spent nearly two hours arguing about whether pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism are genuinely rival theories of knowledge or just two vocabularies for the same underlying idea. Their position was that since both were developed to accommodate the same wavering intuitions about knowledge claims — our reluctance to say someone "knows" something once enough rides on it — the disagreement between contextualism (in the tradition of Lewis, Cohen, and DeRose) and pragmatic encroachment (as developed by writers like Hawthorne, Stanley, and Fantl and McGrath) is largely terminological: a choice between relativizing the truth-conditions of "knows" to a conversational context or instead rejecting invariantism about knowledge itself, with no real difference in what the two views predict. I disagreed, and tried to make my case with an example designed to pull the two apart: a lab technician reads a routine test result as negative and tells a colleague there's nothing to worry about, basing this on perfectly ordinary evidence of just the kind she relies on every day; unbeknownst to her, a great deal in fact turns on whether she's right, though nothing about the situation ever prompts her to suspect this or to check twice. Now suppose two people, afterward, assess whether she "knew": one is idly discussing the case with no stakes of their own, purely out of curiosity, while the other must decide, solely on the strength of her say-so, whether to act. My friend and I went back and forth about exactly what a contextualist and a pragmatic-encroachment theorist are each committed to saying about the technician's own knowledge and about each of the two attributions, and why — without ever settling it to either of our satisfaction.

I'd like you to succeed where we didn't. Please compare pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism as theories of knowledge, being precise about what each view takes to actually fix the standards governing correct applications of "knows" — facts about the subject's own practical situation, on the one hand, versus facts about the attributor's conversational context, on the other — and then work through the case above to show exactly where, and why, their respective verdicts about the technician and the two attributors can come apart. I'm especially interested in the question my friend and I never resolved: once the case is spelled out this carefully, is the disagreement between the two views substantive, or does it collapse into something merely verbal? Please give a definite answer and defend it.
