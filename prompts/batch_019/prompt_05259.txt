question: "Shewmon's cases of 'chronic brain death' are often brought up as a challenge to the classic justification for equating whole-brain death with the death of the organism. In 2–3 paragraphs: (a) state that classic justification, (b) explain how Shewmon's cases challenge it, and (c) explain how the President's Council on Bioethics tried to save the whole-brain standard without relying on that justification."

answer i'm grading:

"The classic justification (the one the 1981 President's Commission used) is that the brain is the integrator of the body -- its what turns a bunch of organs into a single organism. So once the whole brain (including the brainstem) is gone, the body can't function as a unified whole anymore, even if individual organs are still ticking over, and that's supposed to be why we call it death.

Shewmon's cases are a problem for this because he documented brain-dead patients who kept doing tons of "integrating" stuff for months or even years on a ventilator -- healing wounds, fighting off infections, staying in hormonal balance, and in one case even gestating a fetus almost to term. Terri Schiavo is basically the textbook example here: she was on a ventilator for years in a vegetative state, but her body still regulated its own temperature and digestion the whole time, which is exactly the kind of integration that's not supposed to be possible once the brain is out of the picture. So if integration can clearly continue without a brain, the "integrator" argument just seems to be empirically false.

Since the President's Council basically agreed that Shewmon had a point about integration not needing the brain, they gave up on the integration story and switched the justification for the standard over to consciousness instead -- i.e. what actually makes something a "who" and not just a functioning body is whether its capable of awareness, so losing that is what makes total brain failure equal death, not the loss of integration."
