I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm taking a graduate seminar on social epistemology that has spent the last few weeks on formal models of herding. The result that has stuck with me is how easily rational agents can produce what the literature calls an "information cascade": when people act in sequence and can only observe what earlier people did, not what they privately believed, then once two or three early movers happen to agree, everyone after them can find it individually rational to copy that choice and keep their own contrary evidence to themselves, even though the group as a whole would do better if everyone's private information were pooled openly. What makes the result unsettling is how fragile the resulting consensus is: it can rest on almost no real evidence, just on the accident of who went first, and the same logic scales up to entire research communities, where sharing results too quickly and too completely can make a field lock onto a wrong theory faster than a more fragmented, slower-communicating one would have.

I'm also this year's graduate-student representative on my department's PhD admissions committee, and I think I've been watching a live version of this in our own meetings. We each score applicants independently, but on a shared spreadsheet that updates as we type, and readers enter their scores in an order fixed by who was assigned as a given file's first and second reader; by the time the fourth or fifth person enters a number, they can already see everyone before them. On several files this cycle, the numerical scores converged tightly within the first three entries even though the written comments attached to those same scores pulled in noticeably different directions -- and at least two of those files were ones that people later admitted feeling genuinely unsure about. Our chair has asked each of us to send a short memo before we reconvene in early December to set procedures for next cycle, proposing anything we would like to change.

Would you draft that memo for me? I would like it addressed to the full committee, making one concrete and adoptable recommendation about how we score and discuss files next cycle -- not a general call for "more independence" -- and explaining, in terms colleagues who don't work in formal epistemology would find persuasive, why our current setup is (or, if you conclude otherwise, isn't) producing a genuine cascade rather than legitimate deference to better-informed readers. I would also like the memo to take seriously the strongest case for keeping something close to our current practice: that with dozens of files and limited meeting time, a live shared scoresheet lets us triage quickly, that the first two readers are assigned precisely because they are likely the most qualified to judge a given file, and that blind or anonymous scoring could just as easily hide carelessness as prevent conformity. If, on reflection, you think the problem is real only for a particular kind of file -- borderline ones, say, rather than clear admits or rejects -- I would rather the memo said so specifically than proposed a one-size-fits-all fix.
