I don't have any formal training in philosophy - what I know comes from podcasts, a handful of old journal articles I've tracked down as PDFs, and long-running arguments on a forum I frequent - but I've been stuck for weeks on a question that started as a disagreement with a friend over a simple hypothetical. Imagine you can save exactly one stranger's life, or, in the same amount of time, the lives of five other strangers, but not both, and there's nothing else distinguishing the two options. My friend insisted it's obvious you save the five, since five lives are worth more than one, and that was my instinct too, until I came across an old paper by John Taurek called "Should the Numbers Count?", where he argues that this is actually a mistake. As I understand him, he's denying that the deaths of five people add up to some single, greater quantity of loss than the death of one person, on the grounds that a loss is only ever a loss to the individual who suffers it, and there's no one who suffers the "combined" loss of all five. From what I can tell, he concludes that the fairer response is to give each side an equal chance - something like flipping a coin - rather than automatically saving the greater number.

Around the same time, in unrelated reading, I ran into the "separateness of persons" objection to utilitarianism, usually credited to Rawls and sometimes Nozick, which says utilitarianism is mistaken to add up everybody's welfare as though society itself were one big person feeling one net amount of pleasure or pain, when in fact there are only distinct individuals, none of whom experiences that combined sum. This sounds, on its face, like exactly the worry Taurek has about adding up deaths, and I've seen the two mentioned in the same breath online more than once. But Rawls and Nozick seem to be using the idea mainly to attack utilitarian theories of justice and distribution, not to answer the narrower question of whom to rescue when you can't save everyone - and neither of them, as far as I can tell, ends up recommending anything like a coin flip. So I'm left wondering whether Taurek's argument is basically just a special case of the separateness-of-persons objection applied to rescue cases, or whether it's actually doing something importantly different that happens to sound similar. Could you compare the two carefully: what exactly is each one denying, does accepting the separateness-of-persons point commit you to Taurek's conclusion (or vice versa), and could someone consistently accept one while rejecting the other? I'd also be interested in your own judgment of which argument, if either, gives the stronger reason to doubt that numbers should simply be added up.
