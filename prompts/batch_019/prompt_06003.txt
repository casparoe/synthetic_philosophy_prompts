For a research paper in my AP Seminar class, I've been reading John von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" written not long before his death in 1957. What struck me is that his actual argument is more specific than the "brilliant scientist warns of doom" story his name usually gets attached to: he locates the real danger not in any single invention but in the shrinking amount of time societies have to adjust before the next wave of technology arrives, and he explicitly rejects prohibition or centralized advance planning as the fix, arguing instead for political patience, flexibility, and case-by-case crisis management. Reasoning strictly from that framework, rather than from his general reputation for technological pessimism, how do you think von Neumann would have judged a debate he never lived to see: proposals for solar geoengineering, deliberately cooling the planet by reflecting sunlight back into space? Would his own logic treat this as exactly the kind of unafraid, adaptive response to crisis he favored, or as precisely the sort of fast, hard-to-reverse, planet-scale intervention whose consequences he worried we can no longer fully foresee? Please commit to one reading over the other and defend it, noting which parts of your answer the essay itself actually supports and which parts are your own extrapolation.
