I started a part-time contractor gig about a month ago for a small AI evals outfit, and it's honestly got me rethinking stuff I used to take for granted about how we'd ever check AI output we can't verify ourselves. The job: I'm handed a reading-comprehension question and two AI debaters arguing for opposite answers to it, and I never see the actual source passage — only whatever short verbatim quotes each debater chooses to reveal in support of their claim, plus them cross-examining each other's quotes. I'm the "judge" in what they call a scalable oversight setup, basically a live version of that old AI-safety-via-debate idea, where two models argue it out and a weaker judge (me) is supposed to reliably land on the truth without being able to check the underlying facts directly.

Two of the senior researchers on the team got into a genuine argument about this over lunch, and I haven't stopped turning it over since, because I honestly can't tell who came out ahead. (1) Mia's position was that the mechanism has to work in the limit: since I can always verify a quote is accurate even without reading the whole passage, whichever debater is lying eventually has to either dodge producing a quote or produce one that, checked word for word, doesn't say what they claimed — so dishonesty has a floor it can't sink below. (2) Devon pushed back with what he called the obfuscated arguments problem: the actual support for a claim often isn't in any single quotable line, it's spread across the passage (a pronoun resolved three paragraphs up, a qualifier in one sentence bearing on another), so a dishonest debater can always retreat to "you'd have to read the surrounding context to see why this supports me," and I have no way to tell a genuine irreducible inference from a bluff. (3) Mia said that's exactly what recursive decomposition is for: you don't make me referee the whole inference in one go, you make them keep narrowing the dispute — which earlier sentence fixes the pronoun, which clause the qualifier modifies — until it bottoms out at one line I can just read and check myself, and since the passage is finite this terminates in principle, the same way an interactive proof system gets a weak verifier to trust a claim that took way more computation to produce then to check. (4) Devon said "terminates in principle" is doing a lot of work there, since that guarantee assumes a tireless, error-free verifier following the protocol exactly, and every real study he'd seen showed judge accuracy dropping the more rounds of narrowing were needed — whereas actual me, doing eight of these a day, is nothing like that idealized verifier. (5) Mia said fine, but that's an argument for improving the judge, not for ditching debate, since every oversight method degrades on hard cases, and the only question that matters is whether debate degrades slower than the alternative of just trusting one unchallenged model — which, on everything she's seen, it does. (6) Devon's closer was that "degrades slower right now" and "still works once the debaters are miles smarter than the judge" are different claims, and nothing about the mechanism guarantees the second, since generating an obfuscated argument could get cheaper relative to unraveling it exactly as the capability gap grows, meaning the method could quietly fail right when we're depending on it most.
