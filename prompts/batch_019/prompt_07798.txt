Money pump arguments are supposed to show that having intransitive preferences (like A>B>C>A) is irrational, since someone could trade you around the loop, charging a small fee each time, til you end up poorer but holding the exact same thing you started with. There's a classic objection to this though: if you can foresee the whole sequence of trades coming, you'd just refuse one of them up front, so the pump never actually fires -- meaning it only threatens "naive" choosers, not ones with foresight. Can you unpack how serious this foresight objection actually is, and how McClennen's sophisticated-choice vs resolute-choice responses are each supposed to handle it? Basically I want to know whether money pumps really show rationality has a diachronic dimension -- constraints on choice over time, not just on your preferences at a single instant -- or whether the foresight/resoluteness replies basically give up on that idea.
