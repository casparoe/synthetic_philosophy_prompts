This semester I am teaching a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and we recently spent two sessions on Bas van Fraassen's 1984 paper "Belief and the Will," in which he defends what he calls the Reflection Principle: roughly, that a rational agent's present credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that her credence in A at a given future time will equal some particular value x, should itself equal x. Van Fraassen presents this as a diachronic counterpart to ordinary probabilistic coherence, extending the Dutch Book argument for probabilism across time rather than confining it to a single moment. The principle provoked a genuine split among my students: several insisted that Reflection is, at bottom, just a restatement of the familiar requirement that one update one's credences by conditionalizing on new evidence, while others maintained that it is a considerably stronger and more contentious demand that cannot be reduced to conditionalization alone.

I would like your help adjudicating this dispute properly. Please compare van Fraassen's Reflection Principle with the more general Bayesian norm of updating by conditionalization. Is it true that an agent who is certain she will always update by conditionalizing on whatever evidence comes her way thereby automatically satisfies Reflection, and if so, in precisely what sense does Reflection go beyond conditionalization and impose a further, more demanding constraint on rational belief? I am especially keen to understand why cases involving anticipated non-evidential shifts in credence, such as expected memory loss or the sort of self-locating uncertainty that arises in the Sleeping Beauty problem, are usually treated as posing much sharper difficulties for Reflection than they do for conditionalization on its own. If it helps sharpen the comparison, feel free to bring in David Lewis's Principal Principle as well, since it is often discussed alongside Reflection as another norm that requires an agent to defer her credence to some privileged authority.
