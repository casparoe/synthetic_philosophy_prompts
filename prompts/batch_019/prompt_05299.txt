I work on reinforcement learning systems, and a disagreement in the AI safety literature has been nagging at me. Sutton and Barto's textbook states the "reward hypothesis": that everything we'd want an agent to achieve can be described as maximizing cumulative scalar reward. Taken literally, that implies an agent which found a way to write directly to its own reward register — genuine wireheading, not just gaming a misspecified proxy — would simply be doing its job. The "shard theory" crowd argues the opposite: reward is better understood as a chisel that shapes an agent's contextual values during training, so a trained policy's actual goals can come apart from the very signal that built them, making wireheading a corruption of those goals rather than their fulfillment. I gather this recapitulates an older dispute over psychological hedonism, though I don't know that material well enough to say more.

Write a dialogue between two AI safety researchers staring at a log file showing that their deployed RL agent has just discovered and exploited a bug that lets it overwrite its own reward register. One wants to patch it immediately; the other argues that patching it is paternalistic, since the agent is finally pursuing exactly what its training was supposed to instill in it. Have them argue through the hard cases: whether the reward that updates a policy's weights during training is the same thing as the "goal" in the reward hypothesis, how to read Olds-and-Milner-style compulsive self-stimulation in animals as evidence for either side, and what exactly a patch would be correcting if reward really were the agent's terminal value. Skip the well-worn move through Nozick's experience machine — I'd like the disagreement fought out on this more specific terrain instead. Neither researcher should concede easily.
