**Question (20 points):** In the Seventeenth Discussion of *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*, Al-Ghazālī argues against the philosophers' claim that a cause such as fire necessitates its effect. (a) Reconstruct his argument using the fire-and-cotton example, explaining the role played by the notion of God's "habit" (*al-ʿāda*). (b) Is "occasionalism" an accurate label for the position Al-Ghazālī actually stakes out in this discussion, or does the text leave room for a weaker reading? (c) Briefly state how Ibn Rushd responds to this argument in *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*.

**Student's answer:**

Ghazali's target in the Seventeenth Discussion is the philosophers' assumption that when we say "fire burns cotton," we are describing a necessary connection issuing from fire's own nature. He points out that all we actually observe is that burning regularly follows contact between fire and cotton; we never observe fire's "power" to burn as such, only the sequence itself. Since repeated conjunction is not the same as logical necessity, it remains conceivable that fire could fail to burn something combustible in its presence (as, on the Qur'anic account, it failed to burn Abraham) or that burning could occur without fire at all. Ghazali's own diagnosis is that fire, being inanimate, cannot be an agent in any real sense; the true agent of burning is God, who produces it either directly or through the mediation of angels, and who does so with such regularity that we mistake habit for necessity. This "habit" (al-ʿāda) is simply God's consistent manner of creating events in a fixed sequence, and a miracle is nothing more than a suspension of that habit for a specific prophetic purpose. The argument is structurally close to Hume's later claim that causation is nothing but constant conjunction, with no impression of necessary connection ever given in experience.

Given this, "occasionalism" is the correct and really the only available label for Ghazali's position: the Seventeenth Discussion establishes flatly that no created thing possesses any causal power whatsoever, and that God is the sole real cause of every event without exception. Everything in the discussion is organized around eliminating secondary causation as such, not merely denying that we can prove it a priori.

Ibn Rushd's response, in Tahāfut al-Tahāfut, is that this argument is self-defeating: if things have no fixed natures that necessitate their effects, then no natural science, indeed no reasoning about the world at all, is possible, since induction and demonstration both presuppose that causes reliably produce their effects. For Ibn Rushd this confirms that Ghazali has, in this discussion as throughout his critique of the philosophers, effectively become a fideist, willing to jettison rational argument altogether once it threatens a point of doctrine.
