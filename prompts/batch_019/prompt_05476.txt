I'm about two-thirds through a novel where the narrator keeps telling us she values honesty and simplicity above everything, while literally every choice she makes -- who she dates, which job offer she takes, what she does with a surprise bonus -- points to someone chasing status instead. My writing group loved this "unreliable narrator revealed through actions, not words" trick, but it sent me down a rabbit hole into revealed preference theory in economics, which I'd always taken as just a modeling convenience ("infer what people want from what they choose, since you can't measure feelings directly"). Turns out its actually a live philosophical fight about what "utility" even means. Samuelson's whole point in setting up revealed preference theory was to strip "preference" and "utility" of any leftover psychological content and just define them as whatever consistent pattern shows up in a person's choices, no peeking inside anyone's head required, or even any assumption that there's something in there worth peeking at. But then you've got Sen's old "rational fools" line that this can't even tell sympathy apart from commitment apart from plain self-interest, plus the whole behavioral-economics catalogue of preference reversals, framing effects, and people flat-out regretting choices they made five minutes earlier -- and Kahneman's split between "decision utility" (what your choices reveal) and "experienced utility" (how good it actually felt) seems to say the two can come apart completely.

This is bugging me way more than it should for someone who's writing fiction, not econ papers, so I'd love your help settling it instead of just narrating both sides at me. Write me an essay on the question: is utility, at bottom, nothing more than a label for whatever consistent choice pattern a person exhibits (so that my narrator's "true" preference for status just IS what her choices reveal, full stop, and the honesty talk is empty self-narrative) -- or does "utility"/well-being require some independent psychological fact that choices are only ever noisy evidence for, meaning a purely choice-based theory of utility can't be the whole story? Go through the strongest arguments on each side, but don't leave me with an "it's complicated" shrug at the end -- tell me which view is actually right, or exactly under what conditions one beats the other, and argue for it the way you would with a friend who's going to push back over drinks, not the way you'd write a textbook glossary entry.
