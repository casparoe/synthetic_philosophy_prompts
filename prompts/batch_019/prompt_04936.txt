Okay so this idea has been living in my head for weeks now. I don't have any background in logic or CS, I'm just someone who fell down an AI-safety rabbit hole on some forums a while back, and I keep bumping into this thing people call the "Löbian obstacle" (hope I'm even spelling that right). From what I can piece together, there's a theorem, Löb's theorem I think, that says a formal system basically can't get away with assuming "anything I can prove must be true" as a general rule about itself — if it tried to prove that as a blanket fact, it'd end up able to prove literally anything, which breaks the whole system. And apparently this causes real trouble if you want an AI that's meant to design, or grow into, a smarter/updated version of itself and actually trust that successor's reasoning — you can't just have the original AI formally verify "whatever my successor proves is correct" the clean way you'd hope for.

Me and this guy from one of the forums argued about it for like two hours last night and got nowhere. He thinks this basically proves that any "safe" self-improving AI is a pipe dream unless somebody finds an actual proof of successor-soundness, full stop, and that anyone who says otherwise is just hand-waving away a real impossibility result. I think thats backwards — I think the theorem is exactly why demanding that specific kind of proof is the wrong ask to begin with, and that there's gotta be other legitimate ways an agent could extend trust to a successor without that guarantee, even if its messier and less certain than a clean proof. Neither of us could really make the other's case for them. Especially, I want whoever argues my side to actually have to explain what the alternative to proof-based trust looks like, not just insist that one exists.
