Okay so in my Minds and Machines seminar this week we read Turings original 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," and the part that's been stuck in my head isn't the imitation game setup, it's the objection near the end that's literally titled "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception." Turing does not brush this off as a joke - he says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," lists precognition and psychokinesis as things a serious thinker has to reckon with, and even proposes that the human and machine be tested in a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic interrogator can't unfairly win the game. A few of us were arguing about this after class because it seems so at odds with how careful and mechanistic Turing is literally everywhere else in the paper, and our professor said this section basically gets deleted from every intro-to-AI syllabus because its embarrassing.

For my response paper I want to push past the obvious "haha Turing believed in ESP" take and actually think about what he'd say if he could see how this all played out. He died in 1954, decades before things like Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" study, which passed peer review in a mainstream psychology journal with seemingly solid effect sizes claiming evidence of precognition, then completely fell apart under replication attempts and ended up being one of the flashpoints that triggered psychology's whole replication crisis and the push toward preregistration. Given that Turing was already convinced by parapsychology data that was arguably weaker and shakier than what Bem had, what do you think he'd conclude if he saw the full Bem saga plus everything we now know about how these effects tend to vanish under scrutiny? Would he treat it as a straightforward refutation and just drop the ESP objection (and the telepathy-proof room fix) entirely, or is there something in his own reasoning - about induction, about what counts as "overwhelming" evidence, about how much weight a rigorous empiricist should give anomalous statistics before revising deeply held assumptions about physics - that's actually more resilient to this kind of update than it looks? Not looking for a "gotcha" answer, genuinely just curious where you'd land on this.
