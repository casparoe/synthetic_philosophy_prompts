The question was:

"Frege's puzzle asks how a true identity statement like 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' can be informative, given that 'Hesperus is Hesperus' is not. (a) State Frege's own solution to this puzzle. (b) Explain how a direct-reference (Millian) theorist, who denies that ordinary proper names have Fregean sense, must instead account for the same datum. (c) Does the strategy you describe in (b) ultimately collapse back into something like Frege's own solution? Defend your answer."

The student's answer was:

"(a) Frege distinguishes a name's sense (Sinn), the mode of presentation under which its referent is given, from its reference (Bedeutung), the object itself. 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' share a reference -- the planet Venus -- but express different senses, roughly the descriptions 'the star visible in the evening' and 'the star visible in the morning.' Since the thought expressed by 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' combines these two distinct senses, it differs from the trivial thought expressed by 'Hesperus is Hesperus,' which is why the former can be informative and knowable only a posteriori while the latter is knowable a priori.

(b) A direct-reference theorist such as Kripke denies that ordinary proper names have this kind of sense: a name refers to its bearer directly, in virtue of a causal-historical chain leading back to an initial act of naming, rather than via any associated description. Because the name contributes nothing but its bearer to the proposition expressed, 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' and 'Hesperus is Hesperus' express exactly the same proposition, namely that Venus is Venus. Kripke's causal theory therefore predicts, correctly, that both sentences are necessarily true, and treats their difference in apparent informativeness as a merely psychological fact about speakers' epistemic access to the name-referent links, rather than as any semantic difference between the sentences themselves.

(c) I don't think this collapses back into Frege's picture, since the causal theory locates the explanatory work in the external history of a name's use rather than in any descriptive content graspable by the speaker; nothing here plays the role of a Fregean sense that determines reference."
