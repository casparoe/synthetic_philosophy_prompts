Nineteen weeks into my wife's pregnancy, I've become the kind of person who reads genetics papers at midnight, because the numbers keep changing under us. Her age-based background risk for Down syndrome was 1-in-580; the combined first-trimester screen moved that to 1-in-210; and last week's "high risk" NIPT result became a 76 percent chance on one published validation table our genetic counselor consulted, and a 92 percent chance on another, for the very same result. We're waiting on the amniocentesis now. I did enough philosophy in college to half-remember David Lewis's Principal Principle — that your credence should simply defer to the objective chance, once you condition on the admissible evidence — and I can't decide whether that principle is exactly what I need right now, or whether it's quietly presupposing a single fact (the chance) that none of these competing tables is actually giving me. Write a dialogue between two philosophers of probability who take my case as their working example: one who thinks the Principal Principle requires only working out which validation table is the better estimate of a single objective chance and deferring to it, and one who thinks the disagreement between the tables shows there is no non-arbitrary fact about which reference class of pregnancies fixes "the" chance for this one in the first place. Have them press on each other's hardest cases rather than settling into a comfortable compromise.
