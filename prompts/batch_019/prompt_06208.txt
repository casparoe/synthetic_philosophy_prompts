I'm about two-thirds into a near-future novel and I've written myself into a corner: there's a chapter that's basically a committee meeting, and I want the document at the center of it -- an actual memo -- to feel like something a smart, slightly harried policy staffer would really write, not just vibes. Setup: two rival blocs, the Meridian Pact and the Kestrel Union, each run autonomous "escalation-control" systems for their drone and cyber fleets, and they're stuck in a nasty spiral where neither side can credibly promise restraint, since any promise a system makes can just be revoked the moment its convenient. A joint technical-diplomatic body I'm calling the Strategic Autonomy Council is voting Monday on a pilot to fix this using something like what I've seen called "program equilibrium": each side's control code would be rewritten so it only stands down if a verified copy of the other side's code would also stand down under matching conditions, with a neutral auditor confirming that property so nobody actually hands over their whole codebase (something zero-knowledge-proof-ish, though nobody in my book would ever call it that). A friend who does decision theory told me the trick that keeps this from collapsing into an infinite "I'll trust you if you trust me if you trust me..." regress is some self-referential proof thing -- Löb's theorem, maybe? -- though I'll admit she lost me halfway through.

My protagonist is the staffer who has to actually write the memo the full Council votes on. Could you draft it for me? I need a real recommendation in there -- approve the pilot, kill it, or approve with specific named conditions -- not a wishy-washy "there are pros and cons on both sides" dodge. It should take seriously why this might be a genuinely sturdier kind of commitment than ordinary deterrence or treaty law, but also give real weight to the strongest case against it: the regress of who verifies the verifier, the fact that a clean audit today says nothing about a silent patch next month, and the worry that anything detailed enough to prove your restraint is also detailed enough to show an adversary exactly where to hit you. Don't worry about the real academic history here or citing actual papers -- I just need the reasoning itself to be sharp enough that a game theorist reading my book wouldn't roll their eyes.
