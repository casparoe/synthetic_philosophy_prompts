I am several drafts into a novel set on two habitable planets, Earth and a double I have been calling Mirror-Earth, whose populations turn out, through a plot twist late in the book, to be exact physical and psychological duplicates of one another—except for one thing: the clear liquid that fills Mirror-Earth's oceans, while indistinguishable to the naked eye, nose, and tongue from ordinary water, is chemically a different compound. I have more or less lifted this device from Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, and I like how it lets me suggest, in a pivotal scene, that two duplicate characters who have spent their whole lives thinking qualitatively identical thoughts about "water" have in fact never been thinking about the same substance, simply because of a difference in their surroundings that neither was ever in a position to detect. A friend of mine who did graduate work in philosophy of language recently complicated my plans, though: she pointed out that Tyler Burge's arthritis case, from his paper "Individualism and the Mental," seems to reach a similar conclusion about the mind depending on the world beyond it, without needing two planets, two compounds, or even two distinct physical environments at all—on her telling, Burge gets his result just by varying the linguistic habits of a single community while holding everything physical fixed.

Before I decide how to use any of this in the book, I would like your help thinking the two thought experiments through side by side. Concretely, I am tempted to add a scene in which two of my duplicate characters, despite inhabiting chemically and physically identical worlds, belong to communities that use some everyday term for a common ailment in subtly different ways, such that one twin's word for it picks out a narrower range of conditions than the other's, even though neither has personally encountered a case that would ever have revealed the discrepancy to him. Would layering a Burge-style scenario on top of my existing Putnam-style one be philosophically coherent, or would one of the two forms of externalism end up doing all the real work while the other became redundant, or even incompatible with it, once both were supposed to hold at once? More generally, I would like you to compare the two thought experiments on their own terms: what precisely is the difference between the kind of externalism about mental content that Twin Earth is meant to establish and the kind the arthritis case establishes, is one plausibly more fundamental or more radical than the other, and does either leave room for a coherent notion of purely "narrow," internally individuated mental content once its lesson is taken on board?
