Question (worth 20 points): In 2016, ProPublica argued that Northpointe's COMPAS recidivism-risk tool was biased against Black defendants because it had a higher false-positive rate for Black defendants and a higher false-negative rate for white defendants. Northpointe countered that COMPAS was fair because its scores were well-calibrated across race: among defendants who received the same score, the actual reoffense rate was roughly equal for Black and white defendants. Drawing on the relevant impossibility result from the algorithmic-fairness literature, explain (a) how both organizations could be making accurate claims about the very same tool, and (b) the condition(s) under which every fairness criterion at stake could have been jointly satisfied. Name the specific criteria in tension and cite the result you're relying on.

Student's answer: ProPublica and Northpointe were both right, but they were measuring different things. ProPublica showed that among people who did not reoffend, Black defendants were much more likely than white defendants to have been scored high-risk, and among people who did reoffend, white defendants were more likely to have been scored low-risk. In other words, COMPAS did not satisfy equalized odds (also called equal opportunity): the error rates were not balanced across race. Northpointe's defense was that COMPAS was calibrated: within each risk-score bucket, the fraction of people who actually went on to reoffend was roughly the same for Black and white defendants. This is sometimes called predictive parity.

The reason both of these can hold at once is that Black and white defendants in the dataset had different base rates of recidivism. Dwork et al. proved in their landmark 2012 fairness paper that calibration and equalized odds cannot both be satisfied when base rates differ between groups, except in the degenerate case where the classifier predicts perfectly. So there is no way to "fix" COMPAS to satisfy both notions of fairness without either treating the unequal base rates themselves as evidence of bias upstream of the algorithm, or simply abandoning one of the two criteria. The theorem's own escape clause is that if the base rates for the two groups are equal, or the classifier is a perfect predictor, the tension disappears and every criterion can be satisfied together.
