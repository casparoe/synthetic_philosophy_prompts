For a term paper in my school's Enlightenment political philosophy elective, I have been reading Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre*, and one part of his argument still puzzles me. Rousseau insists that Geneva must not build a theatre because doing so would corrode the city's republican virtue, yet he treats the many theatres of Paris as comparatively harmless, even suggesting that shutting them down would do little to make Parisians any more virtuous than they already are. This seems to imply that the very same institution is dangerous only in a society that still has civic virtue left to lose, and largely inert once a society is already corrupt. Could you walk me through the actual mechanism behind this asymmetry — why Rousseau thinks the moral effect of theatre depends so heavily on the character already prevailing in a given society, rather than being uniformly harmful or harmless? I'm also trying to work out whether this is a genuinely defensible position, or more of a special exception Rousseau carves out for Geneva.
