For thirty-four years I ran a physiology lab studying hypothalamic control of osmoregulation and the stress axis in rodents, and it's only since retiring that I've started reading philosophy in any systematic way, prompted in part by a philosophy anthology a former postdoc gave me as a retirement present. I've stayed on as an emeritus member of my medical center's clinical ethics committee, which invites an outside speaker each spring for a public lecture and moderated Q&A, and I've been asked to open the questioning this year. Our guest is a bioethicist who has built much of her reputation defending the whole-brain standard of death — the one written into the Uniform Determination of Death Act, which requires irreversible cessation of "all functions of the entire brain" — against the usual battery of counterexamples; the debate isn't merely academic for our committee, since the hospital's own organ-procurement protocol rests on exactly the standard she defends. Her signature move, as far as I can tell from her published work, is to concede that patients who remain warm, healing, and hormonally active for months or years after a whole-brain-death diagnosis are still "integrated," while denying that the integration is theirs to claim: she calls it externally imposed rather than self-generated, and holds that only self-integration marks a living organism.

I'd like your help drafting eight or nine questions for the Q&A that don't let her simply restate that self/other-integration distinction, but force her to apply it, case by case, to the specific findings that made me suspicious of it before I had read a word of the philosophical literature: the fact that something like half of patients diagnosed dead by whole-brain criteria retain measurable hypothalamic-pituitary function, such as intact antidiuretic hormone secretion, which seems as much a function of "the entire brain" as anything else; the documented cases of brain-dead pregnant women maintained on support until their fetuses reach viability; and the case of Jahi McMath, maintained for more than four years after a whole-brain-death diagnosis, who, according to published clinical accounts, went through puberty during that interval. For each of those three, I want a question that makes her say plainly whether the phenomenon counts as self-integration or not, plus a follow-up keyed to whichever answer she's likely to give — for instance, if she treats the McMath case as a simple misdiagnosis, the follow-up should press on how such an error could go undetected for four years while everyone around the patient kept treating the original criteria as reliable.

Please order the sequence so the self/other-integration distinction gets harder to apply cleanly as the questions go on, not easier, and close with a direct question asking what specific finding, if any, she would accept as genuine evidence against the whole-brain standard rather than as one more phenomenon to be redescribed as non-self-integration. I want to avoid anything generic about why she entered bioethics or what she finds meaningful about the work — these should be questions that could only come from someone who has actually sat with the empirical mess, not just the general shape of the debate.
