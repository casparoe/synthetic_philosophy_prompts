Q3. Epicurus argues that death is "nothing to us." Lucretius, writing within Epicurus's tradition, adds a further consideration involving the vast stretch of time before we were born.
(a) [8 pts] Reconstruct Epicurus's argument and Lucretius's argument as two distinct arguments. Be precise about what premise(s) each one needs and what work each premise is doing — don't just gesture at "death is nothing to us" as if it were their one shared conclusion and stop there.
(b) [7 pts] Explain how Nagel's deprivation account is meant to solve the "problem of the subject of harm" that motivates Epicurus's argument in the first place. Then answer directly: does Nagel's account, just by itself, give the deprivation theorist the resources to block Lucretius's argument? Why or why not?
(c) [5 pts] Bernard Williams also discusses immortality in this general neighborhood of the debate. Is his argument best understood as a version of the Epicurean view, or is it doing something importantly different? Explain.

And here's the answer as turned in (I've kept the student's own paragraph breaks):

(a) Epicurus's argument turns on the idea that badness has to be bad *for* someone, and being the subject of a harm requires existing. Since death is the permanent end of sensation (Epicurus is a materialist about the soul — it's just atoms that disperse at death), there's no time at which the harm of death and a subject who could suffer it ever coincide: while we're alive, death hasn't happened yet, and once it happens we no longer exist to be its victim. So death can't be bad for the person who dies.

Lucretius's argument works differently: he points out that none of us are bothered by the eternity of time before we were born, even though it's just as much a stretch of non-existence as the time after we die. Since the two stretches are symmetrical, and we agree the "before" stretch isn't bad for us, consistency requires saying the "after" stretch isn't bad either. Really, though, this just is Epicurus's argument wearing a different hat: both arguments are ultimately powered by the same thought, that there's no subject present in either period of non-existence to be on the receiving end of any harm, so in neither case is anyone made worse off.

(b) Nagel's move is to reject the assumption that everything good or bad for a person has to be an intrinsic state that person is in at some time. He thinks some misfortunes are comparative — you can be harmed by a gap between what actually happens to you and what would otherwise have happened, even if there's no moment at which you're in a bad state (his stock examples involve things like betrayals the victim never finds out about). Applied to death: dying is bad for you because of the goods you would have had if you'd lived, compared against the zero you actually get, and this doesn't require that you exist at the time of the comparison. That's Nagel rejecting what people call the "existence requirement" — the idea that you have to exist when something is bad for you in order for it to be bad for you.

Once you've dropped the existence requirement, though, Lucretius stops being a problem: if a person doesn't need to exist at the relevant time to be the one who's harmed, it no longer matters that we didn't exist before birth either, and the deprivation theorist can just say both stretches of non-existence are bad for us, symmetrically. That's a perfectly consistent position to hold, so Nagel's account does, by itself, block Lucretius — it just does it by accepting the symmetry rather than denying it.

(c) Williams isn't running Epicurus's argument again. Using the Makropulos case (a woman who takes an elixir and lives to 342, ending up bored, cold, and checked out), he argues that living indefinitely would eventually be bad for us, because a fixed character can only sustain so many "categorical desires" — desires that give you a reason to go on living, rather than ones that presuppose you're already going to be around — before they're exhausted, or before you'd have to change so much to keep generating new ones that it's not clear the person left at the end is still you. So Williams's target is the desirability of endless life, not the claim that death itself is never bad; his conclusion is actually compatible with thinking an early death is still a real loss, which is a pretty different, and in some ways opposed, conclusion from Epicurus's.
