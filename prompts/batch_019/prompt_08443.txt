Here's the setup, roughly: everyone agrees that if you believe P while ALSO believing "my evidence doesn't support P," that's a weird state to be in — its basically the epistemic cousin of Moore's paradox, or of practical akrasia (doing X while judging you shouldn't). The classic cases that motivate this are things like Christensen's drug/impairment examples: you do a careful calculation and feel confident in the answer, then a friend you trust tells you that you were secretly given a drug, known to make people massively overconfident in exactly this kind of reasoning, while making them feel totally normal about it. The question is what you should believe about your original conclusion once you get that higher-order evidence. The "Non-Akrasia" camp (I'm thinking of Sophie Horowitz's paper here, though I might be fuzzy on the exact title) says rationality flat out forbids ending up akratic — if your higher-order evidence undermines P, you actually have to lower confidence in P, not just tack on a hedge. But then there's a "level-splitting" camp — I think this is basically what Worsnip is doing in his evidence-and-coherence paper — who are fine with coherence sometimes just losing: they argue that being coherent (not akratic) and getting-it-right-given-your-evidence are two different demands that can conflict, and when they do, sometimes the rational thing really IS to be akratic, because caving to the higher-order evidence would mean ignoring excellent first-order evidence.
