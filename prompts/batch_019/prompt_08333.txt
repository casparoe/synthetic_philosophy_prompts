I've been reading through the Sequences and assorted LessWrong-adjacent material on Solomonoff induction for years, and it keeps getting invoked as the definitive mathematical vindication of Occam's razor. The standard pitch goes roughly like this: assign each computable hypothesis a prior weight of 2 to the negative length of its shortest generating program, update via Bayes' rule as evidence comes in, and Solomonoff's convergence theorem then guarantees that your predictions will rapidly converge to the truth. In a reading-group discussion of this material last month, someone pushed the argument a step further, pointing out that the invariance theorem shows the choice of universal Turing machine only affects Kolmogorov complexity up to a fixed additive constant, and concluding that the resulting simplicity ranking is therefore essentially language-independent and objective. On this view, the combination of these two results effectively promotes Occam's razor from a pragmatic rule of thumb to a proven theorem, and Hume's problem of induction is basically dissolved: we no longer need to simply assume that the future will resemble the past, since Solomonoff induction supplies a formal guarantee that weighting hypotheses by simplicity actually works.

I found this fairly persuasive in the moment, but it has been nagging at me since, so I would like a sanity check before I repeat it at our next meetup. Does Solomonoff induction really elevate Occam's razor to the status of a proven theorem, and does its convergence guarantee actually solve, rather than merely relocate, Hume's problem of induction? If the argument above overreaches, I would appreciate a careful account of exactly where, whether that has to do with the fact that Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, with what the convergence theorem does and doesn't presuppose about the environment being predicted, with how much work that "additive constant" from the invariance theorem is quietly doing, or with something else I'm not seeing.
