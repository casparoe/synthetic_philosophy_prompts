Two rival principles govern how one should update on purely self-locating evidence: facts about which observer, among various possible observers, one is.
SSA (Self-Sampling Assumption): reason as if you were a random sample from the class of all actually existent observers (past, present, and future) in your reference class.
SIA (Self-Indication Assumption): reason as if you were a random sample from the class of all possible observers; equivalently, weight hypotheses in proportion to the number of observers each predicts.

(a) Explain how SSA alone generates the Carter-Leslie Doomsday Argument's shift in credence toward "doom soon" hypotheses.
(b) Explain why supplementing SSA with SIA is typically thought to neutralize this shift.
(c) Reconstruct Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment and state precisely what it is meant to show, and about which of the two principles.

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

(a) The Doomsday Argument treats my birth rank as a random draw from the reference class of all humans who will ever be born, per SSA. If humanity is destined to grow very large, a birth rank as low as mine (only ~100 billion humans so far) would be a very atypical draw -- I'd almost certainly have a much higher rank. Finding myself this early is therefore evidence favoring hypotheses on which the total number of humans is smaller, i.e., it shifts credence toward humanity ending relatively soon.

(b) SIA adds a further weighting: hypotheses on which more observers exist are, prior to learning your rank, proportionally more likely to contain you at all. This prior boost for large-population hypotheses is exactly the same size as the SSA penalty those hypotheses incur from your low rank, so the two effects cancel and no net doomsday shift survives.

(c) In the presumptuous philosopher case, physicists are choosing between two cosmological theories that fit the data equally well but imply vastly different numbers of observers in the universe. Before they run an expensive experiment to decide, a philosopher announces that no experiment is needed: whichever theory implies more observers is, by that fact alone, overwhelmingly more probable. This is generally taken to be a reductio, and it trades on exactly the same weak point as the Doomsday Argument itself: both arguments let the size of the reference class do all the evidential work, so the real lesson is that any reference-class-relative principle -- and SSA is the paradigm case of a reference-class-relative principle -- will license this kind of a priori cosmology if pushed far enough.
