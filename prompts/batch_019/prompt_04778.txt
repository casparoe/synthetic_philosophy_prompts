I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language. A few of us in the department run a weekly reading group on theories of reference and propositional attitude reports, and this term we worked through Frege's original puzzle about identity statements — why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" seems to report a genuine astronomical discovery while "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial, even though, on a purely referential view of names, both sentences say the same thing about the same planet — before moving on to Kripke's "A Puzzle about Belief" and its Paderewski case: a speaker encounters the name "Paderewski" twice, once in connection with a celebrated concert pianist and once in connection with a Polish statesman, never learns that these are one and the same man, and ends up sincerely assenting to both "Paderewski has musical talent" and "Paderewski has no musical talent."

At our last meeting this produced a real split, and it's still bothering me. The Millians among us wanted to say that both sentences express one and the same, indeed flatly contradictory, singular proposition about the one real Paderewski, and that the speaker's evident rationality has to be located not in the semantics but in the fact that he believes that proposition under one guise, fixed by the concert-hall encounter, while failing to believe it (or believing its negation) under another guise, fixed by the newspaper encounter. A few committed Fregeans were unimpressed, on the grounds that this "guise" talk simply reintroduces Frege's apparatus of sense under a new label, while discarding the one thing that made sense theoretically respectable in the first place: that senses were supposed to be constrained by facts about linguistic meaning and competence, not stipulated ad hoc wherever a substitution puzzle happens to arise. The Millians' comeback was that the Fregean can only handle Paderewski at all by positing a sense so thin — something like "whoever I am now calling by this name" — that it does none of the explanatory work Frege originally wanted sense to do, so that if this is what rescues Fregeanism, the two camps have simply converged on the same posit under different names, and the Fregean has quietly conceded the dispute rather than won it.

I would like to see this argued out properly rather than just gestured at, as we did at the meeting. Could you write a dialogue between a Fregean and a Millian, equally sharp and equally well read, who work through exactly this exchange starting from the Paderewski case? I'd like each side to engage with the specific objection the other has just made rather than restate their own view past one another, to press each other on the weakest point in the opposing account (what individuates a guise, if not something sense-like; whether a sense thin enough to handle Paderewski is still doing any of the work a sense is supposed to do), and, if the exchange genuinely reaches a standoff or exposes a cost neither view can avoid, I would like that to come through rather than have one side simply out-argue the other by the end.
