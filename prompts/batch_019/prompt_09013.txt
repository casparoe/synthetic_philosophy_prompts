During my career as a field ecologist, I spent a good deal of time estimating fitness differentials in wild populations. Now that I am reading philosophy of science in retirement, I was surprised to learn that Karl Popper spent decades regarding natural selection as little more than a tautology -- something closer to a metaphysical research programme than a genuinely testable theory -- before publicly recanting that view late in his career. I would like a careful account of the logical structure of his original objection: is the worry simply that "the fittest survive" collapses into "the survivors survive" once fitness is defined in terms of differential reproduction, or is there a subtler problem about specifying fitness and environment jointly without circularity? And when Popper changed his mind, did he identify a genuine flaw in that argument, or did he merely redraw the boundary between the testable and the metaphysical, conceding that the overarching principle remains metaphysical while granting that its specific applications are testable? I would also value your assessment of whether the propensity interpretation of fitness used in population genetics genuinely dissolves the circularity worry, since that was never obvious to me from the field end of things.
