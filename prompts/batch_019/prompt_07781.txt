In a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, we spent two sessions on Donald Davidson's essays on radical interpretation and truth-conditional semantics ("Radical Interpretation," "Belief and the Basis of Meaning," and "A Coherence Theory of Truth and Knowledge" were among the assigned readings). Toward the end of the second session, while the professor was fielding scattered questions, two classmates, Sofia and Marcus, fell into an extended disagreement about whether Davidson's machinery actually delivers what he claims for it: a refutation of the very possibility of global, Cartesian-style skepticism. Neither conceded anything before class ended, and I'd like a more careful adjudication than they managed to give each other in the moment.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Sofia argued that since a radical interpreter can only assign meanings to a speaker's utterances by simultaneously attributing beliefs, and can only do that by applying the principle of charity — treating the speaker's beliefs as mostly true and reasonably coherent — it follows that any creature capable of being interpreted at all must have mostly true beliefs; since this applies to all of us in principle, the Cartesian picture of wholesale, pervasive error is not merely unlikely but strictly incoherent. (2) Marcus objected that this only shows beliefs must come out mostly true relative to whatever the interpreter already believes, since charity works by projecting the interpreter's own view onto the speaker wherever the evidence permits it; at most this rules out one person's beliefs diverging wildly from a shared scheme, not that the shared scheme itself latches onto an independently existing world. (3) Sofia replied that this ignores triangulation: on Davidson's later view, shared belief is anchored not just to other believers but to a common external cause that reliably triggers matching responses in two or more creatures, so agreement is not mere coherence among minds but is tied to a real, shared object in the world — which is exactly what should rule out systematic error. (4) Marcus answered that triangulation secures reference, not truth: it fixes what a term is about by picking out whatever reliably causes the shared response, but says nothing about whether the substantive beliefs attached to that object are correct. Ancient astronomers' word for "the sun," he said, was perfectly well triangulated onto the sun even though a great deal of what they believed about it was false, so triangulated content and massive substantive error are compatible after all. (5) Sofia's rejoinder was that Davidson never needed "no false beliefs" in the first place, only "mostly true," and that isolated errors like geocentrism are entirely consistent with that; the argument still blocks the more radical possibility a skeptic actually trades on, such as there being no external world at all behind one's beliefs. (6) Marcus's last word was that "mostly" is doing unearned work: a perfectly coherent, thoroughly triangulated set of beliefs generated inside a sufficiently rich simulation, or shared by envatted brains fed matching signals, would satisfy Davidson's charity and triangulation conditions just as well as beliefs about the real world would, so nothing in the argument favors the realist option over a well-run illusion.

Please go through this move by move — Sofia-1 through Marcus-3, in order — and for each one say whether it's Correct, Partially right, or Mistaken as an interpretation and use of Davidson's actual views, with two or three sentences of justification per move; don't just average things out into an overall score. Then add a closing paragraph giving your own considered judgment on the substantive question dividing them — does the combination of radical interpretation and triangulation give Davidson a genuine argument against global skepticism, or does it, as Marcus contends, secure at most a kind of interpreter-relative agreement that leaves the deeper skeptical worry exactly where it was? — and note any consideration you think both Sofia and Marcus are overlooking.
