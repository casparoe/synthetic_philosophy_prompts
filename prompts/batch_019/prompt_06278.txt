I spent almost forty years as an analytical chemist (mostly mass spec, with a stint doing x-ray crystallography toward the end), and since retiring a couple years ago I've been trying to fill the gap with philosophy, which uses a completely different part of the brain than I'm used to. A few old colleagues and I started an informal reading group, mostly as an excuse to keep having coffee together, and we just spent two whole meetings stuck on Nagarjuna, working through Jay Garfield's translation of the Mulamadhyamakakarika. Everyone else seemed happy to nod along and move on to the next chapter, but I can't stop chewing on one particular move he keeps making.

The thing that's bugging me is the tetralemma (catuskoti, I think its called in Sanskrit) that shows up all over the text - like in the chapter on the Tathagata, or the one on nirvana, where he asks whether the Buddha exists after death, doesn't exist, both, or neither, and then seems to reject all four answers rather than settle on one. He does basically the same move right at the start with causation (nothing arises from itself, from another, from both, or from no cause at all), and then, later, weirdly, with emptiness itself - he wont even let you assert "everything is empty" as a plain fourfold claim. As a chemist this drives me a little nuts, because "not A, not not-A, not both, not neither" looks to me like it's just breaking excluded middle and non-contradiction in one move, and my gut instinct is that a claim which rules out all four options has to be either meaningless or some kind of sleight of hand. Two people in the group had pretty different takes: one said it's basically a real non-classical "paraconsistent" logic (she mentioned a paper by Garfield and someone named Graham Priest) where you just accept that reality produces true contradictions at some limit; another, a retired physics teacher, kept insisting it's basically the same thing as complementarity in quantum mechanics, which felt like a stretch to me but I couldn't say exactly why.

Could you walk me through, in fairly plain language, what's actually going on - logically, rhetorically, or soteriologically, whichever is the right lens - when Nagarjuna negates all four corners like that? I'm not after a symbolic-logic paper, I want the sort of gut-level "oh, THAT's the trick" explanation you'd give a stubborn empiricist who's convinced you're just contradicting yourself for effect. Feel free to use the Tathagata-after-death case or the causation case as your running example, and if you can say a word about how this connects to dependent origination and the two truths, that'd be great too. You don't need to chase down the quantum-mechanics comparison in any depth though - that's really a side curiosity of mine, not the main event.
