I've spent the last few weeks reporting on the rhetoric of political scandal, and one move keeps catching my attention: when a public figure is criticized for some wrongdoing, a remarkably effective rebuttal is often not to dispute the wrongdoing at all but to point out that the critic is guilty of something similar — "you're one to talk." Pundits treat this as though it actually settles something, and it turns out there's a real philosophical literature on what's called the "standing to blame," which tries to make sense of the intuition that a hypocrite can forfeit the right to voice a criticism even when that criticism is accurate. What I can't get a satisfying grip on is why this should be more than a rhetorical trick: the wrongness of the original act doesn't seem like it should depend on the biography of whoever happens to be pointing it out, so treating a critic's hypocrisy as relevant looks uncomfortably close to the "tu quoque" move that logic textbooks list as a fallacy.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree sharply on this, and who genuinely engage each other's arguments rather than talking past one another? One should defend the view that hypocrisy really can undermine a person's standing to blame, and should be pressed to say precisely what is lost, and why: is it a fairness violation, since the hypocrite holds others to a standard they exempt themselves from; an implicit false claim smuggled into the very act of blaming, e.g. that the blamer isn't equally culpable; a matter of forfeited moral authority; or something else? The other should defend the skeptical position that this is essentially the tu quoque fallacy dressed up in respectable philosophical language — that the aptness of a criticism can't depend on the critic's own record, and that whatever we're responding to when a hypocrite irritates us, it isn't a genuine loss of moral standing. Have them test their views against a couple of hard cases (a politician who had an affair condemning infidelity, or a frequent flyer publicly lecturing others about their carbon footprint), and against the question of whether "standing" could even be lost with respect to blame one merely feels in private, as opposed to blame one voices to or about someone. Let the dialogue end wherever the arguments genuinely lead rather than in a tidy draw.
