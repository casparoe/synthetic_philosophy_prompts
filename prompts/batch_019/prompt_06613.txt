For a project with my school's philosophy club, I have been reading about the disagreement between Humeans and anti-Humeans regarding laws of nature: roughly, the split between philosophers like David Lewis, who hold that a law of nature is just an axiom or theorem of the best possible systematic summary of everything that actually happens in the world, and philosophers like David Armstrong, who hold that laws must involve some further relation of natural necessitation between properties, one that no amount of mere regularity could ever supply. Since this whole debate is usually traced back to Hume, our faculty advisor asked us to work through some of the primary text ourselves rather than relying only on summaries, so I have been going through Section VII of Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, "Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion." Near the end of Part II, right after his discussion of constant conjunction and custom, Hume writes the following:

> We may define a cause to be "an object followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second." Or, in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed.

What has been bothering me is that Hume introduces these as though they were simply two ways of stating the same thought, joined by a casual "or, in other words" — yet they do not seem equivalent to me at all. The first definition looks like a purely regularity-based claim about which kinds of events happen, as a matter of fact, to be constantly conjoined; the second sounds like a counterfactual claim about what would have happened had things gone differently, which seems to require exactly the kind of "necessary connection" that the rest of the section argues we have no impression of and no right to assert. My advisor mentioned, just as our meeting was ending, that this gap has an actual name in the secondary literature — something like "Hume's two definitions of cause" — and that people have been arguing over it since at least the 1960s, but she did not have time to say anything more before we had to leave.

Could you help me work through this properly? I would like to understand, first, why these two definitions genuinely come apart rather than being harmless restatements of one another; second, what a philosopher would be committing themselves to if they built an entire theory of causation, or of laws, on one definition instead of the other; and third, and most importantly to me, how this specific tension inside Hume's own text maps onto the contemporary Humean/anti-Humean split — does the first, regularity-flavored definition make Hume the true ancestor of Lewis's best-system account, while the second, counterfactual definition quietly smuggles in something closer to what anti-Humeans like Armstrong think laws actually require? Or is there a way of reading the second definition that keeps Hume fully on the Humean side, without any hidden commitment to real necessity in the world? I would rather work through your own reasoning about the passage itself than receive another general overview of Humeanism, since I already have that part from class.
