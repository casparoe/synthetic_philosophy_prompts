I write a weekly newsletter on science and ideas for a general audience, and this week I'm tackling a corner of analytic metaphysics I hadn't revisited since an undergraduate seminar: the trouble philosophers have had defining dispositional properties like fragility, solubility, and even courage in terms of simple conditional statements. I got pulled back into the subject after reading that some contemporary free-will theorists borrow the same "finks and masks" apparatus to answer Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the idea that free action requires being able to have done otherwise, and I realized I should get the underlying metaphysical puzzle straight before going anywhere near that debate in a later issue. I only have about 800 words for this section, so I've tried to compress the technicalities without flattening them. I'd be grateful for your comments on the draft below before it goes to my editor.

Here is the section as it stands, more or less verbatim:

Start with the obvious idea: to call a wine glass fragile is to say that it would break if struck, dropped, or squeezed. Generalize this and you get what philosophers call the simple conditional analysis of dispositions: an object has a disposition to give response R to stimulus S just in case it would give R if S occurred.

In the 1990s, C. B. Martin pointed out a nasty class of counterexamples to this, since known as "finks." Picture a wire that is, in itself, quite dead, but that is hooked up to a hidden device sensitive to the approach of a conductor, which switches the current on an instant before contact. Such a wire satisfies "if touched, current would flow" just as well as a genuinely live wire does, so the simple analysis is forced to call it live, even though, up until the moment of contact, it is nothing of the sort. David Lewis, replying a few years later with his own version of the story (his has a sorcerer keeping watch over the wire), argued that finks don't sink the conditional analysis so much as show that a disposition has to be tied to a stable, intrinsic causal basis, one that persists through the stimulus and does its normal causal work, rather than to the bare truth of a conditional.

Masks are a quieter problem, because they don't touch the disposition at all. Mark Johnston, and later Alexander Bird (who called them "antidotes"), pointed to cases like this one: a dose of poison, taken together with the right antidote, will not kill you, yet the poison hasn't stopped being poisonous for even a moment. The disposition, and the causal basis underlying it, are entirely intact; something has simply intervened further downstream, between stimulus and manifestation.

Here is a case I invented to test my own grip on the distinction. Imagine a wine glass individually wrapped in a "smart" packaging that, the instant it senses an incoming impact, reconfigures the glass's own molecular lattice so that, for that split second, the glass simply is not fragile, before reverting once the danger has passed. My instinct is to call this just an elaborate mask, a first cousin of bubble wrap. And it seems to me that Lewis's fix for finkish cases, requiring a stable basis that survives the moment of stimulation, also takes care of masking cases like the antidote, since both are just something getting in the way of the manifestation from the outside.

That's the draft. I'd welcome your reaction to three things especially: whether I've stated the fink/mask distinction correctly overall; whether my classification of the smart-packaging case is right, or whether it actually belongs on the other side of the ledger; and whether that closing claim, that Lewis's fix for finks also disposes of masking cases, will survive scrutiny, or whether I'm quietly papering over a distinction that matters. I'd also welcome a general reaction to whether the piece is pitched at the right level for an educated non-specialist reader, and whether you can suggest a livelier or more contemporary stand-in for the wires and sorcerers.
