This semester I'm taking a course on democratic theory, and one theme that keeps coming up is how strangely difficult it is to say what it even means for a group decision to "reflect the will of the group" once there are more than two options on the table. I recently ran into what feels like a real-life illustration of that difficulty during a committee vote, and I would like help understanding what actually happened, rather than a hand-wave about committees being messy.

I sit on the nine-person social committee for my dorm, and we needed to choose a venue for the spring formal from four options: the Boathouse, the Rooftop, the Barn, and the Ballroom. A simple show of hands for first choice ended in an exact tie, four votes each for the Boathouse and the Rooftop, with the Barn getting one and the Ballroom none. To settle it, we had everyone rank all four venues from favorite to least favorite and compared them head-to-head. Written out, the nine full rankings were: two members had Boathouse–Rooftop–Barn–Ballroom, four had Rooftop–Barn–Boathouse–Ballroom, one had Barn–Boathouse–Rooftop–Ballroom, and two had Boathouse–Barn–Ballroom–Rooftop. Head-to-head, the Boathouse beat the Rooftop 5–4, the Rooftop beat the Barn 6–3, and, infuriatingly, the Barn beat the Boathouse 5–4, so no venue could beat both of the others. Someone then proposed a points system instead, three points for a first choice down to zero for last, which gave the Boathouse a clear win, 18 to the Rooftop's 17 (the Barn also had 17, the Ballroom just 2). But since the Ballroom so obviously had no chance, another member re-ran the same points system with it struck from every ballot, and the winner flipped to the Rooftop, 10 to 9, even though not a single person had changed their mind about the Boathouse relative to the Rooftop.

Our faculty advisor, overhearing the ensuing argument, said we had basically rediscovered "the reason nobody has ever designed a fair voting system," but had to leave before explaining what she meant. Was she right that something like this was bound to happen no matter which method we used, or did we simply choose badly among the three? Is any one of the simple vote, the head-to-head comparison, or the points system actually the right default to trust, and is there some other method that would have escaped both the circular result and the flip? I would rather understand the real mechanics behind what happened than settle for being told that committees are unpredictable.
