Since retiring from a career in physiology, I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and I keep returning to the strange coda to A. J. Ayer's life. In 1988, while hospitalized for pneumonia, he choked on a piece of smoked salmon, suffered a cardiac arrest, and was clinically dead for several minutes. He wrote about the experience twice, and in the second, more measured piece he walked back any suggestion that he had glimpsed an afterlife. He nonetheless conceded that the episode had somewhat weakened his lifelong confidence that death is simply annihilation, though he said he still hoped it would be.

Ayer died the following year, shortly before the empirical study of near-death experience took off in earnest: laboratory findings of brief, coordinated surges of brain activity around the point of clinical death, and clinical programmes such as Sam Parnia's AWARE studies, which tested cardiac-arrest survivors' reports against objective markers hidden in the resuscitation room. Given what we know of Ayer's empiricism, his rejection of Cartesian dualism, and his own carefully hedged verdict on his own experience, how do you think he would have assessed this later research? Would it have restored the unqualified atheism of his youth, or is there something in it that would, on reflection, make his hedge look more justified rather than less? Please defend a definite answer by engaging with his actual philosophical commitments, rather than with generic reflections on near-death experiences.
