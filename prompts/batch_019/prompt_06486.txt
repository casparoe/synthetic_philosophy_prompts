so I'm in a Plato seminar this semester and we just spent two entire class periods on a chunk of the Laws that genuinely threw me off. Everyone knows the Republic bit about reason needing to keep appetite and spirit in check, so I walked in assuming Plato would basically be anti-alcohol, but instead the Athenian Stranger spends book 1 defending drinking parties. He even sets up this thought experiment about a hypothetical "fear-potion" that could make even the bravest guy act like a total coward, and argues a legislator would obviously want that as a cheap, low-risk "touchstone" for testing people's courage. Then he says that since no such drug actually exists, wine is basically the real-world equivalent of that touchstone for the *opposite* trait -- shame and self-control around pleasure, instead of courage against fear/pain -- and that it doesn't just test that trait, it can actually train it.

Then book 2 lays out this whole age-graded system that I found kind of hilarious: no wine at all before 18 (something about not pouring "fire on fire" onto a young soul), watered-down wine but zero tolerance for actual drunkenness from 18 to 30, and then once your past 40 you're basically encouraged to get properly drunk and invoke Dionysus, because wine apparently works as a "medicine" against the stiffness and crabbiness of old age, softening the soul back up like iron reheated in a forge. There's also this bit about needing sober "generals" to run the party, since obviously a room full of drunk people can't supervise itself. My classmate joked that Plato basically invented the office happy hour, just with an HR rep sitting in the corner -- but our professor insists there's a serious argument buried in here, and I clearly haven't found it yet.

What I can't wrap my head around is the actual logic: why should deliberately dissolving people's inhibitions ever count as a legitimate way to reveal or cultivate virtue, rather than just being a fancy way to practice vice? And how does any of this fit with the picture of the soul from the Republic -- is Plato quietly revising his account of self-mastery/akrasia by the time he writes the Laws, or is there some way these two pictures are actually consistent? I've got a short paper on this due soon and would love an actual deep dive into the mechanics of the argument, not the "everything in moderation" cliché version that keeps turning up whenever I look for summaries.
