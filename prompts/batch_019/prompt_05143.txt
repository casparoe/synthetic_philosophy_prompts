I'm a novelist working on a scene in which one character, confronted about a betrayal, tries to deflect by pointing out that her accuser has done the very same thing. I want the exchange to track real philosophical territory rather than just restage the schoolyard "you're one to talk," so I've been reading into the idea that hypocrisy is what costs a critic the standing to blame someone else. But this can't be the whole story: a person who commits the identical wrong quite openly, never claiming any exemption for himself, still seems to lack standing to reproach a fellow offender -- and yet a genuine hypocrite, someone who cheats on his taxes while loudly moralizing about honesty, seems to keep full standing to call out a friend's unrelated cowardice. If hypocrisy turns out to be neither necessary nor sufficient for losing the standing to blame, what is actually doing the work, and why has it proven so hard to locate the loss of standing in the gap between what someone preaches and what they practice? Please explain this to me in plain, intuitive terms I can put to use in the scene, rather than as a literature review.
