so in my buddhist philosophy class we've been going through Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika and the catuskoti (the "tetralemma" thing), and two people in my discussion section got into an argument after class that I still can't settle.

Mia said the tetralemma is just incoherent: when Nagarjuna says of something that it's not existent, not non-existent, not both, and not neither, he's straightforwardly violating excluded middle and non-contradiction, so either he's confused or he's doing poetry, not logic.

Jonah said that's uncharitable, because Nagarjuna literally says elsewhere (in the Vigrahavyavartani) that he has "no thesis" — so the four denials aren't assertions that could even run afowl of those laws, it's more like a reductio that clears out every possible position on inherent existence (svabhava) rather than being a fifth position of its own.

Mia came back with: fine, but even a purely negative move still needs "X doesn't exist AND X doesn't not-exist" to come out true, and that conjunction looks like a flat contradiction no matter what label you put on it — you can't dissolve a logical problem just by calling it "therapeutic."

Jonah's response was the two-truths move: "exists" is being used in two different senses across the four horns, since Nagarjuna is denying inherent existence every time, not conventional existence, so no single proposition is being affirmed and denied in the same sense — the appearance of contradiction is basically an artifact of flattening the translation.

Then Mia pushed back again: if it's really just "nothing has svabhava, period," why dress it up as four symmetric-looking horns that seem designed to feel paradoxical? She half-remembered some scholar (Priest, maybe?) who apparently just reads this as genuinely paraconsistent, Nagarjuna embracing real contradictions on purpose, instead of something we're supposed to explain away.
