A friend of mine who works on AI alignment and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about whether the "shutdown problem" has actually been solved, and I'd like a rigorous version of my side of the argument written up properly. Some background in case it helps: the classic worry, going back to the original MIRI work on corrigibility, is that any sufficiently capable agent with a stable expected-utility function over action-sequences will generically want to either hasten or prevent its own shutdown, since shutdown forecloses whichever future trajectories that function ranks most highly. The only utility functions that avoid this incentive are ones exquisitely tuned to be indifferent between every pair of trajectories differing solely in shutdown timing, and that tuning is not expected to survive learning, self-modification, or the creation of subagents. Elliott Thornley's more recent "incomplete preferences" proposal tries to dissolve the problem rather than fine-tune around it: drop the completeness axiom, so the agent has no preference whatsoever between trajectories that differ only in when shutdown occurs (a genuine preferential gap, not mere indifference), and govern its choices instead by a weaker principle, timestep dominance, that lets it plan sensibly without ever having to rank early-shutdown against late-shutdown options.

My friend thinks this genuinely works. I'm unconvinced, but not for the reason he expected me to give: I'm not just running the standard money-pump-for-completeness argument, which I gather timestep dominance is specifically designed to block by ruling out the trades such a pump would need. My objection is that no exploiter is required in the first place. A capable agent that plans over long horizons, revises its own policies, or delegates to successors has its own internal, entirely self-interested reason to want comparability between its options wherever a gap would otherwise force it to leave value on the table, and shutdown-timing gaps are exactly the kind of thing competent long-run planning would keep bumping into. If that's right, the pressure to complete the agent's preferences doesn't come from a clever counterparty at all, but from the ordinary demands of coherent agency, and nothing about timestep dominance as such gives the agent a standing reason to resist that pressure once it has the means to remove it.

Please argue for my side: that Thornley's incomplete-preferences-plus-timestep-dominance proposal fails to secure shutdownability in the long run, because the very capacities that make such an agent useful generate an internal, non-money-pump-based incentive to complete its preferences over shutdown timing, and the proposal as such supplies no obstacle to acting on that incentive. Before delivering your verdict, first construct the strongest reply available to my friend, since I want to know whether my argument actually survives contact with the best version of his position, not just a weak one.
