Quick context before the question: I teach a graduate seminar on Plato, and this week we worked through Laws I and II. For years my stock line in the intro course has been that Plato's attitude toward bodily pleasure gets steadily more repressive the older he gets -- the guardians in the Republic can't own property, can't have private families, eat a plain regulated diet, and any poetry showing gods or heroes getting sloppy drunk gets cut by the censors. So it kind of threw my students (and, honestly, me a little) when we hit the Athenian Stranger's long defence of communal drinking parties in Laws I, plus that bit in Book II where he argues the elderly basically need wine to get over their shame enough to sing and dance in the "chorus of Dionysus." My working theory, which I floated in class, is that this is basically late Plato mellowing out -- loosing the ascetic grip of the middle dialogues and getting more realistic and humane about pleasure as an old man.

I want to build next week's lecture around that exact arc, from strict young Plato to permissive old Plato, with the wine material as the centrepiece example. So: what's the strongest evidence from the Laws (or elsewhere in the late dialogues) that actually backs up this mellowing story, and is there anything about how Plato frames the drinking material specifically that I should flag for students so I don't oversell the "permissive old man" reading?
