Question: State Aumann's agreement theorem precisely, being explicit about the roles played by the common prior assumption and by common knowledge — as opposed to mere mutual knowledge — of posteriors. Then assess the extent to which the theorem supports the popular claim that two rational people "cannot agree to disagree," addressing in particular how it bears on ordinary disagreements in which the parties exchange only their opinions rather than their underlying evidence.

Student's answer: Aumann's theorem concerns two Bayesian agents who share a common prior probability distribution over some state space but who receive different private information, represented as partitions of that space. The theorem says that if it is common knowledge between them — meaning each one knows the other's posterior probability for some event E — then their posteriors for E must actually be equal, even though they conditioned on different evidence. Intuitively, once each side knows what the other believes, any remaining difference between their two numbers is itself extra evidence, and a fully rational Bayesian would keep folding that evidence back into their estimate until nothing was left to explain the gap. This is the sense in which the theorem is usually summarized as showing that two rational people who share a prior "cannot agree to disagree."

A crucial feature of the result is that the two agents never need to reveal their underlying private information to each other — they only need to exchange their opinions, i.e., their posterior probabilities for E. In his 1976 paper, Aumann showed that if two agents with a common prior simply announce their current posterior for E to one another, back and forth, the finiteness of the state space guarantees that this process must stabilize after finitely many rounds, at which point their announced posteriors are equal. So mere talk — reporting bottom-line opinions, without ever pooling the evidence behind them — is in principle sufficient to eliminate honest disagreement between two truly rational Bayesians who trust each other's sincerity.

None of this means real-world disagreements are irrational whenever they persist. The theorem assumes a shared prior (sometimes called the Harsanyi common-prior assumption), unbounded logical and computational ability, and common knowledge of each other's rationality and sincerity — none of which ordinary disputants can be sure they have. So while the theorem shows that idealized Bayesian agents cannot knowingly and rationally maintain differing opinions, it is best read as a benchmark for what full rationality would require, not as a diagnosis that anyone who still disagrees after talking must be irrational.
