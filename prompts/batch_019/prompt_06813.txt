My colleague of thirty years, Helen, who taught philosophy of religion in our department until she retired, died last month, and I have been asked to deliver her eulogy at this weekend's memorial service. Helen was both a serious Wittgenstein scholar and a devout, church-going Christian, and she deliberately built her own faith on his remarks in what is usually cited as the 'Lectures on Religious Belief': professing belief in the Last Judgement, she held, is not advancing an empirical hypothesis that evidence could vindicate, but adopting a picture that regulates the whole of one's life. Consequently, she was as impatient with apologists who tried to argue her into faith as with colleagues who assumed her talk of resurrection was really just poetic shorthand for humanist values, and she told me more than once that she wanted no eulogy that quietly resolved this tension in either direction: not 'she knows the truth of it now,' as though death had settled an empirical question, and not 'she meant it all along as a lovely metaphor for kindness.' The room will be divided between philosophy colleagues who will catch every Wittgensteinian move I make and parishioners and family who have never heard the phrase 'language game' and simply want their friend honored. Please write the eulogy: about six minutes long, warm rather than argumentative, faithful to Helen's actual view of what a religious belief is, and able to comfort both halves of the room without collapsing the distinction she spent her career insisting on.
