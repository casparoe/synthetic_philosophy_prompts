So I've been down an AI safety rabbit hole for the last few months (blame a work Slack channel, long story), and I keep running into this idea called "coherent extrapolated volition" - Yudkowsky's old proposal that, roughly, instead of building an AI around what we currently want, you'd want it to do what we'd want if we knew more, had the time and brainpower to actually think things through, and were more the people we wish we were instead of our messy current selves. My first reaction was basically "yeah, obviously, who wouldn't want the smarter version of their own preferences instead of whatever I want at 1am scrolling my phone." So I brought it up with my roommate, who's ABD in philosophy (ethics, not AI - she keeps reminding me none of this is new), and she said this is basically just the AI-flavored version of an old move philosophers make when they define a persons own good in terms of "idealized" or "fully informed" preferences, and that there's a handful of specific problems with that move that never really got settled. Like: different reasonable ways of idealizing someone don't converge on the same answer, so there's no single fact about what your extrapolated self even wants. Or: the idealizing procedure has to already assume some notion of what counts as an improvement - more rational, more informed, whatever - and that notion is doing all the actual moral work, so the whole thing is a bit circular. Or: by the time a version of you has changed enough to count as properly idealized, it might not be continuous with you anymore, and you can't even know ahead of time what it would want, since getting there is exactly the kind of change that would affect what you're capable of valuing in the first place.

We went back and forth about this for like an hour on the couch and neither of us budged, which she says is normal since this fight has been going on for decades under different names, way before any AI people showed up. Could you write that argument out as a dialogue between two people who both know this material cold - one defending the idea that extrapolated/idealized preferences (personal well-being framing or the CEV/AI framing, whichever you think works better) are basically the right target to aim for and has good answers to the objections above, and one who thinks the whole idealizing move is indeterminate, circular, or self-undermining for those reasons. Doesn't have to be me and her specifically, just two people who are actually sharp about it. I don't want either side strawmanned - they should be responding to each other's actual point each round, not just restating their own view louder - and it's totally fine if it doesn't end with a clean winner, since ours didn't either. Doesn't need to be super long, a couple pages is plenty.
