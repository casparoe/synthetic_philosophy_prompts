My company runs an internal prediction market where people bet on things like whether a given project will ship on time, and a colleague recently told me about the no-trade theorem. As I understand it, the theorem holds that two people who each consider the other rational, and who share the same priors, shouldn't be able to agree on odds for a bet motivated purely by private information, since a counterpart's very willingness to take the other side is itself evidence -- and updating on that evidence should make the apparent disagreement dissolve (my colleague says this is closely related to Aumann's agreement theorem). Write a dialogue between two equally sharp colleagues who are about to shake hands on such a bet when one of them raises exactly this objection and argues they should call it off, while the other defends going ahead with the trade. Have them respond directly to each other's strongest points over several exchanges. Points worth pressing on include whether the bet could instead be justified by differing risk exposure rather than differing information, whether "common knowledge of rationality" is remotely realistic between actual colleagues, and what it would mean for a prediction market's entire purpose -- turning everyone's scattered private information into an accurate price -- if genuinely informative trades are supposed to unravel the moment they're proposed. Neither character should concede more than the argument actually warrants.
