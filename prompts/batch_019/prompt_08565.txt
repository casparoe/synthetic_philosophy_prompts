So I've been slowly working through Aristotle's Politics for a reading group (we're in Book I, the bit on household management vs. the "art of getting wealth"), and we got into kind of an arguement about how his framework would even apply to stuff going on in finance today. His whole critique of usury is basically that money's supposed to be a sterile medium for exchange, and charging interest is "unnatural" because it makes money literally give birth to more money (I think the Greek word for interest, tokos, actually means offspring?) — the profit comes from the currency itself instead of from the exchange of real goods it was invented to facilitate. Fine, that's the classic case everyone brings up. But nobody in my group could agree on what he'd say about negative interest rate policy, which is kind of the mirror-image weird case: for awhile, banks in places like Denmark, Switzerland and Japan were literally charging people to hold their savings, so money was shrinking over time instead of "breeding." Would Aristotle think that's somehow less unnatural since its the opposite of usury, or does the very idea of a central bank engineering money's value in either direction break his whole "money is just a conventional measure" framework regardless of which way it goes?

And as a bonus round: what do you think he'd make of high-frequency algorithmic trading, where a position gets held for like a few milliseconds purely to skim a fraction of a cent before being sold again, with zero connection to any household or any actual good changing hands? Is that just the most extreme, purified version of the "unnatural" wealth-getting he already condemned, or does the sheer speed and abstraction of it take it somewhere his categories don't really reach anymore? Trying to get an answer thats actually reconstructed from his real distinctions (oikonomia vs chrematistike, natural vs conventional value, etc) rather than just a "lol Aristotle would hate capitalism" take, if that makes sense.
