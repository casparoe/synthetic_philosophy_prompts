So I've been reading through the AI corrigibility literature for a seminar on AI agency, and I want a dialogue between two safety researchers who disagree about how to actually solve the shutdown problem. One of them thinks the only real fix is to deliberately break the agent's rationality a bit — make it indifferent between being shut down and not, or give it preferences that just don't compare trajectories whenever they differ only in when the shutdown happens — so there's nothing left pushing it to resist or tamper with the button. The other thinks that's the wrong move: don't patch the preferences at all, just make the agent properly uncertain about whether it's understood the goal correctly, so a human reaching for the switch gets read as evidence it messed something up, and deference falls right out of ordinary expected utility reasoning. Have them really go at it — the first one should press the second on what happens once their "stay uncertain" agent gets confident enough that it stops wanting to preserve the option of being switched off, and the second should press back on whether the first one's patch actually stays contained, or just resurfaces as incoherence somewhere else (successor agents it builds, exploitable behavior, etc). They both know this stuff cold, so no cheap wins — they need to be responding to each others actual best argument.
