I'm a political theory PhD student. This dispute broke out in a reading group pairing Aquinas's De Regno with the parallel question in his early Sentences commentary. Two groupmates, Owen and Marisol, spent the session disagreeing about whether Aquinas's remarks on tyrannicide across the two texts are actually consistent, and I'd like a move-by-move verdict rather than an overall one.

(1) Owen noted that in the Sentences commentary (II Sent., d.44, q.2, a.2), answering an objection built on Cicero's defense of Caesar's assassins, Aquinas concludes that whoever "kills the tyrant for the liberation of the country, is praised and rewarded." So by Aquinas's own words, a private individual may licitly kill at least one kind of tyrant, no public authorization needed.

(2) Marisol answered that this is only the usurper case, where a ruler has no legitimate title at all and no higher authority can judge him. De Regno's real focus is different: a legitimately accepted ruler who later abuses his power, where the remedy is deposition by whoever installed him. There Aquinas explicitly warns against private persons killing rulers on their own presumption, since the wicked, who find any firm ruler burdensome, are likelier than the virtuous to exploit that license, so more good kings would be lost than tyrants removed.

(3) Owen replied that this just relocates the problem: calling someone a title-less usurper facing no higher recourse is itself the kind of contestable judgment Marisol wants reserved for public bodies in the abuse case. Why trust private judgment for one classification and not the other?

(4) Marisol said the two judgments aren't equally hard: whether power was taken by force or fraud, without consent or recognition, is close to a checkable fact, while whether an installed ruler's conduct counts as "abuse" is an ongoing evaluative call of exactly the kind self-interest distorts most easily.

(5) Owen pointed out that Aquinas's own showcase abuse case, Rome expelling Tarquin the Proud, a king it had itself accepted, is treated as unproblematic collective judgment, and Domitian's killing is likewise credited to the Senate, which then annulled his acts by decree. Aquinas never explains why a multitude's or a senate's self-interest distorts less than an individual's. So the public/private line may just track concerted versus solitary action, not reliable versus unreliable judgment.

(6) Marisol granted this but said it only shows the line doesn't fully retire the worry, not that the texts disagree: organizing a whole community around a pretext is still harder than one aggrieved person talking himself into one, so the line still does real, if imperfect, work.

Neither of them budged.
