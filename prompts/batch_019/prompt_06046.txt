So this argument happened in our work Slack of all places — a few of us have a channel where we're rewatching Star Trek: The Next Generation, and we hit that episode (I think it's called "Second Chances"?) where it turns out a transporter mishap years earlier had actually created two Rikers: the one who went on to have his whole career on the Enterprise, and a duplicate who got stranded alone on a planet for eight years and eventually goes by Thomas. Both of them have every memory up to the moment of the accident, both are equally "continuous" with the guy who beamed down that day, but they walk off in totally different directions and end up as two seperate people. This kicked off a whole thing between two coworkers, Marco and Leah, about ordinary transporting too (not just the freak-accident kind), and it went a few rounds over the week that I mostly just watched happen. I'm a backend dev, not a philosophy person, so I can't really tell who's winning, but I did eventually look this up and apparently it's a real thing called Parfit's teletransporter problem, which made me want an outside read on it.

Here's roughly how it went, paraphrased: (1) Marco said ordinary transporting is fine, because whoever steps off the pad is really you — all that matters for survival is psychological continuity, same memories/personality/plans carrying forward, not some extra thread tying you to the same physical atoms. (2) Leah said the duplicate-Riker case proves that's wrong: both Rikers are equally continuous with the original, but they can't both BE him, since they immediately became two separate people — so continuity can't be sufficient for being the same person, full stop. (3) Marco conceded that neither duplicate is, strictly, identical to the original, but said that's not a problem for him, it's kind of the whole point: it shows identity was never what actually mattered. Each duplicate gets everything worth caring about — the memories, the relationships, the sense of carrying on the same projects — so ordinary transporting (with only one duplicate coming out) was always just a lucky case where "being identical" and "getting what matters" happened to coincide. (4) Leah called that a shell game: if you can get everything that matters in a case where identity definately fails, Marco can't also promise someone stepping into an ordinary teleporter that THEY specifically will wake up on the other side, because on his own view that isn't even the right question anymore. She added that if this "mattering" relation can belong to two people at once, it can't be the same relation that makes it rational to save for your own retirement instead of some stranger's — his view quietly turns ordinary self-interest into a kind of loyalty toward whoever happens to inherit your memories. (5) Marco said fine, bite the bullet: we already tolerate huge psychological change over an ordinary life (a retiree isn't very psychologically connected to the toddler whose fund it technically is), so prudence was always closer to "loyalty to your continuers" than to some deeper sameness, and fission cases just make that obvious instead of causing it. (6) Leah's last point was that something still feels backwards: whether the guy on the pad counts as a "continuer" of the kind that matters seems to depend on whether some other duplicate happened to get made somewhere else — a fact about a completely different person he might never meet — and it seems wrong that whether you should feel relieved or terrified about stepping into the machine could hinge on an accident happening to somebody else entirely.
