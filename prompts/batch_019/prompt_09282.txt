Ok so I had this whole argument with my friend Priya last night that went in circles for like an hour and I need a second opinion. It started because she sent me some article about factory farming and was defending it on purely economic grounds — her point was basically that yes, keeping chickens in cramped, miserable conditions is bad for the chickens, but it's what makes protein cheap enough for hundreds of millions of poor people to actually afford, and if you tally up all the suffering avoided (malnutrition and whatnot) against the suffering caused to the chickens, the total clearly comes out positive, so its justified, case closed. I said ok, but would you accept that same kind of argument for people — like imagine a factory that treats its human workers brutally, but the cheap goods it makes lift way more people out of poverty than the workers suffer. She said absolutely not, treating workers that way is wrong no matter how the overall numbers shake out. So then I asked why the "just add up total welfare" logic is the right tool for the chicken case but gets completely thrown out for the worker case, and she basically just said people "count differently," without ever really explaining why that isn't just a conveniently placed line.

I've been chewing on this all morning tbh. Part of me thinks shes being inconsistent — you can't use the aggregate-math approach for animals and then switch to a totally different, no-exceptions approach for humans, unless you can actually justify why the switch happens right there. But another part of me shares her gut reaction on both cases (factory farming feels bad-but-tolerable, brutalizing workers feels categorically off-limits even for a "good" total outcome), so maybe there's something real underneath this and not just bias talking. Is there an actual coherent basis for judging animal welfare and human welfare by two totally different moral rulebooks like this, or are we all just picking whichever framework happens to spit out the conclusion we already wanted?
