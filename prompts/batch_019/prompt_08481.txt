My cousin Danny got back from his deployment this past spring and started taking a "War and Morality" class at the community college near us this fall (he swears its just for the extra GI Bill stipend, but he's clearly gotten more into it than he lets on). At my grandma's for Sunday dinner a couple weeks ago he brought up something from class that ended up derailing the whole table: the idea that soldiers on both sides of a war are morally on equal footing as individuals, as long as they fight by the rules, even if their country's cause turns out to be totally unjust. The class's go-to example is a basic German draftee in WWII shooting at Allied soldiers -- the claim being that he wasn't doing anything you could blame him for personally, whatever you think of the war itself. My aunt's partner Priya, who has an old philosophy masters she never got much use out of, was not going to let that slide.

She said that can't be right, because the reason an Allied soldier is a legitimate target at all is that he's using force against an unjust aggression, whereas the German draftee is aiming at someone who hasn't done anything to forfeit his right not to be shot at -- so the two situations aren't actually symmetrical, and the fact that the Geneva Conventions treat captured soldiers from both sides the same way is a legal convenience, not evidence of any real moral sameness. Danny came back with: nobody can seriously expect an ordinary 19-year-old to independently verify the justice of his country's war before he ships out, so if we're judging individuals and not nations, that honest ignorance has to count for something -- and what, on her view, actually distinguishes that German kid from an American one, when both are just doing what they were told? Priya granted that the ignorance gets him off the hook for blame, but said that's a totally different question from whether he had any right to be shooting in the first place, and brought up the case of someone who's been secretly drugged and set loose on a stranger without knowing it: we don't blame that person, she said, but the stranger being attacked can still fight back, so being blameless doesn't mean you're not a legitimate target. Danny wasn't buying that either -- he said if she's right, the entire practice of prisoner-of-war protections, the fact that we don't try enemy soldiers for murder just for having fought, is built on a moral lie that any professor teaching this stuff should be embarrassed to put on a syllabus, and he wants to know why anyone would design the actual rules soldiers live by around a distinction that everyone agrees soldiers can't apply in the field.

It went around like that for another twenty minutes and neither of them budged, so I'm bringing it to you instead of the family group chat. Can you go through the exchange step by step and say who has the better of it at each point, not just an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Priya's drugged-stranger comparison actually does the work she wants it to do, and whether Danny's point about the prisoner-of-war convention is a real problem for her position or just a reason the convention is useful despite not being "true." And if you think there's something both of them are missing, I'd like to hear that too.
