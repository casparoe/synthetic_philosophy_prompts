Here is the question they were answering:

"In no more than 400 words: explain the disagreement between 'realist' and 'deflationary' positions on whether the features that sparse autoencoders find inside language models are genuine internal representations, rather than artifacts of the analysis method. Your answer should (a) explain the superposition hypothesis and why it motivates the use of sparse autoencoders in the first place, (b) give one serious argument for realism about these features and one serious argument against it, and (c) say whether Dennett's notion of a 'real pattern' actually settles the dispute or just relocates it."

And here is the submission:

"Interpretability researchers noticed early on that individual neurons in language models are often 'polysemantic': one neuron might fire for French text, for legal boilerplate, and for some totally unrelated visual pattern, all at once. This is called superposition, and it happens because the model has far more concepts to represent than it has neurons, so it has to pack several unrelated features onto the same units. To untangle this, researchers train sparse autoencoders on the model's internal activations, learning a much larger, sparse set of directions in which, ideally, each direction lines up with one clean, human-interpretable concept instead of the tangle a raw neuron gives you.

The real question is whether the concepts an SAE finds were already 'in' the network or are just a redescription we're imposing afterwards. The strongest argument for realism is that these features aren't merely correlational: you can clamp one to an artificially high value and get a specific, predictable change in the model's behavior, such as the well-known Golden Gate Bridge feature in Claude, which is exactly the kind of manipulability we'd expect from something playing a real causal role rather than a pattern found by curve-fitting after the fact. The strongest argument against realism is that these decompositions aren't unique: change the dictionary size, the sparsity penalty, or even the random seed, and you get a different set of 'features,' with nothing in the method itself telling you which decomposition, if any, is the one the network is 'really' using.

I think Dennett's real patterns view settles this in favor of deflationism. His point, following directly from the intentional stance, is that a pattern is real exactly when it's useful to describe a system that way instead of dropping down to the physical level, so since it's obviously useful to talk about a 'Golden Gate Bridge' feature, that is simply all 'real' can mean here, and the realist/anti-realist debate turns out to be a merely verbal dispute about how strict we want to be with the word 'real.'"
