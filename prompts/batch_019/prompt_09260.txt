I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently serving as a teaching assistant for an undergraduate seminar on medieval Jewish and Islamic rationalism, and I'm in the middle of grading take-home midterm responses on Maimonides' treatment of sacrifice. Since the teaching assistants on this course are expected to calibrate our marks against one another before grades are released, I'd like a second, independent read on one response in particular, partly to check that the standard I'm applying isn't idiosyncratic.

The question, as it appeared on the exam, read:

"In the Guide of the Perplexed (III:32), Maimonides explains why the Torah commands animal sacrifice, despite his own insistence that God has no need of, or inherent desire for, such offerings. Reconstruct his argument in your own words, and explain how it fits into his broader account of the reasons for the commandments (ta'amei ha-mitzvot). Then identify one significant difficulty or tension that this account runs into elsewhere in his corpus."

And here is the student's response, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"Maimonides argues that God permitted animal sacrifice not because He values or requires it, but because the Israelites emerging from Egyptian bondage had been raised in a milieu where every nation worshipped the stars and other created things through sacrificial rites, and it is not in human nature to abandon everything one is accustomed to all at once. He illustrates the point with the example of young mammals, who must first be nursed on milk and only gradually weaned onto solid food, since too abrupt a change would be more than their nature could bear; the commandments, he says, work on the nation in just this gradual way. So the Torah does not abolish sacrifice outright. Instead, it redirects the practice: sacrifice may continue, but exclusively to God, in a single sanctioned location, through a dedicated priesthood, hedged by restrictions that curb the excesses of the surrounding pagan cults. In this way the nation is led gradually away from idolatry and toward the purer worship of prayer, and ultimately toward the intellectual knowledge of God that Maimonides regards as the highest form of worship.

This is, in effect, Maimonides' general theory of the commandments as such: the Law as a whole is best understood as a graduated curriculum of concessions, designed to wean a nation of former idolaters toward monotheism and rational belief one habit at a time. And because the institution of sacrifice is therefore a historically contingent pedagogical device rather than something of any intrinsic worth, Maimonides holds, consistently with this account, that the Messianic future — once the process of weaning is complete — will dispense with animal offerings altogether in favor of prayer and contemplation alone. This is arguably the more coherent reading given his broader rationalist commitments, even if it is not how the tradition ultimately received him."
