I'm TAing for a formal epistemology course this semester, and one of the discussion questions is about diachronic Dutch book arguments — specifically the one for conditionalization — and I keep going back and forth on how much it actually proves. The setup: an agent has credence function P1 at t1, and the Rule of Conditionalization says that if she learns E (and nothing stronger) by t2, her new credence in any A should just be her old P1(A|E). The classic argument, due to a construction of David Lewis's that Paul Teller reported on back in the '70s, is that if she instead plans to shift to something other than P1(A|E) upon learning E, a bookie can sell her a little combination of bets at t1, plus one more bet contingent on whether E comes true, such that she ends up strictly poorer regardless of how things turn out — even though each individual bet was "fair" by her own credences at the moment she accepted it.

Here's what's been bugging me though: this all seems to depend on the agent having already locked in, at t1, some definite alternative plan for how she'd update on learning E — the bookie apparently needs to know that plan in advance to build the trap. So if she simply hasn't committed ahead of time to any particular non-conditionalizing rule (she's just going to "figure it out" when E comes in, whatever that amounts to), it's not obvious the bookie has a strategy that's gauranteed to fleece her. Which makes me suspect the argument really only shows "don't announce a rigid plan to deviate," not "you're rationally required to conditionalize" — and those feel like pretty different conclusions. Could you actually build this into a proper argument? I want the strongest version you can manage that the diachronic Dutch book argument fails to establish conditionalization as a genuine rational requirement — lay out the argument in enough technical detail first that it's clear exactly what's being targeted, then make the case against it, and make sure you actually engage with the best comeback a committed Dutch-booker would have, rather than just gesturing at a list of objections.
