(a) [10 pts] Explain how Aumann's account of common knowledge of rationality is meant to justify backward induction as the solution to this game, including what the players' knowledge would have to look like for the backward-induction outcome to be forced, and discuss the well-known objection that this argument has trouble saying what a player should believe upon reaching a node that, by the very assumption of common knowledge of rationality, should never be reached. (b) [10 pts] Does common knowledge of rationality, by itself, guarantee that the players' strategies form a Nash equilibrium in an arbitrary finite game, and not only in games of perfect information like this one? Here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted: "(a) In the Centipede Game, backward induction starts at the very last decision node and works backwards. At the last node, the mover prefers to take rather than pass, since passing only benefits the other player. Given this, at the second-to-last node the mover can anticipate that the other player will take at the last node, so taking immediately is better than passing and then having the other player take. Repeating this reasoning back to the first node, the first mover should also take immediately, even though both players would end up richer if they cooperated by passing a few times. Aumann (1995) formalized the idea that this outcome is forced once we assume common knowledge of rationality: each player is rational, each knows the other is rational, each knows that the other knows this, and so on without end, and this is common knowledge together with the structure of the game itself. Aumann showed that under a sufficiently strong notion of rationality — one on which a player must be disposed to act optimally at every node consistent with their information, not merely at the nodes actually reached — common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome. The well-known objection, associated with critics such as Reny and Binmore, is that the argument seems to undercut itself: if it really is common knowledge that player 1 is rational, player 1 should never pass at the first node, so if player 2 ever has to move at all, they are already in a situation that common knowledge of rationality says cannot arise. It is therefore unclear what player 2 should believe about player 1's continued rationality at that point, and some critics take this to show that common knowledge of rationality becomes incoherent, or at least radically underdetermined, once play departs from the backward-induction path.

(b) In general games, not just games of perfect information, common knowledge of rationality guarantees that each player's strategy is what game theorists call rationalizable: formally, a strategy that survives the iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies, which is the formal counterpart of the idea that a rational player facing rational opponents can rule out any choice that no reasonable belief about the opponents could make worthwhile, and can keep doing this once the opponents' own irrational choices have been ruled out in turn. This does not, in general, pin down a unique Nash equilibrium, since many games have multiple Nash equilibria, or even rationalizable strategies that belong to no Nash equilibrium at all. Getting the rest of the way to Nash equilibrium is really just an application of Aumann's Agreement Theorem: once the players' beliefs about each other's strategies (their 'conjectures') are common knowledge and the players share a common prior, they cannot agree to disagree, so their conjectures must coincide, and coinciding, correct conjectures about one another's actual strategy choices is exactly what a Nash equilibrium is. So the extra ingredient needed on top of common knowledge of rationality is exactly the common-prior assumption that drives the Agreement Theorem."
