As part of an independent project for my high school's philosophy club, I've been comparing David Lewis's counterpart theory of modality to the rival view that ordinary objects have genuine, non-reductive identity across possible worlds — the view Kripke seems to presuppose in Naming and Necessity. I've come across four standard objections to counterpart theory, and I'd like help figuring out how much weight each one actually deserves:

- Humphrey objection: Kripke's charge that counterpart theory gets the subject matter of de re modal claims wrong, since "Humphrey could have won the election" comes out true merely because some distinct, similar individual wins in another world, rather than because of anything about Humphrey himself.
- Contextual indeterminacy: because counterpart relations track whichever similarities are salient in context, one and the same individual can have different, even conflicting, counterparts depending on how a conversation is framed (e.g., which similarities a speaker happens to be emphasizing), which seems to make de re modal facts less objective than we take them to be.
- Chisholm's Paradox: a step-by-step chain of possible worlds can apparently be built connecting two wildly dissimilar individuals; Lewis blocks the absurd conclusion only by letting the counterpart relation be non-transitive, which critics say shows it isn't really tracking any single, coherent respect of similarity.
- Lumpl/Goliath: Gibbard's case of a statue and the lump of clay that composes it, used to argue that identity can be contingent. Counterpart theorists can resist this only by relativizing the counterpart relation to sortal terms like "statue" or "lump," so that one individual ends up with different modal profiles depending on which sortal is at issue.
