I'm a software engineer who sits on the admissions committee of the small housing cooperative I live in. Our bylaws let the committee reject any prospective buyer by unanimous vote without giving a reason, which as I understand it is fairly typical for co-ops, and in practice decisions have always rested as much on whether someone seems like a trustworthy fit for a community that shares real financial exposure (a shared underlying mortgage, joint upkeep costs) as on hard criteria like income. With far more applicants than open units these days, a reform faction on the board wants to scrap this in favor of fixed, published criteria plus a lottery among everyone who qualifies.

The faction defending the current system argues that we are a voluntary association that has built and financially co-signed for this community over years, and that a group has just as much right as an individual to decide whom to admit into its home and its shared liabilities for reasons that can't always be reduced to a checklist -- several members have pointed out that this is really the same kind of discretion any country claims over who gets to join it, just exercised on a smaller scale. The reform faction answers that nobody currently on the board has any special claim to their unit beyond having arrived earlier, that unreviewable "fit" judgments are exactly the mechanism that has historically been used to exclude people for illegitimate reasons while being nearly impossible to challenge, and that "you can always live somewhere else" stops being a serious answer once rejections from co-op after co-op start to add up in a market this tight.

We vote on whether to keep, amend, or replace the discretionary bylaw at next month's annual meeting. Please draft the short decision memo I'll circulate beforehand: recommend one specific option with a timeframe for implementing it, and whichever way you come down, give the losing side's strongest argument a real hearing rather than a caricature.
