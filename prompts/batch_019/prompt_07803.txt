I spent something like thirty-five years doing visual physiology in amphibians before I retired, and one of my favorite bits of trivia to trot out at dinner parties was the old Lettvin-Maturana-McCulloch-Pitts result on the frog retina -- those ganglion cells that fire like mad for any small, dark, convex blob moving eratically across the visual field, fly or no fly. So imagine my surprise when my niece, who's doing a philosophy MA, sent me a paper on "teleosemantics" and it turned out philosophers have been arguing about my frogs for forty years as a test case for how mental content gets fixed in nature. Now I'm hooked, reading everything I can get my hands on, but I don't have anyone nearby to actually hash it out with, so you'll have to do.

Here's where I've gotten stuck. Ruth Millikan's story, as I understand it, is that a frog's snap-trigger state has the content "fly" rather than "small dark moving speck" because the mechanism producing it was selected for, across many generations, precisely because it correlated with actual flies -- so a BB pellet flicked past the frog only misrepresents, it doesn't change what the state means. Fred Dretske's picture looks similar on the surface, but I get the sense he cashes out the "supposed to detect flies" idea through something more like a recruitment-during-learning story than a strictly Darwinian one. Could you spell out precisely where the two views actually part ways, rather than just the family resemblance? And then tell me: does Davidson's Swampman -- a molecule-for-molecule frog duplicate that pops into existence with zero ancestry -- sink both accounts equally, or does one of them have a cleverer way of wriggling out of it? While you're at it, I'd love to know whether either camp has a genuinely good answer for why the content isn't simply "small-dark-moving-thing-or-BB-pellet" from the start, since as an old data-fitting scientist that disjunctive hypothesis always struck me as the more natural one to reach for.
