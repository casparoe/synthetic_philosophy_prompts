I spent the better part of four decades running a laboratory that studied the visual cortex, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy of mind with any real care. My granddaughter, who is midway through a philosophy PhD, has taken it upon herself to correct my early conviction that the mystery of qualia would simply dissolve once the wiring diagram was complete, and over several months of dinner-table argument we have worked through most of the standard literature on consciousness. Inevitably we arrived at Frank Jackson's Mary's room, and although I still find the underlying puzzle genuinely unsettling, the case itself has, through sheer repetition, come to feel more like a slogan than a live thought experiment: Mary released from her black-and-white laboratory to marvel at a ripe tomato, followed by a paragraph on whether this is fatal or merely embarrassing for physicalism.

I would like to recover some of the argument's original bite through fiction rather than another summary. Could you write me a short story, on the order of 800 to 1,500 words, that builds a genuine analogue of Mary's situation but deliberately avoids color vision as the withheld experience? I would rather the missing quale be tied to some real or at least neurologically credible phenomenon -- congenital insensitivity to pain, blindsight, synesthesia, or a sensory-substitution device are the kinds of thing I have in mind -- so that the gap between physical knowledge and lived experience has to be earned again rather than simply assumed. And rather than closing on an inarticulate moment of wonder and leaving the philosophy for the reader to infer, I would like the story's structure itself to take a stand: have it dramatize, say, David Lewis and Laurence Nemirow's ability hypothesis, on which what is gained on release is a set of recognitional and imaginative skills rather than any new fact, or the phenomenal-concept strategy associated with writers like Brian Loar, on which the very same physical state is simply grasped under a new mode of presentation. I would rather you commit to one such position (or another comparably specific one from the literature, if you think it serves the story better) and let the plot's internal logic, and whatever tension remains unresolved at the end, belong to that position, rather than hedging across all of them.
