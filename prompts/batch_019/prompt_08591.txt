A friend and I have an ongoing argument, resumed most recently on a long drive home, about whether it is morally permissible to eat meat. We have both read a fair amount of the relevant philosophy, we are each familiar with the strongest arguments on the other side, and neither of us has ever managed to find a flaw in the other's reasoning or point to some piece of evidence the other lacks. In other words, we seem to be genuine epistemic peers on this question, and yet we still land on opposite sides. This time, once the object-level argument had exhausted itself yet again, we ended up arguing instead about what that persistent stalemate should do to our confidence.

I found myself defending something close to the "equal weight" version of conciliationism: that discovering a genuine peer disagrees with you is itself strong evidence, and that rationality requires moving your credence substantially toward theirs rather than simply rehearsing your original reasoning to yourself more loudly. My friend defended steadfastness, arguing that if you did in fact respond correctly to the original evidence, you are entitled to keep your credence roughly where it was, even knowing that a peer sees things differently. Where I got stuck was a follow-up move my friend made: conciliationism is itself a contested position among professional epistemologists, and the people on each side of that debate seem to be peers of one another by any standard I would want to apply to our own case. So shouldn't a consistent conciliationist have to lower their confidence in conciliationism itself, given how controversial it is among people who have thought about it far longer than we have? And if the natural fix is to say that foundational methodological commitments like this one are somehow exempt from the very norm they impose on everyone else's first-order disputes, isn't that just a self-serving carve-out? I attempted an answer in the moment and was not satisfied with anything I came up with.

Could you write a dialogue between a committed conciliationist and a committed steadfaster that works through this specific problem: whether conciliationism can consistently apply to disagreements about itself? I would like both characters to be sharp and fair to each other. The steadfaster should press the self-undermining worry as far as it goes, and the conciliationist should get to try the best available responses (for instance, distinguishing the order of the disagreement, or restricting the norm's scope in some principled way), with real back-and-forth rather than either side's strongest point simply going unanswered. It does not need to end in a resolution, but I would like it to end with the crux of the disagreement stated as precisely as possible.
