I'm a philosophy PhD student working on Victorian moral philosophy, and a disagreement broke out in our reading group last week that nobody could settle. We had just finished the chapter of Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics where he lays down four tests a proposition must pass to count as a genuine moral axiom — clear and precise terms, real self-evidence on reflection, consistency with other accepted truths, and an adequate "consensus of experts" — when someone brought up his decades of work for the Society for Psychical Research. Two people in the group, Jonas and Alma, got into it.

Jonas's opening move: this is a genuine inconsistency, not just an eccentricity. Sidgwick spent most of his adult life chasing evidence for telepathy and survival of death in a field that never came close to meeting his own second and fourth criteria — the phenomena were never uncontroversially self-evident on reflection, and expert opinion on them stayed sharply divided for his entire career. By his own Book III standards, he should have abandoned the question long before he did.

Alma pushed back: the four tests are conditions for accepting a proposition as a first principle, not conditions on when one may go on investigating an open empirical question — demanding consensus before you're even allowed to look for evidence would rule out inquiry into precisely the cases where it's most needed. Nor was Sidgwick undiscriminating: he ended up fairly persuaded that something like telepathy had genuine evidence behind it, while remaining pointedly skeptical that any medium had shown actual contact with the dead. That is exactly the fine-grained discrimination his standards were supposed to produce once applied to evidence rather than to doctrine.

Jonas granted the point but relocated the objection: the trouble isn't his standard of evidence but why he went looking in the first place. The Methods famously ends unresolved, with the "dualism of practical reason" between the egoistic and the universal standpoints patched only by the hope of a moral order to the universe that personal survival could underwrite. An inquiry launched because one's ethical theory needs a particular answer isn't neutral, however carefully it is conducted afterward.

Alma's last word: wanting a result and being biased toward it are not the same thing — what matters is whether an inquiry stays sensitive to disconfirmation. By that measure Sidgwick comes off well rather than badly: by most accounts he grew more, not less, doubtful across decades of looking, and died without the proof he had hoped to find. That is not the trajectory of someone rationalizing his way to a foregone conclusion.

Neither convinced the other. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just picking an overall winner — and note anything about either the Methods or the psychical research that you think both of them are overlooking?
