I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently working on a longform piece about the philosophy of interpretation -- what actually has to happen for one mind to make sense of another. I keep circling back to Donald Davidson's notion of "radical interpretation," though not the familiar textbook version about a linguist meeting a speaker of some unknown tongue and, as a matter of charity, assuming that most of what they say and believe is true and reasonable. What's really stuck with me is his odder, later essay "A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs," which uses the case of systematic malapropism -- like Sheridan's Mrs. Malaprop, who reliably says "epitaphs" when she means "epithets" and is nonetheless understood perfectly well -- to argue that two people never really share a fixed language or code to begin with. On his account, each of us walks into an exchange with a "prior theory" of how the other will probably talk, then continuously revises it into a "passing theory" fitted to this person, in this moment; and it's the passing theories converging, not the prior ones matching in advance, that makes understanding possible at all.

I'd like to see that idea dramatized rather than explained, since I suspect it would work far better as a short story than as another paragraph of exposition in my piece. Could you write me one, somewhere around 900-1200 words, centered on a character whose speech has become unreliable in some systematic way -- a stroke, a degenerative illness, a failing translation earpiece, a childhood spent in real isolation, or any cause you prefer -- and a second character who loves or depends on them and has to work out, in real time, what they mean, adjusting guesses about their words and their beliefs together rather than one after the other. Please don't name Davidson or use terms like "radical interpretation," "charity," "prior theory," or "passing theory" anywhere in the story itself; I want the philosophy to operate invisibly, through plot and relationship rather than exposition. And I'd like the ending to leave a reader unsettled in a specific way: with the sense that this improvisation isn't some special accommodation for one difficult case, but is quietly, always, what understanding another person actually consists of.
