I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on vagueness, and while I followed the basic motivation for epistemicism and supervaluationism as rival responses to the sorites paradox, I'm still working out how the two views actually compare once you get past the introductory contrast. As I understand it, epistemicism (principally associated with Timothy Williamson, with roots in earlier work by Roy Sorensen) keeps classical logic and bivalence fully intact by insisting that predicates like "heap" or "bald" have perfectly sharp extensions: there is some exact number of grains, or of hairs, marking the cutoff, and our inability to ever know where it falls is just an unusually stubborn species of ignorance, rooted in the way meaning gets fixed by patterns of use. Supervaluationism (associated with Kit Fine and Rosanna Keefe, among others) instead treats a vague predicate as leaving open a range of equally legitimate ways of sharpening it ("precisifications"); a sentence is true only if it comes out true on every admissible precisification, false only if it comes out false on all of them, and otherwise neither, so borderline cases get a genuine truth-value gap rather than an unknown fact of the matter.

What I'd like is a comparison that pushes past that standard setup on two harder points where I can't tell who comes out ahead. First, higher-order vagueness: it seems just as vague where the clear cases of "heap" shade into borderline cases as it is where the borderline cases shade into clear non-heaps, and my sense is that epistemicists handle this by simply iterating the very same story -- unknowable sharp cutoffs, now backed by something like a margin-for-error principle -- at every level, whereas supervaluationists seem to need either a whole hierarchy of precisifications of precisifications or a "definitely" operator that promptly generates its own sorites series. Does one of these strategies clearly cost more than the other, or does it wash out once you look closely? Second, penumbral connections -- constraints like "whoever is taller than someone tall is also tall" that any admissible precisification is required to respect -- appear to be built into supervaluationist semantics as a further stipulation about which precisifications count as admissible, whereas epistemicists might get such connections for free, simply as classical entailments of a single, if unknown, sharp interpretation. Is that asymmetry genuine, and if so, does it favor epistemicism outright, or does epistemicism pay for it elsewhere -- for instance, in explaining why the unknown cutoff has to respect those connections at all, given that it is supposedly fixed by nothing more than patterns of linguistic use? Please work through both points in some depth and finish with an actual verdict on which view is in better overall shape, rather than a balanced list of considerations on each side.
