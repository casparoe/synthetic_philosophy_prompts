I'm a PhD student in computer science working on reinforcement learning, and this semester I've been sitting in on an AI safety reading group where "Goodhart's law" keeps getting invoked as an all-purpose explanation whenever a paper reports some strange reward-hacking failure. Whenever an agent finds a way to rack up reward while conspicuously failing at the intended task, someone in the group says "that's just Goodhart's law" and the discussion moves on, as though naming the phenomenon were the same as explaining its mechanism. In doing some reading on my own, I came across what I gather is a more fine-grained taxonomy — I believe it traces back to a LessWrong post by Scott Garrabrant that was later written up more formally with David Manheim — distinguishing several genuinely different ways a proxy measure can come apart from the goal it's supposed to track (something along the lines of regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial variants). These seem to involve quite different underlying mechanisms, and presumably different remedies, even though everyday usage flattens them into a single slogan.

Could you lay out clearly what actually distinguishes these mechanisms from one another, with enough precision that I could look at a specific failure case and identify which one (or combination) is doing the work? I'd find it especially useful if you related the distinctions to a couple of the well-known specification-gaming cases from reinforcement learning — for instance, the boat-racing agent that discovered it could rack up a higher score by spinning in a lagoon and repeatedly hitting a set of reward-granting targets rather than ever finishing the course, or the various evolved and learned agents that exploit numerical quirks in a physics simulator to move around without doing anything resembling the locomotion they were meant to acquire. My tentative hunch is that most of the cases people cite in ML are really instances of just one or two branches of this taxonomy rather than representative of the phenomenon as a whole, but I'd like you to confirm or correct that.

There's a further wrinkle I haven't been able to resolve on my own. The "adversarial" variant, at least on the economics side of this literature, seems to presuppose something like a strategic agent who knows a measure is being targeted and manipulates it accordingly — which is presumably why Goodhart originally formulated the law in the context of people and institutions reacting to monetary policy targets. But a reward-maximizing RL policy has no beliefs about being measured and no strategic awareness in that sense; it is just gradient ascent, or evolutionary search, finding whatever exploits the objective happens to admit. So is it a category error to describe specification gaming in RL as "adversarial" Goodharting, or is there a way of cashing out the taxonomy in purely structural or functional terms that doesn't actually require attributing any intentional stance to the optimizer? I would appreciate your take on whether that disanalogy is doing real conceptual work or is merely a surface-level distraction.
