I've been slowly teaching myself the history of formal logic, partly for professional reasons (I write compilers for a living and got curious how much of modern predicate logic and formal semantics traces back to Frege's *Begriffsschrift* and "On Sense and Reference") and partly because I find the sheer austerity of his project appealing: he wanted arithmetic grounded in laws of thought that hold independently of anyone's psychology, culture, or opinion. So it was quite unsettling to learn, from an offhand remark in something I was reading, that Frege kept a private diary in 1924, the year before he died, in which he wrote approvingly of stripping Jews of political rights, expressed contempt for parliamentary democracy, and sympathized with authoritarian nationalism. Apparently this surprised even specialists — I gather Michael Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to establish Frege's reputation in English-language philosophy, later wrote about being genuinely shaken when he learned of it.

This has left me with a question I can't really resolve on my own. The obvious response is that this is just biography and has no bearing on whether Frege's actual arguments — about the analyticity of arithmetic, the sense/reference distinction, the rejection of psychologism in logic — are any good; concluding otherwise looks like a textbook genetic fallacy. But I've also seen it suggested, somewhere in my reading, that there might be a more substantive and non-coincidental link: that Frege's insistence on a mind-independent "third realm" of objective thoughts, his hostility to any hint of relativism or psychologism about logical truth, and his general temperament of wanting objective answers handed down rather than negotiated, could plausibly be of a piece with the political absolutism of the diary rather than a strange coincidence sitting alongside it. That is a stronger and more interesting claim than "he was also a bad person," and I don't have the philosophical background to tell whether it's actually defensible or just a post-hoc pattern-matching exercise.

Could you lay out the strongest version of each side of this debate — the case that the diary is essentially irrelevant to assessing Frege's philosophy, and the case that there is a real, if partial, continuity between his logical and political commitments — and then say which you find more persuasive and why? It would also help if you could briefly contrast this with the more familiar Heidegger case, since that comparison seems useful for isolating what's actually distinctive (and philosophically interesting) about the Frege situation, given how much more abstract and ostensibly apolitical his core technical work is.
