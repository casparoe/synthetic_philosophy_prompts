For thirty-odd years I ran psychophysics experiments on contrast and motion perception; since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to the debate over the "cognitive penetrability" of perception. I have drafted the following notes toward a short essay and would value your comments.

My central claim is that a good number of the textbook demonstrations used to argue that belief, desire, or expectation can penetrate perception itself, rather than merely downstream judgment, trade on an ambiguity that experimentalists are trained to guard against: the difference between a change in sensitivity (d′) and a change in criterion (c). Bruner and Goodman's old finding that poor children overestimate the size of coins more than wealthy children do, and Proffitt's results that hills look steeper to tired or heavily laden observers, are routinely reported as though need and effort reach all the way into the percept. But nothing in a size- or slant-estimation task discriminates a visual system that genuinely represents a bigger coin or a steeper hill from one that represents the stimulus exactly as before while the observer, hungry or exhausted, simply becomes more willing to say or point to "bigger" or "steeper" under uncertainty. This seems close to what Firestone and Scholl are getting at when they fault so much of the "top-down effects" literature for conflating report with experience: wherever cognition shifts only the criterion, there is no cognitive penetration of perception in the sense anyone should care about, only of judgment.

I can already see one reply forming that I do not trust myself to assess: perhaps the sensitivity/criterion distinction is not fit to settle the philosophical question at all, since a criterion could in principle be reset by early, informationally encapsulated visual mechanisms rather than by late, deliberate ones, in which case a "mere" criterion shift might still be perceptual in the sense that matters. I also suspect there may be cases, binocular rivalry perhaps, where what is spontaneously seen, and not merely what is reported, seems to track meaning or familiarity, and which would therefore resist a sensitivity/criterion redescription altogether.

I would be grateful for your comments on three things: whether my gloss on Firestone and Scholl is fair to their actual argument, whether the sensitivity/criterion move is doing the philosophical work I want it to do, and which purported cases of cognitive penetration strike you as hardest for it to handle.
