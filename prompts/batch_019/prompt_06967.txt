I am drafting a novel in which two former PhD advisees, now estranged, meet again at a mechanistic-interpretability workshop and fall into an argument that outlasts the party. I would like to dramatize their disagreement as a proper dialogue.

Elena has spent her career training sparse autoencoders to pull "monosemantic" features out of superposition, and she talks about circuits and features the way a neuroscientist talks about neurons: as structures the network actually contains, waiting to be discovered. Marcus thinks this is a comforting fiction — his position is that any decomposition of a layer's activations into "features" is underdetermined by the network itself, since varying the sparsity penalty, dictionary size, or probing objective yields an equally predictive but non-isomorphic story, so nothing forces one carving of activation space to count as the real one.

Write their exchange as eight to twelve turns in which each responds to the other's actual argument rather than restating their own. Give Elena a real answer to the thought that "many equally good descriptions" refutes realism on its own — underdetermination arguments have a history of proving too much — and have Marcus press her on what could even count as evidence that a feature is really there, rather than a useful gloss on the network's behavior.
