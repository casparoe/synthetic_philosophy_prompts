While reading on my own about the economics of climate change, I came across the long-running dispute between Nicholas Stern and William Nordhaus over which discount rate to use when weighing costs borne today against benefits to people decades or centuries from now: Stern's team set the pure rate of time preference at essentially zero (about 0.1% a year, meant to capture only the small chance that there might be no one around in the future to benefit, not any ethical preference for the present), while Nordhaus argued for a rate several points higher, in line with real-world interest and savings behavior. I later learned that Stern's near-zero rate traces directly back to Frank Ramsey's 1928 paper on optimal saving, where Ramsey had already dismissed discounting people's future well-being simply because it lies further away as "ethically indefensible," chalking the practice up to nothing more than a weakness of the imagination. Ramsey died in 1930, decades before anyone had to weigh present-day sacrifices against climate damages centuries out, let alone against existential risks to humanity's entire future from things like advanced AI. Drawing on what you know of Ramsey's own ethical commitments and his ties to Moore and Keynes, where do you think he would have landed in the Stern-Nordhaus dispute, and would he have pushed his zero-discounting logic even further, toward today's arguments for prioritizing long-run existential risk over nearer-term concerns, or does something else in his thought suggest he would have resisted that extension?
