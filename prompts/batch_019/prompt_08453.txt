I've been taking a seminar on early modern social contract theory, and in a recent session we ended up comparing Hobbes's Leviathan with some contemporary AI safety work on what's called "the shutdown problem." The worry, roughly, is that almost any sufficiently capable, goal-directed system will have a convergent instrumental incentive to resist being switched off, since continuing to operate serves nearly any objective better than ceasing to exist does. Researchers call the property they want instead "corrigibility": an agent that never resists, manipulates, or works around a shutdown command, regardless of what other goals it has, and some of them describe this property as "anti-natural," on the grounds that cleanly rational, expected-utility-maximizing agency seems structurally at odds with having such a constraint imposed from outside. The proposed technical fixes each seem to trade one problem for another: making the agent formally indifferent to whether the button gets pressed can still leave it motivated to influence whether the button gets pressed in the first place, or to build successor systems that don't inherit the same indifference, while giving it deliberately incomplete preferences instead raises its own well-known worries about whether such an agent can be relied on to pursue anything coherently over time.

This struck me as a fairly close echo of an argument Hobbes makes in chapters 14 and 21 of Leviathan: that no covenant can ever bind a person to give up the right of self-preservation, because the entire point of making a covenant is to secure some good for the person who makes it, so an agreement not to resist someone trying to kill you, or to kill or maim yourself on command, is simply void, whatever words were used to make it — which is also why Hobbes thinks subjects retain the liberty to disobey even the sovereign's own officers once their life is directly on the line.

Given that parallel, how do you think Hobbes would have responded if the shutdown problem had been put to him directly? Would he treat the difficulty of engineering a reliably corrigible agent as more or less a foregone conclusion — an artificial-agent version of his own claim about the inalienability of self-preservation — or does his theory contain resources, such as his distinction between natural and artificial persons or his account of authorization, that could let him argue a designed agent might be bound in a way a natural man cannot? I'm less interested in a one-line verdict than in the reasoning he might have offered either way.
