I am preparing a graduate seminar on epistemic game theory, and there is a dispute in this literature I would like to think through carefully before I teach it. In "Backward Induction and Common Knowledge of Rationality" (Games and Economic Behavior, 1995), Robert Aumann proves, within his own semantic model of knowledge, that common knowledge of rationality among the players of a finite extensive-form game of perfect information — the centipede game is the standard illustration — suffices to guarantee that play follows the backward-induction path. Replying in the same journal, Ken Binmore objected that Aumann's notion of knowledge is partitional, and therefore never false, so the model has no room to represent what a player would be entitled to infer, off the equilibrium path, were a deviation actually observed; on Binmore's view, the proof presupposes rather than earns its conclusion. Philip Reny and others have pressed a related but distinct worry: that in sufficiently deep games, common knowledge of rationality cannot even be coherently posited at every node, since reaching certain nodes already presupposes that the assumption failed earlier in the game.

Write an essay that adjudicates this dispute. Explain precisely what Aumann's result does and does not establish, engage seriously with both objections above, and close by defending a definite verdict on whether common knowledge of rationality — properly understood — really does entail backward induction in games of perfect information. A qualified verdict is acceptable (for instance, that the entailment holds only given some further, specified assumption about belief revision off the path), but you must commit to a position rather than leave the matter open.
