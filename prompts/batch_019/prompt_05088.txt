I teach a graduate seminar on early modern British philosophy, and as a side project I co-host a modest podcast on the history of economic ideas. I've assigned Berkeley's The Querist for years as a curious footnote to his immaterialism, but next month's long-form episode is with a guest who thinks that "footnote" is actually the main event. Her whole research program rests on the claim that, beneath its coy, interrogative style — Berkeley never quite asserts anything in it, he only "queries" — the work hides a fully worked-out nominalist theory of money as a mere "ticket or counter" for reckoning claims on labor and goods, and that this theory is continuous with his immaterialist metaphysics rather than a separate bit of applied economics he happened to take up as a bishop worried about Irish poverty. Whenever a skeptical interviewer presses her on how a currency backed by nothing but public confidence differs from the paper schemes of John Law and the South Sea Company — follies still fresh in everyone's memory when Berkeley was writing — she waves the objection away by saying it still has "one foot in the bullion pit," meaning that it covertly assumes money needs a metallic soul to be real.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions that don't let her simply restate the thesis or coast on that line. Three things I want covered, each with a follow-up keyed to whichever answer she's likely to give: why Berkeley chose queries over assertions throughout the work, and whether that hedging reflects genuine Socratic caution or rhetorical insurance against being pinned down; the asymmetry in Berkeley's system whereby "industry" counts as real wealth while money is a mere token, given that on his own premises labor and its products are no less mind-dependent than a coin's exchange value; and a principled — not merely self-congratulatory — reason why Berkeley's own national-bank proposal escapes the very worries he raised about unbacked paper credit elsewhere. Close the sequence with a question testing how far the "ticket or counter" picture is meant to travel: does it straightforwardly vindicate a pure fiat currency with no tie at all to land, agriculture, or a redemption promise, or did Berkeley quietly build in restraints that such a currency lacks?
