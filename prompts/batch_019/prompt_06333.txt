A friend and I spent much of an evening arguing about how David Hume would have responded to Nelson Goodman's grue paradox, a puzzle he obviously never encountered himself. My friend maintains that Hume's psychologism lets him escape it: because resemblance, for Hume, is a felt and primitive relation among ideas rather than a logical one, custom would never form a habit of projecting a gerrymandered, time-indexed predicate like 'grue', so the new riddle of induction simply doesn't arise for him. I argued that this is too quick. Hume grounds resemblance itself in past constant conjunction, and 'green' and 'grue' agree on every emerald observed before the relevant future date, so nothing in his account of custom and habit explains why the mind should settle on the 'green'-based resemblance class rather than the 'grue'-based one; if anything, his theory seems to presuppose exactly the kind of privileged similarity that Goodman's puzzle calls into question. I also want to know whether, if Hume's talk of resemblance does end up privileging 'green', that just amounts to a psychological restatement of what Goodman called 'entrenchment', or whether it is doing some independent philosophical work of its own. Whose reading of Hume do you find more defensible, and how do you think Hume, confronted directly with Goodman's argument, would actually have responded?
