I am writing a novel built around one irrevocable decision: forty years before the story opens, a farmer sends his pregnant, unmarried daughter away, and she never comes home. The book is assembled from five accounts of that decision — the farmer's, the daughter's, a sibling's, a neighbor's, and the daughter's own son's — and they refuse to converge: none of the five is lying, self-serving, or mistaken about the facts, yet the act comes out, with equal force, as both a betrayal and a kind of integrity. My editor wants me to pick a verdict, or else write the whole approach off as an unreliable-narrator trick, and neither option feels honest to what the book is actually doing. What I cannot explain, even to myself, is how several flatly opposed characterizations of a single act could all be true at once without collapsing into "everyone has their own truth," which is not what I mean and would flatten the book if it were. Before I settle the novel's structure, I need to know whether that kind of layered truth can be made genuinely coherent, and on what grounds — or whether my editor is right that I am only dressing up a structural indecision as depth.
