Two weeks ago an early reader posted a long, gleeful thread eviscerating an advance copy of my forthcoming novel — misquoting several passages, flattening a deliberately unreliable narrator into a simple authorial failing, and inviting a pile-on that within a day had produced hundreds of quote-posts from people who had evidently never opened the book. A friend who teaches philosophy offered the dubious comfort of an analogy: she said I had just lived through a miniature version of the Corsair affair, after the Copenhagen satirical weekly that spent much of 1846 lampooning Kierkegaard's appearance and mannerisms in print, apparently in retaliation for a quarrel he had picked with one of its contributors. She pointed me to Two Ages, the literary review he published in that same stretch of months, and in particular to its long closing section, usually excerpted on its own as "The Present Age," where he distinguishes "the public" — a phantom readership summoned into being by the press, capable of rendering collective verdicts that no single member of it would ever sign in his own name — from a genuine association or community, whose members remain answerable to one another as individuals.

Having now read that section more carefully than the average pull-quote about Kierkegaard and modernity requires, I've noticed his argument is considerably more specific than the generic "the internet is corrosive" moral people tend to extract from it. It turns on a handful of moving parts: levelling as a social process distinct from mere criticism, the present age's substitution of reflection and chatter for passion and decisive action, and envy functioning as the quiet, non-confrontational engine that lets a public tear individuals down without anyone in particular having to act as an aggressor.

What I'd like from you is not a biography of Kierkegaard or a survey of existentialism, but the most important implications of that specific diagnosis — the public/association distinction, levelling, reflection without passion, anonymous envy — for how a working novelist today should think about being discussed by an anonymous, algorithmically amplified readership. I'm especially interested in three things: where his analysis genuinely predicts something true about how pile-ons of this kind actually behave; where it fails to transfer cleanly, given that today's "public" is often quite identifiable and organized rather than anonymous and formless in the way his was; and whether his own conduct during the Corsair affair, as distinct from his theory of it, offers any usable model — good or cautionary — for how to respond, or deliberately not respond, when it happens to you.
