I'm reporting a feature on why fact-checking so rarely changes anyone's mind, and I keep running into pop-philosophy invocations of Wittgenstein's "hinge propositions" from On Certainty: the idea that convictions like "the earth has existed for a long time" or "my senses are basically reliable" aren't held on evidence at all, but function as the groundless scaffolding that makes reasoning possible in the first place. This popularized version worries me, because if everyone's deepest convictions are equally groundless hinges, it seems to erase any difference between someone who trusts their senses and someone for whom "vaccines cause autism" plays exactly the same structural role in their thinking. Philosophers who actually work on hinge epistemology, I've learned, split on whether this worry can be answered from within the framework itself: some hold that accepting a hinge is still a rationally assessable act, since we are "entitled" to certain hinges in a specific, non-evidential but normatively constrained sense that not just any conviction can satisfy; others hold that hinges sit entirely outside the space of epistemic evaluation, neither justified nor unjustified, merely the bedrock shown in what we do, which seems to leave no principled way to rank one bedrock certainty above another. Could you write me a dialogue, set backstage after a public panel on misinformation, between an entitlement theorist and a quietist about hinges, as they argue out whether hinge epistemology can actually distinguish a legitimate hinge from an illegitimate one? Neither should land an easy win: each should have to concede a real point before pushing back, and each should be answering what the other has actually just said rather than a caricature of it.
