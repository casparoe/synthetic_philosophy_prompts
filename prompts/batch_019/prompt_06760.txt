Over the past year I've sat through more AI-conference keynotes than I can count in which speakers casually describe the shift from symbolic, rule-based "good old-fashioned AI" to deep learning and now large language models as a "Kuhnian paradigm shift," complete with claims that the two camps are "incommensurable" because they can no longer agree on what would even count as evidence of genuine understanding. The comparison has become so routine that, for a piece I'm writing on how scientific vocabulary gets borrowed and flattened once it leaves academia, I went back to Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions and its 1969 Postscript instead of relying on the secondhand version everyone quotes at me.

Two things stood out on the reread. First, Kuhn was notably guarded about how far his machinery traveled beyond mature, quantitative sciences like physics and chemistry: by his own account, the idea partly grew out of a year spent among social scientists whose constant, unresolved disputes over basic methods and problems struck him as evidence of a field that had never achieved a paradigm at all, rather than one undergoing revolution. Second, "incommensurability" for him was a fairly specific claim about breakdowns in shared lexicons and shared standards of puzzle-solving, not just a dramatic way of saying two camps talk past each other.

That leaves me wanting a sturdier answer than the keynote-circuit version before I put anything in print, and since Kuhn died in 1996, well before deep learning's resurgence and the current large-language-model boom, I obviously can't just ask him. Working strictly from his own published criteria rather than the loosened, popular sense of his terms: do you think Kuhn would have granted that computer science and AI research were even the kind of enterprise capable of having a paradigm in his sense, or would he more likely have placed the field, at least historically, alongside the social sciences as "pre-paradigmatic" and marked by competing schools rather than shared exemplars — or declined to apply the model at all, on the grounds that AI is as much engineering as science? And if you think he'd have granted it paradigm status, does the move from symbolic AI to connectionist deep learning and transformer-based language models plausibly meet his bar for a genuine revolution with real incommensurability — for instance, in the live dispute between researchers like Gary Marcus and deep-learning maximalists over what "understanding" or "explanation" even means — or would he more likely have redescribed it in deflationary terms, as a change of tools and exemplary problems within a broader continuity, or as one more oscillation between competing schools in a field that, on his own criteria, never became paradigm-governed to begin with?
