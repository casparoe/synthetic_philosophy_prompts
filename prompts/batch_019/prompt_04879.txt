I spent almost forty years doing condensed matter physics before I retired, and lately I've been filling the extra time with philosophy of mind, partly because I always felt a bit uneasy about how casually physicists wave their hands at "emergence" and wanted to see whether philosophers do any better. Right now I'm chewing my way through Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism, using a secondhand copy of Mind in a Physical World I picked up at a used bookstore. I brought it up at a little philosophy reading group I joined at the local library and mostly got blank stares, so I'm hoping you have more patience. I think I've got the bones of the argument: the physical world is causally closed, we don't want to say every action is massively overdetermined by two independent sufficient causes, so if a mental property is genuinely distinct from the physical property that realizes it, the physical realizer seems to do all the causal work, and the mental property gets "excluded" unless it just reduces to the physical after all.

Here's my hang-up, and it's the kind of thing that would bother a physicist. Doesn't this same argument threaten more or less every macro-level causal claim in science, not just mental causation? When I say the gas's temperature caused the balloon to expand, that's a higher-level property multiply realized by an enormous number of possible microstates -- structurally that looks just like the mental/physical case Kim is worried about, yet nobody thinks temperature is epiphenomenal. I've seen this generalization worry described somewhere as causal powers "draining away" up through the levels, which is a nice dramatic phrase, but I don't actually understand the mechanics of why Kim thinks he can stop the drain at temperature and fitness while mental causation, or at least phenomenal experience, keeps leaking away. My vague understanding is it hinges on which properties can be functionally reduced and which can't, but I don't have a solid grip on why that move is suppose to save macro-causation generally without also saving the mental. Could you walk me through that generalization worry properly, and then unpack the main rival move I keep seeing cited against Kims argument -- Yablo's idea that mental and physical properties stand in something like a determinate/determinable relation rather than competing as two independent causes -- and tell me honestly whether that solution actually earns its keep or just relocates the problem one level down?
