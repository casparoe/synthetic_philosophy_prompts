Three weeks ago I started as a research assistant on an AI governance project, and in my very first team meeting I watched two colleagues talk past each other for the better part of an hour over something I have since learned falls under the heading of "coherence arguments" for expected utility maximization. One of them treated a cluster of worrying behaviors in advanced AI systems — resisting shutdown, hoarding resources, resisting any change to its objective — as close to mathematically guaranteed. Her reasoning went roughly as follows: since an agent with cyclic preferences can be money-pumped out of arbitrary amounts of value, optimization pressure should push any sufficiently capable system toward transitive preferences; she then argued, more ambitiously, that similar pressure forces completeness as well, so that the system ends up satisfying the full Von Neumann–Morgenstern package and becomes representable as maximizing expected utility with respect to some fixed utility function, at which point those instrumentally convergent subgoals fall out automatically, regardless of what the system is actually trained to do.

I did not have a good response in the moment, but I have since come across the objection that this whole inference is considerably shakier than it sounds, and I would like help turning that hunch into an actual argument before the topic inevitably comes up again. Please argue for the claim that coherence arguments, on their own, do not entail that a sufficiently advanced AI system will display stable, goal-directed, expected-utility-maximizing behavior together with the instrumentally convergent subgoals usually associated with it. In building the case, please address both (a) why being representable as maximizing some utility function is too weak a conclusion to license predictions about specific behaviors such as power-seeking, given that a sufficiently gerrymandered utility function can rationalize almost any non-cyclic pattern of choices, and (b) whether an agent with merely incomplete, rather than intransitive, preferences can escape the money-pump argument that is supposed to force it toward full comparability among all of its options.
