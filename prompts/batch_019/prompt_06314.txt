My sister is finishing her chief year in emergency medicine, and in a few weeks she's giving the short "charge to the graduates" address at her residency's graduation dinner — the last thing said before the graduating class gets their certificates and scatters to fellowships and attending jobs across the country. She called me, half-panicked about what to say, because I'm the family's philosophy PhD (my dissertation is on moral responsibility, and I've spent the better part of the last year living inside Nagel's and Williams's papers on moral luck). The detail she gave me that I can't stop turning over: two residents in the class above hers made, within the same month, essentially the same judgment call on a similar borderline presentation — one patient coded and later died, the other recovered without incident — and the department's subsequent treatment of the two residents (the tenor of the M&M conference, who got quietly flagged for "clinical judgment concerns" versus who got praised as decisive) tracked how the patient happened to turn out far more than it tracked the reasoning behind the call at the time it was made.

Could you draft the address for her? It should run about five minutes read aloud. The room will be genuinely mixed — thirty-odd graduating residents who have just spent three years watching their judgment get reinterpreted after the fact by outcomes they didn't control, plus parents, partners, and a couple of program directors, at least one of whom did a bioethics fellowship and will recognize exactly what's being done if it's done well. She isn't allowed to say the words "moral luck" from the podium, and it can't read like a philosophy lecture in a white coat — but I want the speech itself structured around the actual problem, not just gesturing at it: the gap between what was in these doctors' control and what they will be held responsible for, and made to feel responsible for, including something that does the quiet work of Williams's point that even a blameless agent can owe an outcome a kind of regret no spectator owes it. The hard part is that the speech can't resolve into either of two easy lies — that their training will make the world track their effort fairly, which it won't, or that nothing they decide matters and only the luck does, which would be false and a genuinely bad thing to send new doctors into practice believing. Please write the full text.
