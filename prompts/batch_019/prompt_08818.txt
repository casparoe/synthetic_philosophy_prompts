Free-will threads have a way of resurfacing every few months on the rationalist forums I frequent, and the most recent one settled, as it usually does, on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. The core claim is that being truly (or "ultimately") responsible for what you do requires being truly responsible for the way you are, mentally speaking, which in turn requires having authored that character out of some prior character you never chose; the buck never actually stops, so true moral responsibility turns out to be impossible whether or not determinism holds. The usual rejoinders were duly rehearsed: Dennett's insistence that ordinary praise and blame never needed anything as demanding as ultimate responsibility in the first place, Frankfurt-style mesh theories on which a self becomes authoritative once higher-order desires endorse the lower-order ones they govern, Kane-style appeals to indeterministic self-forming actions. Strawson has a stock answer to all of them: whichever candidate "self" you nominate as the source of the action, that self was itself handed to you by luck, so it cannot be doing the responsibility-grounding work you need it to do.

Nobody in the thread raised what strikes me as the most natural comparison. Strawson's regress is structurally identical to the regress of justification that generates Agrippa's trilemma in epistemology, where any reason offered for a belief itself stands in need of a further reason, on pain of infinite regress, circularity, or an arbitrary stopping point — and most epistemologists regard foundationalism (or reliabilism) as a perfectly respectable way to halt that regress without sliding into global skepticism about knowledge. Please argue that an exactly parallel "compatibilist foundationalism" about character, on which certain facts about who an agent is can ground responsibility without themselves needing to have been self-authored, deserves the same courtesy, and that Strawson has not actually supplied a principled reason for treating the two regresses asymmetrically. In making the case, address directly the most obvious way Strawson could resist the analogy: that authorship, rather than mere reliable connection to some truth-conducive or otherwise good-making property, is specifically what moral responsibility requires.
