In my philosophy of probability seminar, we've been working through Bertrand's paradox as a challenge to the classical principle of indifference: depending on which method of generating "a random chord" is treated as privileged, the very same problem yields incompatible values (1/3, 1/2, or 1/4) for the probability that the chord is longer than a side of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle. E.T. Jaynes argued, in "The Well-Posed Problem," that the paradox arises because the problem statement leaves the circle's size and position unfixed, and that any admissible probability assignment must therefore be invariant under exactly the transformations -- translation, rotation, and change of scale -- that the statement leaves open.

Compare Jaynes's invariance requirement to the classical principle of indifference it is meant to rescue. Is invariance a repaired version of the same underlying idea -- treat symmetric possibilities symmetrically -- or a substantively different justificatory principle that merely agrees with naive indifference in easy cases while diverging from it in hard ones like Bertrand's? Defend your answer, and say what it implies about whether genuinely "objective" prior probabilities can be assigned at all, or whether every principle of indifference is secretly relative to a choice of description.
