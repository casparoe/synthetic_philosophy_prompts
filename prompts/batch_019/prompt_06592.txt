I've been down a research rabbit hole on the Galileo affair for a piece I'm writing about how the 'science vs. religion' story gets oversimplified, and it turned into a whole thing at dinner with two friends, Dan (a lapsed physics PhD) and Mara (finishing a history of science PhD), who ended up arguing about who actually had the stronger position in 1615: Galileo, in his letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, or Cardinal Bellarmine, in his letter to Foscarini. I couldn't settle it, so I'm bringing it to you.

Dan started by saying Galileo basically won on principle: his line (quoting some earlier churchman) that scripture teaches 'how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go' settles the matter, because if scripture's job is moral and spiritual rather than astronomical, theres no good reason to let a literal reading of Joshua stopping the sun override what nature actually shows. Mara pushed back that this is anachronistic: in 1615 Galileo did not have an actual demonstration that the earth moves, his 'proof' from the tides (which he leaned on later, in the Dialogue) turned out to be wrong, and nobody measured stellar parallax until the 1800s -- so Bellarmine's position in his letter, that Copernicanism could be taught as a useful calculating device until someone actually proved it, was the careful position, not obstruction. Dan came back that Bellarmine's proof-standard was a trap: the letter says that merely 'saving the appearances' isn't proof, because some other, not-yet-thought-of arrangement might save them just as well -- a standard demanding enough to reject any physical hypothesis forever, while a literal reading of scripture never has to clear that same bar. Mara said that's not quite fair either, since Bellarmine explicitly wrote that if real proof ever turned up, they'd have to go back and reinterpret the passages, and that it'd be better to admit we don't understand a passage than call a demonstrated truth false -- so it wasn't a permanent trump card, just a sequencing claim, and anyway Galileo's own principle, that 'necessary demonstrations' override the literal sense, just punts on who decides what counts as 'necessary,' which is exactly what they were fighting about in the first place. Dan's last word was that whatever you think of the letter itself, the actual institutional response wasn't 'let's wait for better evidence' -- Galileo was formally ordered in 1616 not to hold or defend the theory in any way, and was tried for it in 1633 -- which is a lot more heavy-handed than the measured tone of the letter to Foscarini suggests.

Nobody budged and we just moved on to dessert. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage -- not just an overall winner -- and point out anything you think both of them are missing?
