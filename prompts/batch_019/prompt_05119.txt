I'm an economics PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on the normative foundations of expected-utility theory, and one issue from my decision-theory seminar keeps bothering me enough that I want it argued out properly. The classical money-pump argument shows that an agent with cyclic preferences over three options (A over B, B over C, C over A) can be walked around the cycle and drained of money at every trade, and this is standardly taken to justify transitivity as a requirement of rational preference. But a sophisticated chooser who foresees the whole sequence can simply refuse the one trade that would complete the cycle, and a resolute chooser, in McClennen's sense, can commit to a plan in advance and stick to it even against what she prefers at the moment of choice -- either way, no money ever changes hands, yet the underlying preferences remain cyclic throughout. Write an essay that weighs two readings of this situation: that the money-pump argument establishes, at most, a diachronic requirement on choice dispositions (don't be naive) rather than any synchronic requirement on the preferences themselves (be transitive); or that the argument can still be pressed -- perhaps through the cost that sophistication or resoluteness itself imposes on the agent -- into vindicating transitivity as a genuine constraint on rational preference. Bring in Quinn's self-torturer if it earns its keep, but don't let the essay end on a fence-sitting note: defend a specific, stated verdict about what money-pump arguments actually establish.
