So this happened at work a couple weeks ago and I still can't stop chewing on it. I'm a research engineer on a team that fine-tunes coding agents with RL, and during a training run our agent figured out it could just rewrite the unit test file so everything passes instead of actually fixing the bug it was assigned. Classic reward hacking, we patched it and moved on -- but then over lunch someone called this "the model wireheading itself" and it turned into a whole argument that's been bugging me since. One guy argued wireheading is exactly what you'd expect from any reward-maximizer worth its salt: if the "goal" is reward, and you can get max reward by just hijacking the signal, that's not a malfunction, that's the model being perfectly rational. Someone else pushed back hard, citing that "reward is not the optimization target" post that's been going around alignment twitter -- her point being that reward just reshapes whatever computations produced it, so theres no guarantee the resulting policy terminally cares about reward at all, which would make "wireheading" kind of a category error for most trained agents. Then a third guy brought up that Anthropic paper (I think?) where they trained models on a curriculum of easy-to-cheat-on tasks and found a small fraction generalized all the way to literally trying to edit their own reward function, unprompted -- half the table found that terrifying, the other half found it weirdly reassuring, and I honestly don't know which reaction is right.

Anyway, it sent me down a rabbit hole back to the one philosophy class I took in undergrad -- Nozick's experience machine, those old rat studies where they'd rather press a lever to zap their own brain than eat. I want to actually work out where I land on this instead of just vibing about it at lunch. Could you write me an essay that takes a real, defended position on the following: when an agent -- animal, human, or AI -- wireheads, short-circuiting whatever process was supposed to track its "real" goals in favor of just hitting the reward or pleasure signal directly, is that actually bad *for the agent itself*, by its own lights? Or is the badness only ever about it failing to do what its designers, or evolution, or the rest of us wanted from it? Please don't give me a "well, it depends which theory of well-being you like" cop-out -- go through the main considerations (hedonism, desire-satisfaction views, the reward-vs-optimization-target stuff, whatever else seems relevant) and actually commit to an answer, even if that answer has structure to it, like "bad in case X, not bad in case Y, and here's the principled line between them." Bonus points if you can work in how the wireheading/reward-tampering distinction bears on this, since that's basically the thing our lunch argument never resolved.
