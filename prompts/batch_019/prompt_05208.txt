I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and for the last couple of weeks a reading group I'm part of has been chewing through Nover and Hájek's "Vexing Expectations" and the responses it provoked from Colyvan, Easwaran, and others. Almost no one I've mentioned it to outside the group has heard of the Pasadena game, which is part of why I'd like to see the disagreement dramatized rather than summarized yet again — watching two people who each find the other's position obviously mistaken argue it out seems like a good way to figure out which considerations are actually doing the work.

Here is the setup, in case it helps to have it on hand: in the Pasadena game, you toss a fair coin until it first lands heads, on toss number n, and you are then paid (-1)^(n-1) × 2^n/n dollars — so you win $2 with probability 1/2, lose $2 with probability 1/4, win $8/3 with probability 1/8, and so on, forever. Summed in the order the tosses actually happen, the probability-weighted payoffs form the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2. But that series is only conditionally convergent, not absolutely convergent, so the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees that its terms can be reordered to sum to any real number you like, or to diverge to positive or negative infinity, without changing a single probability or payoff in the game itself. Nover and Hájek take this to show that expected utility theory falls silent exactly where it ought to speak: we surely want to say, for instance, that "Altadena" — the same game with every payoff raised by a dollar — is better than Pasadena, since it dominates it in every possible outcome, yet if neither game has a well-defined expectation, orthodox theory cannot even underwrite that comparison.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists who disagree sharply about what to conclude from this? One should argue that the paradox is a first-class problem — that it exposes a genuine defect in expected utility theory as a general normative theory of choice under risk, serious enough to demand new axioms or a replacement decision rule (relative or comparative expected value, "weak" expectations, dominance reasoning, or whatever else they want to reach for). The other should argue that the puzzle can be contained without any such revision — whether by restricting expected utility theory to gambles whose payoff series converge absolutely, by insisting that there is after all a privileged order of summation (namely, the order in which the coin tosses actually happen), or by denying that our dominance intuitions about exotic infinite gambles are trustworthy enough to override the formalism in the first place. I want the two of them to really engage with each other's specific moves rather than talk past one another: if one leans on the privileged-ordering response, the other needs to say something substantive about why the standard notion of expectation resists it, and so on for several rounds. Please don't let either character be the obvious loser — I would like to come away having watched a real, close philosophical fight rather than a strawman getting quietly dismantled. If it helps to give them a concrete occasion — a seminar room, a long drive back from a conference, even a bookmaker who has just offered to run the game for real and wants an answer — feel free, but the substance matters far more to me than the scene-setting.
