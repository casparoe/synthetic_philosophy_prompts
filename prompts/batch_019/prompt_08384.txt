I'm part of a small reading group that has been working through classic puzzles in decision theory this term, and two weeks ago we covered Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle. Afterwards a few of us went for a drink, and two of the group — Nadia and Ben — ended up arguing about it for nearly two hours while the rest of us just watched; I ended up driving them both home afterward and got the whole argument replayed at me from the back seat, so I've had plenty of time to turn it over since. For anyone who needs the setup restated: an eccentric billionaire puts a vial of toxin in front of you. Drinking it won't kill you or leave any lasting damage, but it will make you painfully sick for a full day. He tells you that if, by midnight tonight, you form the genuine intention to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon, he will transfer a million dollars into your account before tomorrow even arrives — and he can somehow tell, without fail, whether you've really formed that intention or are merely pretending to. You don't need to actually drink the toxin to collect; the intention alone is what's paid for, and by the time tomorrow afternoon comes, the money question has already been settled one way or the other. The puzzle is that forming the intention looks obviously worth it, and yet it's not clear you can actually manage it, since you already know that tomorrow, drinking will get you nothing further and will only cost you a day of misery — so it seems you can already tell, now, that you won't go through with it, which makes it doubtful you can sincerely intend to.

Nadia and Ben's disagreement went roughly like this. (1) Nadia said the puzzle dissolves once you notice that people form intentions to do unpleasant things and then carry them out all the time — keeping a hard promise, showing up to the dentist — even though there's nothing further to gain at the moment of actually doing the unpleasant thing; drinking the toxin is no different, so you just resolve, sincerely, and then follow through. (2) Ben answered that those comparisons smuggle in something the toxin case specifically removes: when you keep a promise or go to the dentist, there is still something at stake at the moment of performance — your teeth, someone's trust in you, your own future dealings with them. The billionaire is a stranger you'll never see again, and the money is already decided one way or the other by the time you'd drink the toxin, so tomorrow-you has been built to have nothing left to gain from following through; doing it anyway isn't resolve, it's acting for no reason at all, which isn't what we mean by doing something because you intended to. (3) Nadia replied that this proves too much, because deciding-in-advance obviously can generate reasons later — that's exactly how self-binding works, as with Ulysses ordering his crew to tie him to the mast so he wouldn't be able to give in to the Sirens' song later. If a past decision can't supply a reason to act on it later, this kind of case becomes mysterious too, and yet people clearly do bind themselves like this successfully. (4) Ben's response was that Ulysses actually supports his own view rather than hers: Ulysses doesn't trust his future self to honor a mere resolution once the Sirens are singing, which is exactly why he has himself physically restrained rather than just firmly resolving not to give in. That, Ben said, is a concession that bare intention isn't enough to guarantee rational follow-through once the later self has nothing to gain and everything to lose — an external device is doing all the real work, not the intention. (5) Nadia's last word was that Ben's whole picture assumes rationality has to be recalculated from scratch at every single moment, with no weight given to your own prior decisions; she said an agent who reasoned that way — discarding every past commitment the instant it stops paying off locally — would actually do worse over a lifetime than someone capable of sticking to resolutions, so it's this picture of moment-by-moment rationality that should be rejected, not the possibility of sincerely intending to drink the toxin.

Neither of them budged, and honestly both of them said things over the course of the night that struck me as genuinely sharp. Could you go through the exchange move by move — point (1) through (5) — and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether you think there's a bigger consideration that both of them are overlooking, and what you take the toxin puzzle actually to show about the relationship between intending to do something and having a reason to do it.
