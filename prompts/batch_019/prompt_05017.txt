Ok so this has been rattling around in my head for like a week and my rationalist-adjacent group chat was zero help, so I'm bringing it to you instead. I'm most of the way through Jaynes' Probability Theory: The Logic of Science (the book half of LessWrong quotes without ever finishing, myself very much included), and I hit the part where he actually tries to dissolve Bertrand's paradox instead of just shrugging at it the way my old intro stats class did. Quick recap in case it's fuzzy: you ask for the probability that a "random chord" of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and depending on whether you generate the chord from two uniform random points on the circumference, a uniform random point along a radius (drawing the perpendicular chord through it), or a uniform random point inside the disk, taken as the chord's midpoint, you get three different "obviously correct" answers -- 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 respectively. The principle of indifference tells you to spread your credence equally over "the possibilities," but it never tells you which of these sampling setups counts as the possibilities, so the exact same innocent-sounding instruction hands you three confident, contradictory answers.

Jaynes' fix (I think it's from his '73 paper, something like "the well-posed problem," but don't quote me on the title) is to say the question as usually posed is simply underspecified, and that once you demand the answer be invariant under the symmetries the problem statement itself hands you -- translation, rotation, and rescaling of the circle -- only one of the three setups survives, so the paradox dissolves once your careful about what you're actually asking. What I can't shake is the suspicion that this just relocates the original problem one level up: instead of arguing over which variable to be indifferent over, you're now arguing over which transformation group is the "natural" one for the problem, and geometry might just be the one domain where that group happens to get handed to you for free. So here's what I actually want from you: compare the classical principle of indifference with Jaynes' invariance move in some real depth. Is he offering a genuinely different, non-circular way of fixing priors, or is "require invariance under the obvious symmetry group" just the principle of indifference wearing a group-theory costume? And as a bonus round -- does this trick have any hope of working in places with no geometry to exploit, like choosing a reference class in anthropic reasoning (Sleeping Beauty, the Doomsday argument, that whole family of problems), or is that exactly the kind of case that shows the circle version got lucky?
