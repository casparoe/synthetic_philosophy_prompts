I've spent the past several months reporting on the more philosophically minded corners of AI-safety research, and one puzzle keeps pulling me back to Gödel. It resurfaced again last week when a computer scientist I was interviewing mentioned, almost as an aside, that the technical obstacle he had just spent an hour explaining to me is a direct descendant of Gödel's own incompleteness results.

The problem, known in that literature as the "Löbian obstacle," runs roughly as follows: an agent that trusts only those conclusions it can formally prove within some fixed proof system cannot, in general, also prove that its future self, or a successor built on the very same axioms, will act only on conclusions that are actually true — because any attempt to formalize that trust runs straight into Löb's theorem, a result that threatens to let the system "prove" its own reliability in a way that collapses into proving absolutely anything. Researchers associated with the Machine Intelligence Research Institute have used this to argue that self-modifying AI systems face a genuine formal barrier to certifying their successors, one that seems to force either an infinite regress of ever-stronger proof systems vouching for one another, somewhat reminiscent of Turing's and Feferman's older progressions of theories built by iterating consistency statements, or some fundamentally different, non-proof-theoretic notion of trust.

What I can't shake is the question of what Gödel himself would have made of all this, given that Löb's theorem is essentially a generalization of his own second incompleteness theorem, and given how insistent he was, most famously in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, that the mathematical reasoning of the human mind cannot be exhausted by any single fixed formal system, or "machine." Would he have read the Löbian obstacle as one more confirmation of his lifelong anti-mechanist convictions — evidence that no formal agent, however it modifies itself, could ever fully close the loop on trusting itself? Or would he have resisted the whole analogy between minds and self-modifying, proof-using machines that the AI-safety literature seems to presuppose? I would be grateful for your best-informed reconstruction of the position Gödel would most plausibly have taken, anchored as closely as possible to his actual documented philosophical commitments rather than to whatever merely sounds Gödel-esque.
