Two months ago I confronted my older brother at a family dinner about the way he keeps "borrowing" money from our elderly mother and never paying it back, and the fallout has been worse than the original problem. Nobody in the family disputes that what he's doing is wrong; the trouble is that he pointed out, correctly, that I did much the same thing to our mother for the better part of two years in my late twenties, before I sorted my finances out. Half the family now thinks I had no business raising the issue at all — my aunt's line was that I'm "the last person who gets to lecture him" — and I've been turning that thought over ever since. It turns out this is an actual live question in moral philosophy under the heading of "standing to blame," rather than just a debating trick people reach for to change the subject, and I'd like to understand it properly rather than go on gut feeling.

What I'm hoping you can do is walk me through the shape of that debate rather than just define hypocrisy for me. I've come across G.A. Cohen's essay asking who has the standing to condemn terrorists, and I think there's a later paper by R. Jay Wallace that ties standing to blame to some idea of addressing another person as a moral equal; from what little I understand, these writers don't agree on why hypocrisy would matter even if the underlying accusation is true. Some seem to treat "lacking standing" as a distinct normative disability, almost like losing legal standing to sue, that's separate from whether the blame itself is deserved; others apparently think that framing is overwrought and that what's really happening is just ordinary evidence of the blamer's insincerity or discredited authority. I'd also like to understand how philosophers distinguish genuine hypocrisy from mere past inconsistency, since I've actually reformed rather than currently doing what my brother does, and whether the accusation "you're a hypocrite" is ever more than the tu quoque fallacy wearing a nicer outfit. I'm less interested in being told whether I was right to speak up than in getting the conceptual map correct: the main positions on offer, the strongest case for thinking hypocrites genuinely lose something when they blame, and the best objections to that case.
