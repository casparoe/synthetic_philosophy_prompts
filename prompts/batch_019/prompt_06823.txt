Ok so I've fallen down a bit of a YouTube rabbit hole on quantum entanglement lately, and I thought I'd finally cracked the whole Bell's theorem thing. The way I understood it after watching like five different explainer videos: Bell came up with a math proof that no "local" theory (nothing secretly sending signals faster than light) could ever match what quantum mechanics actually predicts, then Clauser and Aspect and Zeilinger spent decades running the experiments, the results lined up with QM and not the local theories, and that's apparently why they split the Nobel prize a couple years back. So to me that seemed like the end of the story — nonlocality, aka "spooky action at a distance," is now just a confirmed fact about reality, same status as any other experimentally verified bit of physics, case closed.

Except then I watched a video by this physicist named Sabine Hossenfelder (not sure I'm spelling that right) where she says that conclusion is too fast, because theres a "superdeterminism" idea that lets you keep locality completely intact as long as you're willing to give up something else instead, and separately I've seen other people say you can dodge nonlocality by giving up "realism" instead. That really threw me, because I thought Bell's theorem plus all those experiments were supposed to have settled this, not left it as some menu where physicists just pick whichever assumption they personally find least annoying to ditch. So which is it — is nonlocality actually a proven fact of nature at this point, or isn't it, and if its not, what exactly did Bell and those Nobel-winning experiments actually nail down?
