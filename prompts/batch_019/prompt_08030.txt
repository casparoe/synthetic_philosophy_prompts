After a career in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading the philosophy I never had time for, and I have become fascinated by social choice theory as one of the few corners of political philosophy that yields genuine theorems. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that once we require at least three possible outcomes, allow voters to hold any preferences whatsoever, and rule out any single dictator, no voting rule can guarantee that sincere voting is always a voter's best strategy. This struck me as a sharper descendant of the cyclical-majority paradox Condorcet identified two centuries earlier—and yet Condorcet remained confident that well-designed procedures, run by an informed citizenry, could reliably track the truth about matters of common concern, an optimism his jury theorem was meant to underwrite. Setting the anachronism aside: how do you think Condorcet would have reacted to being shown that his own preferred method of pairwise comparison is no exception to the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result? Would he have regarded it as fatal to his larger ambitions for a mathematically grounded politics, or might he have found, somewhere in his own thought, a way to argue that his hopes survive largely intact? Defend a specific answer, grounded in particular elements of his work, rather than a general statement of his optimism.
