I'm putting together a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, and one particular week's session has been giving me trouble. It pits David Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation against the interventionist approach that grew out of the causal-modeling literature associated with Woodward and, behind him, Pearl. On the surface the two can look like variations on a single idea, since both ultimately cash out claims like "C caused E" in terms of some counterfactual along the lines of "had C not occurred, E would not have occurred either." In past years, students have tended to treat interventionism as basically a technical refinement of Lewis's view rather than a genuinely distinct theory of what causation is, and this time I'd like to push back on that without setting up a straw man on either side.

Could you help me work out, in some detail, what actually separates the two positions once you get past the shared counterfactual vocabulary? I'm especially interested in three things: first, how each theory handles classic cases of redundant causation—say, two shooters where either bullet would have killed the victim on its own, but one arrives a fraction of a second before the other—given that Lewis had to substantially revise his own 1973 account to cope with cases exactly like this, while the structural-equations framework is usually advertised as handling them more gracefully; second, whether grounding counterfactuals in idealized interventions on the variables of a causal model actually earns interventionism that advantage, or whether it just relocates the difficulty into the prior choice of variables and equations; and third, what we should make of the fact that Lewis wanted a fully reductive analysis of causation in non-causal terms, while Woodward seems quite willing to take a causal notion like "intervention" as already understood. If, after working through all this, you conclude that the two views end up closer together than my framing suggests, I'd genuinely like to hear that too—I just want to get past the one-paragraph textbook version of the contrast before I stand in front of a room and present it as settled.
