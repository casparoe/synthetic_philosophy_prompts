A few colleagues and I have an informal lunchtime reading group — mostly engineers with no real philosophy training — and we've been working through Louis Menand's "The Metaphysical Club," which sent me back to the primary source for Peirce's account of truth. In "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), after several pages arguing that genuine inquiry, whatever its starting point, gets pushed toward a single outcome by something outside any individual investigator's will, Peirce sums up his position like this:

"The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real. That is the way I would explain reality."

I get the general pragmatist move of defining truth in terms of where inquiry is headed rather than some prior "matching" between statement and world, but three things about the mechanics of the claim are tripping me up. First, doesn't the definition risk circularity: "investigation" and "inquiry" already seem to presuppose a notion of getting things right, so explaining truth via what inquiry converges on looks like it borrows the very concept it's supposed to ground. Second, "fated" seems to be doing real work — is Peirce claiming something like guaranteed, near-deterministic convergence, or is this really just a counterfactual claim ("would be agreed upon if inquiry were carried far enough")? And if it's the counterfactual reading, what stops it from being satisfied trivially, say by gerrymandering who counts as an "investigator" so that convergence is guaranteed by definition? Third, what happens to some mundane fact nobody will ever look into — the number of pebbles on a particular stretch of beach on a particular afternoon, say — does the theory really commit Peirce to saying there's no truth about that, or is there some idealization built into "investigation" that's meant to handle it?

Separately, I've read in a couple of secondary sources (so I hold this loosely) that James's version of pragmatist truth is more individualistic and psychological than Peirce's — true beliefs are ones that "work" for the believer and pay off in experience, rather than Peirce's picture of an entire community of investigators converging through the scientific method specifically. Is that actually the right way to draw the contrast, and if so, is it just a difference in emphasis, or does it mean Peirce and James were not really defending the same theory of truth at all — which would explain why Peirce later felt he had to rename his own view "pragmaticism"?
