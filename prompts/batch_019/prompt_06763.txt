In a reading group I run on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, two of my doctoral students, Marcus and Elena, fell into a running dispute after our session on the "hinge" passages, and I would value an outside opinion on how it played out. (1) Marcus opened by arguing that Wittgenstein dissolves radical skepticism: doubting a proposition like "I have two hands" or "the earth has existed for many years" only makes sense against a background of things that are simply not in question, so the skeptic's demand for grounds is a category mistake, on a par with asking what justifies the rules of chess while playing it. (2) Elena replied that this doesn't answer the skeptic so much as rename the problem: Wittgenstein himself calls such certainties "groundless," and if the entire structure of justified belief ultimately rests on something groundless, that just is the skeptic's worry, wearing a more comfortable label. (3) Marcus came back that "groundless" does not mean "arbitrary": a hinge is not an unjustified answer to a question we could in principle have asked, because no such question is available from inside the very practice the hinge makes possible. (4) Elena's rejoinder was that this is exactly what makes the position suspect: on those terms a flat-earther's or a conspiracy theorist's hinges are equally "groundless but not arbitrary," and Wittgenstein gives us no way to say ours are better, only that they are ours. (5) Marcus's last move was that hinges earn their keep through the sustained practical success of the ways of acting built on them, and that one that kept leading to failure would eventually stop functioning as a hinge at all. (6) Elena was unmoved, pointing out that "success" is itself measured by standards internal to the very framework under dispute, so the move only pushes the circularity back one step. Neither budged. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move -- saying who has the better of it at each numbered step -- and close by flagging anything you think both of them are overlooking?
