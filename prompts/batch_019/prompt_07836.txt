For the past three years I have worked as an analyst building causal-inference pipelines for a mid-sized logistics company — propensity-score matching, difference-in-differences, the occasional instrumental-variable model — and until recently I was content to use words like "cause" and "effect" without asking too hard what they meant. That changed a few months ago, when a colleague who had done a philosophy MA before switching careers looked over one of my causal diagrams and asked, not unkindly, what actually makes an arrow in that diagram a causal arrow rather than a merely correlational one, and whether my go-to justification — "this is what would happen under an intervention" — was doing real metaphysical work or just dressing up Humean regularities in new notation. That conversation sent me down a rabbit hole into the philosophy-of-causation literature, and it has since become the excuse for a small podcast the two of us now co-host, interviewing philosophers of science for an audience of practitioners like me who never had to think about any of this before.

Our guest next episode is a philosopher whose work defends Woodward-style interventionism about causation against the older Lewisian counterfactual theory. Her standard line is that Lewis's approach — analyzing causation as counterfactual dependence between events, adjudicated by an overall ranking of similarity across possible worlds — never really recovered from the standard preemption cases (the backup assassin who would have fired had the first one missed) without increasingly ad hoc repairs, whereas cashing out causation in terms of what would happen under a surgical, modular intervention on a variable in a structural equation handles those cases naturally and travels much better into the sciences she actually cares about, like economics and biology.

I would like help drafting nine or ten interview questions that go beyond a restatement of that position; I can already predict the polished version of her answer to "what is an intervention," and that isn't what I want. Please include at least one question on each of the following, and for each, a short follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: first, whether defining "intervention" in partly causal terms makes her theory non-reductive in a way that should disappoint anyone drawn to it hoping for a Lewis-style analysis of causation in wholly non-causal terms; second, whether the view really escapes needing something like Lewis's similarity metric, given that "holding everything else fixed" during an intervention is already a substantive judgment about which changes count as staying fixed; third, how the account handles so-called fat-handed interventions, and why the invariance and modularity conditions used to rule them out are any less gerrymandered than Lewis's own weighted system of respects of similarity; and fourth, whether talk of interventions keeps any literal content in domains — macroeconomics, intentional psychology, written history — where no one could ever perform the relevant surgical manipulation even in principle, or whether it quietly turns back into just another counterfactual theory once you are there. Feel free to reorder or combine these, but I would rather the conversation end on the hardest of the four.
