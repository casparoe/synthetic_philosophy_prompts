So I had this dumb argument with a coworker last week that I can't stop thinking about. We maintain a service at work thats been in continuous production for about 7 years now. Over that time, through a long series of incremental migrations, we've rewritten every module, switched the language it's written in twice, migrated the database three separate times, and at this point I'm fairly confident there isn't a single line of the original code left anywhere. But it's kept the same name the whole time, basically the same api contract, the same on-call rotation, the same customers hitting it — there was never one big "day zero" relaunch, just thousands of small PRs merged one at a time over years.

My coworker's take is that it's obviously still "the same service," since it never stopped running and never stopped being itself, even mid-migration — continuity is what matters, full stop. My take is that this is kind of a fiction we tell ourselves, since if you diffed the original commit against today's HEAD you'd get zero overlap, so in what sense is it "the same" thing and not just a chain of totally different services that happen to share a name and a Slack channel? He brought up the ship of Theseus thing at this point, which I vaguely remember from some video years ago, but honestly that just restates my confusion back at me, it doesn't resolve anything.

Is there actual rigorous philosophical work on what it even takes for something to count as "the same X" across a total, gradual replacement like this, something with more meat to it than the ship analogy? And if there is, does it actually vindicate one of our positions, or does it just show we're both smuggling in some assumption that doesn't hold up? Kind of want to be armed for round 2 of this at the next retro.
