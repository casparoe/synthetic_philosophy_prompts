I'm a novelist assembling a linked collection of short stories, each one built around a real philosopher's argument pushed to its most uncomfortable extreme, and the entry I've struggled most to write concerns A. J. Ayer and the meaning of an afterlife. Ayer's logical positivism, first laid out in Language, Truth and Logic, held that a statement only counts as meaningful if some possible observation could confirm or refute it, and applied to talk of surviving one's own death, this led him to argue that such talk isn't false so much as empty: nobody can specify what observation would show that an experience occurring "after" a body's death belongs to the very person who died, given that our ordinary way of individuating persons depends on exactly the bodily and psychological continuity that death cuts off.

What makes the case irresistible to me is that Ayer got to test this commitment against his own body. As the story is usually told, late in life, while hospitalized with pneumonia, he choked on a piece of smoked salmon and his heart stopped for several minutes; he came back describing a vivid, almost hallucinatory episode involving a painful red light and beings apparently charged with administering space itself, and was said, in the disoriented hour afterward, to have told someone he might have to go back and revise everything he had written. Once he had recovered his composure, though, he wrote the experience up under the title "What I Saw When I Was Dead," insisting it hadn't restored any belief in God, and had only left him slightly less confident that his own approaching death would be, simply, the end of him.

I'd like a short story built on this collision, though I'd rather you invent a philosopher in Ayer's position than write about him directly. Close third person, something like 1,800 to 2,500 words, staying inside the hours just after such an experience, as she tries to explain to herself, and perhaps to a visitor she didn't ask for, why the sheer vividness of what happened to her doesn't settle anything philosophically — while feeling, for the first time in her career, how thin that answer sounds. I don't want the story to resolve into either a conversion or a vindication; I want it to end with her still caught between the two, in a way the reader can feel rather than have explained.
