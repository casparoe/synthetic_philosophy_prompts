My grandmother recently learned that the hearing loss she's had for the last several years is coded by her insurer as "age-related" rather than as a covered medical condition, so she is paying out of pocket for hearing aids that would have been reimbursed under a different label. I mentioned this at dinner last week, which set off a long argument about whether "that's just normal aging, not a disease" is even a coherent thing to say. Most people at the table eventually dropped out, but two friends, Marcus and Elena, kept at it for a good twenty minutes, and neither has since admitted defeat. I was there for the whole exchange but don't know the underlying literature well enough to say who actually came out ahead, if anyone did.

(1) Marcus, who took a philosophy of medicine seminar years ago, opened by defending the insurer's underlying logic, if not its stinginess: on the most influential naturalist theory of disease, health just means statistically typical functioning for members of your reference class, where the class is fixed by age and sex rather than by whatever happens to suit the case at hand. Compare an 80-year-old's cochlea to other 80-year-olds', he said, and ordinary presbycusis is exactly what you'd predict, so by that theory's own lights it isn't disease, and no value judgment enters the definition anywhere. (2) Elena objected that this proves too much: if "normal for the class" settles the matter, then nothing that happens to every member of a class could ever count as disease, however disabling, since the class average will always chase whatever the outcome turns out to be. That, she said, isn't a value-free description of disease; it just relocates the value judgment to the choice of reference class, since treating 80-year-olds as the relevant comparison group already encodes a judgment that their decline doesn't matter enough to flag. (3) Marcus granted that gerrymandered reference classes would be a problem but denied that this is one: the classes are restricted to age and sex precisely so nobody can narrow them further to manufacture a verdict, and the theory still counts a universal age-related decline as disease whenever a part functions worse than its own age-adjusted statistical baseline. If Grandma's hearing is worse than the typical 80-year-old's, he said, it's disease on naturalist grounds too; if it isn't, then it really is just aging. (4) Elena replied that this concession costs more than it buys: to fix a "baseline" for an aging cochlea at all, you need an independent answer to what hearing is for, and the standard naturalist move is to cash that out evolutionarily, as a part's contribution to survival and reproduction. But hearing was never shaped by selection for its usefulness to 80-year-olds, since selection pressure on a trait plausibly wanes once reproduction is over, so evolutionary history simply has nothing to say about what a properly functioning elderly ear would even look like, and whatever verdict Marcus's theory reaches has to be smuggled in from somewhere else. (5) Marcus's next move was that function needn't be cashed out evolutionarily at all: you can instead define it causally, as a part's contribution to some capacity of the organism as a whole, which is how philosophers of biology handle cases evolutionary stories don't cover, and this would still be a fact independent of anyone's values. (6) Elena's comeback was that this kind of function-talk is if anything more permissive, since it never tells you which capacity of the whole organism you're supposed to be measuring hearing's contribution to -- following conversation at a dinner table, or catching a smoke alarm at 3 a.m. -- and picking one over the other already reflects what we happen to care about, so the value judgment hasn't been removed, only pushed one step back.
