So I've been on a bit of a Spinoza kick lately - never studied philosophy in school or anything, just been chewing through the Ethics and some commentary on my own time, and it's honestly rewired my brain a little. Anyway, I got into his political writings and ran into something that's been bugging me ever since. In the Theological-Political Treatise he argues democracy is the most "natural" form of government, basically because it's the one where people hold onto the most of the freedom and equality they had in the state of nature - nobody hands their voice over completely to somebody else. Real forward-thinking sounding stuff for the 1600s. But then in the Political Treatise - which he never actually finished, since he died right around the time he was writing the chapter on democracy - pretty much the last thing he does before it cuts off is argue that women shouldn't count as citizens. His reasoning is basically: if women were naturally just as capable of ruling as men, surely by now some nation somewhere would've had men and women ruling jointly, and since that never really happens (he brings up the Amazons just to wave the example away), nature simply didn't give women equal standing. Which strikes me as a really weak, almost custom-based argument coming from a guy who's whole philosophical MO is not trusting inherited prejudice and appearances, and instead reasoning everything out from nature and necessity.

So here's what I'm curious about. Knowing what we know now - women running countries and companies same as anybody - do you think Spinoza, given his own principles (his determinism, the idea that your "natural right" is really just whatever power you actually have, his refusal to let nature have built-in goals or purposes) would've actually revised that view if he were around to see it? And zooming out further: given the gap between "democracy is the regime that best preserves everyone's natural freedom" and "but actual citizenship should probably be limited to a fairly narrow group of independent men," what do you think he'd say about a modern democracy where literally every adult gets a vote, no exceptions for sex or property or who signs your paycheck? Would that look to him like democracy finally living up to its own logic, or like exactly the unstable, mob-driven mess he kept warning about elsewhere? Don't just give me an "on one hand, on the other hand" answer - tell me what your actual best guess is and walk me through why, based on what he actually wrote.
