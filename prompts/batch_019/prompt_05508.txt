So my book club read Franklin's autobiography last month and I got weirdly into the "arriving at moral perfection" section — enough that I actually started my own version of his 13 virtues chart (little grid, one virtue a week, mark a dot every time you screw up). I'm on week 3 right now, which is Order, and it is NOT going well, lol. Anyway this came up at dinner on saturday and two friends who were also in the book club, Marcus and Liv, ended up in this whole argument about whether Franklin's project actually worked, and I can't stop turning it over bc I genuinely don't know who had the better of it. Roughly how it went:

1. Marcus said the project was basically a failure by Franklin's own admission — for Humility he literally writes "I cannot boast of much success in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but I had a good deal with regard to the appearance of it," then describes training himself to say things like "I conceive" or "it appears to me" instead of stating opinions outright, purely because people then argued with him less. His point: that's not humility, that's a debate trick, and Franklin admits as much himself.

2. Liv said keep reading — Franklin also says that put-on manner "became at length so easy, and so habitual to me" that for fifty years nobody heard him say anything dogmatic. That's just habituation, basically Aristotle's whole thing: you act like a just person before you actually are one, and eventually it stops being an act. Starting from a strategic motive doesn't mean it stays strategic.

3. Marcus said fine, maybe for that one virtue, but look at Order, which he tracked hardest and never once fixed. Instead of admitting that, Franklin tells a story about a guy getting an ax ground who gives up halfway and decides he likes a "speckled ax" best, then concludes a "benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance." That's not insight, Marcus said, it's rationalization — anyone who quits a resolution could say the same thing about themselves.

4. Liv said that's too harsh — it's basically Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" move, that being maximally virtuous in every single respect would actually make you kind of unbearable to be around, so there's a real reason not to want total perfection, not just sour grapes. Plus Franklin's own bottom line is that despite falling short everywhere, the attempt made him "a better and a happier man" than he'd otherwise have been, and he says outright he owed "the constant felicity of his life" to this "little artifice." So by his own standard, gaps on the scorecard don't equal failure.

They just kept restating these points louder after that. Can you go through it move by move and say who actually had the stronger case at each of the 4 points, not just who won overall? And if there's something bigger you think they're both missing about what Franklin's project actually shows, I'd want to hear that too.
