I've spent the better part of the last decade reading LessWrong, the EA Forum, and adjacent blogs, where the impossibility of interpersonal utility comparisons gets invoked constantly as a philosophical curiosity — usually right before everyone shrugs and goes back to comparing utils anyway. I now sit on the small grants committee of a private foundation that funds effective-altruism-style interventions, modeled loosely on GiveWell's approach, and the problem has stopped being academic for me.

This cycle we have $1.4 million uncommitted and two finalists we cannot both fund at the levels their theories of change require. The first is a bednet-distribution charity whose cost-effectiveness is reported in dollars per DALY averted; the second is a corporate-campaign group working on cage-free egg commitments, whose cost-effectiveness is reported in dollars per "suffering-year" averted, a unit built on contested cross-species moral-weight estimates for how a hen's capacity to suffer compares to a human's. Board discussion has stalled into a genuine philosophical dispute rather than a numbers dispute. One faction, invoking Robbins, argues that the DALY-to-suffering-year exchange rate isn't measuring anything — it's a value judgment wearing the costume of arithmetic — and that the honest response is to split the money down the middle rather than smuggle in an interpersonal (here, interspecies) utility comparison the committee can't actually defend. The other faction points out that a fifty-fifty split isn't a neutral, comparison-free default either: it implicitly asserts that the two grants produce equally valuable marginal outcomes, which is exactly the kind of claim the first faction says nobody can justify.

I have to circulate a two-page memo before next week's vote, and I'd like your help drafting it. It should recommend one specific allocation, expressed as a dollar figure or ratio rather than a vague range; it shouldn't dodge the underlying problem by quietly pretending DALYs and suffering-years are commensurable after all; and it should give the losing side's argument a genuinely fair hearing rather than a perfunctory nod before the recommendation.
