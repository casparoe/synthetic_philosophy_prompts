So we're doing a "meaning of life" unit in my philosophy elective and one of the readings was this short essay called "Epilogue" by Frank Ramsey — the guy who apparently died at like 26 but crammed in a genius-level math/economics/philosophy career before that. In the essay he's basically arguing you shouldn't feel small or depressed just because the physical universe is way bigger than us — something like, the stars can't think or love so their size shouldn't impress us more than human qualities do, and his mental picture of the world is drawn "in perspective," with people huge in the foreground and the stars tiny, not an accurate scale model. So meaning comes from human-sized stuff, friendship, conversation, intellectual work, games (I think he even mentions chess specifically), not from cosmic significance or humanity lasting a long time.

Then totally seperately, I'm in my school's EA club and we were reading about the economics of climate discounting, and it turns out Ramsey ALSO wrote the founding 1928 paper on how economists think about discounting future generations ("A Mathematical Theory of Saving"), where he argued that discounting future people's wellbeing just because they're later in time, as opposed to discounting for genuine uncertainty, is basically a moral mistake and not just a technical modelling choice. My club leader said this exact argument gets cited today by longtermist people (Toby Ord/Will MacAskill types) to justify caring enormously about the far future and stuff like AI extinction risk, since if your not allowed to discount future people just for being far away in time, a universe with trillions of future humans ends up mattering way more than basically anything happening this century.

This feels like a real tension to me, not just a coincidence: "Epilogue" Ramsey seems pretty unbothered by grand cosmic/long-run scales and just wants you focused on your own human-sized life, while "discounting" Ramsey seems to morally demand you take the vast future dead seriously. If Ramsey had somehow lived to see modern longtermism as an actual movement, would he have basically endorsed it, or would his "Epilogue" outlook have made him pretty skeptical of the whole project? Can you actually reason through both sides of his thought instead of just picking whichever one sounds cooler, and be upfront about which parts of your answer follow pretty directly from stuff he actually wrote vs. where you're just extrapolating?
