I'm working on a magazine feature about the current vogue for scientific claims that free will is an illusion—Robert Sapolsky's recent book is the obvious peg—and in trying to give the piece some philosophical ballast, several of the philosophers I've interviewed kept steering me toward Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility as a more rigorous cousin of the neuroscientists' case. As I understand it from his 1994 paper "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility" and the various places he has restated the idea since, the argument runs roughly like this: you do what you do, in any given situation, because of the way you are; to be truly, or "ultimately," responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the mental respects that matter; and you cannot be responsible for that, because any act of shaping yourself would have to spring from some prior self you did not choose, which merely pushes the question back a step instead of answering it. The regress is supposed to show that "true" responsibility—the sort of deep desert that could make ultimate praise or blame genuinely appropriate, the kind of thing that might make eternal reward or punishment actually fitting—is not available to anyone, and, crucially, not simply because determinism happens to be true.

The part I have not yet managed to explain to my editor in plain language is exactly that last wrinkle: Strawson insists the argument goes through even if determinism is false, even if real, physical indeterminism—quantum randomness, say—feeds into how a person's character and values got formed in the first place. Intuitively, I would have guessed that a dash of genuine chance at least dents the airtight causal chain that hard determinists complain about, so why doesn't it help? I would like an explanation, pitched at an intelligent-generalist level rather than a technical one, of why sprinkling randomness into the story of how someone became the kind of person who wants what they want and values what they value does nothing to secure the sort of self-authorship—being, as the phrase borrowed from Nietzsche has it, causa sui, cause of oneself—that "ultimate" responsibility seems to require.

As a related, slightly tangential question: Galen Strawson is P.F. Strawson's son, and P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" is famous for arguing, in a much more relaxed spirit, that our practices of holding one another responsible rest on natural "reactive attitudes" like resentment and gratitude rather than on any metaphysical fact about ultimate responsibility, and that these practices would survive the truth of determinism largely unscathed. Is there a genuine disagreement buried in this father-and-son pair, or were they simply asking different questions—one about whether we can stop reacting to each other as responsible beings, the other about whether anyone ever really deserves it in the deepest sense—such that both verdicts could be true at once?
