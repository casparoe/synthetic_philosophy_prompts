I'm a PhD student in theoretical computer science, working at the intersection of logic and multi-agent systems, and this term a reading group I'm part of has been working through the program equilibrium literature: Tennenholtz's original result that letting players submit programs instead of actions — programs that can condition on one another's code — turns cooperation into a genuine equilibrium even in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, and then the later provability-logic constructions (FairBot, PrudentBot, the so-called Löbian handshake) that use Gödel numbering and Löb's theorem so that two agents can actually prove, rather than merely hope, that they will cooperate with each other. One of the people in the group called this "the most romantic result in game theory," which has stuck with me, and it made me want to see the idea handled in fiction rather than in a proof.

Could you write me a short story (something in the 800–1500 word range) that takes this machinery seriously as its central device, rather than using "AI cooperation" as vague set dressing? I don't want to dictate the plot, but I'd like it to hinge on something structurally faithful to the actual setup — for instance, two automated agents (corporate negotiators, probes representing rival factions, or whatever setting you find compelling) who must decide whether to expose their source code to each other, and who reach for something like a Löbian proof of mutual cooperation instead of a promise, with real tension coming from what such a proof can and cannot guarantee once you introduce bounded proof search, bugs, or a mismatch in the strength of the two agents' reasoning systems. If you want to give the agents themselves (or the humans who built them) real stakes and an interior life, that's welcome; I'd rather the story earn its ideas than be a dressed-up formal exercise.
