During the postmortem for an outage that took down checkout for twenty minutes, two senior engineers at my company had a back-and-forth I never saw resolved, and I'd like a neutral read on it. The outage traced back to a pull request that dropped a null check without adding a replacement guard; the PR's author, a third engineer named Priya, wasn't in the room, and the draft postmortem's 'root cause' line named exactly that missing check. Marcus defended the line: the outage counterfactually depended on that specific omission -- if the check had been there, there'd have been no outage -- and that dependence is just what causation is, so the doc is simply stating a fact. Dana objected that the same counterfactual test is satisfied just as well by the reviewer's not asking about edge cases, a two-year-old decision not to enable stricter compiler flags, and whoever ran the staging tests that missed it -- so it licenses dozens of equally valid 'root causes,' which makes singling out Priya's line arbitrary. Marcus replied that it isn't arbitrary: Priya specifically owned that code path, so unlike the reviewer or the tester, there's a principled reason to point at her omission rather than theirs. Dana said that reply concedes her point rather than answering it: 'whose job it was' is a claim about duty, not about what caused what, so the doc isn't identifying a cause at all -- it's assigning blame and dressing it up as root-causing, exactly what blameless postmortems are meant to avoid. Neither budged before the meeting moved on. Could you go through their exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each step, and flag anything important both of them seem to be missing?
