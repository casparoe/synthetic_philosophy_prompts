So this whole thing got started because of one of those self-driving car trolley problem arguments at work (we were arguing about whether an AV should ever be programmed to sacrifice its own passenger to save a bigger number of pedestrians), and a coworker who minored in philosophy said something like "you know that whole intend-vs-foresee distinction you keep dancing around is basically just the doctrine of double effect, and that goes all the way back to Aquinas." I'd never heard of it, so I went and found the actual passage where he supposedly lays the whole thing out — Summa Theologica, Part II-II, Question 64, Article 7, on whether its lawful to kill a man in self-defense. Problem is, I'm extremely not used to reading 700-year-old scholastic arguments, and got pretty lost, especially toward the end.

Here's the core part, his actual answer (skipping the objections beforehand, which are mostly Augustine quotes insisting self-defense is never ok):

"Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental. Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in 'being,' as far as possible. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful, because according to the jurists, 'it is lawful to repel force by force, provided one does not exceed the limits of a blameless defense.' Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good, it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority, who while intending to kill a man in self-defense, refer this to the public good, as in the case of a soldier fighting against the foe, and in the minister of the judge struggling with robbers, although even these sin if they be moved by private animosity."

The first chunk I think I basically get: ordinary self-defense is fine because you only intend to save yourself, the attacker dying is just a side effect, and that's presumably the bit everyone cites as the birth of double effect. But then right at the end he suddenly carves out this seperate category about how only people "with public authority," like soldiers or a judge's officers, are allowed to actually intend the killing. That's the part that lost me. If ordinary self-defense is already justified because you never intend the killing in the first place, why does he even need this extra rule about officials being permitted to intend it? Is he saying there are basically two different routes to justified killing here — unintended side-effect for regular people, versus actually-intended-but-for-the-common-good for officials — and if so, does that mean the "double effect" reasoning people invoke for trolley problems today is only doing part of the work Aquinas actually needed it to do back here? Would appreciate someone smarter than me on this stuff untangling what's actually going on in the argument, because I clearly don't have the background to do it on my own.
