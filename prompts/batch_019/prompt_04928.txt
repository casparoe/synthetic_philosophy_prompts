"The Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem says that for any voting rule that picks a single winner from three or more candidates, and that can produce any of those candidates as the outcome depending on how people vote, there is no way to design it so that voters never have an incentive to misrepresent their true preferences — unless the rule is a dictatorship (it always picks one particular voter's favorite). So honest reporting just isn't a dominant strategy in general. This only really becomes a problem once you have a reasonably sized electorate, though, since with very few voters there usually isn't room for one person's misreported ballot to flip the outcome, so in practice small committees can mostly get away with simple rules. The theorem is often called the strategic twin of Arrow's theorem, and you basically get it for free once you already have Arrow's proof: a voter benefiting from lying about their ranking is really just another way of producing a violation of independence of irrelevant alternatives, so dictatorship falls out the same way it does for Arrow. A rule that escapes the conclusion is majority rule under single-peaked preferences (e.g. a committee voting on a single budget number): the resulting median voter mechanism is strategy-proof and non-dictatorial, because it violates the unrestricted domain condition rather than the requirement of three or more possible outcomes."
