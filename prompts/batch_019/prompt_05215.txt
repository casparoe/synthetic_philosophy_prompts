Over the last few months I have been bothered by something that started at an art museum and that I still haven't quite been able to shake off. I was standing in front of a large, fairly austere abstract painting that did nothing for me, when the friend I was with, who actually studied painting in college, walked me through the composition and called it "a masterpiece of restraint." A week later, at a dinner party, I caught myself repeating that exact phrase to someone else as though it were my own considered opinion, even though I couldn't honestly say I had seen any of that in the painting myself. It left me wondering whether I was entitled to that opinion at all, in a way that felt different from believing, say, some fact about history or chemistry simply because a reliable person told me it was true.

Reading around on the subject afterward (I have no formal training in philosophy), I discovered that this is apparently a live dispute in aesthetics, usually discussed under the heading of "aesthetic testimony," where people tend to split into two camps, sometimes called pessimists and optimists. The pessimists think something real goes missing when an aesthetic verdict is simply handed to you rather than reached through your own encounter with the work: they lean on things like an "acquaintance principle" — the idea, traceable to Hume and Kant, that a genuine judgment of taste has to be grounded in your own first-hand response and can't just be adopted secondhand — and on a further distinction between passing along the concrete features that make a work good and just passing along the verdict itself. The optimists think all of this is a bit overwrought: that aesthetic testimony is not fundamentally different from testimony about anything else we aren't personally positioned to check, and that whatever discomfort remains is really about what we want out of engaging with art, not about what we are entitled to believe.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people, equally sharp and equally committed, who hold these two positions — maybe even staged as a conversation happening right in front of that same painting, with one of them having just read the museum's placard aloud? I would like them to work through a single running example rather than trade slogans, and to genuinely engage each other's specific moves: if the pessimist leans on the acquaintance principle, the optimist should take that on directly rather than sidestep it, and vice versa. At some point I would also like one of them to raise whether the very same puzzle shows up with moral testimony — being told by someone you trust that an action is admirable or wrong — and whether that comparison actually helps their own side or undercuts it. I am less interested in a tidy resolution than in seeing exactly where the strongest pressure points in this disagreement lie.
