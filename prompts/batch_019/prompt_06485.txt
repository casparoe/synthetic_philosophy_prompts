I'm several chapters into a novel whose emotional core is a long night in a hospital corridor, where two adult siblings keep their dying father company and end up arguing about something that has nothing to do with logistics or grief management: whether his death, once it comes, will actually be bad for him, as distinct from being bad for the people who loved him. One sibling is a hospice nurse who has absorbed a kind of folk Epicureanism from years of sitting with dying patients; the other left a philosophy doctorate unfinished and can't let the easy version of that view go unchallenged. Before I draft the scene in my own voice, I'd like to see the underlying argument run at full strength on both sides, so that neither character in my draft ends up as a mouthpiece for a position I've secretly simplified.

Could you write a dialogue between an Epicurean and a deprivationist (roughly in the line of Nagel's account of death as a deprivation of goods) on whether death is bad for the person who dies? Don't let the Epicurean stop at the bare "where death is, I am not" line — have her use it to raise the sharper problem of timing: if there's no subject left to be harmed once death has occurred, and nothing yet to be harmed beforehand, there seems to be no time at which death's badness could actually be located, which is a puzzle in its own right, prior to any question about whether deprivation is real. The deprivationist should meet this directly rather than simply re-asserting that death forecloses future goods — I want to see how a comparative, "no specific time" notion of badness is actually supposed to work, and why it doesn't quietly reintroduce a subject who persists long enough to suffer the loss. At some point, have the Epicurean also press the Lucretian symmetry argument — our equanimity about the eons of nonexistence before we were born, used as a mirror for how we should regard nonexistence after we die — and have the deprivationist answer it in earnest rather than waving it away.

Keep the exchange tight enough that it could plausibly sit inside a novel scene — more duel than seminar — and don't let either character land a clean knockout. I need both positions to still be standing, and still recognizable as the strongest versions of themselves, by the time the dialogue ends.
