I am several drafts into a novel organized around a will: a dying, eccentric shipping magnate promises his fortune to whichever of his three estranged children can convince his notary, by a fixed date, that they have genuinely resolved to give the money entirely away again after living on it for a year. The inheritance is released the moment the resolution is certified as sincere, well before the year of austerity would actually have to be lived through, and nothing further happens to an heir who quietly abandons the plan the day after the will is read. Working out how my protagonist, the eldest of the three, might honestly talk herself into, or out of, such a resolution sent me back to Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle, which obviously supplied the bones of the plot, and I got stuck on exactly the question she would have to argue through: can a perfectly rational person form a genuine, sincere intention to do something costly and, in retrospect, pointless, purely because forming it, and being seen to have formed it, pays off immediately?

Rather than have her argue the matter out alone in her head, I would like to give her the argument as a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree sharply but are evenly matched, so that I can see the strongest version of each side before deciding what she actually does. One of them should hold that intentions are the kind of state that can be rationally adopted and then rationally left unreconsidered: perhaps because rationality properly attaches to whole plans rather than to each moment's choice taken in isolation, or because intentions carry stability norms of their own that do not collapse, moment to moment, into whatever the balance of first-order reasons happens to recommend. On this view, forming the intention now and actually complying a year later can both be fully rational by the same standard. The other should deny this: an intention which its own holder can already predict she will have decisive reason to abandon is not a rational intention at all, and whatever gets an heir through the year is better described as a fact about her character than as a choice she goes on rationally making. I want the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's best moves rather than talk past one another; in particular, I would like them to wrestle with whether "it is good to be the sort of person who would comply" can be turned into "it is rational to comply" without equivocation, and whether the notary's ability to certify sincerity in advance is doing the same suspicious work that the predictor does in Newcomb's problem. Please do not let either side concede early or let the exchange settle too neatly — I would like a genuine, sustained disagreement, since that is closer to where I actually need to leave things for the book.
