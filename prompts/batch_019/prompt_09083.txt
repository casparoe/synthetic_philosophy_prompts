I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, currently TAing an undergraduate seminar on theories of atonement. Next week's session is built around David Lewis's short, dry, and surprisingly unsettling paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", in which he presses the doctrine that Christ was punished in the sinner's place by asking whether punishment can really be transferred the way a debt can. My professor has noticed that the class defaults to reciting pro- and con- talking points about the cross rather than actually locating where the philosophical pressure lies, so I want to open discussion with a dialogue instead of a lecture summary.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophically serious characters. The first is a contemporary defender of penal substitutionary atonement in the Reformed tradition, who accepts Lewis's central distinction -- that a monetary debt is fungible and can be discharged by anyone, whereas retributive punishment seems bound to the specific wrongdoer's desert -- but argues the doctrine survives that concession once we get clearer about what exactly is supposed to be transferred (guilt, penal liability, and imputed legal standing need not all travel together). The second is a philosopher extending Lewis's own skepticism, who argues that every way of rendering the transfer coherent either turns punishment into a bookkeeping fiction no retributivist should accept, or quietly trades retributivism for a picture of justice on which suffering merely needs to occur somewhere to balance the books -- which isn't the picture of justice the tradition wants either. Neither character should be a mouthpiece the other simply knocks down; each has to actually answer the specific objection just raised rather than restate an opening position in new words.

I'd like the dialogue to run long enough for at least two or three real exchanges per side, not just an opening volley and a single rebuttal, and I'd rather it end with the disagreement sharpened and localized than with either side converting or a tidy synthesis being reached -- since that's honestly closer to where Lewis leaves the reader anyway.
