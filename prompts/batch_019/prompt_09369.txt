I cover philosophy of mind and AI for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I've been drafting a feature on the frame problem that keeps stalling because every explainer I consult — including a couple of my own earlier pieces — reaches for the same handful of stock scenarios. First there is Dennett's fable of the robot R1, which tows its battery out of a room in time to save it from a bomb, only to have the bomb, which was sitting on the same wagon, go off anyway, because R1 never deduced that pulling the wagon out would bring the bomb along with it. Then there's its unhappier successor R1D1, built to deduce the implications of its acts, which dutifully proves that moving the wagon won't change the colour of the walls and is partway through a proof that the wagon's wheels will turn through more revolutions than the wagon has wheels when the bomb detonates. Next comes R2D1, which tags implications as relevant or irrelevant before acting and ends up sitting outside the room, "busily ignoring some thousands of implications" that it has judged irrelevant, until it too runs out of time. There is also the Hanks-and-McDermott "Yale shooting problem" with its gun and its turkey. And for anyone who has actually read the Stanford Encyclopedia entry rather than a secondhand summary of it, there is the robot sent to fetch a teacup from the cupboard, or the block-world case in which painting an object and then moving it leaves classical logic unable to certify that the paint job survived the trip.

All of these genuinely illustrate the frame problem, but I've come to suspect they quietly do double duty for two things philosophers of AI treat as distinct: the narrow problem that McCarthy and Hayes originally posed in 1969, of representing the non-effects of an action in formal logic without an absurd list of axioms along the lines of "and this doesn't change the wallpaper," and the broader epistemological worry that Dennett and Fodor pressed afterward, about how any finite reasoner bounds its search for what is relevant to revise at all, given that almost any of its beliefs could in principle bear on almost any of its actions.

What I need for the piece is a genuinely new example, set outside robotics and formal planning altogether, that isolates that second, broader problem clearly enough for a general reader to see why a tidy solution to the narrow logical version — some efficient non-monotonic formalism for tracking an action's effects and non-effects — would leave the deeper problem completely untouched. Could you work through such a scenario in enough detail that I could render it for lay readers in three or four paragraphs, and explicitly flag the moment within it where the search for relevant considerations actually explodes combinatorially, as distinct from the moment where the action merely has some further side effect that needs tracking?
