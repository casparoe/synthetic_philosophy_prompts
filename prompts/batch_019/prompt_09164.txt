A friend of mine, Mara — she's finishing a PhD in developmental psychology but has sat in on more epistemology seminars than most philosophers I know — and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about testimony, without ever really getting anywhere. The trigger was almost embarrassingly minor: a mutual acquaintance had passed along a piece of gossip that turned out to be false, and we couldn't agree on whether the person who had believed it had actually done anything epistemically careless. From there it turned into a real argument about whether Elizabeth Fricker's "local" reductionism about testimony can survive the regress worry that Coady-style anti-reductionists press against reductionism more generally. Mara thinks the view is a dead end; I kept insisting there was a way to save it but could not get the argument into decent shape out loud, so I wrote it up properly once I got home. I would rather have your comments on it than keep re-litigating it with her.

Here, more or less, is what I worked out that evening. Reductionists about testimony hold that a hearer's justification for believing what she is told is never sui generis: it ultimately cashes out in perception, memory, and inference, typically some inductive or abductive case for the reliability of testimony in general, or of this particular speaker, built from independently secured premises. Anti-reductionists deny this and treat testimony as a basic source on a par with perception itself: for Reid, we come equipped with principles of credulity and veracity, dispositions to trust and to speak truly that are not and should not be run through an inductive argument first; for a more contemporary, rationalist version, Burge's Acceptance Principle gives us a standing a priori entitlement to accept what we are told, defeasible but not derived from anything more basic. The interesting territory, though, is not really the choice between Hume and Reid; it is what happens once reductionism tries to get more modest. Fricker does not ask the hearer to possess a global argument for testimony's reliability as a class; she asks only that belief be responsibly monitored on each occasion, tracking markers of the speaker's sincerity and competence rather than trusting blindly. That looks like it should dodge the regress that sinks the more ambitious, global version.

Except Mara's objection, which is really a version of the one Coady presses against reductionism generally, seems to catch the local version too. The markers a hearer monitors for — confident tone against nervous hedging, consistency with things already known, the thousand small cues separating a plausible witness from an implausible one — are not read off some private inductive base assembled from scratch. Most of us acquired them exactly the way we acquired almost everything else about how people behave: by being told, directly or by example, by parents and teachers and books, which is to say, testimonially. If the monitoring that is supposed to do the reducing itself leans on an enormous, undischarged fund of testimonial belief, the reduction has not actually been paid for; it has been financed on credit from the very source it was meant to underwrite.

Here is the reply I want to make. Perceptual justification is standardly treated as basic in exactly the sense reductionists deny to testimony: it is not backed by a prior inductive argument that the senses are reliable. But detecting perceptual defeaters plainly draws on background beliefs of just the same testimonial pedigree — that sodium lighting drains colour from things, that two drinks impair your sense of balance, that mirrors reverse left and right. Nobody thinks this makes perception secretly reductionist, or generates a vicious regress underneath it. If leaning on testimonially acquired background beliefs to calibrate a basic source is harmless in the perceptual case, the fact that Fricker's monitoring does the same should not, by itself, be what sinks local reductionism.

The trouble is that I am no longer sure this defends reductionism rather than quietly abandoning it. If a source can lean on an undischarged fund of background belief and still count as basic, the whole point of insisting on active local monitoring — as opposed to simply helping yourself to a default entitlement, defeasible by specific evidence to the contrary, along the lines Audi wants for testimony anyway — starts to look unmotivated. It may be that what I have actually produced is a roundabout argument that testimony is a basic, defeasible source exactly like perception, which is the anti-reductionist's conclusion, dressed up in reductionist vocabulary I no longer have a right to.

What I would like from you: first, tell me whether I have described Fricker's and Coady's positions fairly enough that a specialist would not wince, since I am working mostly from memory of a seminar a couple of years ago and may be flattening things. Second, and this is the part I actually care about, tell me whether the perception analogy does the work I want it to, or whether, as I half-suspect in the last paragraph, it actually just proves the anti-reductionist's point for her. Third, if this move already exists somewhere in the literature in a cleaner form, I would rather know that than keep presenting it to people as though it were mine. And if you think Mara is simply right and the whole thing is a well-known dead end not worth developing further, say so; she will be pleased, and I would still rather hear it from you than keep arguing about it on trains.
