The question on the problem set read as follows:

"Consider the following case. A driver's fuel gauge is, as a matter of fact, generally reliable, though she has never checked it against any independent source of information about her tank. On a given occasion the gauge reads 'full'; she believes on this basis that the tank is full, and she separately notes, just by looking at the dial, that the gauge reads 'full.' Combining these two beliefs, she infers that the gauge was accurate on this occasion. Repeating the procedure across many occasions and then generalizing inductively, she concludes that the gauge is a reliable indicator of the fuel level.
(a) Explain why, on a view that permits 'basic' (non-inferentially justified) beliefs about the external world, each individual step in this argument looks legitimate, and why the conclusion nonetheless strikes most people as illegitimately 'bootstrapped.'
(b) Explain how Cohen's discussion of this kind of case generalizes the underlying problem beyond the reliabilist setting in which it was first raised.
(c) State what you take Cohen's own considered position to be on whether the bootstrapping problem shows that basic-knowledge theories of perceptual justification must be abandoned, or whether we should instead accept some version of 'easy knowledge' as a real but tolerable cost of any workable foundationalism, and briefly defend your reading."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"In the gas-gauge case, the driver's belief that the tank is full counts as basic, non-inferentially justified belief if reliabilism is true and the gauge is in fact reliable, since reliabilism only requires that the belief-forming process be reliable, not that the subject already know or believe this. Her belief that the gauge reads 'full' is just an ordinary perceptual belief about the position of a needle, so that step is unproblematic on any view. Conjoining the two beliefs licenses the further belief that the gauge was accurate on that occasion, and since deduction and induction are both truth-preserving, repeating the procedure and generalizing lets her conclude, apparently without any loss of justification, that the gauge is reliable overall. What makes this feel illegitimate is that she has never checked the gauge against any independent source of information about the tank: she has certified the instrument using only outputs of that very instrument, which amounts to getting a substantive, general conclusion 'on the cheap.'

Cohen's discussion shows that the problem is not specific to reliabilism: any view that allows for basic knowledge, including dogmatist views on which having an experience as of P immediately justifies believing P without any antecedent justification for the reliability of perception, will license the same bootstrapping pattern, since all such views let a subject acquire a foundational belief without first justifying the source that delivers it. Cohen takes this generalized result to show that basic-knowledge theories of perceptual justification cannot ultimately be salvaged: because nearly every serious reliabilist or foundationalist view has this structure, he treats the bootstrapping problem as, in effect, a reductio of the whole basic-knowledge approach, rather than as a cost that a workable foundationalism might simply have to accept."
