The line that got me (paraphrasing, I don't have the book on me) is basically: people could never agree on what's truly just, and even if they somehow did, you couldn't get everyone to actually follow it — so instead of making the just thing powerful, we made the powerful thing "just." Whatever's got enough force behind it — law, contract, a signed order — gets to wear the label, and everybody downstream just executes their piece without re-checking whether it's actually right, because the second everybody starts re-checking, the whole machine seizes up.

That is basically my job description. The bank decides, the court signs off, I show up with a tow truck at 6am. Nobody in that chain has to personally believe the outcome is just — not the loan officer, not the judge, not me — we each just have to believe our own step correctly follows from the one before it. Pascal's got another fragment about how custom is the entire foundation of law's authority, and the moment you ask "wait, is this custom actually right," you've already destroyed it. Which is exactly the question I'm apparently not supposed to ask at 6am on someone's driveway.

So here's where I get stuck: is Pascal saying this substitution is basically fine, even the best humans can do, since real justice was never actually on the table to begin with? Or is he saying it's a permanent scandal we've all quietly agreed to stop noticing? Because those two readings send someone in my position to very different places. One says relax, you're just a cog. The other says the whole chain, me included, is doing something bad, and "I was just following the order" isn't an excuse — it's just a description of how injustice usually gets carried out.

Right now my notes just stop there, on that question, which might be exactly the problem.
