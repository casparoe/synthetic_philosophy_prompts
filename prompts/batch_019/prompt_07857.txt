I sit on the editorial standards committee at the science magazine where I work, and we've reached a genuine impasse. A reporter's draft on a proposed new fundamental particle stated flatly that 'physicists have discovered' it. One committee member now wants us to adopt a standing rule: any claim about unobservable theoretical entities must be hedged (e.g., 'current evidence suggests'), since history is littered with once-confident theories -- phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether -- that turned out not to exist, so today's confident talk of quarks or dark matter deserves the same caution. A more senior editor thinks this is a serious overcorrection: applied consistently, it would make our coverage of evolution, plate tectonics, and climate change sound as tentative as our coverage of string theory or the multiverse -- which he thinks misrepresents how settled that science actually is and hands ammunition to denialists. Both have a real point, and neither will budge. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Monday's meeting: give reporters and editors usable criteria -- not just 'it depends' -- for when and how much to hedge, show how those criteria would sort the examples above, and take seriously what's right about the blanket-hedging instinct before explaining why the committee is or isn't adopting it.
