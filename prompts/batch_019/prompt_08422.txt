Since my daughter was born this past spring, I've been spending her nap times working through some of the philosophical literature on what, if anything, we owe future generations, largely to give some intellectual shape to a worry about climate change that otherwise just sits in my chest at odd hours. That reading led me to Parfit's discussion of the non-identity problem in Reasons and Persons, and in particular to his example contrasting a resource-intensive "Depletion" policy against a more modest "Conservation" one, a framework that later writers on environmental ethics have applied fairly directly to present-day climate policy. What unsettled me is his observation that these policies would not simply leave the same future people better or worse off; several generations out, they would result in different people existing altogether, since who marries whom, and when a couple has children, is sensitive to the whole shape of the economy they live in. If that is true of climate policy as well, it seems to follow that no person actually alive in, say, 2150 could truthfully claim to have been harmed by our present inaction, since a lower-emissions path would not have made their life better; it would instead have prevented them, specifically, from ever existing, and produced some other, better-off person in their place, as long as their own life remains worth living at all.

That argument strikes me as basically sound on its own terms, and yet it leaves me somewhere I would rather not be: it implies that the obligation I feel toward my daughter's generation, and every generation after hers, has no determinate object, since there is no fixed population of future people who stand to be harmed by what we do now, only ever a choice about who gets to exist in the first place. Before I let that conclusion settle, I would like to understand whether philosophers who work on this actually think the non-identity problem forces it, or whether I am missing something in how Parfit, or those who have built on his work, intend this kind of case to be used, because it seems strange that a puzzle originally about population ethics should end up dissolving the entire idea of responsibility to future generations.
