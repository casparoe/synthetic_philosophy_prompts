In my formal epistemology reading group this term we've been working through the literature on the epistemology of disagreement, including Aumann's agreement theorem. Two regular participants, Elena and Marcus, have spent the better part of six months quietly disagreeing about the odds of a U.S. recession this year; discussing the theorem apparently prompted them, for the first time, to say their numbers aloud to one another -- Elena at 60%, Marcus at 25% -- which touched off the exchange below, reconstructed here from what I overheard.

(1) Marcus said: "We're both Bayesians. We've each now heard the other's number, and we each know the other heard it. Aumann's theorem says common knowledge of posteriors forces them to be equal. Since ours didn't move, one of us isn't actually updating rationally on that fact."

(2) Elena replied that the theorem also assumes a common prior, and that they plainly don't share one: he spent a decade in macro trading, she spent hers doing market-microstructure research, and it would be strange if that history left no mark on where each of them started. Differing priors, she said, are not a failure of rationality.

(3) Marcus answered that positing brute prior differences is itself suspicious. Harsanyi's doctrine holds that two rational agents who differ only in what they've learned should have started from the same prior; any further divergence needs its own explanation in terms of information, and information is in principle shareable. Elena, he said, doesn't get to posit different priors and stop there.

(4) Elena said that even granting a common prior, the theorem needs the posteriors to be common knowledge in the full, iterated sense: not just each knowing the other's number, but each knowing that the other knows, and so on. Saying a number out loud once gives them mutual knowledge at one level, not the infinite tower the proof actually trades on.

(5) Marcus said that concedes less than it seems to. Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis showed that agents with a common prior who simply keep alternately announcing their posteriors will converge to a common value after finitely many rounds, with no infinite tower required up front. He and Elena had, by then, been going back and forth for twenty minutes -- if anything, closer to the theorem's conditions than when they'd started -- and their numbers still hadn't moved.

(6) Elena said that Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis's own result comes with a caveat worth remembering: the value two agents reach by repeatedly announcing posteriors need not match the value they would have reached by actually pooling the information behind those posteriors. So "we agree" would not even mean "we have pooled what we each know," let alone that either of them understands why the other believes what they believe, or that the resulting number is correct.

Could you go through the six numbered turns and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than jumping straight to an overall winner? I'm also curious whether either of them is misreading what Aumann's theorem, or the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result Marcus invokes in (5), actually establishes, and what -- if anything -- both of them are still missing about what their own six-month standoff shows.
