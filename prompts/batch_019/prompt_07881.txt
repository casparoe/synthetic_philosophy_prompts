I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate epistemology course this semester, and I am concurrently taking a graduate seminar covering much of the same material. Before I submit grades for the take-home final, I would like an independent, careful read on one student's response to the following question, so that my own grading is well-justified rather than a gut impression.

Question (20 points): State Agrippa's trilemma and explain how foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism can each be understood as accepting one horn of it as unproblematic. Then explain Peter Klein's "availabilist" reply to the objection that infinitism demands an infinite series of reasons that no finite believer could ever possess, and briefly assess whether the reply succeeds.

Student's response:

Agrippa's trilemma holds that when we ask what justifies a belief, and then ask what justifies that justification, and so on, we are forced into one of three unattractive options: an infinite regress of reasons, a chain of reasons that eventually circles back on itself, or a stopping point that is asserted without any further support. Foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism can each be read as accepting one of these horns and denying that it is really a problem.

Foundationalists accept the third horn. They hold that some beliefs are basic, justified without owing their justification to any other belief — either because they are self-justifying (as on a Cartesian picture, where a belief like "I think" is supposed to be infallible) or because they rest on a non-doxastic state, such as a sensory experience, that does not itself need justification because it is not the kind of thing that can be true or false. All other beliefs are justified by inference from these basic ones. The classic worry is that the stopping point looks arbitrary: nothing explains why these particular beliefs, rather than some others, get to be exempt from the demand for justification placed on everything else.

Coherentists accept the second horn. On this view A can be justified by B, B by C, and C by A, so that the beliefs justify one another as a closed loop rather than terminating in anything basic. Since no member of the loop is more fundamental than the others, there is no arbitrary stopping point, and the objection that this is "just circular reasoning" is less an objection than a description of what coherentism already says about itself.

Infinitists accept the first horn. Klein holds that a belief is justified only if there is a non-repeating, non-terminating series of further reasons behind it, one that never loops back and never bottoms out, which lets it avoid both arbitrariness and circularity.

The natural objection is that infinitism asks more of us than we can give, since no finite believer could ever actually produce infinitely many reasons. Klein's availabilist response is that justification does not require that the believer has already rehearsed all of these reasons, only that the infinite series exists and stands in the appropriate relation to the belief, independently of whether the believer could ever call any particular member of it to mind. A finite mind is therefore no obstacle to justification, since what does the justifying is the existence of the series, not anyone's grasp of it.

I don't think this fully succeeds. It answers the letter of the objection, since a series can exist whether or not anyone has traversed it. But this seems to buy off the objection by quietly turning infinitism into an externalist theory, on which my justification depends on facts I may have no access to whatsoever, which sits uneasily with the internalist sympathies that motivated the theory in the first place.
