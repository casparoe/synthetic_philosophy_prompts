And here's the answer I need graded:

"Fitch's paradox is an argument that the Knowability Principle (KP) -- the idea, central to semantic anti-realists like Dummett and Quine, that every truth is at least in principle knowable -- can't be true unless we're willing to accept that literally everything true is already known by someone. Formally, KP says: for any proposition p, if p is true then it's possible for p to be known, i.e. p → ◊Kp.

The proof is a reductio. Besides KP, we assume Non-Omniscience: that there's at least one actual truth nobody currently knows, i.e. some p such that p & ~Kp is true. If we plug this conjunction itself into KP, we get ◊K(p & ~Kp): it's possible for someone to know both that p is true and that nobody knows p. But this can't be, because of two basic facts about knowledge: Distribution (if you know a conjunction, you know each conjunct, so K(p&q) → Kp & Kq) and Factivity (knowledge implies truth, Kp → p). Apply Distribution to K(p & ~Kp) and you get Kp & K~Kp. Apply Factivity to that second conjunct, K~Kp, and you get ~Kp. So you're left with Kp & ~Kp -- an outright contradiction, derived with no assumptions beyond Distribution and Factivity. That means K(p & ~Kp) is not just false but necessarily false, so ◊K(p&~Kp) has to be false too, which flatly contradicts what KP handed us above.

So KP and Non-Omniscience can't both hold. Since it would be absurd to deny Non-Omniscience -- obviously not everything true is already known by somebody -- the standard move is to reject KP instead, at least in its unrestricted form. This makes the paradox a distinctively anti-realist headache rather than a problem for everybody: realists never wanted KP in the first place, since part of the realist picture is precisely that truth can outrun our capacities to verify it, so they're loosing nothing by rejecting it. Anti-realists, by contrast, need something like KP to make their whole account of truth as tied to verification work, so they're the ones stuck either giving up their theory of truth or accepting the paradox's very implausible payoff. Tennant's response is to restrict KP to what he calls 'Cartesian' propositions, excluding weird self-referential cases like p&~Kp, so the restricted principle can no longer be instantiated in the way the proof needs.

As for whether Fitch 'showed there are truths that can never be known' -- I'd say that's basically the right takeaway. The argument starts from a single unknown truth p (whatever that happens to be) and shows that p is a truth that can never be known, which is a striking thing to get out of such a minimal starting assumption."
