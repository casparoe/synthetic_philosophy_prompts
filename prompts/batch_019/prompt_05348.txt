I'm a retired analytical chemist, and now that I've finally got free time I've been working through a secondhand anthology on causation and explanation that my niece (she's doing a philosophy PhD) passed along to me. Most of it strikes me as interesting in a fairly abstract way, but it collided with real life last month in a way that's been bugging me ever since, and none of my old lab buddies have managed to settle it for me either.

I was in a car accident. I was going through an intersection about 8 mph over the speed limit when another driver ran the red light and broadsided me. Seemed completely clear-cut to me - until the insurance adjuster came back arguing I was partly at fault too, with a piece of reasoning I haven't been able to shake since. His logic runs like this: if I hadn't been speeding, I would have reached the intersection a couple of seconds later than I actually did, and by then the other driver would already have run his red light and cleared the intersection - meaning there'd have been no collision at all. So, strictly speaking, my speeding was just as much a but-for cause of the crash as his running the light was, and he wants to knock a chunk off my payout to reflect that. Every friend I've described this to agrees it's obviously a cheap trick, and that the other guy is really the one who caused the accident - but not one of us, myself included, can actually explain why the two "causes" shouldn't count equally, becuase as far as I can tell every individual step of his argument is true.

I'm not after legal advice (got an actual lawyer for that headache already). What I want to know is whether there's a real flaw in the logic somewhere, or whether forty years of running gas chromatographs just left me without the right concepts to see that the adjuster has a genuine point buried in there. Feel free to use whatever's the cleanest way of thinking about "cause" to sort it out - I just want to actually understand what's going on, not just be told what feels fair.
