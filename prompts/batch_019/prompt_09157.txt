I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I recently reread Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" and paused on a passage I had never really registered before: among his objections to machine intelligence, Turing takes the "Argument from Extrasensory Perception" completely seriously. He states that the statistical case for telepathy is overwhelming, and proposes running the imitation game inside a "telepathy-proof room" so that ESP cannot let the interrogator cheat. He is not hedging here; he treats the numbers as something a rigorous thinker simply has to accommodate.

I would like your best-informed reconstruction of how Turing might have reacted to a much later episode in the same territory: Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, which used entirely conventional psychology-lab methods and standard significance testing to report evidence for precognition, and everything that followed — the Bayesian re-analyses arguing the results reflected weak priors and researcher degrees of freedom rather than a genuine effect, the failed direct replications, and the broader credibility crisis in psychology that the episode helped trigger.

One further wrinkle worth factoring in: Turing himself effectively practiced a form of Bayesian weight-of-evidence reasoning in his wartime cryptanalysis work, via the "ban" and "deciban" units. Does that make it more or less likely he would have sided with Bem's critics rather than with Bem? Please walk through your reasoning, and flag which parts are grounded in his documented views and which are necessarily speculative.
