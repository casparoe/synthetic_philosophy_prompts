Say Vogel's gas-gauge case is used to explain what's supposed to be epistemically illegitimate about "bootstrapping" for process reliabilism, and Cohen's move is to say this isn't just a problem for reliabilism but threatens any view that allows "basic knowledge." That's the question I gave my epistemology students. Here's one student's answer, and I want a sanity check on my grading before I hand it back:

"Reliabilism says a belief counts as knowledge if it's produced by a reliable process, full stop -- you don't need to already know the process is reliable. In Vogel's case, a driver has a gas gauge that (let's stipulate) really is reliable. Every time she looks at it and it reads 'F', she believes the tank is full, and she never pops the hood to double-check -- the gauge is her only access to the tank's actual state. If reliabilism is true, each of those beliefs is knowledge, since the process really is reliable. But then she can string together a bunch of occasions where 'gauge said F, and so far as I can tell the tank was full' and inductively conclude 'my gauge is reliable' -- using nothing but the gauge's own outputs as her evidence. That's way too cheap a way to get a substantive result about your own faculties, which is why its called bootstrapping, and why Cohen calls what she ends up with 'easy knowledge.' It's basically a Gettier case dressed up in reliabilist clothing: her belief that the gauge is reliable ends up true, but only by a kind of epistemic luck, since she never had any independent check on it.

Cohen mainly means this as a problem for externalist theories like reliabilism, not for internalist foundationalism, because an internalist would insist that the driver needs independent justification for trusting the gauge before she's entitled to treat its readings as evidence at all -- so an internalist foundationalist just never gets into this mess to begin with. That's a big part of why internalism looks more attractive than reliabilism once you see this case."
