In a seminar on bioethics and mental health law, we recently discussed a real category of legal instrument sometimes called a "Ulysses contract": a psychiatric advance directive in which a person with a condition like bipolar disorder specifies, while she is well, that she consents in advance to hospitalization and medication during a future manic episode, and explicitly waives the right to revoke that consent for the duration of the crisis itself — precisely so that the crisis cannot be used to undo the plan that produced it. After the session, two classmates kept arguing about whether such a directive should ever be enforced against the person's in-the-moment refusal, and I've been turning their exchange over since without landing on a verdict of my own.

Elena opened by arguing that overriding the crisis-self's refusal is not paternalism at all, but simply carrying out the person's own settled instructions — exactly as Ulysses's sailors kept him lashed to the mast no matter how he begged once he actually heard the Sirens. Farid answered that the analogy fails at precisely the moment that matters: Ulysses's crew had no discretion, they were executing a fully specified order to the letter, whereas clinicians applying a psychiatric directive must judge, in real time and under real uncertainty, whether a given refusal is the illness talking or a legitimate change of mind — a judgment call the document cannot make on their behalf, which turns what looks like the patient's own precommitment into the clinician's paternalism wearing the patient's signature. Elena replied that this problem is not peculiar to Ulysses contracts: any sufficiently specific advance directive, a standard living will included, has to be triggered by someone's judgment that its stated conditions have been satisfied, and we don't treat that as fatal to living wills. Farid pushed back that the comparison actually cuts against her: whoever signed a living will is not around afterward to contest how it gets applied, whereas here the "later self" is right there in the room, awake, coherent, and actively arguing her own case, and overruling someone who is currently and articulately asserting a view takes more justification than simply pointing to an earlier version of her who signed a form. Elena's rejoinder was that, taken seriously, this would have stopped Ulysses from ever being bound in the first place: he too was right there, fully conscious, straining and pleading to be released, and the sailors disregarded him anyway — so if a live, articulate objection automatically defeats a precommitment, no precommitment could survive the one moment it actually exists to govern, which amounts to giving up on the idea entirely. Neither conceded the point, and the seminar moved on before anyone said who had the better of it.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and adjudicate who has the better of the argument at each stage, rather than simply awarding an overall winner? I'd also be interested to know whether there is a consideration that both of them are overlooking entirely.
