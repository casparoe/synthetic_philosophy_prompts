"The dogmatism paradox is usually traced back to some unpublished lectures by Saul Kripke, although most people encounter it through Gilbert Harman's discussion of it. It starts from something that looks completely obvious: if I know that P, then P has to be true. But if P is true, then whatever evidence shows up later against P has to be misleading evidence — meaning evidence that's been faked or distorted somehow, since real evidence, the thought goes, couldn't very well point away from something that's actually true. And because I already think I should ignore evidence that I know is misleading, it looks like the moment I know P, I'm entitled to know in advance that I can disregard any future evidence against P, no matter how convincing it looks when it actually shows up. That's what makes it a paradox: simply knowing something seems to give me a license to write off all future counter-evidence before I've even seen what it is, which feels like exactly the kind of close-mindedness we're not supposed to have.

The fix we discussed in class is the defeasibility theory of knowledge, which Kripke put forward to repair the traditional justified-true-belief definition after Gettier's counterexamples showed that definition was too weak. On this theory, I only know P if P is true, I believe P, I'm justified in believing P, and there is no further true proposition (a 'defeater') which, if I came to believe it, would remove my justification for believing P. Applied to the paradox, this attacks the very first move: if strong evidence against P really is sitting out there undiscovered, then defeasibility theory says I didn't know P in the first place, even though I was perfectly justified given what I had at the time. So the paradox doesn't actually show that knowing something lets you wave off any future evidence against it; it just shows that in cases where a genuine defeater is lurking, you were never in a position of knowledge to begin with.

Overall, I think this response works, but not for free: it makes whether I know something depend on whether a defeater exists anywhere in the world, whether or not I could ever find out about it, which seems to make ordinary knowledge (like knowing my own middle name) far more fragile than it intuitively feels."
