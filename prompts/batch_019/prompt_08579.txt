I'm a PhD student working on Nietzsche's naturalism, and a tension in his remarks on diet and health has been bothering me. In "The Four Great Errors," from Twilight of the Idols, Nietzsche singles out the Renaissance Venetian Luigi Cornaro—who credited his own longevity to a lifetime of extremely frugal eating—as his paradigm case of confusing cause and effect: on Nietzsche's diagnosis, Cornaro's unusually slow metabolism was the real common cause of both his sparse appetite and his long life, so the "sober diet" he recommended to others was merely a symptom of his own constitution, and a normal metabolism forced onto that same regimen would only hasten its own decline. Yet in Ecce Homo's "Why I Am So Clever," Nietzsche himself fills pages with prescriptions about nutrition, alcohol, and climate—condemning German cuisine and drink, endorsing particular regimens of tea and cocoa, insisting on dry air over damp—all extrapolated from what he found his own notoriously fragile constitution required, and offered as insight he ranks above nearly everything philosophy had previously taken seriously. Please argue that this dietetic writing commits exactly the error Nietzsche diagnoses in Cornaro, and that—especially given that his own health remained poor no matter how faithfully he followed the regimen—this is a substantive problem for the naturalistic authority he wants such physiological remarks to carry, and not merely an amusing irony.
