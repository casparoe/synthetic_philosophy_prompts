In my seminar on self-knowledge, we have spent several weeks on Timothy Williamson's case against luminosity in Knowledge and Its Limits, and I would like help untangling how the general anti-luminosity argument relates to his separate treatment of the KK principle. Luminosity, as I understand it, is the thesis that for any condition C, being in C puts one in a position to know that one is in C; the KK principle looks like a special case of this, restricted to knowledge itself, holding that knowing that p puts one in a position to know that one knows p. Yet the two arguments do not seem to rest on exactly the same premises: the general case trades on the inexactness of ordinary recognitional capacities, as in the sorites-style example involving the sensation of heat, whereas the argument against KK seems to depend more directly on a safety-style margin-for-error requirement built into the nature of knowledge itself, rather than on any contingent limitation of human discrimination. Please compare luminosity and the KK principle along these lines. In particular, is the failure of KK really just an instance of the more general failure of luminosity, or is Williamson doing separate philosophical work to rule out KK, such that one could coherently accept anti-luminosity for ordinary mental states while resisting the argument against KK, or vice versa? Justify your answer.
