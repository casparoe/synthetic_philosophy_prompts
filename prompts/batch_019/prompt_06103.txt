Ever since I retired three years ago (38 years running a molecular biology lab) I've fallen down a bit of a philosophy rabbit hole, mostly because my granddaughter is doing a philosophy PhD and finally wore me down into reading actual books instead of just nodding along at Thanksgiving. Something from my old career has been nagging at me for months — I have a hunch it's actually one of those philosophy questions in disguise, but I can't work out which one, and my book club certainly couldn't help (two hours of arguing last week and we definately made zero progress).

I still get roped onto my old department's research-integrity committee once or twice a year, since apparently being retired makes you "neutral." Last spring we had a case: a second-year postdoc, well liked, on a visa tied to the position, under a lot of pressure from his PI about an upcoming grant renewal, had spliced two lanes of a gel image together in a manuscript draft to hide a smudge, instead of just rerunning the blot like he should have. A sharp-eyed co-author caught it before the paper ever went out the door. We had him redo the experiment properly, and the real result backed up the paper's conclusion just fine — the doctored image hadn't even been necessary. He admitted everything immediately and was clearly mortified.

The committee split right down the middle. Half wanted the maximum response — a formal misconduct finding and a letter to his funding agency, which would effectively end his career before it started — reasoning that if we go easy on him just because a co-author happened to catch it and the science happened to hold up anyway, we're telling every trainee watching that cheating is fine as long as you're lucky. The other half (myself included, depending on the day) wanted something much lighter, like a serious reprimand and closer supervision, since nobody was actually harmed, the flawed figure never entered the scientific record, and ending a 28-year-old's career over one bad afternoon under enormous pressure seemed wildly out of proportion. What really gets me is that both camps seem to think the other side is being obviously unreasonable, and I cant tell if they're actually disagreeing about the same question or just talking past each other. Is one side simply wrong here, or is there some real fork in the road I'm missing?
