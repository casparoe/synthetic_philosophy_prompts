So I spent a bit over thirty years running a lab that studied beetle population dynamics, and now that I'm retired I've finally got time to read the philosophy of science books that were always on my list -- I'm most of the way through Deborah Mayo's book on severe testing right now, which is fantastic but also kind of maddening in places. Anyway, I still sit on the editorial board of a smallish ecology journal, and we've been going round in circles for months on a statistics policy for authors, kicked off by a couple of embarrassing failed replications in our subfield that came up at the last society meeting.

One camp on the board, mostly people ten or twenty years younger than me who grew up fitting hierarchical models in Stan, wants us to require posterior probabilities and credible intervals and basically discourage or outright ban conventional null-hypothesis testing in submitted papers. Their argument is that p-values get misread as "the probability the null is true" even by experienced referees, that you can't actually interpret a p<0.05 without some sense of the prior odds there was a real effect to find in the first place, and that the whole apparatus rewards exactly the collect-more-data-till-it's-significant behaviour that's been tanking our field's replication rate. The other camp, closer to my own vintage, thinks this mixes up the disease and the treatment: the real culprits are p-hacking, forking-path flexibility in how messy field data get cleaned and analysed, chronically small sample sizes, and publication bias, and none of that goes away just because you swap in a posterior -- priors and model structure can be shopped around just as easily as thresholds can. Their fix is preregistration, power analysis up front, and correction for multiple comparisons, and they don't think it should matter much which camp your in statistically. There's a quieter, third position, closer to mine, that a journal mandating a specific stance on what probability even *means* is overstepping what an editorial policy should be doing, whatever its merits as philosophy.

Could you draft the short position memo I'd actually bring to next months board meeting? I need it to land on a real recommendation rather than a "reasonable people disagree" shrug, with a few concrete things we'd ask of authors and reviewers starting with the next volume, and I need it to take the Bayesian camp's diagnosis seriously on its own terms rather than just waving it off in favour of the proceduralist line -- otherwise it's only going to convince the people who already agreed with me going in.
