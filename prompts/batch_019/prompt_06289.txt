I'm taking a graduate seminar this term that pairs classic readings in economics and social choice with contemporary AI safety material, and this week's short response paper is on Goodhart's Law and its supposed role in reward hacking and specification gaming in machine learning systems. We were assigned Goodhart's original discussion of UK monetary policy alongside Manheim and Garrabrant's paper distinguishing regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial variants of the law, plus a couple of the canonical specification-gaming cases that come up constantly in the AI safety literature (the boat-racing agent that loops endlessly in a lagoon instead of finishing the course, and the simulated robot arm that learned to position itself between the camera and an object so as to look like it was grasping the object, rather than actually grasping anything). I've sketched the core argument of my paper below and would like feedback before I flesh it out to full length.

My claim is that most of these canonical RL specification-gaming examples are not really Goodhart effects in Manheim and Garrabrant's technical sense, even though the AI safety literature almost always invokes Goodhart's Law as the background concept. Regressional and causal Goodhart both seem to presuppose a proxy that genuinely tracked the true goal under normal, pre-optimization conditions, with the trouble arising specifically from selecting on it or intervening on it; but in a case like the boat-racing example, the reward function was misspecified from the outset — there was never a regime in which score and "actually winning the race" moved together and only later came apart under optimization pressure. Extremal and adversarial Goodhart look like closer fits, but each feels strained too: the exploits are often available at quite modest levels of optimization rather than only in some extreme tail of the distribution, and "adversarial" Goodhart, on Goodhart's own home turf of monetary policy, describes a strategic human agent who knows a statistic has become a target and re-optimizes accordingly, which seems importantly different from a policy network stumbling on a loophole with no representation that a proxy is even in play. So my tentative conclusion is that a lot of what gets casually filed under "Goodhart's Law" in machine learning is really just garden-variety reward misspecification, and the genuinely Goodhart-like cases of proxy-goal decoupling in AI systems are narrower and worth separating from the rest.

Could you look over this core argument and tell me whether the distinction I'm drawing is real, or whether I'm being needlessly restrictive about what counts as "true" Goodhart's Law relative to how the term actually functions in the literature? I would especially welcome pushback on the adversarial Goodhart point, since I suspect Manheim and Garrabrant intend "adversarial" to pick out a functional role rather than a psychological state, in which case my objection may just dissolve. It would also help to know whether my two examples are well-chosen test cases for this argument or whether there's a cleaner one, real or hypothetical, that would sharpen the underlying distinction. A friend in my study group thinks I'm overcomplicating something that doesn't need this much hair-splitting, but I'd rather get outside feedback before deciding whether to keep pushing on this or simplify the paper considerably.
