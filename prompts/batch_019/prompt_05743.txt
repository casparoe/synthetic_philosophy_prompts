So this came out of a meetup that's basically the in-person offshoot of an old SSC/ACX discussion server. We'd just finished Hanson and Simler's "The Elephant in the Brain" as the group's book pick, and someone brought up the peacock's tail as the standard intro-to-signaling example, which somehow turned into a genuinely heated back-and-forth between two people in the group, Ravi and Lindsay, about what the handicap principle actually requires. I know enough about this stuff to follow the argument but not enough to referee it, so hoping you can do that part. Ravi's opening move (1) was basically the pop-sci version: the whole point of a costly signal like the tail — or a human equivalent, like torching money on a status object, or grinding through a PhD that doesn't teach you any real skills — is that it actually hurts the signaler. If it didn't cost them something real, cheap fakes would flood in and the signal would stop meaning anything, which is why Zahavi called it a "handicap" and not just a "signal": the cost isn't incidental, its the mechanism. Lindsay's response (2) was that this isn't what the math actually says once you look at how Grafen formalized it: the condition for an honest equilibrium isn't "the signal is costly," it's that the marginal cost of signaling harder is higher for a low-quality individual than a high-quality one, a single-crossing condition, basically the same thing that makes Spence-style job market signaling work. At equilibrium the top-quality signaler can end up paying almost nothing extra, since no lower-quality type finds it worth mimicking them — a genuinely healthy peacock, or a genuinely brilliant grad student, might barely feel the "handicap" at all. The waste isn't what's doing the work, the differential is.

Ravi's comeback (3) was that if the honest top type's cost can go all the way down to basically zero, what stops a mediocre signaler from evolving, or learning, to pay that same ~zero? Something has to actually cost the top type or the whole equilibrium unravels, so isn't Lindsay just smuggling the waste back in while claiming to have gotten rid of it? He also pointed out that Zahavi's own language is explicitly about waste, so on Lindsay's reading, Zahavi apparently misdescribed his own theory. Lindsay conceded (4) that you do need some real cost differential to hold the whole thing together, and admitted she vaguely remembers a later paper — possibly with "fallacy" literally in the title — arguing that the popular statement of the handicap principle conflates "costly" with "costlier specifically for cheaters," and that Zahavi's own waste-talk is a big part of why people keep misreading him. But she held her ground on there being a real, separate category from handicaps: an "index," like a deep voice tracking body size, which is honest because of a physical constraint rather than because anybody's paying for anything.

That's when Ravi pivoted (5) back to the human case that had started the whole thing: if a cheap "index" shortcut for something like conscientiousness or raw talent were actually available to us, we'd expect a lot more low-effort people successfully faking their way through elite credentialing than we actually see, so the persistence of expensive, time-wasting signals among humans suggests that, whatever the cleanest math allows for in principle, cost-qua-cost is doing real work in practice. Lindsay granted that's a fair empirical point, but said it answers a different question from the one they started arguing about, and doesn't rescue his original "cost is the mechanism, full stop" claim. Neither of them budged, and I walked away just as confused as when it started.
