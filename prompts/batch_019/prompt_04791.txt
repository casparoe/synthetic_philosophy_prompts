I'm the undergraduate representative on my university's Educational Policy Committee, and at this week's meeting we have to respond to a proposal from the Provost's office that would let any department opt into what it calls "open-resource" exams: students could consult the internet and AI chatbots during timed assessments, provided their submitted answers are written in their own words. The proposal's argument is blunt — what an exam is ultimately supposed to certify is that a student ends up with true beliefs about the material, and if a true belief reached with a tool open in front of you produces exactly the same downstream competence as a true belief reached through years of internalized study, the university has no principled basis for insisting on the second route. Roughly half the committee, mostly from the humanities, thinks this hollows out the point of a degree; the other half, mostly from the professional schools, thinks it's just candid about what employers and licensing boards already assume of graduates. The timing is a little uncanny for me, because in my epistemology seminar we've spent the last few weeks on exactly this gap: the passage in the Meno where a person with mere true opinion about the road to Larissa gets you there just as reliably as a person who actually knows the way, the swamping problem, which asks why a true belief that was reliably produced should be worth any more than a true belief that simply happens to be true, credit theories that tie knowledge to the exercise of your own cognitive abilities, and Kvanvig's case that it's understanding, rather than knowledge, that actually resists being swamped. I can't shake the feeling that the committee is fighting over a policy version of the question we keep circling in seminar: why, if at all, is knowledge better than merely true belief?

Could you draft the memo I need to bring to the committee? It should recommend one clear option — adopt the policy as proposed, reject it, or adopt it with specific conditions — and the recommendation has to rest on an actual position about whether, and for what purpose, knowledge beats true belief in this setting, rather than on the enforcement and cheating worries the committee has already hashed out in a separate memo. Whichever option you argue for, please give the strongest version of the alternative its due, since this will go out under the committee's name as our considered judgment rather than as a one-sided brief.
