I'm a bioethics PhD student drafting a dissertation chapter on triage ethics, and I find that most treatments of the "numbers problem" simply lay Taurek's position and the standard aggregative rebuttal side by side rather than letting them actually fight it out. Write a dialogue between two physicians on a hospital's mass-casualty triage team who must decide, within minutes, how to deploy their one remaining surgical team: it can save Patient A, who will otherwise certainly die, or be redirected to save Patients B through F, five other patients who will otherwise equally certainly die -- but there is no time, and no way to split the team, to do both. One physician holds, in Taurek's spirit, that the mere fact of their being more numerous is not itself a reason to save that side, and proposes flipping a coin instead; her colleague objects that calling such a toss fair ignores that C, D, E, and F are, in effect, being told their deaths don't count for anything once the choice is framed as one against one. Push the exchange past this opening move: the first physician should have to say whether her view leaves her just as willing to toss a coin for one against five thousand as for one against five, and the second should have to say whether letting the numbers decide here commits her to thinking that a person's claim on life is always something that enough other claims can, in principle, outweigh. Close with what the team actually does.
