So over the summer I read Judea Pearl's "The Book of Why" and got kind of obsessed with this idea that you can define causation through interventions -- like, X causes Y if forcibly wiggling X, holding everything else fixed, changes Y, and you can draw the whole setup as a graph with equations attached to each arrow. Then I fell down a forum rabbit hole where people were arguing that this "interventionist" idea is actually a different theory from the older philosophical one, going back to David Lewis, where X causes Y basically just means Y wouldn't have happened without X. Somebody in the thread posted this old case, I think originally from a legal philosophy book (Hart and Honore? not sure im spelling that right), that's supposed to show where the two views pull apart: a guy is about to cross a desert, one enemy secretly poisons his water canteen, and a second enemy, who has no clue about the poison, punctures that same canteen so all the water leaks out before the guy ever takes a sip. He dies of thirst out there. Everybody in the thread agreed that the puncturer is "the" cause of death and the poisoner isn't, but nobody could agree on whether that's actually what either theory says once you're careful about it -- like, do you count "his death" as the effect, or "his death specifically by thirst," and does that choice basically decide the whole case before you even get started.

I don't have any background in this stuff, it's just become a thing I chew on during my commute apparently.
