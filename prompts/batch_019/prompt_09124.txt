Here is the student's answer in full: "Aristotle's puzzle in NE V.5 is that a house and a pair of shoes seem to share nothing in common, so it's unclear how a builder and a shoemaker could ever exchange fairly. His solution is that all goods can in fact be measured against one real standard: need. Since need is too diffuse to use directly in transactions, money steps in as its practical stand-in, which is also why money exists by convention rather than by nature -- we could collectively agree tomorrow to stop honoring a given currency and it would become worthless overnight. In effect, money is what actually renders the builder's house and the shoemaker's shoes equal in value, and this equalization is what lets the exchange, and by extension the city itself, hold together.

This connects directly to the Politics, where Aristotle grows suspicious of money once it stops serving this function. He splits acquisition into oikonomike, the natural management of a household, and chrematistike, the unnatural art of wealth-getting, and treats commerce and trade generally -- kapelike -- as falling under this unnatural, censured category, since it aims at accumulating money as an end in itself rather than at meeting real household needs. Usury is the extreme case, and the one Aristotle condemns most strongly: interest (tokos, literally "offspring") makes money breed more money directly, with no productive activity mediating the two, which is the furthest thing from money's natural purpose as a medium of exchange. This is also the passage that contains Aristotle's own labor theory of value -- the idea that labor is the true common measure beneath exchange value -- which Marx would later develop much further in Capital."
