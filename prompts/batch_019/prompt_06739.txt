I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of quantum mechanics this term, and I'm drafting the final essay question. Rather than let students rehearse the standard "three interpretations" survey — Copenhagen collapses, Everett branches, Bohm has hidden trajectories — I want to press on a point that I think popular treatments tend to paper over: the status of the Born rule. Both Everettian and Bohmian mechanics are, at bottom, fully deterministic theories (the universal wavefunction evolves unitarily on Everett's picture, and particles follow determinate trajectories fixed by the guidance equation on Bohm's), and yet both are somehow meant to recover the very same |ψ|² statistics that orthodox Copenhagen simply posits as a primitive law governing collapse. That convergence has always struck me as suspicious: positing a probability rule as a brute axiom is one thing, but deriving or explaining that same rule from an underlying deterministic dynamics without quietly presupposing it is quite another.

Before I circulate the question, I'd like to see a strong sample answer, so that I can check it is fair and answerable within the space I've allotted. Please write such an essay, addressing whether either the Everettian or the Bohmian program actually succeeds in making sense of quantum probability without circularity. On the Everettian side, engage with the decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule associated with Deutsch and Wallace, and with the objection — pressed by critics such as Kent and Albert — that talk of "credence" or "uncertainty" is incoherent once an agent knows for certain that every outcome will be realized in some branch. On the Bohmian side, engage with the "quantum equilibrium hypothesis" and its associated typicality argument, as developed by Dürr, Goldstein, and Zanghì, and consider whether an appeal to a typicality measure over initial configurations is really any less question-begging than the Everettian program's decision-theoretic axioms. Having weighed these considerations, reach and defend a definite verdict: is one deterministic program more successful than the other at explaining the Born rule, and does either genuinely improve on Copenhagen's brute postulation, or is the appearance of explanatory progress illusory in both cases?
