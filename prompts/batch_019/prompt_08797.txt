The question on the midterm was: "In Section X of the Enquiry, Hume writes: 'no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact which it endeavours to establish.' (a) Reconstruct the reasoning that leads Hume to this maxim, making clear what work the notions of a 'law of nature' and of 'proof,' in Hume's technical sense, are doing in the argument. (b) In the second part of the essay, Hume gives several further, independent considerations for distrusting reports of miracles. Summarize these and explain how they relate to the argument in (a). (c) Does Hume conclude that miracles are impossible? Justify your answer by reference to the text."

Here is one student's response, exactly as submitted apart from two corrected typos:

"Hume's argument starts from the idea that our belief in any 'law of nature' is not an a priori certainty but is itself the product of experience: we call something a law of nature only because we have a completely uniform experience of events occurring in that pattern, without exception. Because that experience is 'firm and unalterable,' Hume says it amounts to a full 'proof' in his technical sense — not a demonstrative, logically certain truth, but the strongest kind of evidence experience can ever give us, one with no counter-instances. A miracle, by definition, is a violation of one of these laws. So whenever someone reports a miracle, we are always weighing two things against each other: on one side, the massed, unbroken experience of everyone who has ever lived that events of this kind do not happen; on the other side, the testimony of the witness or witnesses that this one time, it did. Since the uniform experience behind a law of nature is already about as strong as an argument from experience can ever be, the report would need to be almost inexplicable on any hypothesis other than the miracle's having actually happened in order to outweigh it — hence the maxim that the falsehood of the testimony must be more miraculous than the event itself. Because no merely human testimony could ever really meet that bar, Hume's overall conclusion is that miracles are impossible and that no rational person can believe that one has ever occurred.

In the second half of the essay, Hume adds that, quite apart from this general argument, the actual testimony we have for miracles is especially weak: witnesses to miracles are rarely people of the sort of education, integrity, and reputation that would rule out either their being deceived or their deceiving us, and human beings take a positive pleasure in the surprise and wonder that stories of the miraculous produce, which makes both the tellers and the hearers of such stories credulous. Hume also points out that miracle stories are concentrated among 'ignorant and barbarous nations,' which is really the same point as his famous remark that the miracles of rival religions cancel each other out: since these are exactly the communities least able to check such reports, and since their holy books all contradict one another about which miracles actually happened, we end up with many mutually inconsistent testimonies of equally dubious quality, none of which can be trusted over any other.

So the two halves of the essay work together: the first shows that even the best possible testimony could barely ever succeed, and the second shows that the testimony we actually have is far short of the best possible."
