Question (15 points). Consider the following sorites argument concerning the vague predicate "rich":

(1) A person with $50,000,000 in assets is rich.
(2) For any n, if a person with $n in assets is rich, then a person with $n − $0.01 in assets is rich.
(3) Therefore, a person with $0 in assets is rich.

Explain how an epistemicist and a supervaluationist about vagueness would each respond to this argument. Your answer should address: (a) which premise each theorist rejects, and why (5 points); (b) how each treats the truth-value of a claim like "a person with $R in assets is rich" for some individual R in the borderline range (5 points); and (c) how well each view accommodates higher-order vagueness — the fact that it is itself vague where the borderline cases themselves begin and end (5 points).

Student's answer.

Both views reject premise (2), but for different reasons and with different costs.

The epistemicist (Williamson, following Sorensen) holds that "rich" has a perfectly sharp cutoff — some exact n* such that a person with n* dollars is rich and a person with n*−0.01 is not — we simply cannot know where it is. This lets the epistemicist keep classical logic completely intact: bivalence and excluded middle hold without exception, the sorites argument is valid, and it is premise (2) that is simply false. Our not knowing the cutoff is not a special semantic phenomenon but is continuous with ordinary empirical ignorance, of the same kind as not knowing someone's exact weight without a scale: in principle, sufficiently fine-grained information about a person's finances and about how the community uses "rich" would let us pin the cutoff down, it's just that in practice the precision required is far beyond what we can achieve, which is why the boundary feels unknowable.

The supervaluationist (Fine, Keefe) instead says that (2) is false because it is false on every admissible way of making "rich" precise (every "specification"), even though no specification is privileged as the correct one; a sentence is true (super-true) iff it comes out true on all admissible specifications, false iff false on all of them, and neither true nor false otherwise. Since some specifications draw the line at $8,432,110 and others at $8,200,000.50, "a person with $8,300,000 is rich" is neither true nor false — it is a genuine truth-value gap, and in this respect supervaluationism is basically a three-valued logic in the style of Kleene, with "rich," "not rich," and "indeterminate" as the three statuses a case can have. The supervaluationist's chief selling point over epistemicism is that they avoid saying there is one particular dollar amount where richness cuts off; they only have to say that some cutoff or other is drawn on each specification, without committing to which.

On higher-order vagueness, the epistemicist has an easy time of it: since "definitely rich" is just another vague predicate with its own sharp, unknowable cutoff, the same story simply iterates, so there is no special extra problem. The supervaluationist has a harder time, since it looks like there should be a sharp fact about which specifications are admissible, and specifying that boundary just reintroduces the kind of precision the theory was meant to avoid; supervaluationists standardly reply by treating "admissible" itself as vague and re-running the supervaluationist construction one level up.
