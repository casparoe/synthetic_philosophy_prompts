Question: Bernard Williams argues that every genuine reason for action is an "internal" reason. Explain the internalism/externalism distinction as he draws it, making sure to say what work the agent's "subjective motivational set" (S) and the idea of "sound deliberation" are doing. Then explain what the gin-and-tonic/petrol example is supposed to show, and briefly lay out one influential objection to Williams's position.

Student's answer: On Williams's view, a sentence like "A has a reason to φ" is true only if A could reach a motivation to φ by a sound process of deliberation that starts out from something already in A's "subjective motivational set" (S) - his desires, projects, loyalties, and so on. External reasons theorists want to say that A can have a reason to φ even if nothing in S, however much A deliberated, would ever get him to φ. Williams thinks that kind of claim isn't really coherent, because a reason has to be able to explain why someone acted, and nothing that's entirely cut off from what the agent cares about could ever do that explanatory job.

Because of this, Williams basically ends up defending a Humean view of practical reason: reason is only ever instrumental, a matter of calculating how to get what you already want, and "sound deliberation" just means getting the means-end reasoning right.

The gin-and-tonic case is meant to keep internalism from collapsing into "anything you're motivated to do, you have reason to do." Someone wants a gin and tonic; the glass in front of him actually contains petrol, though he believes it's gin. Because the desire to drink it rests on a false belief about what's in the glass, deliberation from that desire isn't sound, so - Williams says - there's no internal reason to drink it after all: the false belief blocks the move from "motivated" to "has a reason," rather than just being absorbed into S.

The most influential objection is Korsgaard's: she argues that a person can undergo a kind of ethical conversion that gives them a wholly new value, one that doesn't trace back through any deliberative route to something already in their S - which would mean Williams is wrong that a reason must already be latent in the agent's existing psychology.
