So two friends from my decision theory cohort (I'm doing a philosophy PhD, decision theory/formal epistemology track) ended up on opposite sides of an argument after both being subjects last week in another student's dissertation study — a one-shot, fully anonymous prisoner's dilemma for real money, where you're told you're paired with "another random participant from the subject pool," no way to ever find out who, no repeat game. Jonah defected, Leah cooperated, and they've been going at it since.

1. Jonah says cooperating was just a mistake — defection dominates no matter what your partner does, so Leah left money on the table for nothing.
2. Leah says that's too quick here specifically: she'd just read about Hofstadter's "superrationality" idea, and since everyone in that subject pool is a grad student who'd work through the problem the same way, the real choice isn't "what do I do given some fixed partner" but "which joint outcome do I want" — and (cooperate, cooperate) beats (defect, defect), so she cooperated.
3. Jonah says the partner's choice is already fixed independently of hers, full stop — nothing she does now reaches back and changes or reveals it, and whatever the partner did, she'd have made more money defecting. Refusing dominance reasoning here isn't deep, he says, it's just wrong.
4. Leah says it's not backwards causation, it's evidence — if she's the kind of person who cooperates after reasoning like this, that's evidence her partner (drawn from the same pool, thinking it through the same way) cooperated too, and ignoring evidence just because it isn't causal is its own bias.
5. Jonah says that proves too much: you can't get richer by choosing to feel good about a correlation you like the sound of, and if this "superrational" move really paid off, people in these experiments who've never heard of Hofstadter and just defect out of plain selfishness should do worse on average, wich they don't.
6. Leah says the pool is doing real work that his counterexample ignores — these are specifically people trained the same way and told explicitly they're drawn from the same population, that's an actual structural fact and not wishful thinking, so random selfish defectors elsewhere aren't a fair comparison.

Neither of them was in my cohort originally so I genuinely have no horse in this race. Can you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point (they can both be partly right, or both wrong), and then say what you think the one thing is that both of them keep missing?
