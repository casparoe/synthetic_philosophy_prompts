I am drafting a short section on the reference class problem for a paper on single-case probability, and I would value your reactions before I try to develop it further.

The problem, in its classic form, is this: to assign a probability to a single case — say, the chance that a particular 54-year-old woman, newly diagnosed with a specific cancer subtype, survives five years — we need some reference class of similar cases whose observed frequency we can borrow. But she belongs simultaneously to indefinitely many such classes: 54-year-olds, women, women with this cancer subtype, patients on this treatment protocol, patients with this genetic marker, patients with that marker who also have some further comorbidity, and so on without any natural stopping point. Each class carries its own survival frequency, and nothing in the statistics themselves tells us which class is the relevant one for her. This is essentially the predicament Stephen Jay Gould described working through after being given a grim median survival figure for his own rare cancer, and having to reason out, largely by hand, which subclass of that population he actually resembled.

Reichenbach's own answer — use the narrowest class for which we still have reliable statistics — has always struck me as more of a practical maxim than a genuine solution. Pushed hard enough, "narrowest class with reliable statistics" collapses toward the class containing the individual alone, at which point there is no frequency left to speak of, only the trivial fact of what already happened. So the maxim needs a principled stopping point, and I do not think Reichenbach, or anyone since, has supplied one that is not ultimately pragmatic: chosen for the purposes at hand rather than read off the world.

What I want to argue, and what I am least confident I have right, is that this is not a problem peculiar to frequentism that rival interpretations get to watch from the sidelines. Propensity theorists present their view as sidestepping reference classes altogether, since propensities are supposed to attach to the single chance setup itself rather than to a class of cases. But specifying "the setup" still requires deciding which of its indefinitely many properties are chance-relevant, which just relocates the reference class problem into the individuation of setups instead of eliminating it. Something similar happens to Bayesians who want frequency data to constrain credence via something in the vicinity of Lewis's Principal Principle: they still have to decide which known frequency counts as admissible evidence, and that choice is not itself delivered by the frequencies.

If this is right, the reference class problem is not a curiosity for statisticians to tidy up; it suggests that single-case objective probability talk is either frankly perspectival, a modeling choice made for practical purposes without a further fact of the matter behind it, or it requires brute, freestanding propensities that answer to no frequency data at all, in which case the frequencies we do have are merely evidence for single-case chances and never constitutive of them.

I would be grateful for your reactions on three points in particular: whether I am being fair to propensity theorists and Principal-Principle Bayesians, or simply assimilating them to the frequentist's predicament by stealth; whether the closing dichotomy is genuinely exhaustive, or there is a serious intermediate position I am failing to engage; and whether the argument proves too much, that is, whether it would equally undermine kinds of probability talk we are clearly not prepared to give up, such as climate projections or actuarial pricing.
