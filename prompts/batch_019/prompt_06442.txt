And here's the answer, copied exactly as the student wrote it, typos included:

"Bradley's regress starts from a question about unity: what makes 'Socrates is wise' one unified fact rather than just three separate items -- Socrates, wisdom, and some sort of tie between them -- sitting next to each other? Say the relation 'is wise' is what does the tying. Then we can ask what relates that relation to Socrates and wisdom in turn. If we answer with a further relation, we need another relation to relate that one to the first relation and its terms, and so on forever. Bradley's point is that this series never closes, so treating relations as separate items that need to be hooked onto their terms can never actually produce a unified fact -- it just pushes the problem back a step every time. He used this, plus his other arguments against relations having any stable sense on their own, to argue that the relational, many-membered world of ordinary experience isn't ultimately real but merely 'appearance'; what's ultimately real is the single, all-inclusive Absolute, in which the distinctions we draw between related things get transcended.

Russell took the regress seriously because his logicist project needed relations to be fully real and mind-independent, not just useful fictions we impose. His way out, in the Principles of Mathematics, was to say the very same entity -- take the relation of loving -- can occur in a proposition in two different ways: as a mere logical subject (a term, the sort of thing you could refer to with a name like 'loving'), or as actually relating its terms, the way it occurs in 'Desdemona loves Cassio.' This is essentially the distinction Frege draws between saturated and unsaturated entities: an unsaturated concept, like an unsaturated relation, has a gap that its terms fill, and once it's filled no further glue is needed, so the regress never gets off the ground. Because the relating relation doesn't need some third thing to connect it to its terms, Russell's distinction blocks Bradley's regress right at the first step.

Russell held onto and refined this basic solution for the rest of his career; by the time he developed the multiple relation theory of judgment, the same core idea -- that a relation can unify its terms directly, without a further relating relation standing over them -- was doing the work of explaining not just how propositions are unified but how judgments are, which is why Russell treated the theory of judgment as the natural extension of his 1903 answer to Bradley rather than a break from it.

So Russell's move succeeds: because it blocks the specific regress Bradley describes, the unity of the proposition is secured."
