Ever since I hung up my lab coat a couple of years back (spent about three decades doing experimental particle physics), I've been using the free time to fill gaps in my education, mostly reading around in decision theory and epistemology, which I never had time for as a working scientist. Somewhere in this reading I ran into something called Good's theorem, apparently due to the statistician I.J. Good back in the 1960s, which says that for a Bayesian agent facing a decision, the expected value of taking a free look at some extra evidence before choosing is never negative -- you either end up better off or exactly where you'd have been anyway, never worse, at least on average. This rang a bell, because half my old career was spent in arguments with collaborators over exactly this kind of question: do we have enough data yet, or do we run the detector a while longer before committing to a result.

What I'd like from you is the intuitive, back-of-the-envelope version of why this has to be true, not the full measure-theoretic proof, just whatever core trick guarantees it -- the kind of explanation you'd give an old labmate over a beer. My hunch is it has something to do with always being able to fall back on ignoring the new evidence and just doing what you would've done anyway, so that looking can only open up better options and never close any off -- but I can't quite get that intuition to feel airtight on its own.

There's also a wrinkle that's been nagging at me. I came across something in economics called the Hirshleifer effect, where making certain information public apparently wrecks insurance arrangements and can leave everybody worse off, because once its common knowledge who's going to suffer some bad outcome, no one sane will insure them anymore. On the face of it that looks like a flat contradiction of Good's theorem: more information, and yet people end up worse off. So is it actually a counterexample, or is there some principled reason it doesn't count -- maybe because it's a genuinely different kind of setup than the single-decision-maker case the theorem covers? I'd like to actually understand where the theorem's gaurantee stops applying, rather than just taking on faith that the two results are somehow compatible.
