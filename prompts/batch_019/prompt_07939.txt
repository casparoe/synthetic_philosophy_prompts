I'm a novelist, and every book I finish ends the same way: a line in the acknowledgements admitting that "any errors that remain are, of course, my own." I've come to think this ritual disclaimer is more philosophically loaded than it looks, and I've drafted the following short essay for a personal newsletter. I'd welcome your comments before I post it.

By the time a novel of mine goes to the printer, I believe, of every sentence in it, that it is accurate — the dates line up, a character's eyes are the same colour on page 40 as on page 400, the bridge really does span the river I say it does. Yet I also believe, with something close to certainty, that the finished book contains at least one error I never caught. Philosophers have a name for this: the preface paradox, described by Makinson as a mundane cousin of the lottery paradox that Kyburg had posed a few years earlier. A ticket-holder can rationally believe, of each of a thousand tickets, that it will lose, while also believing that some ticket will win; an author can rationally believe every individual claim in her manuscript while believing that the manuscript, taken as a whole, is not entirely error-free. Both cases seem to violate the modest-sounding principle that rational belief is closed under conjunction: if it is rational to believe P and rational to believe Q, it should be rational to believe P and Q together.

The standard responses are to blame the Lockean thesis (the idea that sufficiently high credence licenses outright belief), to distinguish "believing" from merely "accepting for practical purposes," or to give up conjunction closure and accept a slightly disunified notion of rational belief. What I want to press on is whether the lottery case and my case really call for the same medicine. My belief that ticket #457 will lose rests on nothing but the arithmetic of the draw; I have no ticket-specific evidence whatsoever. My belief that chapter fourteen correctly gives a character's birthday as being in March rests on a specific fact I researched, drafted, and had a copy-editor check — evidence of a completely different shape, even though I also hold the general, statistical-feeling belief that a manuscript this long probably has a slip in it somewhere. If that difference is real, perhaps the two paradoxes should not receive a single, unified solution.

There is a further wrinkle particular to my trade: a lottery is drawn once and settled forever, but a novel goes through editions. The "guaranteed" error is a fact about a draft at a given time, not about the book as such, and it can be edited away the moment it is found. I cannot decide whether that observation genuinely weakens the paradox's force or merely restates an existing move in new dress.

Could you tell me, first, whether I have represented the two paradoxes and the standard responses to them accurately; second, whether the statistical-versus-specific-evidence distinction I am drawing tracks a real fault line in the literature or is simply confused; and third, whether the "editions" wrinkle amounts to a genuinely new consideration or just repackages a solution that is already on the table?
