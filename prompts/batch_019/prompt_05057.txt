I spent several hours last night arguing with a friend from my reading group about how much philosophical weight to put on Henry Sidgwick's decades of work for the Society for Psychical Research, which we are covering this month alongside the concluding chapters of The Methods of Ethics. My friend's position was that the psychical research is best treated as a biographical curiosity — a Victorian gentleman's reaction to the "crisis of faith," worth a footnote but essentially detachable from Sidgwick's standing as the most careful of the classical utilitarians. I think that is too quick, and afterward I wrote up the following few paragraphs to work out why. I would like your comments on this bit of reasoning, in particular: (a) whether I have the history and the internal logic of Sidgwick's own argument right; (b) whether the central philosophical move I attribute to him actually holds together, or whether it trades on a confusion between normative and empirical questions that I'm failing to see clearly enough to state; and (c) whether there is a better-known or more damaging line of criticism in the Sidgwick literature that I should be engaging with rather than re-deriving, imperfectly, on my own.

Here is the reasoning. The concluding chapter of the Methods leaves practical reason at an impasse Sidgwick cannot argue his way out of: he can find no flaw in the egoist's claim that it is uniquely rational for each person to aim at their own greatest good, and no flaw in the utilitarian's claim that, from what he calls the point of view of the universe, one is rationally bound to aim at the general good instead. Since the two verdicts can conflict and neither position can be shown to be simply confused, he names the result the dualism of practical reason and suggests that, absent some further premise, ethics as a unified rational discipline threatens to dissolve into two rival codes rather than one. The premise he floats, without ever quite endorsing it outright, is that the dualism would be resolved if we could suppose a moral government of the universe that guarantees a coincidence of duty and self-interest — a benevolent deity who apportions happiness to virtue, presumably across a life that continues after death.

My claim is that Sidgwick's SPR work, from his co-founding the Society in 1882 through his and Eleanor Sidgwick's involvement in projects like the census of hallucinations, should be read as a continuation of this argument by other means rather than a retreat from it. He is not a philosopher who happened to also dabble in ghost-hunting; he is a philosopher who, having failed to derive the needed premise from reason alone, went looking for evidence of it, holding the inquiry to the same punishing standards of self-scrutiny that make the Methods so exhausting to read. What worries me, and what my friend pressed on, is whether this really is a coherent research programme rather than a category mistake: even perfect evidence of personal survival and of a cosmic moral order would only show that duty and self-interest happen, as a contingent matter of cosmic bookkeeping, to coincide — it would do nothing to show that the egoist's reasoning was ever invalid, or that universal benevolence has rational authority over someone for whom, per impossibile, the two still diverged. So it seems Sidgwick may have needed his empirical research to settle a question about the rational authority and unity of practical reason that no empirical finding, however cooperative, could actually settle.
