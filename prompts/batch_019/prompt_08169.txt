I'm taking a course on American constitutional theory this term, and this week's unit concerns nineteenth-century abolitionist constitutionalism, with a focus on Frederick Douglass. As I understand it, Douglass started out as a Garrisonian, accepting that the Constitution was, in Garrison's phrase, a "covenant with death"; then, around 1851, he broke publicly with Garrison and, following arguments he credited to Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner, came to insist that the document itself is antislavery -- pointing out that it never uses the words "slave" or "slavery," and arguing that its ambiguous clauses should be read in light of the Preamble's stated ends rather than in light of what particular framers may privately have intended. He later made a similar move against Chief Justice Taney's opinion in Dred Scott, denying that the framers' presumed racial attitudes could settle what "We the People" had actually enacted.

Suppose Douglass were briefed on the present-day dispute between "original public meaning" originalism and living constitutionalism. Given the specific interpretive moves he actually made against Garrison and Taney -- not just his general antislavery convictions -- would he recognize himself as an early public-meaning originalist, or does his reliance on background natural-law principles put him closer to the living-constitutionalist side? Please reconstruct his likely position, and note any places where his reasoning resists being sorted neatly into either modern camp.
