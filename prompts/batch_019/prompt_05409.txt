I'm a retired physicist (forty-odd years doing condensed matter work before I hung up the lab coat), and in retirement I've fallen into philosophy, currently working my way through a history of modern logic. Frege became a bit of a hero of mine along the way - the guy basically invents quantification theory and the sense/reference distinction, and comes across, in everything he published, as this almost inhumanly careful, precise thinker with zero patience for sloppy "psychologistic" reasoning. So it knocked me sideways to find out that in 1924, the year before he died, he kept a private diary - only surfaced and got published long after his death - thats full of ugly, virulently antisemitic, anti-democratic, right-wing ranting against the Weimar Republic, socialists, Catholics, the French, and especially Jews. Apparently even Michael Dummett, the philosopher most responsible for making Frege central to English-language analytic philosophy, admitted decades into his career of writing about the man that finding this out was genuinely distressing.

Part of what bugs me is that I've seen a version of this before in my own field - Shockley went from Nobel-winning transistor work to peddling eugenics and "race science" later in life, and nobody thinks that biography changes a single equation in solid-state physics. But logic feels like it should be an even cleaner case than physics, since there isn't even an empirical result that could in principle get entangled with a guy's prejudices - it's pure inference and definition, about as far from politics as thought can get. Except there's a wrinkle that keeps nagging at me: I gather that in "Der Gedanke," Frege argues that thoughts belong to a "third realm" (his own phrase, "drittes Reich" in the German) distinct from the physical and the mental - an objective, timeless realm graspable by reason but not the senses. That's presumably pure coincidence of vocabulary, decades before the Nazis got hold of the word "Reich" - but it did make me wonder whether theres any real thread connecting his insistence on one objective logical truth that isn't up to individual minds, to his contempt for pluralism and democracy in politics, or whether I'm just pattern-matching where there's nothing there.

I brought this up at the little philosophy discussion group I joined and it turned into an argument nobody won, so now I'd like a proper essay on it. Don't just give me "separate the art from the artist, done" or "everything's political, done" - actually weigh the case for treating the diary as irrelevant to the logic against the case that an author's character or commitments can legitimately bear on how we read and use even highly technical work, and then land on an actual verdict about whether, and how, this diary should change how we think about Frege's philosophy. I want a defended, specific answer, not a "well, it's complicated" shrug.
