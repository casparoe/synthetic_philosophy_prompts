I spent my career as an exploration geophysicist, and only since retiring have I started reading philosophy in earnest, which has left me newly suspicious of certain habits of reasoning in my own field. Early on, a survey of ours turned up a small, unexplained gravity anomaly (on the order of a few milligals) over a particular basin, which we simply logged in an appendix for lack of any theory to explain it. Decades later, a former student of mine built a general model of how salt domes rise through overlying sedimentary layers, calibrated only on basins on another continent; by her own account she wasn't thinking about our old anomaly at all, and only discovered, after a colleague pointed it out, that her model reproduced its size and shape almost exactly. Our research group now treats this as some of the strongest evidence for her model, but it nags at me that the anomaly had already been sitting in our records for thirty years before her model existed, so no one actually risked being wrong about it. Is there a genuine reason this kind of old, already-known data should count for less than a fresh prediction would have, or am I simply being needlessly suspicious of a result my colleagues are right to celebrate?
