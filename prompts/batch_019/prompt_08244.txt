So last night my friend Priya and I got into a whole thing about the Sleeping Beauty problem — the one where a fair coin gets flipped, and if it lands heads Beauty is woken up once (Monday) and interviewed, but if it lands tails she's woken up Monday AND Tuesday, with a memory wipe in between so she can never tell which awakening she's in. Every time she wakes she's asked her credence that the coin landed heads. Priya's a committed thirder — her big move is the long-run frequency thing, where if you ran this a thousand times, two out of every three awakenings would be tails-awakenings, so 1/3 is the number that actually pays off if you bet on it. I've always found 1/2 more intuitive, because nothing informative happens between the coin flip and Beauty waking up, so why would her credence move at all?

But then I went and read more about David Lewis's actual reply to Elga (the classic halfer comeback), and it turns out his view has this consequence that's kind of humiliating for my side: if Beauty's credence in heads is 1/2 on waking without knowing the day, then the second she's told "it's Monday," plain conditionalization forces her credence UP to 2/3. Priya pounced on this imediately — how could learning what day it is possibly be evidence about a coin flip? And doesnt the fact that the thirder view gives exactly 1/2 once you condition on Monday (matching the coin's actual objective chance) show that thirding is the view that's well-behaved here, while halfing is the one that goes haywire?

I don't want to just concede this, so can you help me build the strongest possible defense of the Lewis-style halfer position — one that doesn't just wave the Monday result away but actually argues it's the correct, non-crazy implication of the view rather than a reductio of it? I'd really like it to directly engage with the Principal-Principle-style worry (that this seems to pull credence away from the coin's known objective chance for no good reason), since that was the point that actually won Priya the argument last night.
