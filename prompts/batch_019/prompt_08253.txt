I've been falling down a rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing on Victorian scientists who moonlighted in the occult, and Henry Sidgwick keeps snagging me. Everyone who took a philosophy class knows him for "The Methods of Ethics," but apparently he was also the actual first president of the Society for Psychical Research, spending decades chasing mediums and exposing frauds right alongside working out utilitarian theory. What's got me stuck is how that connects to William James, who was doing basically the same thing on the American side with the medium Leonora Piper — except James seems to have walked away from it all a lot more willing to think there was "something there," while Sidgwick reportedly stayed pretty agnostic to the end, even on his own deathbed.

My working theory, which you should absolutely correct if its off, is that these two guys weren't just dabbling for the same reasons. Sidgwick scholars talk about his "dualism of practical reason" — basically that he couldn't get egoism and the utilitarian "point of view of the universe" to rationally line up without some cosmic guarantee that virtue actually pays off, which is maybe why he needed hard proof of survival so badly and also why he was such a merciless critic of his own side's evidence. James, meanwhile, had that whole pragmatist "will to believe" thing going, which let him treat messy, non-repeatable evidence as worth taking seriously without holding out for the kind of airtight proof Sidgwick wanted.

Could you compare the two men's approaches to psychical research along those lines — the philosophical motivations underneath the hobby, and how differently they each handled ambiguous evidence? And then, as the real question: which of them had the more defensible epistemic stance given what they actually had in front of them, and why? Bonus points if you can work in what to make of the fact that after Sidgwick died, some of his own former colleagues at the SPR became convinced his circle was still trying to reach them through the "cross-correspondences."
