This term I'm taking a seminar cross-listed between philosophy and economics on collective rationality and judgment aggregation, and this week's meeting worked through the discursive dilemma -- what Kornhauser and Sager originally called the doctrinal paradox in the legal literature -- using the standard multi-member court example. A three-judge panel has to decide a breach-of-contract suit governed by the rule that the defendant is liable if and only if a valid contract existed and the defendant breached it. Judge A finds that a contract existed but that there was no breach; Judge B finds that no contract existed but that there was conduct that would have amounted to a breach; Judge C finds that both conditions were satisfied. Voting premise-by-premise, a majority (A and C) affirms that a contract existed, and a separate majority (B and C) affirms that a breach occurred, so applying the liability rule to those two majority findings yields liability. But voting directly on the bottom-line question, only Judge C favors finding the defendant liable, so a majority goes the other way -- the opposite result from the very same three ballots.

A few of us stayed behind after seminar arguing about what this actually shows, and I keep coming back to what seems like the obvious diagnosis: since these two procedures can't both be tracking the right answer, and majority voting is just counting, the contradiction surely has to originate in one of the three judges holding a set of findings that doesn't actually cohere with the liability rule. For my response paper, I want to argue that a court in this situation should run each judge's three findings back through the rule, flag whichever judge, if any, turns out to be internally inconsistent, and aggregate only the panel members who pass that check -- treating the dilemma as evidence of one bad panelist rather than a genuine problem with majority rule itself. Could you help me work out which of the three judges above is the inconsistent one, so that I have a worked example for the paper? And if flagging the incoherent judge really is the right fix, I'm not sure why List, Pettit, and the rest of the judgment-aggregation literature still treat this as a serious open problem rather than a solved bookkeeping issue -- is there something about the structure of the dilemma that I'm missing?
