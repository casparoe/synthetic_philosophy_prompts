I'm writing a historical novel about Galileo, and there's a scene I can't get right on my own. In late February 1616, on the Pope's instruction, Cardinal Bellarmine summoned Galileo to a private audience to warn him away from the Copernican opinion, shortly before the Congregation of the Index formally ruled on it. I want to reconstruct what might genuinely have passed between them, without reducing either man to a mouthpiece for "faith" against "reason": Bellarmine held a serious methodological position, and Galileo's confidence in heliocentrism at that point rested on less than he later liked to claim. Write the dialogue so that it turns on the real point of leverage each man has against the other. Galileo should invoke the Augustinian principle that scripture must be read accommodatively wherever it conflicts with a natural truth that has been properly demonstrated. Bellarmine should press him specifically on whether he possesses a demonstration in that strict sense, or merely a hypothesis that saves the appearances the way epicycles do for retrograde motion; give him room to make his own ex suppositione distinction on its own terms, rather than as a strawman for Galileo to dispatch easily. Let Galileo answer only with what he could honestly have appealed to by that date, not evidence he did not yet possess. Both positions should come out looking reasonable given what each man actually knew, and neither should simply concede: end with the matter still unresolved, much as it still was three months later, when Bellarmine gave Galileo a signed certificate stating only that he had been notified of the Index's decree and had not been made to abjure anything.
