A friend of mine—a physicist by training—and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about quantum immortality. Her position was the standard Everettian one: if the many-worlds interpretation is correct, then in a quantum-suicide setup (a gun wired to fire or not depending on the decay of a radioactive atom) you could never actually observe yourself dying, since your stream of first-person experience only ever continues down the branches where the trigger fails, no matter how vanishingly small those branches eventually become. I pushed back on personal-identity grounds: even granting Everett's physics, why think the survivor in the surviving branch is numerically the same person as the one who stepped into the box, rather than a distinct successor who merely inherits her memories and feels continuous with her? She retreated to a broadly Parfitian line—identity doesn't matter, only psychological continuity does—which felt like changing the question rather than answering it, and we never really settled anything.

Thinking it over afterward, it struck me that we were just re-litigating, in quantum dress, debates about individuation and modality that predate quantum mechanics by centuries—which got me wondering what Leibniz specifically would have made of the whole scenario. He seems like an unusually well-equipped, if unpredictable, judge. His correspondence with Arnauld commits him to denying that one and the same individual can exist across two diverging courses of events at all: an "Adam" who sinned and an "Adam" who didn't are, for Leibniz, simply two different possible people, since each substance is individuated by a complete concept containing every predicate it will ever have (the same point he later dramatizes with Sextus Tarquinius in the Theodicy's vision of the palace of the fates). Yet his principle of sufficient reason, together with his picture of monads as causally "windowless" substances unfolding a pre-programmed complete concept under pre-established harmony, seems to leave no room for the kind of brute, unexplained branching that Everettian physics posits—except that his principle of plenitude, on which the best world is also the one with the greatest possible variety of compossible things, might make him more sympathetic to something world-multiplying than one would expect.

So: working it out on Leibniz's own terms rather than just gesturing at "he believed in possible worlds," what do you think his considered verdict would actually have been on quantum immortality and the rationality of deliberately playing quantum Russian roulette? I'm especially curious whether his commitments push him toward dismissing the whole scenario as a category error, toward a Bohmian-style denial that any real branching or objective chance is going on in the first place, or toward some more surprising accommodation of it—and, either way, whether a rational Leibnizian stepping into the box should feel reassured, or should conclude that "immortality" of this sort secures nothing worth caring about.
