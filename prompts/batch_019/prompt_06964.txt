I've been putting together a "history of AI" lightning talk for my team, so I actually sat down and read Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" paper instead of just going off whatever summary of the imitation game everyone already knows. Buried in the section where he runs through objections to his own test, there's one literally called "The Argument from Extrasensory Perception," and he does NOT treat it as a throwaway joke — he flat out says the statistical evidence for telepathy is basically overwhelming, and he's genuinely worried it breaks his test (his example: if the interrogator asks both the hidden human and the machine to guess the suit of cards, a psychic human could beat chance while the machine obviously can't, which gives the game away). His fix is to stick both contestants in what he calls a "telepathy-proof room" so nothing can leak through.

That sent me down a rabbit hole on my lunch break. From what I can tell, the big quantitative ESP research of that era (Rhine's Zener-card experiments at Duke, plus similar card-guessing work in Britain around the same time) is exactly the kind of "overwhelming" evidence Turing would've had in mind, and a decent chunk of it was later shown to be seriously compromised — I recall one of the British researchers eventually being shown to have fudged his random number sequences, and that only came out decades after Turing had already died. Then in 2011 a legit psychology professor got a paper claiming evidence for precognition (peoples future choices statistically leaking backwards into their past behavior) published in a mainstream, well respected journal, and its become one of the go-to examples people cite for kicking off the whole "replication crisis" conversation in psychology, since it basically fell apart under later scrutiny.

So here's my actual question: knowing how Turing reasoned in that paper (and generally, as a mathematician who took rigor pretty seriously), what do you think he'd conclude if he somehow got briefed on all of that history? Would someone that statistically careful just shrug and go "fine, that specific data turned out to be junk, but my argument still goes through, just plug in whatever the best ESP evidence is today"? Or does the pattern of "overwhelming" evidence repeatedly dissolving under scrutiny undercut something deeper in how he was reasoning? And as a side puzzle — his fix was specifically a "telepathy-proof room," but Bem's result was about precognition, not telepathy — does a shielded room even make sense as a defense against precognition, or was that patch only ever going to work for some of the four phenomena (telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, psychokinesis) he lumps together? Walk me through your actual reasoning rather than just handing me a verdict, and be upfront about which parts are solidly grounded in the text versus your own extrapolation.
