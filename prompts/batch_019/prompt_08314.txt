I've spent a fair amount of time on LessWrong and in the older Overcoming Bias archives working through the St. Petersburg paradox -- the game where a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, on toss n, and you're paid 2^n, so that naive expected-value maximization says you should pay any finite amount to play, even though hardly anyone would actually pay much for the privilege. I'd more or less filed that away as an illustration of the general lesson that unbounded gambles break expected-value reasoning, and moved on. Then a footnote in a comment thread pointed me to a variant I hadn't run into before, which I believe is due to Alan Hájek and Harris Nover and is usually called the Pasadena paradox, and which is apparently meant to be a strictly worse problem than St. Petersburg -- not just counterintuitive, but genuinely pathological.

The setup keeps the same coin-flipping game, but the payout on the toss where heads first appears, toss n, is (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n rather than just 2^n. Multiplying each payout by its probability of 1/2^n, the 2^n terms cancel exactly, and the expected value becomes the alternating harmonic series: 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which sums to ln 2 if the terms are added in that order. The trouble, as I understand it, is that this series is only conditionally convergent -- the series of absolute values is the ordinary, divergent harmonic series -- so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem the very same countable set of terms can be reordered to sum to any real number whatsoever, or made to diverge to either positive or negative infinity, or to oscillate without ever settling down. So the 'expected value' of the game isn't ln 2, and it isn't infinite either, the way St. Petersburg's is -- it's supposed to be strictly undefined, which is meant to be a worse verdict than merely large or divergent.

What I haven't been able to develop any real intuition for is why this counts as a distinctively deep problem for decision theory, rather than a somewhat theatrical way of dressing up a nineteenth-century fact about conditionally convergent series that has nothing intrinsically to do with rational choice. Two things in particular are bugging me. First, given that St. Petersburg's expected value is already well-defined but useless -- infinite, and so unable to tell you what to pay or how to rank the game against other unbounded gambles -- what exactly does Pasadena's being undefined, rather than infinite, add to the indictment of expected-value theory? Is 'undefined' actually a worse thing for a decision theory to hand you than 'infinite,' and if so, in what sense? Second, is there a principled reason not to simply privilege the toss-by-toss ordering, given that it's the order in which the coin flips and the payment actually happen in time -- or does insisting on that 'natural' ordering already smuggle in the very assumption the paradox is supposed to be calling into question? I'd like your actual intuition on these two points, rather than mainly a restatement of the rearrangement theorem itself, which I already follow.
