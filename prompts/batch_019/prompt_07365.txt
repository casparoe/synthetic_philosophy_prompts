and here's the answer i have to grade, verbatim (typos included, not mine to fix):

"Both traditions are trying to solve the same underlying problem: how do you set values or policy for something that has to serve lots of people with different interests, without secretly just picking one group's values and calling it impartial. Rawls and Harsanyi both use a veil of ignorance to get at this -- you pick principles for society without knowing which position in it you'll end up occupying. Because you might end up anywhere, including at the bottom, both Rawls and Harsanyi conclude that a rational chooser behind the veil would maximize average utility across all positions, since that's what you'd want if you had an equal chance of being anyone. So the veil of ignorance is basically an argument for utilitarian aggregation as the fair, neutral solution.

CEV is answering a different question. It's not 'how do we fairly aggregate what people currently want,' it's 'what would people want if they knew more, thought faster, and were more the people they wished they were.' So CEV isn't a vote or an average over current preferences, its an extrapolation, and the AI only acts on the parts where humanity's extrapolated volitions actually converge.

Because of that, I don't think CEV counts as liberal neutrality in the Rawlsian sense. Liberal neutrality means the state (or the AI) doesn't take sides between conceptions of the good, it just enforces fair procedures. CEV does the opposite -- it assumes there's a specific extrapolated answer that's the real target, and it's built to find and implement that answer rather than stay agnostic between people's actual current values. That's not neutral, it's just a much more sophisticated way of taking a side.

On interpersonal comparison: veil of ignorance approaches need it openly, since 'maximize average utility' requires a common scale to average over. CEV gets sold as avoiding this because it's not literally summing utility, but I don't think it escapes the problem -- once you have to decide what counts as 'convergence' among volitions that don't perfectly agree, you need some way to weigh whose extrapolated preferences move the needle more, and that's an interpersonal comparison wearing a trenchcoat."
