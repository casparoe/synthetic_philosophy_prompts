Over dinner last night, a friend and I fell into a long argument about whether Thomson's lamp really shows that supertasks are impossible. My view was that the paradox only shows the scenario is underspecified: nothing in the sequence of switches settles the lamp's state at the limit, which is a gap in the description rather than proof that completing infinitely many tasks in finite time is incoherent. My friend countered with the Ross–Littlewood vase-and-balls paradox, where, at each of infinitely many accelerating stages, ten numbered balls are added to a vase and one is removed; depending solely on the removal rule, the vase can be proven to end up either empty or containing infinitely many balls, even though the count at every finite stage is identical and grows without bound. Her point was that since this supertask does have a rigorous, determinate answer, the real lesson is that supertasks are coherent and it's our intuitions about limits that fail — making Thomson's lamp a weaker case against supertasks than it looks, not a stronger one. Neither of us budged. Could you compare the two paradoxes directly: does the determinacy of the Ross–Littlewood case actually undercut the conclusion Thomson wanted to draw from his lamp, and which of the two paradoxes poses the deeper problem for someone who wants to defend the metaphysical possibility of supertasks?
