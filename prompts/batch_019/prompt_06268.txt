I'm a student tutor for my school's dual-enrollment epistemology course, and this week the professor asked the tutors to do a first pass of grading on practice short-answer responses using her rubric while she's traveling at a conference. I'd like an independent read on this one before I turn in my comments.

The question given to students was: "Reconstruct the argument from illusion in standard form, and explain how disjunctivism is meant to block its conclusion. Is the disjunctivist strategy successful?"

Here is the student's response:

"The argument from illusion begins with everyday cases like a straight oar that looks bent when half-submerged in water, or a round coin that looks elliptical when viewed at an angle. In each case, the object doesn't actually have the quality we seem to perceive it as having, so it seems we cannot be directly aware of the object itself in having the experience; there must instead be something else we're aware of that does have the apparent quality — a 'sense-datum.' The argument then generalizes: an illusion can be subjectively just like a veridical perception of an object that really does have the perceived quality (a genuinely elliptical coin viewed head-on would look exactly like the tilted circular one), and since the illusory case has already been shown to involve awareness of a sense-datum rather than the object itself, the veridical case must involve the very same kind of awareness — the only difference being that the sense-datum happens to match the object's real properties. If sound, this undermines naïve realism, the common-sense view that ordinary perception is a direct relation between a perceiver and mind-independent objects, since it implies that even in the best case we are only ever aware of private mental intermediaries, not the world itself.

Disjunctivism, the view defended by sense-datum theorists such as A. J. Ayer, resists this by denying that a veridical perception and a matching illusion can really be indistinguishable, since the two are fundamentally different kinds of mental state: the veridical case is a direct relation to the object itself, while the illusory case is not. Because the two experiences share no 'highest common factor,' nothing about the nature of the illusory case carries over to the veridical one, so naïve realism about genuine perception survives untouched.

I think this is a fairly strong move, since it targets the generalizing step specifically rather than denying that illusions happen at all, which would be a much harder position to hold onto."
