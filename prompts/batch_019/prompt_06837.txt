So last night I got into it — like a solid hour-and-a-half argument — with my friend Marcus about whether he "has a reason" to patch things up with his brother. Backstory: the two of them fell out hard over how their dad's estate got split four-ish years ago, they barely speak now, and Marcus swears up and down he feels nothing about it anymore, no guilt, no longing, nothing. I kept saying "dude, you have a reason to at least send him a text," and he kept firing back with "I hear you, but I genuinely don't care, so there's no reason there for me, your just projecting your own values onto me and calling it a fact." I couldn't actually explain why he was wrong, if he even was, and it's been bugging me all day.

Lying awake after, I remembered some stuff from a seminar on reasons for action I took years ago — Bernard Williams' internalism thing. The idea, if I'm remembering it right, is that a claim like "A has a reason to X" is only true if A could actually get motivated to X via some sound deliberative route starting from what's already in their real motivational set — not just immediate desires, but also standing projects, loyalties, cares, that kind of thing. And if there's no such route available, then an "external reason" claim like the one I was making to Marcus isn't just unproven — Williams thinks it's basically a category error dressed up as a reason-claim, more like a disguised evaluation or a "requirement." There's an example he uses (Owen Wingrave, I think it's from a later essay of his on blame, could be misremembering) where a father tells his son he has "reason" to join the army because it's the family trade, even though the son has zero interest in soldiering — and Williams's point is that the father's claim doesn't actually hold up as a genuine reason for Owen. But I also vaguely recall this isn't the end of the story — McDowell wrote a whole response arguing that practical wisdom/virtue can basically generate new reasons that outrun whatever's currently sitting in your motivational set, Korsgaard has some Kantian line where reasons come from just being a reflective rational agent, period, and Michael Smith tries to split the difference with an idealized-advisor version of internalism.

Anyway, I'd like to actually understand what's at stake here instead of just vibes-arguing with Marcus next time. Please describe the most important implications of the internalism/externalism debate about reasons (Williams' view plus the main externalist replies) for: (a) whether my "you have a reason to reach out to him" claim to Marcus was actually true or even meaningful in the first place, and (b) more generally, how much weight I should give it when someone plays the "I just don't care, so there's no reason for me" card as a conversation-ending move. I'd rather you actually engage with the machinery — motivational sets, sound deliberation, all that — than give me a generic "some philosophers think X, others think Y" summary.
