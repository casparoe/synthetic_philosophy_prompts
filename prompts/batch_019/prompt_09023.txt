A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about how Alan Turing would have reacted to the "Löbian obstacle" that comes up in contemporary discussions of self-modifying AI. The obstacle, roughly, is this: Löb's theorem shows that a formal system can prove "if I can prove P, then P" for a given statement P only when it can already prove P outright. As a result, an agent that tries to grant itself unrestricted trust in the future proofs of its own reasoning, or of a successor it builds (what alignment researchers call a "tiling" agent), collapses into inconsistency rather than gaining any real assurance. My friend insists this is just a technical restatement of the "Mathematical Objection" that Turing already raised and dismissed in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" — that machines, like people, needn't be infallible or fully self-transparent to qualify as intelligent — and that his original reply disposes of it equally well. I think this overlooks what is specific to the Löbian obstacle, since it concerns an agent's ability to formally justify trusting another reasoner exactly as powerful as itself, not merely the existence of individual undecidable statements.

Reasoning from what Turing actually argued, what do you think his considered verdict on the Löbian obstacle would have been: would he have folded it into the same rebuttal he gave the Mathematical Objection, or is there something about self-trust between equally powerful reasoners that his original argument doesn't cover?
