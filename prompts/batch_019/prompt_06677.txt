A friend and I spent the better part of three hours arguing about Wittgenstein's On Certainty over dinner last night, and by the time we parted ways she had more or less talked me around to a conclusion I still find unsettling. We are both revisiting the book for a reading group on epistemology, and the argument started over her claim that hinge propositions erase any principled distance between scientific and religious conviction. Her reasoning, which I keep finding myself repeating to other people, runs roughly as follows: Wittgenstein shows that even a paradigm certainty like "I have two hands" is not known or justified by evidence in the ordinary sense but simply stands fast for us, exempt from doubt, the hinge on which our whole practice of doubting and giving reasons turns. If our most basic empirical convictions are groundless in precisely this way, resting on nothing firmer than their role in a shared form of life, then she thinks it is arbitrary to keep treating a religious believer's bedrock convictions any differently, calling theirs "unfalsifiable" while quietly exempting our own hinges from the same charge. On her reading, the physicist and the oracle-consulting king Wittgenstein describes clashing near the end of the book are in exactly the same epistemic position, so nothing in On Certainty actually entitles either of them to the view that their own framework is more rational, only that it is theirs.

I have not been able to shake the feeling that this moves too fast, but I could not, in the moment, say exactly where. Granting that hinge propositions really are groundless in Wittgenstein's technical sense, how should I think about whether we are nonetheless entitled to treat some hinges, or some whole frameworks, as more rational than others? Or did she basically get the book right, such that any honest reckoning with our own hinges commits us to that kind of even-handedness between, say, a modern physicist and a king who trusts an oracle?
