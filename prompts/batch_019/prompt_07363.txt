My grandmother, a retired philosopher of science who built her career on logical positivism and counted A. J. Ayer as something close to a personal hero, died last week at eighty-six. Six years ago she went into cardiac arrest during a routine procedure and was without a pulse for several minutes; when she came around, she said the experience matched almost exactly the one Ayer describes in his essay "What I Saw When I Was Dead." Like him, she was afterward scrupulous about exactly how much this had changed her mind, which for a committed verificationist was no small needle to thread: she still didn't believe in God, still didn't expect to survive her own death in any personal sense, and still said she'd rather death turn out to be a full stop than not -- but her confidence that it would be had softened by some specific, small amount. An update, she insisted, not a conversion. I've read enough rationalist blogs over the years to recognize that as close to the platonic ideal of updating a credence in proportion to the evidence, and I've always privately thought of her that way. She left instructions that the eulogy respect this precisely: no "she's watching over us now," but also no flat claim that she died certain this was the end, since by her own account she wasn't, and she'd have found either kind of false certainty a little insulting. I'm giving the eulogy for a room that will mix family with no philosophical background and a few old friends from her decades-long philosophy reading group who will catch the Ayer reference immediately. Please write it so that it comforts the first group without misrepresenting her to the second; aim for about five to seven minutes.
