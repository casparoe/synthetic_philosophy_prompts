Since retiring from a career in geophysics, I've been sitting in on a free-will reading group that a local philosophy department runs for retirees and other non-specialists. At our last session, two of the regulars, Renata and Dev, got into an exchange over Frankfurt's attack on the principle of alternative possibilities that I haven't been able to settle for myself.

(1) Renata opened with the standard Jones-and-Black case: Black will force Jones to perform a certain action if, and only if, Jones shows some prior sign that he is about to decide otherwise; as it happens Jones decides on his own, Black never has to intervene, and Jones is plainly responsible even though Black's presence made any other outcome impossible — so, she concluded, moral responsibility doesn't require the ability to do otherwise. (2) Dev answered with the "flicker of freedom" reply: Jones still had one alternative open to him, namely, displaying the prior sign, so some alternative possibility is still doing the work of grounding his responsibility, and all Frankfurt has really shown is that the required alternative can be far thinner than actually doing otherwise. (3) Renata came back with what she called Fischer's robustness point: an incipient sign that Jones doesn't control, and may not even be aware of, isn't the kind of thing anyone could be held responsible for producing or withholding, so it can't be what makes Jones responsible — and the one alternative that could do that work is exactly what Black's setup rules out. (4) Dev then raised a dilemma he credited to Widerker: either the sign only imperfectly predicts Jones's decision, in which case Black can't actually guarantee the outcome and a genuine alternative survives after all, or it guarantees the outcome only because it deterministically fixes the decision, in which case the example just assumes that determinism is compatible with responsibility, which is exactly what's in dispute. Renata's closing reply was that this is old news, since Mele and Robb had already built a version using a deterministic backup process running alongside Jones's own decision-making, designed specifically to escape that dilemma, and that Dev was arguing against the 1969 case rather than the current state of play.

Both of them clearly know the material, and I don't think either is being careless — but trained as I am to suspect an unmodeled parameter whenever two competent people read the same case and reach opposite conclusions, I can't tell at which points the exchange turns on a real insight rather than a debater's move.
