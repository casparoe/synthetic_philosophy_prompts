I am a junior at my high school and co-run our philosophy club, which records a short interview for the club's podcast each month and shares it with the rest of the school. Next month's guest is a philosophy postdoctoral researcher at a university near us whose work is on moral psychology, and specifically on whether hypocrisy can strip a person of the standing to blame someone else, as distinct from simply making that blame unpleasant to hear. In an email describing her position, she explained that someone who condemns another person for doing X, while doing X themselves and never acknowledging or renouncing it, forfeits the standing to blame for that particular fault, even when their moral judgment about X is entirely correct and the other person really is guilty. She mentioned that she builds on R. Jay Wallace's idea that blame functions as a kind of moral address that presupposes equal standing between the parties involved. She also takes seriously G.A. Cohen's question of whether a government with a serious record of wrongdoing of its own retains the standing to condemn terrorism carried out by others, and she has to answer critics such as Macalester Bell, who deny that "standing to blame" is a separate thing that can be forfeited at all, rather than blame simply being tactless, unkind, or badly timed.

I became interested in this after a disagreement in my AP Government class over whether corruption charges against a senator made it illegitimate for him to criticize a rival politician's corruption, and I would like the interview to get past the textbook version of the view within the first couple of questions. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, sequenced so the conversation builds toward the harder objections, that go beyond having her simply restate the position? I would like at least one question on the worry that this whole framework hands genuine wrongdoers a ready-made rhetorical shield against true criticism ("you are a hypocrite, so you have no standing"), one on how she distinguishes losing standing from the blame merely being false, unkind, or badly timed, one on whether standing is forfeited completely or only by degrees and what, if anything, would let someone earn it back, one that tests whether hypocrisy, rather than something like mere complicity in the same wrong without any double standard, is really doing the work in her account, and one on whether the view is meant to extend to collective agents such as governments or institutions, given the example she raised about condemning terrorism. For each question, please add a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, the way a good interviewer pushes back rather than moving straight to the next topic.
