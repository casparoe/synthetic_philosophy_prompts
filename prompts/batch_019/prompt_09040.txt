Two months ago, my partner and I lost out on a house after raising our offer four separate times past the ceiling we had explicitly agreed on beforehand, telling ourselves after each increase that it would definitely be the last one. When I recounted this afterward to a friend who works in decision science, she said we had basically lived out Martin Shubik's "dollar auction": a game in which a dollar is auctioned to the highest bidder as usual, except the second-highest bidder also has to pay their final bid and gets nothing for it, so that once you're in second place it can seem to make sense to bid once more rather than eat a certain loss. I'd already run into this game once before, in a negotiation workshop at work, where it was presented as a clean illustration of the sunk cost fallacy and of how otherwise sensible people get emotionally trapped into escalating commitment. My friend, though, pushed back on that framing rather forcefully: she argued that once you allow for mixed strategies and think about the game the way a game theorist would, a good deal of the "irrational" overbidding you see even among fully rational players just falls out of the structure of the game itself - closer to a biological war of attrition, where escalation is itself part of the equilibrium, than to a bias sitting inside anyone's head.

I'd like to actually understand this disagreement rather than just adopt whichever framing sounds more sophisticated, since it bears on how I should think about my own behavior with the house. Could you write a dialogue between a psychologist working in the escalation-of-commitment tradition and a game theorist who studies auctions of this kind, in which they try to work out whether the runaway bidding the dollar auction produces is better explained by a genuine bias in the bidders or by the incentive structure of the game itself? Please have them engage each other's actual evidence rather than just restate their own side's favorite examples past one another - for instance, whatever the game theorist wants to say about equilibrium play should have to confront the fact that similar escalation shows up even in purely solitary decisions with no strategic opponent at all, and whatever the psychologist wants to say about bias should have to confront why real bidders' behavior tracks the game's payoff structure as closely as it does. I'd rather they leave some real tension unresolved than have either side concede too neatly.
