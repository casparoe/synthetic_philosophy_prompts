**Question 3 (20 points).** Imprecise probabilism represents a rational agent's credal state not by a single probability function p, but by a set of such functions P (a "representor" or "credal set").

(a) [8 points] Using the contrast between a coin known to be fair and a coin of completely unknown bias, explain what advantage the imprecise probabilist claims over standard "sharp" Bayesianism — and why it isn't enough for the sharp Bayesian to reply that the principle of indifference delivers p(heads) = 0.5 in both cases anyway.

(b) [12 points] Adam Elga has argued that imprecise credal states cannot be paired with any reasonable rule for choosing among bets without generating irrational behavior once decisions are made in sequence. Reconstruct the argument, including the concrete betting example, and then assess it: does it succeed in showing that imprecise probabilism should be abandoned?

**Student's answer:**

(a) On the standard Bayesian picture, your degree of belief in a proposition is given by a single probability function p, and rational learning is just conditionalization on p. The imprecise probabilist replaces p with a set of functions P, sometimes called your "representor" — think of it as a committee of possible probability functions, all consistent with your evidence, that haven't been narrowed down to one.

The point of doing this shows up once we compare two superficially similar cases. Suppose coin 1 has been tossed a thousand times and has come up heads roughly half the time, so you're now confident it's fair. Suppose coin 2 you know nothing about at all — it might be two-headed, might be biased 90% toward tails, you have no idea. A sharp Bayesian, invoking the principle of indifference, assigns p(heads) = 0.5 to both coins, since nothing distinguishes heads from tails in either case. But this can't be the whole story: we are, and should be, much more willing to bet on coin 1 at even odds than on coin 2, and a single number can't record that difference. This is exactly the contrast Ellsberg drew between the "weight" and the "balance" of evidence: coin 1's 0.5 is backed by a weight of evidence, coin 2's 0.5 reflects mere balance — an absence of any consideration pulling one way rather than the other. The imprecise probabilist captures the difference by writing P(heads) = {0.5} for the fair coin but P(heads) = [0,1] for the unknown coin: same midpoint, very different epistemic situations, and the sharp Bayesian's formalism has no room to mark the distinction at all.

(b) Elga's target is exactly this kind of maximally imprecise state. Suppose your credence that a coin lands heads is P(heads) = [0,1], and you're offered two bets in succession: bet A pays -10 if heads and +15 if tails; bet B pays +15 if heads and -10 if tails. Since your credal committee disagrees about how likely heads is, the expected value of A ranges across the interval [-10,15] as you range over the committee, and likewise for B; refusing either bet guarantees 0. Because neither accepting nor refusing dominates the other for every member of the committee, a natural permissive decision rule counts accepting and refusing as incomparable, so refusing is always a permissible choice. That looks fine bet-by-bet, but if you refuse both A and B you walk away with 0 for certain, whereas accepting both nets you +5 for certain regardless of the coin's outcome (heads: -10+15=5; tails: 15-10=5). So a policy that is permissible at every individual choice point is, taken as a whole, strictly dominated by another available policy. Elga takes this to show that the trouble is not a fixable glitch in one particular decision rule but a structural feature of pairing imprecise credences with any permissive rule for choice, and he concludes that this is a decisive mark against imprecise probabilism as an epistemology of belief, not merely a curiosity in decision theory. I think this is right: no decision rule for imprecise credences can avoid licensing this kind of policy, so an agent who takes imprecise credences seriously is committed, at least sometimes, to passing up a sure gain for no compensating benefit. That's enough to justify the orthodox insistence that rational credence be sharp, whatever the costs of sharpness turn out to be elsewhere.
