So in my decision theory seminar this week we worked through the Pasadena paradox (not the St. Petersburg game everyone already knows -- its weirder, less famous cousin), and it's been bugging me since. Quick recap in case it's useful: you get paid based on how many tosses of a fair coin it takes to land heads for the first time, but the payoffs are rigged so that each term of the expected-value sum comes out to (-1)^(n+1)/n -- i.e. the whole expectation is just the alternating harmonic series, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges, but only conditionally, not absolutely. That matters a lot, because a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to sum to literally any real number you want, or made to diverge, or to oscillate forever without settling, and nothing about the gamble itself privileges one ordering of the outcomes over any other. So unlike St. Petersburg, where the expected value is at least a clean (if useless) infinity, Pasadena doesn't even hand you a well-defined number to be shocked by in the first place.

I'm a 2nd year philosophy PhD student (I work mostly in formal epistemology/decision theory), and the reason I can't let this go is that a bunch of my friends from undergrad are pretty deep into effective altruism and longtermism now, and their whole case for prioritizing stuff like existential risk reduction over nearer-term causes leans really hard on expected-value calculations involving tiny probabilities and astronomically huge, poorly-bounded stakes. Whenever I bring up Pasadena at the pub people kind of wave it off as a cute math curiosity with no bearing on real reasoning, but the more I sit with it the less sure I am that's right, since "poorly-behaved sums over huge, uncertain outcome spaces" is basically the whole territory longtermism operates in.

Could you walk me through what you think are the most important implications of the Pasadena paradox for this kind of expected-value-driven longtermist reasoning? I already know the standard "expected value can just diverge, à la St. Petersburg" objection so I'm less interested in that one, and more in wether Pasadena specifically teaches us something extra -- something about whether expected value can even coherently function as a decision rule once the outcome space gets sufficiently wild, and what (if anything) should replace it if it can't.
