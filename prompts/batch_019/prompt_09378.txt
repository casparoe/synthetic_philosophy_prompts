My father was given a terminal diagnosis six weeks ago, and for the first time since childhood I've caught myself wondering whether I should try to talk myself into a religious belief I don't actually hold, purely for whatever comfort it might give either of us. That question sent me back to Pascal's Pensées, past the "infinite expected value" summary everyone quotes, to the actual text. I've gotten stuck on the passage where Pascal's imagined skeptic, having granted the force of the wager argument, objects that he is "so made that [he] cannot believe" and asks what he is supposed to do about it. Here is Pascal's answer, in W. F. Trotter's standard translation:

"I confess it, I admit it. But, still, is there no means of seeing the faces of the cards?" Yes, Scripture and the rest, etc. "Yes, but I have my hands tied and my mouth closed; I am forced to wager, and am not free. I am not released, and am so made that I cannot believe. What, then, would you have me do?"

True. But at least learn your inability to believe, since reason brings you to this, and yet you cannot believe. Endeavour, then, to convince yourself, not by increase of proofs of God, but by the abatement of your passions. You would like to attain faith and do not know the way; you would like to cure yourself of unbelief and ask the remedy for it. Learn of those who have been bound like you, and who now stake all their possessions. These are people who know the way which you would follow, and who are cured of an ill of which you would be cured. Follow the way by which they began; by acting as if they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said, etc. Even this will naturally make you believe, and deaden your acuteness. "But this is what I am afraid of." And why? What have you to lose?

A footnote in my edition points out that the phrase Trotter softens to "deaden your acuteness" is "vous abêtira" in Pascal's French — something closer to "will make a beast of you." That gap is what I can't get past. Pascal has just spent several paragraphs calling the wager argument "demonstrable," but the moment his interlocutor accepts the argument and still can't believe, Pascal doesn't offer a better reason — he prescribes a regimen of ritual and imitation aimed at wearing down the very faculties that were doing the reasoning a moment earlier. Is that pivot a quiet admission that the decision-theoretic argument was never going to be sufficient by itself to produce belief, or is it continuous with what Pascal thought belief was all along? And is there a genuine problem, not merely a rhetorical embarrassment, in advising someone to blunt their own critical faculties as a means to an end chosen on prudential grounds? I want to understand what this move is actually doing before I decide whether it has anything to offer me.
