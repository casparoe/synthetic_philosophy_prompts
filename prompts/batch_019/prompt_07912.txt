The essay prompt, as posted by the instructor, reads:

"Kant argues in the Groundwork that an action's moral worth depends not on its conformity to duty but on whether it is done from duty, and illustrates this with the shopkeeper who does not overcharge a naive customer, and with the 'friend of humanity' whose case he considers twice: once while his sympathy for others is intact, and once after it has been clouded over by a sorrow of his own. Michael Smith's fetishism objection charges that some pictures of moral motivation make the good agent's concern for the people she helps worryingly indirect, as though duty itself, rather than the person in front of her, were the real object of her concern. Nomy Arpaly's discussion of Huckleberry Finn — who helps the escaped slave Jim while sincerely believing that doing so is a sin for which he will be damned — is offered as a challenge to any view on which moral worth requires acting on a correct, consciously held judgment about what duty requires. And here is the submission I need to grade:

"Kant thinks that whether an act has moral worth is independent of whether it conforms to duty; it depends on the agent's motive. His shopkeeper keeps one fixed price for everyone, including customers who would never notice if they were overcharged, but he does this only because a reputation for fair dealing is good for business. Since his motive is self-interest rather than duty, Kant denies the act moral worth, even though it is the very same act a scrupulously honest shopkeeper would perform. Kant makes a parallel point with the 'friend of humanity' who enjoys helping people and does so gladly: because he acts from a pleasant inclination rather than from duty, his kindness, however 'right and amiable' (Kant's phrase), has no moral worth. But suppose sorrow has extinguished this person's sympathy, so that he no longer takes any pleasure in others' happiness, and he still drags himself out of his own indifference and helps because it is his duty — only then, Kant says, does the act have genuine moral worth. In both examples the point is the same: moral worth attaches to the will's motive rather than to the outward act, and the version of the story where inclination is absent is used to isolate duty as the operative motive.

Smith's fetishism worry targets exactly this idea, if it is read as requiring the good person's direct, occurrent motive in every case to be something like 'this is my duty' or 'this is the right thing to do.' A virtuous person's concern for the people she helps should be direct: she helps her friend because her friend needs help, not because helping is required by morality. If the thought 'this is right' is doing the actual motivational work, her connection to her friend looks accidental or mediated, the way a fetishist's desire is displaced from a person onto a substitute object. So the objection is aimed at views that make de dicto concern for rightness the proximate motive of every virtuous action, not at the weaker idea that a good person cares about morality at all.

Arpaly uses Huckleberry Finn to refute Kant's theory of moral worth. Huck helps Jim escape even though he consciously and sincerely believes that doing so is theft and a sin, and he even resolves to 'go to Hell' for it. Since Huck acts against his own explicit judgment of duty, and yet we want to say that he does the right thing and deserves credit for it, moral worth cannot require acting from a correct conscious judgment about what duty requires — which is exactly what Kant's picture of 'acting from duty' demands.

The Coincident Reasons Thesis tries to have it both ways: it keeps the idea that moral worth requires being moved by the reasons that actually justify the act, but denies that those reasons must include the thought that the act is right. Huck is moved by Jim's suffering and humanity, and Jim's suffering is among the considerations that in fact make helping him the right thing to do, so on Markovits's view Huck's act has moral worth for exactly the reason Arpaly says it does. Since the two views agree about the Huck Finn case and are both explicitly designed to avoid Smith's fetishism problem by refusing to require de dicto concern with rightness, Markovits's Coincident Reasons Thesis and Arpaly's account are really just two formulations of the same underlying view."
