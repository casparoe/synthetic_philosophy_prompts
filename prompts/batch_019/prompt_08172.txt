I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and for a piece on the current revival of interest in Wittgenstein's philosophy of religion, I've been sitting in on a small monthly reading group that has spent this year working through the "Lectures on Religious Belief" -- the students' notes from Wittgenstein's 1938 Cambridge lectures, which argue that believing in the Last Judgement is not the same kind of mental state as believing an ordinary empirical prediction. Two of the regulars, whom I'll call Elena and Daniel, got into a running argument across two sessions that struck me as more genuinely unresolved than most of what's been published on the topic, and I'd rather get an outside verdict than render one myself in the piece.

(1) Elena opened by defending Wittgenstein's claim that believing in the Last Judgement is not a matter of having more or less evidence, the way believing a weather forecast is; the word "believe," she said, changes its grammar entirely between the two cases, so asking what evidence the believer has is a category mistake, on the order of asking for evidence that a bishop in chess may only move diagonally. (2) Daniel objected that this only redescribes a thinned-out, philosopher's religion: most actual believers take the Last Judgement to be a straightforward future event, on the model of a historical occurrence, so Wittgenstein isn't analyzing their belief but quietly substituting one that can't be wrong -- which is just Kai Nielsen's old charge that the whole approach is "Wittgensteinian fideism" wearing the costume of description. (3) Elena replied that the unfalsifiability Daniel was objecting to is something Wittgenstein observed rather than invented: believers don't abandon the Last Judgement in light of counter-evidence the way they'd abandon a forecast, and she likened this to the "hinge" propositions of On Certainty -- claims held with total confidence, immune to revision, that aren't thereby irrational because they're what make ordinary evidence-weighing possible at all. (4) Daniel answered that the analogy backfires: On Certainty's hinges, like the proposition that the earth has existed for a long time, are things no one, believer or atheist, actually disputes, whereas the Last Judgement is exactly what's in dispute, so relabelling it "grammatical" rather than "factual" doesn't give Wittgenstein's promised neutrality -- it just makes the atheist's objection literally unstatable, which is a strange kind of accident for it to keep landing on the believer's side. (5) Elena's closing point, before the group broke up for the night, was that this can't be a hidden thumb on the scale, since Wittgenstein insisted he wasn't a religious man himself and thought unbelief has its own hinges too; the real question, she said, is whether "belief" names one state that believer and atheist actually share and disagree over, or whether -- as Wittgenstein suspected -- they are, in some sense, talking past one another.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of it at each step, including any places where both are partly right, they're talking past each other, or they're both missing something? I'd also like a final judgment on the question their argument left hanging: is Wittgenstein's treatment of religious belief a genuine discovery about the grammar of "belief," or does Nielsen's fideism charge stick after all? Please end your reply with that verdict on its own line, in the form Verdict: [your answer, in one or two sentences].
