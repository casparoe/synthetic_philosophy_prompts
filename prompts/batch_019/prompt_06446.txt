I first got interested in split-brain patients after watching an old documentary clip of people who'd had their corpus callosum severed to control severe epilepsy, in which each hand of the same patient seemed to want something completely different — and something about that image has stayed with me for months. Since then I've read whatever I could find on the subject, including some of Michael Gazzaniga's own popular writing (he was one of the original researchers on these cases), and I even ended up arguing about it over dinner with friends without either side budging. The question I can't settle for myself is the one these lab tests seem to force on you eventually: once the two hemispheres can no longer talk to each other directly, is the person on the table now two separate conscious subjects sharing one skull, or still just one person whose experience has merely become badly coordinated?

The popular version of the story is the dramatic one, and it's the one I keep running into: two minds, one head, each hemisphere with its own perceptions and preferences, often illustrated with anecdotes like a patient's hemispheres supposedly disagreeing when asked separately about religious belief (though I have no idea how solid that particular anecdote really is). But I gather that at least one well-known philosopher pushed back on this fairly early on, arguing that the evidence doesn't actually let us say definitively "one" or "two" — that our ordinary notion of a single subject of experience might simply have no clean application here. More recently, I came across a study, I believe by a researcher named Pinto, reporting that split-brain patients could still integrate information across the hemispheres well enough on certain tasks that the classic "two isolated minds" picture starts to look overstated — which apparently reopened the debate rather than settling it.

What I'd like is an essay that actually picks a side instead of just surveying the landscape. Please lay out the strongest case for there being two subjects of experience, the strongest case for there being one subject with merely disrupted access or coordination between the hemispheres, and the case that the question itself may have no determinate answer — and be explicit about what each position is assuming "unity of consciousness" requires in the first place, since that seems to be where the real disagreement is hiding. Then commit to a specific verdict about how many subjects of experience are present in a split-brain patient during one of these classic lateralized tests, defend it against the strongest objections to it, and say what your answer implies, if anything, about the unity of consciousness in an ordinary, non-split brain like mine.
