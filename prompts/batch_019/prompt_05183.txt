In my reporting on public controversies — a climate activist whose own carbon footprint is invoked to dismiss her criticism, a politician's family-values platform undone by personal scandal — the charge of hypocrisy keeps functioning as a conversation-ending move, as though it settles whether the underlying criticism was ever sound. Digging into the philosophical literature on the standing to blame, I've come across R. Jay Wallace's influential argument that hypocritical blame is disqualified in a distinctive way: because the hypocrite exempts herself from a standard she imposes on others, she implicitly denies the equal standing that moral address presupposes between blamer and blamed, and it is this denial of equal standing, rather than mere inconsistency, that costs her the standing to blame. I'd like you to argue against this account. Defend the position that hypocrisy is never, by itself, sufficient to undermine a person's standing to blame, and that whatever residual force Wallace's cases have is fully explained by independent factors, such as the blamer's own complicity in the wrongdoing at issue or an insincere commitment to the very norm being invoked. Please explain specifically where the equal-standing argument goes wrong in treating hypocrisy as a freestanding standing-defeater, rather than as a symptom of one of these more basic problems.
