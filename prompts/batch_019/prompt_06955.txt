I co-run the AI safety discussion group at my high school, and this term we've started recording short interviews to share with the rest of the club afterward. Our guest next week is a machine learning researcher who works on goal misgeneralization and is known within our club, at least, for arguing that this failure mode -- rather than classic reward hacking or specification gaming -- is the central inner alignment risk for future systems, and that faint versions of it are already visible in how today's large language models behave. Whenever someone in a Q&A objects that a given failure looks like "just distributional shift" or "just a capabilities bug," her standard response is that the competence of the behavior is exactly what should worry us, since a system that had simply failed to generalize would be incompetent, not competently aimed at the wrong target. I've spent the last few weeks working through the DeepMind and Anthropic write-ups on this for a separate club project, and while the inner/outer alignment distinction makes sense to me in the abstract, I keep losing track of exactly where goal misgeneralization stops and ordinary reward hacking begins, which is part of why I don't want this interview to stay at the level of definitions.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push past her standard line rather than let her repeat it? I'd like the set to include at least one question that makes her justify carrying the mesa-optimizer framework from the "Risks from Learned Optimization" line of work -- built around procedurally generated RL agents like the CoinRun case, where the trained agent kept heading for the end of the level even in test levels where the coin had been placed somewhere else entirely -- over to transformer models trained by pretraining and RLHF on broad, heterogeneous, real-world data rather than inside a small synthetic environment; one that presses her for a concrete criterion for telling a goal misgeneralization failure apart from ordinary specification gaming, given that the two can look identical in any single held-out test; one on why we should expect deceptive alignment specifically, rather than merely misgeneralized but non-deceptive goals, given that deception requires a degree of situational awareness no one has clearly demonstrated yet; and one asking how anyone could identify, before deployment, which "training-compatible" goal a model actually has, if several distinct goals would by definition have produced identical behavior on the training data. For each question, add a short follow-up that anticipates her likely answer and specifically blocks a return to the "competence is what's worrying" line, and close the set with a question about what concrete observation -- not a thought experiment -- would actually count as evidence against her view.
