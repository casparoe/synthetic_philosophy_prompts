I never studied philosophy, logic, or computer science formally, but over the past year I've spent a lot of my spare reading time working through old LessWrong posts and MIRI-adjacent papers on decision theory, and one idea keeps nagging at me more than the rest: "program equilibrium," where instead of two players simply choosing moves in a game like the Prisoner's Dilemma, each one submits a piece of source code that is allowed to read the other's source code before deciding what to output. What I find almost eerie about it is the trick — I keep seeing the name "FairBot" attached to it — of using something like Löb's theorem so that two suitably written programs can end up reliably cooperating with each other, and with exact copies of themselves, without either collapsing into naive unconditional cooperation (which any opponent could simply exploit) or getting stuck in an infinite regress of "I'll cooperate if you cooperate if I cooperate if you cooperate...". I mentioned all this to a friend who does competitive programming, and he shrugged it off as a cute trick with no real content; I disagreed, but I couldn't explain why it feels different from just building in trust by fiat, since I can't actually follow the provability-logic machinery myself.
