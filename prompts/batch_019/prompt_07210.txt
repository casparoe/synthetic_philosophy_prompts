A friend and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about the White Horse Dialogue, the Warring States-era text (3rd century BCE) in which a debater defends the claim that "a white horse is not a horse," and we never managed to settle the question the whole exchange turns on. My friend's position, echoing the ancient Confucian and Mohist critics of the School of Names, is that the whole thing is verbal sleight of hand dressed up as argument. I think there is a real, if compressed, piece of logical analysis buried in it, and that dismissing it as mere sophistry is too quick. We kept circling the same stretch of text without getting anywhere, so I am hoping you can do better than we did.

Here is the passage, in my own rendering of the classical Chinese (checked against a couple of published translations for accuracy):

Objector: Can one maintain that "a white horse is not a horse"?
Advocate: One can.
Objector: How so?
Advocate: "Horse" is the word by which we name a shape; "white" is the word by which we name a color. What names a color is not what names a shape. Hence I say: a white horse is not a horse.
Objector: If there is a white horse, you cannot say there is no horse. And if you cannot say there is no horse, isn't that just to say there is a horse? Since having a white horse counts as having a horse, how can its being white make it not a horse?
Advocate: If someone seeks a horse, a yellow or a black horse will satisfy him. If someone seeks a white horse, a yellow or a black horse will not satisfy him. Suppose "white horse" were the same as "horse" -- then what is sought in the two cases would be one and the same thing. But if what is sought is the same, why is it that a yellow or black horse will do in the one case and not in the other? Acceptable and unacceptable plainly exclude one another. So a yellow horse and a black horse are alike in this: each can answer to "there is a horse," but neither can answer to "there is a white horse." Hence it is settled: a white horse is not a horse.

What I would like help with is the Advocate's second argument, the one about seeking a horse versus seeking a white horse. Please reconstruct it as explicitly as you can, premise by premise, and tell us whether it validly establishes some real, if modest, claim -- say, that "horse" and "white horse" are non-identical as terms or concepts -- or whether it only looks valid because it quietly trades on two different senses of "is not," sliding between "is not the same as" and "is not a member of," so that the triumphant final line claims more than the premises actually deliver. If there is a slide, tell us exactly where it happens.
