I teach a graduate seminar on classical American pragmatism, and every autumn I assign the opening chapter of Dewey's 1927 *The Public and Its Problems* — partly because it is his fullest attempt at a naturalistic political epistemology, and partly because it reads as a direct, if oblique, answer to Lippmann's argument in *The Phantom Public* (1925) that the "omnicompetent" democratic citizen is a fiction, since ordinary people cannot possibly have the knowledge needed to govern consequences they never directly experience. This term a sharp student pressed me on the chapter's key definitional sentence hard enough that I no longer trust my own gloss, and I'd like a second opinion before I face her again. Here is the passage, with the definition arriving at the end:

"We distinguish private and public buildings, private and public schools, private paths and public highways, private assets and public funds, private persons and public officials. It is our thesis that in this distinction we find the key to the nature and office of the state. It is not without significance that etymologically 'private' is defined in opposition to 'official,' a private person being one deprived of public position. The public consists of all those who are affected by the indirect consequences of transactions to such an extent that it is deemed necessary to have those consequences systematically cared for. Officials are those who look out for and take care of the interests thus affected. Since those who are indirectly affected are not direct participants in the transactions in question, it is necessary that certain persons be set apart to represent them, and see to it that their interests are conserved and protected."

My student's objection concerned the clause "deemed necessary": Dewey never says by whom. A few pages later he restates the same sequence as a "hypothesis" — indirect consequences are perceived, perception "generates a common interest," and only once the affected parties "form a group distinctive enough to require recognition and a name" does organization through "representatives" enter the picture at all. But if that is the actual order of operations, then whatever is doing the "deeming" in the definitional passage has to be operating before officials, agencies of inquiry, or anything resembling expertise exists to sort serious indirect consequences from trivial ones — which looks uncomfortably like requiring the public's own organized capacities as a precondition for the very act of perception that is supposed to bring the public into existence in the first place. I don't want an answer that simply gestures at Dewey being "holistic" about knowing and organizing; I would like you to actually work through whether this is a vicious circle, or whether his more general experimentalist account of inquiry — where a warranted resolution and the determinate situation it resolves are supposed to emerge together rather than the first waiting on the second — gives him a principled way of describing something that looks circular without its being viciously so. If you think the latter, please spell out concretely how that resolution would apply to this particular passage, rather than just asserting that it would.
