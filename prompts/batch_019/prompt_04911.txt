On rationalist blogs and forums, Aumann's agreement theorem gets invoked with a strong moral attached: two truth-seeking people who each trust the other's rationality shouldn't be able to sustain an honest disagreement once they've traded views. Having followed various rounds of this argument across Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, and their descendants over the years, I'd like an essay that presses on this moral rather than restating the folklore version of it.

It's worth being precise about the underlying results rather than just the slogan. Aumann's 1976 theorem shows that agents sharing a common prior cannot have common knowledge of differing posterior probabilities for the same event. Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis later sharpened this into a dynamic claim: if two such agents simply announce and re-announce their current posteriors to each other, without ever revealing the private information behind them, the exchange is guaranteed to converge to agreement within finitely many rounds. The related Milgrom-Stokey "no-trade theorem" adds that agents starting from a Pareto-optimal position can never agree to a speculative bet grounded purely in private information, since the very offer to bet is itself informative, and a rational counterpart updates on it.

None of this squares easily with how much even careful, well-informed people -- including on the blogs that popularize the theorem -- go on disagreeing after long, apparently good-faith exchanges. Write an essay that weighs two responses to this tension and then defends one of them. The first, in the spirit of Hanson and Cowen's work on "self-favoring priors," treats persistent disagreement itself as evidence that at least one side is not being fully honest, or is unknowingly privileging its own judgment, whether or not anyone can say which side it is. The second treats the theorem as telling us little about real disagreement, on the grounds that its idealizations -- a genuinely shared prior, unbounded computation, and true common knowledge of each other's rationality -- essentially never hold between actual people. Don't end on a split-the-difference note: defend a specific, general answer to when, if ever, extended disagreement with someone you consider an epistemic peer should itself move your credence, and when it is fully compatible with both of you reasoning correctly.
