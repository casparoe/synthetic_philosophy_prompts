Now retired after a career as an experimental physicist, I have been using my new-found leisure to fill gaps in my education by reading philosophy of physics, and it is in this way that I came across the modern "Boltzmann brain" problem in cosmology. It strikes me that Boltzmann himself, in his reply to Zermelo's recurrence objection, already proposed something structurally similar: that our observed universe might be an enormous, rare statistical fluctuation out of an overall equilibrium, with the "arrow of time" we experience being nothing more than the local direction of entropy increase within that fluctuation. Boltzmann died in 1906, decades before de Sitter space or eternally inflating cosmologies existed, yet today's cosmologists redeploy essentially his own fluctuation idea within those settings, arriving at a far more corrosive conclusion: that disembodied, momentarily existing "Boltzmann brains" with false memories should vastly outnumber observers like us, undermining any observer's grounds for trusting their own past and their own reasoning. Suppose Boltzmann could be briefed on this later, cosmological descendant of his own argument. Do you think he would have accepted it as a natural continuation of his statistical mechanics, or resisted the skeptical turn it takes -- and which of his own methodological commitments do you think would end up doing the most work in his reaction?
