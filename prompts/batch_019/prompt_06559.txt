In my formal epistemology seminar, we've been discussing imprecise probability as an alternative to orthodox Bayesianism: instead of assigning a single sharp credence to a proposition, a rational agent's doxastic state is represented by a whole set of probability functions whenever the evidence fails to settle on just one. This is usually motivated by cases like the Ellsberg paradox, where any particular sharp credence about the ambiguous urn seems unwarranted by the evidence. But I'm not convinced the framework earns its keep: whatever the "correct" function within that set is supposed to be, I can simply place a precise second-order probability distribution over the candidate functions, reflecting my confidence in each, and then average them out via the law of total probability to recover a single sharp number. That suggests imprecise credences are just precise credences that haven't been fully cashed out yet, so a sufficiently reflective Bayesian should always be able to sharpen up rather than stay genuinely imprecise. Given this, why do formal epistemologists still treat imprecise probability as philosophically distinct from ordinary higher-order Bayesian uncertainty, rather than as a dispensable stopgap on the way to a sharp credence?
