I co-host a pretty small podcast on decision theory and formal epistemology with a friend from my program (we're both econ PhD students; subscriber count is embarrassing, mostly just people from our cohort who feel obligated). Next episode's guest is a decision theorist with a new book defending I.J. Good's 1967 theorem -- the result that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can never do worse in expectation by looking at free evidence before acting than by acting blind -- as basically the best argument anyone's given for an unrestricted principle of total evidence. Her go-to move whenever someone brings up a case where information seems to make people worse off is "thats not a counterexample to the theorem, thats a hidden cost," and I really don't want the whole hour to just be her deploying that line while we nod along.

Could you draft me 8 to 10 interview questions that actually make her work for it? I want at least one where she has to walk through the Jensen's-inequality/convexity step in the proof in plain terms and say exactly what would have to be true of a decision problem for "look before you leap" to stop dominating "leap before you look." I want one on the gap between the theorem being a claim about the value of information in expectation, before you see it, and the fact that one specific realized bit of news can leave you strictly worse off than if you'd never looked at all (my advisor's go-to example here is a false-positive biopsy) -- is that just an unfortunate fact about regret that the theorem was never meant to speak to, or does it actually limit what the result is good for? I want one that leans on the information-avoidance literature (people skipping free genetic tests, investors not checking their portfolios during a downturn) to push on the "hidden cost" move until it either does real explanatory work or turns into an unfalsifiable way of relabeling every counterexample after the fact. And I want one bringing up Hirshleifer's old point that information which is privately good in exactly Good's sense can still be socially bad, because it can destroy the basis for risk-sharing before the underlying uncertainty resolves -- ask her whether that means her result, true as it is, doesn't actually carry the "we should always let people find things out" policy upshot she wants to hang on it. For each of those, add a quick follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give, the way an actual back-and-forth in the room would go rather than just moving on to the next topic -- and throw in one or two more questions of your own if you can see an obvious angle on the theorem I'm missing. Please, no warm-up questions like "what first got you interested in decision theory."
