I'm a software engineer who got into Bayesian updating through probabilistic programming, and that curiosity eventually led me to Richard Jeffrey's "probability kinematics." As I understand it, when evidence shifts your credence across a partition {E_1, ..., E_n} without making you certain of any single E_i, Jeffrey's rule says your new unconditional probability for any proposition A should be P'(A) = Σ_i P(A|E_i)·P'(E_i), where the conditional probabilities P(A|E_i) are held fixed through the update (the "rigidity" condition). Unlike ordinary conditionalization on evidence learned with certainty, this rule is not commutative: shifting your credence first over one partition and then over a second can leave you with different final beliefs than updating in the reverse order, even when the two partitions seem to concern independent matters. Defenders reply that this isn't really paradoxical, since each rigid conditional probability is implicitly indexed to your total evidential state at the moment of the shift, so "the same" partition encountered later isn't really the same update at all. Please argue that this reply doesn't fully succeed, and that the order-dependence of Jeffrey conditionalization is a genuine cost to its standing as a general norm of rational updating, rather than a harmless technicality.
