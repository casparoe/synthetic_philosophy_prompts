I've been lurking on LessWrong and reading ACX for like a decade now, and my in-person rationalist-adjacent book club just finished reading L.A. Paul's Transformative Experience, which lead to a two hour argument that kind of derailed the whole meeting (in a good way, we're picking it back up next week). For anyone who hasn't read it: Paul's basic point is that choices like becoming a parent, or her go-to weird example of becoming a vampire, are "epistemically transformative" -- you can't know what the experience is actually like, what she calls its revelatory content, until you're already on the other side of it -- and also "personally transformative," meaning it'll change what you even value once you're there. So ordinary expected-utility reasoning, where you weigh outcomes by how much you'd want them times how likely they are, doesn't even get off the ground, because the "you" doing the weighing doesn't have access to the values the post-transformation you would be using to grade the outcome. Somebody in the group pointed out this is basically the same shape as the old MIRI "ontological crisis" writeups about AI agents whose utility function doesn't obviously carry over once their world-model changes out from under them, which I thought was a pretty neat connection.

Anyway, what I'd actually love from you is a short story that sits inside that decision problem instead of just gesturing at it the way most pop-sci summaries of the book do. Setting's up to you -- doesn't have to be the vampire thing, that's a bit played out at this point -- but I'm picturing something like a person a day or two out from a one-way, irreversible procedure that would radically expand what they're capable of experiencing and caring about, where the catch is that literally nobody who's gone through it has ever managed to explain to the "before" people what it's like on the other side, their testimony just doesn't transmit. I don't want a tidy ending where the character just "takes the leap of faith" and its framed as obviously the right call -- Paul herself doesn't think there's a clean fix for the decision problem, so its fine, even good, if the character's actual reasoning at the end doesn't really follow from the expected-value calculation they were trying to run at the start. A few hundred to a thousand words is plenty, doesn't need to be a whole saga.
