Over the past year I've covered half a dozen "rapid attribution" stories: a heatwave in one region, a flood somewhere else, and within days a research consortium such as World Weather Attribution puts out a report concluding that climate change made the event, say, three times more likely, or twenty percent more intense. Reading through their methods pages, I noticed they frame the whole exercise as a comparison between the actual world and a "counterfactual world" — in practice, a climate-model run with the forcing dialed back to pre-industrial greenhouse gas levels — which struck me as exactly the closest-possible-world idiom that philosophers such as David Lewis built into counterfactual theories of causation.

When I mentioned this to a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD, she pushed back: that isn't really how most causal modelers would justify the method, she said. They would more naturally reach for the interventionist, or manipulationist, picture associated with thinkers like James Woodward and Judea Pearl, on which a cause is whatever you could, in principle, change through a "surgical" intervention while leaving the rest of the causal structure untouched — with the model's forcing variable simply being the dial you turn in a controlled simulation, rather than a whole alternate possible world you have to imagine into being.

I'd like to know whether that distinction is more than a scholastic quibble before I write my next piece. Could you lay out the most important implications of the difference between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation for how I think and write about this science? I'm especially interested in two things: first, how much epistemic weight I, and my readers, should put on a headline figure like "four times more likely"; and second, how carefully I should phrase causal-responsibility claims when this same body of science gets invoked in loss-and-damage negotiations or lawsuits against fossil-fuel companies, where what's really being sought is closer to a singular, "but-for" verdict about one specific disaster than a shift in probability. I'm not after a side-by-side summary of Lewis versus Woodward for its own sake; I want to know what actually changes, in terms of what attribution scientists are entitled to claim and how a journalist should responsibly report it, depending on which picture of causation is doing the justificatory work underneath the number.
