I'm putting together a session for my formal epistemology seminar on the disagreement literature, and I keep tripping over how to present Aumann's agreement theorem to students without either oversimplifying it or making it sound more relevant to real disagreement than it actually is. The math itself is clean enough -- two Bayesians who share a prior can't have common knowledge (not just mutual awareness, actual common knowledge) of differing posteriors on some event -- but the second you try to use that to adjudicate the conciliationist/steadfast debate about peer disagreement, everything starts to hinge on idealizations that nobody thinks real arguers satisfy. My officemate thinks this makes the theorem basically a red herring for the philosophical debate; I think that's too quick, partly because of the Geanakoplos-Polemarchakis result showing you don't actually need common knowledge of the posteriors from the outset -- you can get there just by honestly trading numbers back and forth, over and over, as long as the priors were shared to begin with. We've had this argument twice now over coffee and neither of us budged an inch, so instead of a third round, could you just write the dialogue for us?

I want it to be a real, sharp back-and-forth between two equally competent stand-ins for our positions (name them however you like), not a strawman on either side. The pro-Aumann character should have to do more then just cite the theorem -- push them on whether "our priors just differ, full stop" is even something a rational agent can coherently say, or whether it secretly needs a story about where the difference came from. And the skeptic shouldn't get away with just saying "people aren't ideal Bayesians" -- make them say specifically what, in ordinary disagreement, blocks even the weaker iterated-announcement route to convergence that Geanakoplos-Polemarchakis gives you. I'd rather the dialogue end without a clean winner than have either side fold too easily.
