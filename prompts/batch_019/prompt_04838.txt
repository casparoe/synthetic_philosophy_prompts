At my school's philosophy club, we spent a meeting discussing something that had happened to one of our members: he'd promised his debate partner he would spend the weekend before their biggest tournament finishing their case with her, but that same weekend his younger brother's therapist could only fit in a long-postponed, urgently needed appointment if he came along. He went with his brother, missed the prep session, and the team defaulted. Two other members, Maya and Owen, ended up arguing about whether this counted as a genuine moral dilemma.

(1) Maya said there was no real dilemma: once it's clear his brother mattered more, the promise to his partner stopped generating any actual obligation, so he did nothing wrong.

(2) Owen replied that this can't be the whole story, since he still seems to owe his partner real compensation, not just an explanation, and we don't normally think compensation is owed for choices that were entirely blameless.

(3) Maya answered that owing her something might just be ordinary kindness, not proof of wrongdoing, and that treating leftover guilt as evidence of a genuinely broken obligation confuses a psychological residue with a moral one.

(4) Owen said that if the promise had truly lost all its force once it was outweighed, there would be nothing left to compensate for, and the fact that everyone expects him to do something specific for her, not just show general kindness, suggests the obligation never fully vanished.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who has the stronger argument at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether there's an important consideration about moral dilemmas that neither Maya nor Owen brought up.
