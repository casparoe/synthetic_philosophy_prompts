After a workplace injury left me with a permanent partial disability, my lawyer has laid out two options: accept a structured settlement now for a guaranteed, if modest, sum, or take the case to trial, with an estimated 60% chance of a substantially larger award and a 40% chance of losing outright and recovering nothing. In trying to think this through carefully, I moved from standard expected utility theory to Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory, which she presents as a way of vindicating degrees of risk-aversion that the orthodox theory cannot accommodate even once a concave utility function is allowed for. Could you lay out the most important implications of REU theory, as against the orthodox theory, for how someone in my position should think about the trial-versus-settlement decision — in particular, what extra work the "risk function" is doing that diminishing marginal utility of money does not already do, and whether taking REU seriously should actually change my decision, or would just supply a fancier redescription of whatever choice I was already inclined to make?
