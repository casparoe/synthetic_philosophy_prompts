I'm in the final stretch of writing my dissertation, and last week my advisor and I ended up in a long tangent about the acknowledgments section. I had written the fairly standard line thanking my committee and noting that "any errors that remain are, of course, my own responsibility." She pointed out, half as a joke, that this sentence is the textbook illustration of what philosophers call the preface paradox: I am justified in believing each individual claim across my three hundred pages, yet I am also justified in believing, on ordinary inductive grounds, that a document this long almost certainly contains at least one mistake somewhere.

Curious, I went looking into this further and found a forum thread linking it to the lottery paradox, where one is justified in believing, of any given ticket, that it will lose, while also being justified in believing that some ticket will win. One commenter argued that both puzzles are really proofs that any sufficiently large body of belief is provably inconsistent, and drew an explicit comparison to Gödel's incompleteness theorems, on the grounds that both results supposedly show that a sufficiently rich system cannot be both comprehensive and internally consistent.

That comparison hasn't stopped bothering me. If the preface and lottery paradoxes really do establish, with something like Gödel's rigor, that any document containing hundreds of separate claims is logically guaranteed to harbor a contradiction, it starts to feel pointless to spend my remaining weeks hunting for tensions between chapters, checking that my conclusion doesn't quietly undercut an argument from chapter two, or asking committee members to flag internal inconsistencies. Shouldn't I just treat inconsistency at this scale as a logical inevitability and spend that time elsewhere, rather than chasing a coherence that these paradoxes seem to have already ruled out?
