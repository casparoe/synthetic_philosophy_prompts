I recently launched a pseudonymous newsletter analyzing the mechanics of online pile-ons, and a subscriber has already accused me of hypocrisy: I condemn anonymous cruelty while declining to attach my own name to what I write. Turning to Kierkegaard's *Two Ages* for support, I found that the diagnosis I wanted to borrow—that 'leveling' is the work of an anonymous, press-manufactured 'public'—appears in a book Kierkegaard signed with his own name, yet was written by someone who had spent the preceding several years publishing his major works through invented personas such as Johannes Climacus, Vigilius Haufniensis, and Johannes de Silentio. Please argue that there is a principled distinction between this kind of pseudonymous authorship and the anonymity Kierkegaard attributes to the leveling public, such that he is not guilty of the vice he diagnoses. I'd like the argument to engage seriously with his account of indirect communication, rather than resting on the too-easy move of noting that he eventually claimed authorship of the pseudonyms in the "First and Last Explanation"—an admission that came years later and doesn't by itself show that the original act of publishing pseudonymously differed in kind from journalistic anonymity.
