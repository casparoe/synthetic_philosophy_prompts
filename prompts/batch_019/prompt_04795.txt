Last weekend I had a long, increasingly technical argument with my housemate, who is finishing a minor in formal logic, about whether genuine moral dilemmas are even possible — the sort of situation in which an agent is morally required to do two different things (keep a promise and rescue a stranger, say, or, more dramatically, Sartre's student torn between joining the resistance and staying to care for his mother, or Agamemnon at Aulis) but simply cannot do both, so that whichever option is chosen, something genuinely wrong has been done. I had always assumed this was a live and unsettled question in ethics — I know Bernard Williams and Ruth Barcan Marcus, among others, spent real effort defending the idea that such conflicts are genuine rather than merely apparent — but my housemate insists the whole issue dissolves once you take "ought implies can" seriously.

Her argument runs roughly as follows. Nobody can be under a genuine obligation to do something they are unable to do — that, she says, is just what "ought implies can" means. In a dilemma, however, the agent cannot do both of the two supposedly obligatory things at once; doing one forecloses the other. So it cannot be true that the agent is really obligated to do A and really obligated to do B, since holding both would amount to saying the agent is obligated to perform the two together, which is exactly the impossible combination that "ought implies can" rules out. From this she concludes that every apparent dilemma must really be a conflict between one genuine obligation and one that only looks obligatory until it is examined more closely, even if in practice it can be hard to tell which is which.

I could not locate the flaw in the moment, since I accept the starting premise too, but it also strikes me as suspicious that a question taken seriously by so many serious ethicists for decades would turn out to be resolvable by a single uncontroversial principle. Could you work through her argument carefully and tell me whether it is actually valid as stated? If it is not, I would be grateful if you could pin down exactly which step is smuggling in more than "ought implies can" alone can deliver, and how someone who wants to defend the reality of moral dilemmas would resist it.
