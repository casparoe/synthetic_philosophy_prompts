A friend of mine, Priya, told me last week that she's seriously saving up for cosmetic limb-lengthening surgery. She's twenty-six, five-foot-one, and has no underlying skeletal condition — she just doesn't like being short. From what she's described, the procedure involves cutting through both femurs and inserting internal telescoping rods that are then extended a fraction of a millimeter at a time, day after day, for a couple of months, followed by several more months of physical therapy and bone consolidation before she can put full weight on her legs again. It's essentially the same hardware and technique originally developed for children with genuine leg-length discrepancies or skeletal dysplasias, just being used electively on someone who's short, not sick.

When I mentioned this over dinner with another friend, Marco, he was pretty appalled. He argued that this is different in kind from ordinary cosmetic surgery: you're not reshaping cartilage or injecting filler, you're deliberately breaking healthy bones and putting someone out of commission for the better part of a year, purely to gain a couple of inches, and any surgeon willing to do that electively is exploiting insecurity rather than practicing medicine. I found myself pushing back, mainly because the disadvantages attached to being a short man are so well documented — in dating, in hiring, in average earnings — that if we already think it's legitimate to give a genuinely short child growth hormone partly to spare them exactly those disadvantages, I don't see why correcting the same disadvantage surgically, later in life, counts as doing something categorically different, even if the method is more drastic. Marco thought this argument proved far too much, since almost any intervention could be justified this way as long as you can point to some social disadvantage it offsets. We went around in circles for the better part of an hour at the restaurant and never really settled it.

I keep coming back to it. Is there a principled way to draw the line Marco wants, between correcting Priya's height and correcting a diagnosed growth disorder, or does my argument show that the line most people assume is there is a lot less stable than it looks once you actually lean on it? I'd be curious how you would untangle the disagreement.
