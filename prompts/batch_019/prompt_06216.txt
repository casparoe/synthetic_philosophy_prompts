So I'm about a third of the way into a novel where my main character, Nora, is basically a present-day update of Dorothea Brooke — idealistic, a little naive, desperate to do some "unhistoric" good in the world instead of just marrying well or finishing a thesis nobody reads. In the book she falls in with an effective altruist crowd in grad school (80,000 Hours quizzes, arguing about bed nets vs. AI safety over cheap wine, the whole scene), and I want a chapter where shes basically arguing with herself over whether this is what Eliot was actually reaching for with all that "growing good of the world" stuff at the end of Middlemarch, or whether its a kind of betrayal of it.

Which got me curious about the real Eliot rather than just my fictional stand-in for her sensibility. She translated Strauss and Feuerbach, drifted into a humanism that didn't need God to justify duty, and stayed close to English Positivists like Frederic Harrison — and Comte, who she and Lewes admired, is literally where we get the word "altruism" from. But her novels seem almost suspicious of doing good in the abstract: the whole moral arc of Middlemarch is Dorothea learning to aim her energy at the particular, flawed people in front of her instead of some grand impersonal scheme. So here's my actual question: if you sat Eliot down across from a 25-year-old effective altruist calmly explaining that she should skip a caring but "inefficient" nursing career for AI policy work because the expected value is higher, would Eliot recognize that as her own ethic grown up, or would she think something essential had gotten lost along the way? I keep flip-flopping on how Nora should land on it and would love an actual considered take, not a both-sides shrug.
