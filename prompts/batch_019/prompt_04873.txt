So I've been going down a rabbit hole for a newsletter piece on decision theory and predictors (the Newcomb's problem stuff), and a philosopher I interviewed for it kept bringing up this old puzzle called "Death in Damascus" that I can't stop thinking about now. It's basically a decision-theory remix of the old "Appointment in Samarra" fable: a guy runs into Death in Damascus, gets spooked, and has to decide whether to stay put or flee to Aleppo -- except Death is basically a perfect predictor who's already headed to wherever the guy will end up, so whichever way he decides, that decision itself is what gives away his location. The philosopher said this is the case people use to show that causal decision theory doesn't even have a stable "ratifiable" choice here: the moment you settle on fleeing, that very act becomes evidence Death predicted "flee," which makes staying look better, which makes fleeing look better again, and so on forever. Kind of like matching pennies against a mind reader, except its your life on the line and there's no way to just refuse to play.
