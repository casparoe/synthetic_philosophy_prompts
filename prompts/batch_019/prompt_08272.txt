I'm on the board of my apartment co-op and we're stuck on something. When the building converted from rental decades ago, the original shareholders wrote themselves a permanent perk into the bylaws: lower monthly maintenance than everyone else, forever, just for having signed first. No stated reason, it was basically a sweetener to get the conversion done. Only two of those original people are even left, but a bunch of our other bylaws (how quorum works, how fees split between unit types) are the exact same deal -- nobody remembers why, they're just what got written down once and never touched again.

The board's split, and it's not really about whether the founders' discount is fair anymore, nobody thinks it is. One faction wants to quietly repeal just that clause through a boring technical amendment and say as little as possible about why, because if we admit out loud that it never had a real justification, people are gonna start asking that about everything else, and honestly we don't have principled answers for most of it. The other faction wants a memo that says plainly: this was arbitrary, here's why we're ending it -- and that a board that manages people by hiding how thin its own rules are doesn't deserve their trust, whether or not the hiding "works."

Somebody on the board quoted this bit from Pascal's Pensees at us: that custom is basically the whole of what we call justice, purely because it's accepted, and that tracing any custom back to where it actually came from destroys its authority -- so it's dangerous to let people see that a law isn't really grounded in anything but itself. Now both factions think Pascal's on their side: one hears "so never expose the root," the other hears "and that's exactly the manipulative move a body like ours shouldn't be pulling on people who are supposed to consent to this."

Can you draft the actual memo I'd bring to the board? I need a real recommendation on the founders' clause and on how much we disclose and to who, not a "both sides have a point" shrug -- and don't just wave away the quiet-fix camp, since I think there's something real in the worry that you can't put every bylaw's justification on trial at once without the whole governance structure falling apart.
