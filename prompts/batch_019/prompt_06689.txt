I am taking a seminar on metaethics this semester, but the question below comes from something that happened to me over the summer. While applying for a research internship, I gathered from a few pointed remarks that the professor running the lab only takes on students who express genuine enthusiasm for her late mentor's landmark early paper—a paper I had actually found overrated when I read it for a class. Wanting the position, I told her how much that paper had shaped my thinking, and I got in. She has since begun citing my reaction, together with a couple of similar ones from other new students, as evidence—in talks and in a grant renewal—that the paper "still resonates with a new generation of researchers."

Something about this arrangement strikes me as off, but I am struggling to say exactly what. I did have a genuine reason to say what I said: I wanted the internship, and wanting an internship seems like a perfectly ordinary reason to act on. My roommate thinks I am overanalyzing a harmless bit of flattery, since a reason is a reason. Is she right, or is there a more precise problem with how my professor is now using my reaction?
