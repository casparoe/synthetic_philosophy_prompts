In my school's AI ethics club, we've been arguing about Anthropic's "Golden Gate Claude" demonstration, in which researchers used a sparse autoencoder to isolate a single direction in a language model's activations that reliably corresponded to the Golden Gate Bridge, and showed that artificially amplifying that direction made the model steer almost any conversation back to the bridge. Half the club thinks this shows the network genuinely has internal representations; the other half thinks "representation" is just a flattering story we tell about glorified statistics.

Could you write a dialogue between an interpretability realist and an interpretability skeptic, staged as though they are two people looking at the feature dashboard from that demo together? Both should be equally capable debaters. The realist should argue that a feature this isolable, causally load-bearing under intervention, and stable across contexts meets something close to what philosopher William Ramsey called the "job description challenge" for genuine representation, rather than being just a pattern we impose from outside. The skeptic should resist this by invoking the superposition/polysemanticity problem — the same activations admit multiple, equally well-fitting decompositions — and by raising the possibility that a feature which looks clean on the examples we happened to test is an "interpretability illusion" that would not survive off-distribution. Have each side directly rebut the other's strongest point at least once rather than simply restating their own view, and let the dialogue end with the disagreement sharpened rather than resolved.
