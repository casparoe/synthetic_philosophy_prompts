I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and for a piece I'm currently reporting I've been shadowing two professional forecasters whose disagreement has started to bother me more than it probably should. Both have built long public track records on the same forecasting platform, and independent scoring shows their calibration is almost indistinguishable: when either one says an event is "70 percent likely," it comes true close to seven times in ten, and has for years. Both tell me they work from the same polling averages, the same economic releases, and the same historical base rates, and each volunteered, unprompted, that the other is among the most careful and honest people in the field.

And yet, on the very question I'm reporting on, one of them has landed on a 65 percent probability and the other on 35, and the gap hasn't closed by more than a couple of points despite months of the two of them publicly dissecting each other's reasoning, including in a long recorded debate in which each clearly grasped exactly where the other stood. When I finally asked both of them, separately, why they hadn't converged, I got versions of the same answer: "we're just weighing the same facts differently." That struck me as too easy an out, though I couldn't quite say why.

So here's what I can't shake: if two people are genuinely equally skilled and honest, are drawing on essentially the same public evidence, and each knows precisely what the other has concluded, can a gap like that really persist indefinitely without one of them having made some kind of mistake? Or is "weighing the same facts differently" a legitimate place for two fully rational people to land — and if it is, what does that tell us about how much work the phrase "the same evidence" is actually doing?
