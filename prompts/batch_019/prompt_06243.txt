Most of my working life was spent in observational cosmology, where one's location among all possible observers is sometimes treated as itself a form of evidence -- as in J. Richard Gott's Copernican arguments about the future lifespan of the human species. So it came as a surprise, once I started reading formal epistemology in retirement, to discover that an ostensibly simple puzzle about self-location, the Sleeping Beauty problem, remains unresolved among philosophers who reason about probability far more carefully than my own field ever demanded.

As a reminder of the setup: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, and a fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday, and the experiment then ends. If tails, she is woken on Monday and, following administration of a memory-erasing drug, again on Tuesday. On any waking she cannot tell which day it is or how many times she has already been woken, and she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads.

Please write an essay adjudicating between the two leading answers. Lay out Adam Elga's argument for a credence of 1/3, which turns on a restricted principle of indifference between the subjectively indistinguishable Monday and Tuesday awakenings under tails, and David Lewis's argument for retaining a credence of 1/2, which turns on the claim that waking up gives Beauty no new evidence capable of moving her from her prior. Note that Lewis's own assignment of probabilities commits him to a specific, and to some readers rather awkward, value for Beauty's credence in heads should she additionally be told upon waking that it is Monday; derive this value and say whether it counts as a cost of his position. Weigh the considerations and close with a definite verdict -- a qualified answer such as "thirding is correct only if self-locating propositions are the sort of thing ordinary conditionalization can act on" is acceptable, but studied neutrality is not.
