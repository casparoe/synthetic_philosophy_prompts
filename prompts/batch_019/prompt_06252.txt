As a journalist who writes fairly often about the history of ethics, I have been re-reading G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* for a longer piece on early twentieth-century intuitionism, and I have gotten stuck on a tension in Chapter VI, "The Ideal," that a philosopher friend and I could not fully resolve over dinner last week. Moore uses what he calls considering something in "absolute isolation" to argue that a beautiful world containing no observers whatsoever is better than an equally unwitnessed, hideous one, which he takes to show that beauty carries genuine intrinsic value quite apart from anyone's awareness or enjoyment of it. Only a few pages later, though, his account of "the ideal" — aesthetic appreciation and personal affection, what we would loosely call love and friendship, as the two greatest goods we know — leans heavily on his principle of organic unities, on which it is specifically the combination of true cognition and fitting emotion in someone's mind that generates almost all of the value, far more than the object's own qualities or the feeling alone would contribute.

What I would like help untangling is how much weight the isolation test can really bear once the organic-unity story is in place. If a wholly unwitnessed beautiful world already counts for a good deal by Moore's own lights, that seems to leave comparatively little extra value for conscious appreciation to add, which sits awkwardly with how central he makes that appreciation to "the ideal." My friend also pushed back when I tried running the same isolation test on personal affection: imagine a world containing an admirable but utterly unloved and unknown person, versus an equally unknown but despicable one. It is not obvious to me whether Moore's framework licenses the same verdict here that it does for beauty, or whether something about affection resists being pulled apart from the mind that feels it in a way beauty does not. I would like an explanation of this puzzle that actually engages with the structure of Moore's argument in that chapter, rather than the naturalistic-fallacy material he is usually remembered for.
