I've been reporting on the "right not to know" debate in genetic counseling for a few weeks now, and it followed me home: at dinner last week, my friend Naomi (does Bayesian stats for a living) and her housemate Marcus (a genetic counselor) got into a real argument over a client of his whos declining a free test for Huntington's.

Naomi's opening move: there's an actual theorem (I.J. Good's, from the 60s) proving the expected value of free information is never negative for a rational Bayesian, since you can always just ignore what you learn and act exactly as you would've anyway — so turning down a free, accurate test is flatly irrational.

Marcus: "free" is doing a ton of work there. Where his client lives, a positive result can end up disclosed to certain insurers down the line, so the test isn't cost-free once you count what happens after you know.

Naomi: fair, but that's a fact about this insurance regime, not a flaw in the theorem. Stipulate a version where the result is completely private. The theorem still says get tested.

Marcus: fine, take that version. His client isn't choosing between "act on the info" and "act without it," like the theorem assumes — living the next twenty years under a diagnosis versus under uncertainty are just different experiences, not the same life plus or minus one decision. The theorem treats knowledge as an input to action; it has nothing to say about cases where the knowing itself is most of what's at stake.

Naomi: then fold that into her utility function, dread's an outcome like any other. But then I don't think you get to also claim "ignoring the result costs nothing" — once a result exists in a file somewhere and she's choosing not to look, that's already a different state than never having tested at all. You can't run the theorem's proof and make that move at the same time.

They went back and forth for a good half hour and I honestly ended up loosing track of who was ahead. Could you go through it move by move and say who's got the better argument at each step, not just who wins overall — and flag anything you think both of them are missing? I'm especially curious whether "you can always just ignore what you learn" is doing more work than either of them noticed.
