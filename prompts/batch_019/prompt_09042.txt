Question (as Marcus wrote it on our shared doc):
"Judy Benjamin is dropped into a region divided into four equally likely areas: Headquarters Company Area and Second Company Area (together, 'Red' territory), and Third Company Area and Fourth Company Area (together, 'Blue' territory). Her prior is 1/4 on each area. She receives the message: 'If you're in Red territory, you're three times as likely to be in the Second Company Area as in the Headquarters Company Area' -- treat this as the constraint P(Second | Red) = 3/4. Find the updated distribution over all four areas by minimizing the KL-divergence of the new distribution from the prior subject to this constraint, report the four resulting probabilities, and say whether P(Red) is still 1/2. Is that result philosophically troubling?"

Daniel's answer:
"Let h, s, t, f be the updated probabilities of Headquarters, Second, Third and Fourth, each with prior 1/4. We minimize Σ p_i ln(p_i/p0_i) subject to Σp_i = 1 and s = 3h. Setting up the Lagrangian L = Σ p_i ln(p_i/p0_i) + λ(Σp_i - 1) + μ(s - 3h) and differentiating gives h = p0_h·exp(3μ-1-λ), s = p0_s·exp(-μ-1-λ), t = p0_t·exp(-1-λ), f = p0_f·exp(-1-λ). Since p0_t = p0_f, we immediately get t = f: the message says nothing about Blue territory, so the symmetry between Third and Fourth is exactly preserved, which is a nice sanity check on the setup.

By the same logic, the message only bears on the internal split of Red, not on whether Judy is in Red at all, so there is no lever in the problem that could move probability mass across the Red/Blue boundary -- h and s have to rebalance between themselves while h + s stays fixed at 1/2. Combined with s = 3h, that gives h = 1/8 and s = 3/8, and by the same symmetry argument, t = f = 1/4, unchanged from the prior. So the final distribution is (1/8, 3/8, 1/4, 1/4), and P(Red) = 1/2 exactly, same as before the message arrived.

This is just what Jeffrey's rule predicts, since Jeffrey conditionalization is built to leave alone any probabilities that don't bear on the partition mentioned in the evidence, and 'Red vs. Blue' isn't that partition here. So I don't think there is a real paradox lurking in this problem at all -- the sense that something strange is going on comes from applying Jeffrey's rule naively to {Second, Headquarters} in isolation, without remembering to hold the Red/Blue split fixed. Once you do the constrained optimization properly, as above, the worry dissolves and you land on exactly the distribution naive intuition would have given you anyway."
