For a seminar on animal ethics, I've been comparing Peter Singer's utilitarian case against speciesism with Tom Regan's rights-based case against it. Both deny that species membership alone can justify discounting an animal's interests, but they reach that conclusion by different routes: Singer through the equal consideration of interests, Regan through the ascription of inherent value to "subjects-of-a-life," who may never be treated merely as a means. I follow the difference in the abstract, but I'm struggling to see what turns on it in practice. Could you devise a specific scenario — one in which no animal suffers, or is even aware that anything is at stake — in which the two theories would still prescribe genuinely different courses of action? Please also identify the structural feature of each theory, for instance its treatment of aggregation versus its commitment to inviolable rights, that is doing the work in producing that disagreement.
