I got kinda obsessed a while back with Quine's gavagai example - the one where a linguist is out with a native speaker, a rabbit runs by, the guy says "gavagai," and Quine's point is that you could translate that as "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit-stage," and literally no possible amount of behavioral evidence could ever settle which one's correct. He's not saying its just hard to figure out, he means there's no actual fact of the matter.

What I can't stop thinking about is what he'd make of todays AI translation. These models learn entirely from huge piles of text and mostly do a genuinely solid job, and different models, trained totally differently, still tend to land on pretty similar translations for the same sentence. Does that convergence count as evidence against Quine, like maybe there's a determinate right answer after all? Or would he say that's beside the point, since his whole "radical translation" setup assumed the linguist has zero dictionaries or bilingual help to lean on, which is basically the opposite of how these models get trained, on mountains of already-translated text? And does his argument even survive if we're just checking whether whole sentences line up, given that I've read the gavagai case specifically is about what individual words refer to, which he apparently thought was the deeper, harder problem?

Would love an actual argued out answer on what Quine's verdict would be here, not just a quick "he'd probably find it fascinating" hand-wave.
