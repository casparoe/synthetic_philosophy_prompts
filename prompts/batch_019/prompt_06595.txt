The question was: "State the Condorcet Jury Theorem and the assumptions required for it to hold. Suppose a jury of 3 members decides by majority rule between 'guilty' and 'not guilty,' and each juror independently has a 70% chance of voting for the correct verdict. (a) Compute the probability that the jury's majority verdict is correct, and compare it to the probability that a single, randomly-chosen juror is correct. (b) Describe one way the theorem's assumptions could fail in an actual jury, and explain what happens to the theorem's conclusion in that case." (15 points)

And here's the answer as turned in: "The Condorcet Jury Theorem says that if a group of voters each independently choose between two options, and each voter has some probability p > 1/2 of picking the correct option, then the probability that the majority vote lands on the correct option increases as the group gets larger, approaching 1 as the number of voters goes to infinity. Interestingly, the theorem holds even when p < 1/2: since each additional independent voter contributes more information, the extra votes average out individual error the same way that averaging repeated measurements averages out noise, so larger groups still converge on the truth more reliably than individuals even when members are individually worse than a coin flip. This is part of why the theorem gets invoked so often as a formal defense of majority rule in 'wisdom of crowds' arguments for epistemic democracy. (a) The jury's majority is correct if 2 or 3 of the jurors vote correctly. P(exactly 2 correct) = 3(0.7)^2(0.3) = 0.441, and P(all 3 correct) = (0.7)^3 = 0.343, so P(majority correct) = 0.441 + 0.343 = 0.784. Since 0.784 > 0.7, the three-person jury does better than a single juror, exactly as the theorem predicts. (b) The independence assumption is the one most likely to fail in an actual jury, since jurors deliberate together and can anchor on whoever speaks first or most confidently, making their votes correlated rather than independent. This is really the same instability Condorcet identified elsewhere: once you drop clean pairwise independence, majority rule can stop tracking anything stable at all, which is exactly what happens once you have three or more verdict options on the table (say, degrees of the charge) -- 'guilty of murder' can beat 'guilty of manslaughter,' which beats 'not guilty,' which beats 'guilty of murder' again, so there's no well-defined majority winner. So a deliberating jury doesn't just weaken the theorem's convergence result, it can undermine there being any determinate majority verdict at all."
