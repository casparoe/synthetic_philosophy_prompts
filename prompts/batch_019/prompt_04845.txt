I'm working on a longer magazine piece about the strange afterlife of philosophical thought experiments, and Rawls's veil of ignorance is the obvious centerpiece. While reading around for it, I went back to the original-position literature and was struck by how directly John Harsanyi's "impartial observer" argument anticipates Rawls's device: both ask a rational chooser to select principles for a society while stripped of any knowledge of which position within it they will occupy. Yet Harsanyi used essentially the same setup, roughly two decades earlier, to argue for average utilitarianism, while Rawls insisted that his own version rules utilitarianism out and instead licenses the difference principle. As far as I can tell, that divergence turns less on the veil itself than on what each writer assumes a rational party would do once it is drawn: Harsanyi has them maximizing expected utility on the assumption of an equal probability of occupying any position, while Rawls has them reasoning via maximin, for reasons that include the uniqueness and irreversibility of the choice, the "strains of commitment" the chosen principles must survive, and his wariness about assigning probabilities at all behind the veil.

For a sidebar, I'd like to lay this disagreement out properly rather than just gesture at it. Could you compare the two constructions in some depth, focusing on that decision-theoretic crux—maximin versus expected-utility maximization—and on the substantive case each side makes for why theirs is the rational response to this particular kind of uncertainty? I'd also value your own judgment on the matter: does Rawls's argument for maximin actually succeed in blocking Harsanyi's utilitarian conclusion, given Harsanyi's charge that maximin amounts to a kind of infinite risk-aversion nobody would accept in ordinary life, or does the dispute simply relocate the underlying disagreement about utilitarianism one level down, into a quarrel about decision theory under uncertainty? Please justify your answer.
