I spent thirty-five years as a research chemist before I retired, and only in the last couple of years have I actually started reading philosophy instead of just picking it up secondhand at dinner parties. Lately I've been working through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed (the Pines translation, since apparently that's the one everyone insists on) and got completely stuck on the section in Book III where he explains animal sacrifice as basically a concession — God meeting the Israelites where they actually were, habituated to slaughtering animals for idols in Egypt and among the "Sabians," and gradually weaning them off it toward prayer instead, the same way a doctor weans a patient off a bad diet rather then cutting them off cold turkey. Chapters 32 and 46, if I've got the numbers right. As a former scientist I love this move on its own terms — it's got the flavor of a good parsimonious model that explains a weird feature of the data as a leftover of initial conditions rather than as designed for its own sake.

The trouble is, the more commentary I read around it (starting with Nahmanides, who goes after this pretty hard in his own Torah commentary) the more objections and complications I keep running into, and I honestly can't tell anymore which of these are the kind of thing that actually sinks the theory versus the kind of nitpicking that every tidy idea attracts once enough smart people stare at it for eight hundred years. My study partner thinks I'm overthinking this and should just enjoy the Guide, but that's obviously not going to happen.

So here's what I'd like from you: below are six complications people have raised (some classical, some more modern) against Maimonides' "sacrifice as concession" theory specifically as he states it in III:32/46, not against some flattened version of it. Sort them into a tier list by how much genuine damage each one does to the theory as he actually argues it, S tier meaning "this basically breaks it," down to F tier meaning "not really a problem once you sit with it for five minutes." A sentence or two of reasoning per item would help.

1. Sacrifice already shows up, approvingly, with Abel and then Noah after the flood, long before Israel had any idolatrous cult of animal-worship to be weaned off of — Nahmanides' opening move against the theory.
2. The Torah repeatedly calls the smoke of the offering a "pleasing odor" to God, which reads like sacrifice has positive value to God rather than being merely tolerated as a stopgap.
3. Explaining why sacrifice-in-general survived doesn't obviously explain the very specific menu of the sacrificial code — which species, which cuts, which mixtures — and Maimonides' usual escape hatch (that the general category has a reason even where the particulars don't) feels like its doing more work here than he lets on.
4. Jewish law, including Maimonides' own law code, anticipates the sacrificial service being restored once the Temple is rebuilt, which is a strange thing to legislate for if sacrifice were only ever meant as a rung on a ladder you climb past on the way to something higher.
5. Maimonides' whole picture of the "Sabian" idolatry being weaned off of leans on his understanding of ancient star-worship that, from what I can tell, rests on sources modern scholars don't think are nearly as ancient as he believed — so the historical scaffolding under the theory is shakier than he seems to assume.
6. Push the "concession" logic all the way, the way Strauss and people reading him that way tend to, and it starts threatening not just sacrifice but the idea that any commandment is simply what God wants rather than what the multitude can currently stomach — is that actually a problem for Maimonides, or just the theory doing its job one level up?
