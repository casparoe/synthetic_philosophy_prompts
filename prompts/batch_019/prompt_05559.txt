I've followed the fight over Integrated Information Theory on and off since Scott Aaronson's "Unconscious Expander" post on Shtetl-Optimized made the rounds in the rationalist blogosphere, and the 2023 open letter -- signed by well over a hundred consciousness researchers, explicit in its use of the word "pseudoscience" -- pulled me back into it. What I've never managed to settle for myself is whether the specific technical objections actually sink the theory, or whether they're just the kind of counterintuitive-but-survivable commitment that any sufficiently ambitious theory of consciousness might have to accept. Tononi's exclusion postulate forces a sharp, winner-take-all boundary between overlapping candidate systems, so that whether something counts as conscious can hinge on a razor's-edge comparison between the integrated-information values of two near-identical structures. Aaronson's own construction goes further, building simple, sparse, expander-graph-like circuits with arbitrarily large Φ that do nothing anyone would call thinking, which he takes to show that Φ is tracking something other than whatever we mean by consciousness. And the "unfolding argument" that circulated a few years later points out that any recurrent network can be "unfolded" into a feedforward network that reproduces its input-output behavior exactly, even though IIT assigns the two systems wildly different (indeed, zero versus nonzero) Φ -- which critics take to show that the theory's verdicts are unmoored from anything we could ever observe in a subject's behavior.

IIT's defenders have substantive replies, however. The theory did generate a distinctive, falsifiable prediction about sustained posterior "hot zone" activity, as opposed to the prefrontal "ignition" predicted by Global Workspace Theory, and when the two predictions were pitted against each other in a large, pre-registered adversarial collaboration, the results did not straightforwardly favor Global Workspace Theory either. Defenders also point out that panpsychism-adjacent commitments aren't automatically disqualifying for a scientific theory (plenty of respectable physics has weird entailments too), and that being hard to test with current tools is not the same thing as being unfalsifiable in principle, which is the distinction most philosophers of science actually invoke when they reach for the word "pseudoscience."

Could you write an essay that actually adjudicates this, rather than just cataloguing the considerations on each side? I'd like you to weigh the exclusion-postulate worries, Aaronson's counterexample, and the unfolding argument against IIT's empirical track record and its defenders' replies, and then commit to a specific, substantive verdict -- not "reasonable people disagree," but something with real content, along the lines of "the pseudoscience charge is basically fair," "IIT is probably false but pseudoscience is the wrong diagnosis," or "the theory isn't well-formed enough to be scored true or false in the first place." Whatever verdict you reach, please defend it against what you take to be the strongest form of the objections to it.
