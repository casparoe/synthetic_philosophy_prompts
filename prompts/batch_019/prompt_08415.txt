So I've been on a bit of a Vienna Circle kick since I retired from the lab (35 years doing polymer chemistry, and now I've finally got time to read the philosophy I only skimmed back in grad school). What's been surprising me is how political the whole "logical positivism" project apparently was underneath all the technical stuff about protocol sentences and verification. Carnap and Neurath weren't just tidying up language for the fun of it — Neurath actually ran the central economic planning office for the Bavarian Soviet Republic in 1919 and got put on trial for it once that government collapsed, if I'm remembering the book right, and that famous 1929 manifesto, "The Scientific Conception of the World," reads less like a philosophy paper and more like a movement pamphlet, tying "unified science" to the settlement-house and workers'-education stuff going on in Red Vienna at the time.

Here's the little essay-argument I've been chewing on, and I'd like you to poke holes in it before I embarass myself showing it to my reading group: I think Carnap's anti-metaphysics campaign (pseudo-statements, "elimination of metaphysics through logical analysis of language," all that) and his later principle of tolerance in Logical Syntax of Language were both, at bottom, doing the same job his politics wanted done — clearing away the kind of unfalsifiable, tradition-bound talk (national destiny, organic society, all that Hegelian fog) wich gets used to block rational social reform, while the tolerance principle then keeps any single reformed "logic" from calcifying into its own new orthodoxy, so that social planning stays revisable and piecemeal instead of dogmatic. Basically I'm reading Carnap's technical philosophy as a kind of scientist's political program in disguise, with the actual politics mostly kept off the page because, per that Reisch book on the Cold War, that's exactly the sort of thing that became career-dangerous for people like him once he'd settled in the US.

Can you help me see where this is shaky? Specifically I'd like comments on: (1) whether I've got the Neurath/Bavaria and manifesto history roughly right or whether I'm garbling it, (2) whether reading the tolerance principle as a kind of political pluralism is an actual line of interpretation people take, or whether I'm just projecting my own hopes onto what was meant as a narrow technical point about logic, and (3) whether Carnap himself, in his own words, ever endorsed anything like this connection, or if I'm unfairly mashing him together with Neurath, who was clearly the more openly political one of the pair. I don't need a full survey of logical positivism, just some honest pushback on this specific argument.
