For the last couple of months I've been stuck on an idea that I don't think I'll ever be able to unlearn, even though I have no formal training in any of this and may not have every detail exactly right. As far as I understand it, essentially all of mathematics — arithmetic, calculus, the sort of thing engineers and physicists ultimately build bridges and rockets out of — rests on a short list of axioms, things like the Peano axioms for numbers or the ZFC axioms for sets, that we simply agree to accept without proof, on the assumption that they never secretly contradict one another. I learned that something like this has actually happened before, on a smaller scale: Frege spent years deriving arithmetic from what he took to be self-evident logical laws, only for Russell to write him a letter pointing out that one of those laws lets you derive an outright contradiction, and the project never really recovered. Gödel later showed something stranger still: that no system powerful enough to do ordinary arithmetic can ever prove its own consistency from the inside, provided it really is consistent. So underneath everything there is this thin thread of doubt that can never quite be cut — we trust that no one will ever find a hidden contradiction, even though, logically speaking, one might already be sitting out there somewhere, an enormous number of deductive steps away, simply waiting to be found.

A friend of mine who is doing a PhD in computer science mentioned that mathematicians increasingly rely on automated proof assistants that check huge formal proofs symbol by symbol, with no room for hand-waving, and that got me imagining the day one of these programs actually surfaced a genuine contradiction somewhere completely ordinary — not in some exotic, little-used corner of set theory, but in plain arithmetic, the sort of foundation every other proof quietly depends on. Since a single contradiction lets classical logic "prove" literally anything, every theorem ever derived from that foundation would become suspect in the same instant. I would love a short story built around that moment, centered on whoever is first alone with the result and has to decide what, if anything, to do before telling another living soul. I am less interested in a lecture on the logic itself than in what the discovery would actually feel like, both for that one person and, eventually, for everyone else — to find that the ground everybody had quietly assumed was solid had never really been guaranteed at all.
