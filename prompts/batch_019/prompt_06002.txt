I teach a graduate seminar on just war theory, and last week we'd just finished Anscombe's "Mr Truman's Degree" -- the short pamphlet she put out in 1956 objecting to Oxford handing Truman an honorary degree over the atomic bombs -- when two of my students, Marcus and Elena, ended up arguing about it in the hallway for a good twenty minutes after everyone else had left. Marcus's opening was that her argument is basically airtight: Truman ordered the intentional killing of a city's civilian population as a means of forcing surrender, intentionally killing innocents as a means is murder, and that verdict doesn't budge no matter how many lives it supposedly saved down the line, because consequences of that particular kind just aren't the sort of thing that gets to overturn it. Elena said that's too quick, since the murder verdict depends entirely on what was actually intended -- Hiroshima had a garrison and factories in it too -- and if the thing being targeted under the operative description was military, with civilian deaths as a foreseen side effect rather than the whole point, then double effect is at least in play; she thought Anscombe mostly asserts the "civilians were the target" reading rather than really arguing for it.

Marcus came back with the actual target selection: the cities were picked over more purely military or industrial sites partly for their expected effect on civilian morale, the same reasoning behind Dresden and Tokyo, so this was never collateral damage from hitting a legitimate target -- it was closer to the textbook case double effect exists to rule out. Elena granted him the history but said the ground had quietly shifted: they'd gone from "intentionally killing the innocent as a means is absolutely wrong, full stop" to a dispute about a specific 1945 targeting decision, which is a much narrower thing to have shown. So she pushed him on the hypothetical -- if the invasion really would've killed far more people, Japanese and American both, does the prohibition hold with zero exceptions, or does it bend somewhere? If it never bends, Marcus has taken on a serious and revisionary committment that the history of one bombing decision doesn't let him off the hook for.

Marcus said biting that bullet is the whole point of Anscombe's argument, not an embarrassing cost of it: her real target is the reasoning that lets a prohibition on murder get outweighed by a big enough number, since that kind of reasoning has no natural stopping point and will eventually justify anything, and anyway, the "it saved more lives than an invasion would have" figures were mostly produced after the fact to justify a decision already made, not derived beforehand. Elena's last word was that those are two seperate questions -- whether the wartime estimates were honest, and whether an honestly dire estimate would flip the verdict -- and that most people who actually work within double effect and just war theory let proportionality matter once you're past the question of who's being targeted, so Marcus is quietly committed to something stronger and lonelier than he's letting on. They got cut off by the next class before either gave any ground, and I genuinely couldn't tell you who came out ahead -- my sympathies kept sliding around the whole time I was listening. Could you go through the exchange move by move, Marcus's opening through both of their closing points, and say who has the better of it at each individual step, not just an overall winner -- and then, separately, tell me what you think the two of them, between them, still haven't figured out?
