Two nights ago, at the rehearsal dinner for my closest friend Elena's wedding, I got into a long and increasingly stubborn argument with our mutual friend Ravi about Aumann's agreement theorem. Elena and her fiancé Marcus are both economists who met in a graduate seminar on epistemic game theory, and over the six years they've been together they've developed a running bit: whenever they disagree about something trivial — how many minutes to leave for the airport, whose parents' cooking is better — one of them says "let's just Aumann it," they each name a number, average it, and move on, laughing, as if citing the theorem had actually settled anything. Ravi, who is not a philosopher but enjoys sounding like one after a couple of glasses of wine, called this "basically just a cute way of saying you trust each other." I found myself insisting, more loudly than the occasion warranted, that this gets the theorem exactly backwards: Aumann's result isn't about people who trust each other reaching agreement; it says that two people who already share a common prior, and whose posteriors on some question become common knowledge between them, cannot go on rationally disagreeing — and the whole philosophical bite of the result is in how rarely real people, even loving and honest ones, actually satisfy either condition. Ravi's counter was that repeated back-and-forth, like the couple's little ritual, might be doing some of the work a shared prior is supposed to do, which he thought he remembered being the point of a later refinement of Aumann's result, though neither of us could reconstruct it clearly enough to settle anything.

I'm the maid of honor, and I'm giving the toast tomorrow evening. I can't stop turning this over. Roughly half the room will be Elena and Marcus's academic friends, who will notice immediately if I either garble the theorem or wheel it out as decoration; the other half is family, who has never heard the phrase "common prior" and simply wants to be moved. Could you write me a toast of about five minutes that puts the actual content of Aumann's theorem — the common-prior assumption, the common-knowledge condition, and how demanding both really are — to genuine use, rather than treating it as trivia or a punchline? I don't want it to land on either "aren't they lucky, they always just agree" (which is both saccharine and not what the theorem says) or a seminar on Bayesian epistemology dressed up in wedding clothes. I want the turn that actually gets people a little emotional to come out of the gap between what the theorem demands and what these two have spent six years quietly building in order to approximate it. It should be funny enough to survive an open bar, precise enough that Ravi can't heckle it afterward, and warm enough that Elena's mother cries a little.
