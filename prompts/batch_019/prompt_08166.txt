For my IB Extended Essay on the history of probability theory, I've been working through Laplace's *Essai philosophique sur les probabilités*, and his rule of succession — inferring the chance that a run will continue from nothing but a count of how often it has held so far — struck me as an ancestor of sorts to the doomsday argument. In its Carter-Leslie form, later sharpened by Bostrom's self-sampling assumption, that argument treats your own birth rank among all humans who will ever live as a random draw from that population and uses Bayes' theorem to argue that this should shift your credence toward an earlier rather than a much later end for the species. I'm curious how you think Laplace himself, given his commitment to the principle of insufficient reason and his own use of inverse probability on thin samples, would have reacted to this argument if it had been put to him. Would his own methods have committed him to accepting it, or would he have pushed back — for instance, on how the relevant reference class of "observers" gets chosen? Please reconstruct the reasoning you think he would have actually given, rather than just stating whether he'd agree.
