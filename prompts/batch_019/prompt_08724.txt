I belong to a giving circle of about thirty members that pools everyone's annual charitable pledges and, each December, allocates the total according to a written recommendation from a seven-person allocations committee that I chair this cycle, which the full membership then votes to ratify or amend at the annual meeting. This year's pool comes to roughly $180,000, and after a fall of vetting submissions the committee has narrowed the field to two finalists. The first is a bednet-distribution charity backed by several independent randomized evaluations and a well-audited cost-per-life-saved figure; the second runs corporate campaigns that pressure retailers and food producers to move their egg and pork supply chains away from cages and gestation crates, on a theory of change that plays out over a decade or more through market and regulatory pressure rather than through any single countable event.

The committee is deadlocked 4-3, and the split has turned out to be more philosophical than practical. The majority wants to give everything to the bednet charity on the grounds that we owe it to our donors to prefer the option whose effects we can actually verify. The minority answers that this is a double standard: saving human lives predictably increases meat consumption as incomes rise, so the bednet charity's downstream welfare effects are every bit as unknown as the campaign group's — we simply have not been forced to reckon with them the way we have with the campaign's more speculative theory of change. One of the minority members says this general predicament, of being unable to responsibly trace an action's consequences far enough downstream to compare it against the alternative, has a name in the philosophical literature; that sent me reading over the weekend, but nothing I found told me what a committee actually facing this choice on a two-week deadline should do.

That deadline is the real problem now: I need to produce the two-page memo that goes out to the full membership ahead of the vote. It has to recommend an actual split of the $180,000 (or some other definite course of action, if a split turns out to be the wrong instinct) rather than simply restating the dilemma for a vote, and it needs to take the "prefer what we can verify" position seriously on its own terms, since it looks likely to be the losing position and its proponents deserve a real response rather than a vote count. Could you draft that memo for me?
