During a Theory of Knowledge class, a discussion of G. E. Moore's old example, "It's raining, but I don't believe it" — the case philosophers call Moore's paradox — turned into a running argument between two classmates that outlasted the period. Maya called the sentence a flat contradiction, no different from "it's raining and it's not raining," reasoning that if it's actually raining, a person is bound to believe it, so the two halves cancel each other out. Dev disagreed: plenty of people fail to notice rain, so the weather and Maya's own state of mind are logically independent, and both halves can easily be true together; the only real oddity, he said, is in asserting the sentence about yourself, which runs afoul of some rule against insincere assertion rather than any fact about truth. Maya granted the point about truth, but objected that the same rule should misfire whenever sincerity is violated, yet "she believes it's raining, and it isn't" and "it was raining, and I didn't believe it until I looked outside" both sound perfectly fine; she added that silently believing the combination, without ever saying it aloud, seems just as impossible as asserting it, so the trouble can't be only about speech. Dev answered that an assertion always voices a live, current belief, so only first person plus present tense forces you to lean on a belief while denying you have it, whereas other people's beliefs, or your own past ones, create no such clash. He admitted the silent-belief version was harder to explain but insisted it must reduce to the same phenomenon. Could you go through their four moves in order, say who has the better of it at each stage rather than just overall, and settle the question Dev never quite answered: does his account of assertion really explain why merely believing "it's raining, and I don't believe it" feels just as irrational as saying it out loud?
