So I've been slowly working through Jaynes's probability book (yes, the Logic of Science one everyone on here recommends) and I hit the chapter where he deals with Bertrand's chord paradox, and now I can't stop turning it over in my head. Quick recap in case you need it: you're asked for the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and depending on whether you (a) pick two uniformly random endpoints on the circumference, (b) pick a random radius and then a uniformly random point along it and draw the chord through that point, or (c) pick a uniformly random point inside the disk to be the chord's midpoint, you get 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 respectively. Every method looks like a perfectly reasonable way to cash out "at random," and yet they flatly contradict each other -- that's the paradox.

What's been bugging me is Jaynes's move: he says the paradox only arises because the problem as stated is underspecified, and once you demand the solution be invariant under the rotations, translations and rescalings that the problem itself doesn't rule out, only method (b) survives, so 1/2 is the objectively correct answer. That seemed clever to me at first, but a friend who's way more into philosophy of probability than I am insists this doesn't actually rescue the principle of indifference in general -- it only works here because Jaynes quietly smuggled in extra physical structure (a specific randomizing procedure, like tossing straws onto the circle) that wasn't part of the original "pick a chord at random" statement, and that the real lesson of the paradox is that "equally likely" is basically meaningless on continuous outcome spaces unless a privileged parametrization has already been handed to you from somewhere else.
