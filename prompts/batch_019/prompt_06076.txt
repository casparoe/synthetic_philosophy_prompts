Student answer: "Putnam's no-miracles argument holds that scientific realism is the only philosophy that does not turn the predictive success of our best theories into a miracle. Laudan attacks this by compiling a list of theories — phlogiston chemistry, the caloric theory of heat, the effluvial theory of static electricity, and the elastic-solid ether, among others — that were genuinely successful in their day and are nonetheless now considered false, including in what they said about the unobservable entities responsible for that success. If empirical success did not track truth in those cases, we have no non-question-begging reason to think it tracks truth now, so the realist's inference from success to approximate truth is undermined.

Worrall's structural realism tries to save realism by narrowing what the realist is committed to. Rather than believing in whatever unobservable entities a successful theory happens to posit, the realist should only be committed to those entities that can be independently detected and manipulated — in Ian Hacking's sense that if you can spray them, they are real. On this view, Fresnel's theory of light turns from a counterexample into a success story: although the elastic-solid ether does not exist, the electrons and photons that his equations were really describing were carried over intact into Maxwell's theory, and this continuity of entities is exactly why structural realism blocks the pessimistic meta-induction. Because mathematical structure, unlike claims about underlying natures, is strictly cumulative, the same pattern shows up when Newtonian mechanics is absorbed as a limiting case into general relativity. I conclude that Worrall's strategy succeeds: what survives theory change is precisely what the realist needs to survive, and what gets jettisoned was never really essential to the theory's success in the first place."
