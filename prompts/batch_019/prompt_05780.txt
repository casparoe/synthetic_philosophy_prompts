Question 3. In Unended Quest (1976), Popper concluded that "Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research programme." In a lecture delivered at Darwin College, Cambridge -- published in Dialectica in 1978 as "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind" -- he went on to describe himself as making "a recantation."

(a) (8 points) Explain the tautology worry that originally motivated Popper's 1976 verdict, and state precisely what he did and did not take back in 1978.
(b) (8 points) Some commentators have read the "metaphysical research programme" verdict as, in effect, Popper's way of placing Darwinism in the same category as the pseudosciences he discusses elsewhere in his work, such as astrology. Is that a fair reading of Popper's own considered view, and why or why not?
(c) (4 points) Briefly explain how Popper's remarks on this episode bear on the more general worry that his falsifiability criterion cannot accommodate historical or evolutionary explanation.

Student's answer:

(a) The worry is that "survival of the fittest" is empty unless there is some independent way of identifying the fittest other than by seeing who survives; if fitness is simply defined as reproductive success, the principle collapses into "those who survive, survive," which no observation could ever falsify. This is what led Popper, in Unended Quest, to call the theory of natural selection "almost tautological" and to conclude that Darwinism -- by which he specifically meant the mechanism of natural selection, not the historical claim that species change over time -- was a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable theory. By the time of the Darwin Lecture, Popper had decided this verdict had been too harsh: he now held that natural selection, properly formulated, yields specific, checkable claims (for example, about the rate at which a population should adapt to a new environment), and that formulated this way the theory is not even strictly universally true, since exceptions occur. Having shown that the theory could be made testable after all, he dropped the "metaphysical research programme" language and treated natural selection from then on as an ordinary, if unusually general, empirical theory.

(b) I think the reading is basically correct. Popper's complaint about astrology is that it can always be rescued from a failed prediction by blaming the horoscope rather than the theory, so that no observation is ever allowed to count against it; his complaint about natural selection is structurally the same, since "fitness" can always be adjusted after the fact to match whatever happened to survive. Once a theory has been immunized against refutation in this way, calling it "metaphysical" rather than "pseudoscientific" is really a matter of tone rather than substance -- Popper is simply more polite about Darwinism, which he personally admired, than he is about astrology, which he didn't.

(c) Popper is explicit that none of this was meant to strip paleontology or "the history of the evolution of life on Earth" of scientific status, since particular historical hypotheses -- about what should or should not turn up in a given rock stratum, for instance -- remain testable even when the general selective mechanism does not reduce to one crucial experiment. This blunts, though it does not fully answer, the older worry, associated with Hempel's covering-law model of explanation, that accounts of unrepeatable past events cannot be genuinely scientific, since it shows that singular historical claims can still carry testable consequences.
