The question was: "Explain Frank Jackson's knowledge argument (the 'Mary's Room' thought experiment). Then explain how the ability hypothesis and the phenomenal concept strategy each try to block the argument's anti-physicalist conclusion, bringing out clearly what the two responses agree and disagree about. Which do you find more successful, and why?"

Here is one student's answer, verbatim:

"Jackson asks us to imagine Mary, a brilliant scientist who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room and has learned every physical fact there is to know about the science of color vision, including the complete neurophysiology of human sight. When she is finally released and sees a ripe tomato, she learns something new: what it is like to see red. Since she already possessed all the physical information beforehand, Jackson concludes that this new item of knowledge must be a non-physical fact, so physicalism—the thesis that all the facts are physical facts—is false.

Two well-known physicalist replies try to block this conclusion. Jackson's own preferred response was the ability hypothesis, which denies that Mary gains any new factual knowledge at all. On this view, what she acquires on leaving the room is a set of abilities—to recognize red, to imagine it, to remember it—rather than a proposition she did not believe before. Knowing what an experience is like is knowing-how, not knowing-that, so no new fact is added to her stock of information and physicalism is preserved.

The phenomenal concept strategy takes a different line. It grants that Mary gains new propositional knowledge but denies that this shows the existence of a new, non-physical property. She simply acquires a new phenomenal concept, a mental file that refers to the very same brain state her old physical concepts referred to all along, so the fact is old even though her way of grasping it is new—much as 'water' and 'H2O' pick out one substance under two different guises.

Of the two, the phenomenal concept strategy seems the stronger position, because it takes seriously the intuition that Mary really does learn something new on leaving the room, an intuition that the ability hypothesis, by denying she acquires any knowledge on her release, cannot accommodate."
