I am preparing a graduate seminar session on Chapter VI of Moore's *Principia Ethica* ("The Ideal"), and I would like help thinking through a tension I expect my students to raise. Here is the passage under discussion:

"Let us imagine one world exceedingly beautiful. Imagine it as beautiful as you can; put into it whatever on this earth you most admire — mountains, rivers, the sea; trees, and sunsets, stars and moon. Imagine these all combined in the most exquisite proportions, so that no one thing jars against another, but each contributes to increase the beauty of the whole. And then imagine the ugliest world you can possibly conceive. Imagine it simply one heap of filth, containing everything that is most disgusting to us, for whatever reason, and the whole, as far as may be, without one redeeming feature. The only thing we are not entitled to imagine is that any human being ever has or ever, by any possibility, can, live in either, can ever see and enjoy the beauty of the one or hate the foulness of the other."

Moore concludes that it would still be irrational to deny that the beautiful world is the better of the two, purely on account of its beauty, even though its beauty would be witnessed by no one. A little further on in the same chapter, he declares that almost everything of great value in life reduces to just two things: personal affection and the enjoyment of beautiful objects.

Here is my difficulty. Moore's own doctrine of organic unities holds that a beautiful object together with someone's contemplation of it is worth far more than the sum of the values of the object and the contemplation taken separately — the whole exceeds its parts. This appears to imply that a beautiful world witnessed by no one should count for comparatively little. Yet Moore treats our preference for the unwitnessed beautiful world as close to self-evident, and then goes on to make the appreciation of beauty, rather than its mere existence, central to his account of the supreme goods, alongside personal affection.

Could you reconstruct, on Moore's own terms, an argument that would let him hold both positions at once? And is there, in your view, a genuine inconsistency between this "isolation" thought experiment and his considered view that what matters most is the appreciation of beauty together with personal affection, rather than either beauty or affection alone?
