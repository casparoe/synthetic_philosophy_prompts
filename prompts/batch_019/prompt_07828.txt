"The demarcation problem is the question of how to draw a line between genuine science and things that only look like science, such as astrology or pseudo-science in general. Popper's answer is that what makes a theory scientific is that it sticks its neck out: it makes risky prediction that could in principle be shown false by observation. He was bothered, like the logical positivists before him, by theories such as Marxism and Freudian psychoanalysis that seemed to be compatible with literally any evidence, so he concluded that such theories are actually meaningless rather than genuine factual claims about the world, whereas Einstein's prediction that starlight would bend by a specific, measurable amount during the 1919 eclipse was a real scientific test because the theory could have failed it and didn't. However, the Quine-Duhem thesis complicates this: since theories are only ever tested together with a bunch of auxiliary assumptions, any single falsification can always be blamed on one of those assumptions instead of on the main theory, so strictly speaking nothing is ever conclusively falsified. Lakatos took this further, arguing that we should think of the history of science in terms of paradigms: a dominant paradigm holds until enough anomalies pile up to cause a crisis, at which point its overthrown by a new, incommensurable paradigm, meaning theory-choice ends up being partly a matter of the values of a scientific community rather than pure logic. Given all this, I don't think the demarcation problem has been fully solved, since we still don't have a knock-down criterion that cleanly sorts science from non-science in every case."
