I'm about two-thirds into a historical novel set in 1857-58, and there's a scene I keep putting off because I honestly don't know how to write it fairly. I've got two abolitionist characters at a convention — one standing in for William Lloyd Garrison, the other for Frederick Douglass — and they're supposed to go head to head over whether the Constitution itself is a pro-slavery document. I know Garrison's side called it "a covenant with death and an agreement with hell" and refused to even vote, pointing to things like the three-fifths clause and the fugitive slave clause, and they treated the Dred Scott decision as basically proof they'd been right all along. And I know Douglass used to agree with all of that but broke with Garrison around 1850-51 and spent the rest of his life arguing the opposite: that since the words "slave" and "slavery" appear no where in the actual text, the document should be read as neutral at the very worst, and secretly on the side of liberty at best. Problem is, I don't actually know who was right, and I refuse to write the scene as a strawman where one guy is obviously the smart one and the other's just there to lose the argument.

So could you write me something like a short essay that really gets into the weeds of it — the specific clauses, how much weight you give the framers' actual documented intentions versus the plain wording they chose to use, whether something like Dred Scott should count as evidence for how the document actually functions or is just irrelevant to what it technically says, and whether Douglass's timing (right around when he'd gotten cozy with Gerrit Smith and his money and the political abolitionists, who badly needed the Constitution to be usable) makes his change of heart look a little convenient — and then actually commit to an answer? Not a "both men had a point" cop-out, an honest verdict on whose reading holds up better, with the reasoning laid out. My writing group keeps telling me a novelist's whole job is to just stage the disagreement and let readers make up there own minds, but I don't think I can write this scene with any real conviction until I've quietly decided, myself, who's winning the argument.
