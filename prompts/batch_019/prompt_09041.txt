Last weekend, over dinner, I made the mistake of asking my friend Marcus, who is finishing a dissertation on population and infinite ethics, to look over the toast I'm giving at my college roommate Priya's wedding next month. We ended up arguing about infinite ethics for the better part of two hours instead of eating, and I still haven't recovered, partly because I suspect he's more right than I'd like, and the rehearsal dinner is in ten days.

The line he objected to was the sentimental centerpiece of the whole toast: I'd written that I hoped the happiness Priya and her fiancé Dev feel now would simply keep compounding, year after year, with no ceiling, for as long as they both lived. Marcus's point was that this is exactly the kind of claim infinite ethics gives you good reason to distrust. Once you're comparing open-ended futures rather than finite ones, he said, there is no single agreed way to rank a life of ever-compounding joy above, say, a life with one extraordinary decade followed by a long, ordinary plateau: depending on whether you compare such futures by long-run averages, by something like an overtaking or catching-up criterion, or by any of the other standard proposals for aggregating infinite streams of value, you get different and sometimes flatly opposed verdicts, each with its own well-documented paradoxes. Push the same reasoning further, he added, and you reach the more unsettling idea some philosophers call infinitarian paralysis: against a backdrop that is already infinite, no finite improvement — no marriage, no kindness, no toast — changes the total by anything at all, which is a strange note to hit in a wedding toast, even by accident.

I don't want to give up on the sense of boundlessness a wedding toast is supposed to have, but I also don't want to stand up in front of a room that will be roughly half philosophy department — Priya and Dev met in a graduate seminar on decision theory, and Marcus will be sitting right there — and say something that infinite ethics gives good grounds to wince at. Could you write the toast itself? It should run three to four minutes read aloud, move the relatives who have never heard of any of this, and either earn its way honestly to some version of forever or make peace with doing without one — but it can't dodge the problem by smuggling the same move back in under a different name, and it can't turn into a philosophy lecture.
