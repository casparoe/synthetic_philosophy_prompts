I'm a software engineer with no formal background in biology or philosophy, but I recently fell down a rabbit hole that started with Michael Spence's job-market signaling model in economics, the idea that an otherwise pointless, costly credential can be economically rational to acquire simply because it's a hard-to-fake signal of underlying quality, and ended up at Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle in evolutionary biology. What struck me is that Darwin himself never got anywhere near this idea, even though he's the one who first had to explain traits like the peacock's tail. In a much-quoted 1860 letter to the botanist Asa Gray, he apparently confessed that the mere sight of a peacock's tail feather made him feel sick, precisely because so extravagant and costly an ornament seemed so hard to reconcile with natural selection. Darwin died in 1882, almost a century before Zahavi proposed in 1975 that it is precisely the cost of a signal that makes it evolutionarily reliable, and well before Alan Grafen gave that idea rigorous game-theoretic footing in 1990 by showing that honest signaling could be an evolutionarily stable strategy.

To complicate matters, my understanding is that Darwin's own preferred explanation for such ornaments leaned heavily on the idea of female "taste" or aesthetic preference, a move that Alfred Russel Wallace publicly rejected in favor of more strictly utilitarian, survival-based explanations of animal coloration and display. So even within Darwin's own lifetime, his account of sexual selection was already under attack for smuggling in an unexplained psychological preference instead of grounding male ornamentation directly in fitness.

Given all that, suppose you could sit Darwin down today and walk him through the full modern picture: the handicap principle, Grafen's formal vindication of it, the rival Fisherian runaway account of exaggerated ornaments, and the way the same signaling logic has since been exported well beyond biology, into economics and into explanations of costly human behaviors such as religious ritual or conspicuous generosity. Would he most likely embrace the handicap principle as finally supplying the missing mechanism behind his own idea of female choice, effectively vindicating him over Wallace, or are there aspects of his own stated views on beauty, taste, and the limits of what selection can produce that would lead him to resist or misread the idea even after having it explained to him? I would like an answer that actually engages with the specifics of what Darwin wrote and argued, rather than a generic "he would be amazed by modern science" response.
