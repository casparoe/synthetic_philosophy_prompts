And here's the student's answer, copied exactly (typos and all):

"The money-pump argument is meant to show that if an agent's preferences are cyclic - say they prefer A to B, B to C, but also C to A - then that agent can be exploited by a series of trades. Suppose the agent starts out holding C. Since they prefer B to C, they'd be willing to trade C for B plus pay a small fee. Since they prefer A to B, they'd trade B for A plus a small fee. And since they prefer C to A, they'd trade A back for C plus a small fee. Now they're back to holding C, exactly what they started with, except poorer by three fees. Because this can be repeated indefinitely, the agent can be pumped for an arbitrary amount of money. The lesson is supposed to be that rationality requires transitive preferences, on pain of being a 'money pump.'

The argument is naturally framed as being about diachronic rather than synchronic rationality because the exploitation only shows up over a sequence of choices made at different times - the agent isn't being asked to rank A, B and C all at once, but is instead making a series of separate, myopic trades, each of which looks fine in isolation given their current preference. Nothing about the agent's state at any single instant is incoherent in the way that holding contradictory beliefs at the same time is incoherent; the problem only becomes visible once you look at the whole sequence of choices over time. This is why some philosophers think the real requirement being motivated isn't transitivity of preference as such, but something more like consistency of planning across time.

The money-pump argument is essentially the same kind of argument as a Dutch book argument, just applied to preferences instead of credences: a Dutch book shows that an agent with incoherent credences can be induced to accept a set of bets that guarantees a loss, and a money pump shows that an agent with cyclic preferences can be induced to accept a set of trades that guarantees a loss. Both arguments have the same basic structure - exploit a violation of a formal coherence requirement to extract a sure loss - and both are used to argue that rational agents must satisfy the relevant coherence axioms (probabilism for credence, transitivity for preference).

Finally, there are a couple of ways an agent might try to avoid being pumped without simply giving up their cyclic preferences. One is 'resolute choice,' associated with McClennen, on which the agent commits at the outset to a transitive ranking of the available options and sticks to that ranking throughout the sequence of trades, rather than reasoning about each trade separately - this guarantees they'll never accept a trade that leads back to a worse outcome. Another is 'sophisticated choice,' on which the agent reasons backward from the end of the sequence, anticipating what they would do at later choice points, and uses this to decide what to do now; a sophisticated chooser facing the trade sequence above would foresee that accepting the first trade eventually leads to a loss and would refuse to trade away from C in the first place. Overall, the money-pump argument is a powerful tool for motivating transitivity (and, with small modifications, completeness) as a requirement of rational choice, though these responses suggest the deeper requirement might really be about diachronic planning rather than about the shape of one's preferences per se."
