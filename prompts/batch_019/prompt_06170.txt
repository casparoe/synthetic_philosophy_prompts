For my seminar on formal epistemology, we have spent the last two weeks on the Doomsday Argument in its Carter–Leslie form: the claim that, treating my own birth rank among all humans who will ever live as a random sample from that class, I ought to update against hypotheses on which vastly more humans are yet to be born, since such hypotheses make my comparatively early position in the sequence statistically surprising. We also covered Gott's independent "Copernican" derivation of a similar conclusion (complete with the obligatory Berlin Wall anecdote), Bostrom's discussion of how the self-indication assumption can partly offset the self-sampling assumption on which the argument relies, and the standing objection that the whole apparatus presupposes we can non-arbitrarily fix the relevant reference class of "observers" in the first place.

After class, a few of us fell into an argument about which pre-twentieth-century thinker would have had the most interesting reaction to all this, and I kept coming back to Condorcet rather than the more obvious candidates, like Laplace or Bayes. He strikes me as a genuinely hard case rather than a cute pairing, because his two most relevant commitments pull in opposite directions. On one hand, he was among the first to build real technical apparatus for reasoning probabilistically about large populations of people — the jury theorem in his 1785 Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions rendues à la pluralité des voix (his essay on the probability of majority decisions) is the standard example — so he had the tools to engage with something like self-sampling reasoning on its own terms, rather than just react to it intuitively. On the other hand, the Esquisse, drafted while he was in hiding from the Terror, contains perhaps the single most uncompromising statement of progress-optimism from the entire Enlightenment: in the tenth epoch, he argues that there is no principled ceiling on the moral, intellectual, or even organic improvement of the human species, up to and including how long human beings live.

What I would like help with is reconstructing, as concretely as you can, how those two commitments would actually interact, rather than just concluding that "he would have found some way to stay optimistic." Would his own probabilistic instincts have pushed him to take self-sampling reasoning at face value, or does his specific picture of what future human beings would be like give him principled grounds — to do with exactly which reference class the argument is entitled to use — for resisting the inference instead? I would much rather have a substantive attempt at this than a gesture at his general temperament.
