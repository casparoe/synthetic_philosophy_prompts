(a) [5 pts] State the assumptions, beyond the ordinary probability calculus, that Lewis needs about the conditional and about the relevant class of probability functions in order to derive his result.
(b) [8 pts] Sketch why these assumptions force the conclusion that A and B must be probabilistically independent.

And here is the submission:

"(a) Beyond the ordinary probability axioms, Lewis needs two further assumptions. First, 'if A, then B' must denote a genuine proposition, call it A > B, belonging to the same algebra as A and B, so that P(A > B) is well-formed and the algebra is closed under forming conditionals out of any two of its members. Second, the Equation has to hold not merely for our actual credence function P, but for every probability function we would have if we conditionalized P on some proposition already in the algebra -- the Equation must be preserved under updating on new information, not just satisfied by one fixed distribution.

(b) Let C abbreviate A > B. The Equation applied to P itself gives P(C) = P(B|A). Because the Equation is also assumed to hold for the conditionalized function P(·|B), we get P(C|B) = P(B|A&B), which equals 1 as long as P(A&B) > 0, since A&B entails B. Symmetrically, conditionalizing on ¬B gives P(C|¬B) = P(B|A&¬B) = 0, as long as P(A&¬B) > 0, since A&¬B rules out B. The law of total probability then gives P(C) = P(C|B)P(B) + P(C|¬B)P(¬B) = P(B). But the Equation already told us P(C) = P(B|A), so P(B|A) = P(B): A and B come out probabilistically independent. Since A and B were arbitrary propositions subject only to the mild conditions that P(A&B) and P(A&¬B) are both positive, and ordinary conditionals plainly relate propositions that are evidentially dependent on one another, the Equation cannot hold across the board.

(c) Lewis's moral is that the Equation is false as a general claim about the probabilities of propositions, but he does not give up on indicative 'if' having a truth-conditional reading. Since he is independently committed, via the similarity semantics he had already developed for counterfactuals, to giving 'if'-sentences truth conditions rather than treating them as mere expressions of high conditional credence, the natural move for him is to extend that same similarity-based, possible-worlds treatment to the indicative case as well, letting the tie to conditional probability fall out as a defeasible pragmatic effect rather than a semantic one. This is where he parts ways with Adams and, later, Edgington, who take the triviality result as a reason to deny that indicative conditionals are truth-bearers at all, and who build a semantics purely in terms of assertability tracking conditional probability."
