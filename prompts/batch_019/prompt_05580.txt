For my AP U.S. History research paper, I've been working through several of Frederick Douglass's speeches and letters on the Constitution, and what strikes me most is how completely his position reversed over the course of his life. Through the 1840s, as an ally of William Lloyd Garrison, he treated the Constitution as an irredeemably pro-slavery document that no genuine abolitionist could support in good conscience; but after his public break with Garrison in the early 1850s, he argued almost the opposite. In a widely discussed 1860 speech delivered in Glasgow on whether the Constitution was pro- or anti-slavery, he pointed out that the words "slave" and "slavery" never actually appear anywhere in the original document, that the framers used euphemisms like "persons held to service" instead, and that ambiguous clauses ought to be read in light of the Preamble's promise to "secure the Blessings of Liberty," not against it. A decade later, in his 1869 speech "Our Composite Nationality," he extended that same text-and-principle-driven idea of American belonging to a group he had no obvious personal stake in defending: Chinese immigrants, whom he championed against a rising tide of nativist calls for exclusion.

I'd like help pushing that reasoning toward a question Douglass never had to answer directly, since it only became a live legal dispute after his death in 1895 — first for a different immigrant group, in the Supreme Court's Wong Kim Ark decision three years later, and then much more contentiously in today's debates over unauthorized immigration: whether a child born on U.S. soil to parents without legal immigration status is automatically a citizen under the Fourteenth Amendment, given the disputed phrase "subject to the jurisdiction thereof." Working strictly from Douglass's own interpretive habits — his preference for the Constitution's plain text over inferred backroom compromises, and the inclusive theory of nationhood underlying "Our Composite Nationality" — what do you think he would most plausibly have argued, and how solid is that inference, really? My teacher mentioned that even historians disagree about how far his reasoning would actually travel, so I would rather hear where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, or cuts against my own guess, than receive a tidy answer that makes him sound more predictable than he probably was.
