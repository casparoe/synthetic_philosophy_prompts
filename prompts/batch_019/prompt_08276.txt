For my humanities elective, we just finished Book V of Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, where he tries to reconcile God's complete foreknowledge of everything we will ever do with the claim that we still choose freely. His central move is to argue that God is not really "foreseeing" anything, since God exists entirely outside time: eternity, for Boethius, is the whole and complete possession of life all at once, so God simply beholds every moment of history — past, present, and future — the way we behold something happening right in front of us, and just as my watching you cross a room does not force you to cross it, God's eternal "seeing" of my choice does not force me to make it. He backs this up with a second, more technical move: he distinguishes a thing's being necessary only in a conditional sense (necessary given that it is already known) from its being necessary in an absolute or simple sense, and argues that mere knowledge of an event, however certain, only ever imposes the first, weaker kind of necessity on it.

Separately, a friend who is really into decision theory mentioned Newcomb's Problem to me: an extremely reliable, though not literally infallible, Predictor scans your brain and, based on what it predicts you will do, either places $1,000,000 in an opaque Box B or leaves it empty; a transparent Box A always contains $1,000; you then choose between taking only Box B or taking both boxes, knowing that the Predictor's decision was locked in before you ever chose. It occurred to me that this is basically a secular, miniaturized version of Boethius's problem, except the "knower" is a finite scientist or machine sitting inside time with you rather than an eternal God outside it. So here is what I cannot work out on my own: does Boethius's solution actually depend on the predictor being eternal in his specific sense, so that a merely time-bound predictor — however accurate — would leave your choice genuinely necessitated after all, or does his separate argument about conditional versus simple necessity save your freedom even when the predictor is just a very good in-time forecaster? And once that is settled, if you sat Boethius down in front of the two boxes, do you think he would tell you to take one or two, or would he say the whole dilemma rests on a confusion he had already diagnosed centuries before Newcomb ever posed it?
