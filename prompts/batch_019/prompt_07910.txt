"Laudan pointed to a graveyard of once-successful theories -- phlogiston, caloric, and the crystalline spheres of pre-Copernican astronomy -- whose central terms we now think dont refer to anything real. If predictive success didn't stop those theories from being false, the realist's inference from 'our best theories are successful' to 'our best theories are approximately true' looks shaky, because success apparently isn't a reliable guide to truth after all.

Kitcher and Psillos respond with a divide-and-conquer move: not every part of a historical theory was actually doing the work that generated its successful predictions. Fresnel's theory said light was a wave in a literal elastic-solid ether, and that mechanical medium was really just idle scaffolding -- it wasn't what generated the correct predictions, like the famous bright spot behind a circular disc, which Arago predicted mathematically and Poisson then confirmed by experiment. What did the real work was the mathematical part of the theory, Fresnel's equations describing transverse wave motion. Since that part got carried forward into Maxwell's theory, realists should only ever have been committed to the working posits in the first place, so Laudan's list is full of idle posits that nobody serious was really committed to.

Worrall's structural realism is usually treated as a separate strategy because instead of sorting old theories into working and idle parts case by case after the fact, it makes one principled, general restriction: we should only believe in the structure or the mathematical relations a theory describes, and stay agnostic about the underlying entities. This is essentially Hacking's point that we should trust the electrons we can spray but not the theories about them -- Worrall just applies the same idea to light, so we can go on trusting the entities that Fresnel's and Maxwell's theories agree on, like the ether, without buying either theory's specific story about what light ultimately is. That's why structural realism counts as its own strategy and not just a special case of Kitcher and Psillos's move."
