I'm a junior on my high school's student council, and this spring a few of us are drafting a proposal to replace our plurality-vote system for electing the council president with ranked-choice (instant-runoff) voting. The push started because two years ago, in a three-candidate race, the eventual winner was widely regarded around school as the least popular of the three; an informal poll a friend ran afterward suggested that each of the other two candidates would have beaten the winner head-to-head, but because they split the "anti-winner" vote, a plurality was enough to carry the election. While researching for the proposal, I read a popular book on voting theory (I believe it was Poundstone's *Gaming the Vote*) that introduced me to Arrow's impossibility theorem, and I went on to read Arrow's own statement of the conditions involved — unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. Almost every online discussion I have found since treats "Arrow's theorem" as a blanket proof that no voting reform can ever be truly fair, but a few more careful sources mentioned a distinct result, the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem, concerning the impossibility of a non-dictatorial voting rule that is fully immune to strategic voting.

I would like help getting precise about how these two theorems actually relate, because I cannot tell whether people are using them interchangeably out of carelessness or because there is a genuine deep connection between them. Could you lay out exactly what each theorem assumes and establishes, being careful about the fact that Arrow's theorem concerns aggregating complete preference orderings into a single social ordering, whereas Gibbard-Satterthwaite concerns rules that just select one winner? I would then like you to address whether one theorem can be derived from the other, or from some shared underlying lemma, or whether they are genuinely independent results that merely happen to rely on a similar combinatorial argument involving a "decisive" coalition or a pivotal voter.

Finally, I would like to use your answer to stress-test our own proposal. Does adopting ranked-choice voting resolve the kind of problem Arrow's theorem is about — namely, some seemingly reasonable axiom such as independence of irrelevant alternatives being violated — or does it really only address the separate manipulability problem that Gibbard-Satterthwaite describes? If possible, please construct a small worked example, with three or four candidates and a few voter blocs, in which instant-runoff voting still violates independence of irrelevant alternatives, or fails to elect the Condorcet winner, even though it would have avoided the specific vote-splitting failure from our past election.
