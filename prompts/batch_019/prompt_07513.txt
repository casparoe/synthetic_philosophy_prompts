I fell down a reading rabbit hole recently: I was reading about effective altruism, followed a reference back to Peter Singer's drowning-child argument, and from there ended up, unexpectedly, in Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments — written two centuries before either of those existed. There's a passage where Smith imagines a "man of humanity" in Europe hearing that an earthquake has just destroyed the entire empire of China, killing a hundred million people he's never met. Smith says the man would sigh, muse a little about the effect on trade, and then go about his evening in perfect comfort — whereas losing his own little finger tomorrow would cost him a night's sleep. Smith then asks whether that same man, given the choice, would actually let the hundred million die to save the finger, and answers no — not because his sympathy for such distant strangers is strong (Smith insists it isn't), but because something else in him, which Smith calls the impartial spectator, recoils at the thought.

I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but that looks to me like a direct ancestor of Singer's argument. Except Smith's man is only being asked to refuse an active trade of catastrophe for comfort, not to sacrifice comfort in order to prevent one, and elsewhere in the same book Smith treats it as natural and proper that our real efforts stay concentrated on the people close to us. Arguing strictly from Smith's own psychology and his own stated principles, do you think he ends up with genuine grounds to resist Singer's further leap to a demanding duty of rescue, or does his own logic commit him to it?
