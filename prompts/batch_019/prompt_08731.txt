I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a good deal of time over the last year reading old books on art I picked up secondhand, and two of them have left me stuck on something. The first is Tolstoy's "What Is Art?," where he argues that a work only counts as genuine art if it "infects" its audience with a feeling the artist has actually lived through, and that the highest art is whatever feeling is universal enough to unite people across classes rather than flatter a narrow, cultivated taste. The second is Collingwood's "The Principles of Art," where he likewise insists that art is essentially about emotion rather than imitation, which made me expect him to more or less agree with Tolstoy. Instead he seems to treat Tolstoy's "infection" idea as an example of exactly the confusion he's trying to clear away — using a known technique to produce a predetermined feeling in an audience, which he lumps in with things like ceremonial or propaganda art rather than with what he calls genuine "expression." Could you compare the two theories for me and explain as precisely as you can why Collingwood doesn't count "infecting" people with a feeling as the same thing as "expressing" one? I'd also value your own judgment on whether that objection actually defeats Tolstoy's view, or whether Tolstoy has the resources to answer it.
