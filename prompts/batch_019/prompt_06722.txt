I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, mostly, thirty-some years in a university lab before I hung up the coat), and for the last year or so I've fallen hard into philosophy, especially the decision-theory and probability stuff since that's closest to territory I already knew something about. Someone from an old reading group of mine pointed me at a paper by Alan Hájek and Harris Nover, I think it was in Mind back in 2004, about something called the Pasadena paradox, and its been bugging me for weeks now.

Here's the setup as I understand it: you flip a fair coin until it first comes up heads, and if that happens on toss n you get paid (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n/n dollars. Since the chance of first-heads-on-toss-n is 1/2^n, the expected value works out to the sum of (-1)^(n+1)/n over all n -- the alternating harmonic series, which every calc student knows adds up to ln(2) if you sum it in the obvious order, toss 1 then toss 2 then toss 3 and so on. But the whole point of the paper, apparently, is that this game has no expected value whatsoever, not ln(2) and not even a divergent infinity like you get from the plain old St. Petersburg game, because the series is only conditionally convergent, so by Riemann's rearrangement theorem you can shuffle those exact same terms into any order you like and land on any sum you want, or blow it up to infinity, or make it wander forever without settling anywhere.

I don't doubt the math, I ran into rearrangement gotchas plenty of times doing perturbation theory back in the day. What I can't get my head around is why that fact should have any decision-theoretic bite at all. The tosses happen in a real temporal sequence, so why isn't summing in that "natural" order simply the correct way to compute the expectation, with every other reshuffling dismissed as a mathematical curiosity with no bearing on an actual gamble? I gather there's also some follow-up "Altadena" game meant to sharpen the same worry using a dominance argument instead, but I confess that part lost me. Could you help me build some real intuition for why the rearrangement fact is supposed to be philosophically decisive, and what it would even mean for an expected value to be "order-independent" in the relevant sense?
