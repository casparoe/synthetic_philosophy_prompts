Longtime readers of Shtetl-Optimized and the wider rationalist blogosphere will remember the running battle over Integrated Information Theory that culminated in the 2023 open letter branding the theory pseudoscience. I've noticed that critics tend to reach for whichever objection is closest at hand rather than asking which one actually threatens IIT's core claim — that a system's conscious experience is identical to, and quantified by, its maximal irreducible cause-effect structure (Φ) — so I'd like a more disciplined assessment.

Please rank the following six objections to IIT from most to least damaging to that core claim:

1. Aaronson's "unconscious expander" argument: circuits built from expander graphs, of the kind used in classical error-correcting codes, would be assigned enormous Φ despite doing nothing anyone would call conscious.
2. The unfolding argument: any recurrent causal structure can be unfolded into a feedforward network with identical input-output behavior but a different Φ, so no external, behavior-based test could ever adjudicate between the two.
3. The bare falsifiability charge pressed by the 2023 open letter itself.
4. The exclusion postulate's requirement that consciousness attach to exactly one maximally irreducible complex at exactly one spatiotemporal grain, which sits awkwardly with the overlapping, multi-scale way real physical systems actually decompose.
5. The intractability objection: exact Φ cannot be computed for any system larger than a few dozen elements, leaving the theory untestable on the very brains it purports to explain.
6. The "panpsychism by the back door" objection: IIT assigns nonzero Φ, and hence a flicker of experience, to simple feedback systems such as thermostats or photodiodes — a consequence some proponents embrace and critics treat as a reductio.
