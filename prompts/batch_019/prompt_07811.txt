I'm a software engineer with a side interest in decision theory, and I keep running into Douglas Hofstadter's notion of "superrationality" in AI-alignment-adjacent writing. The core argument runs: in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two agents who reason identically and know this about each other, neither can reach a conclusion the other doesn't also reach, so the only self-consistent outcomes are mutual cooperation or mutual defection; a self-interested agent then picks whichever of those two it prefers, i.e., cooperates. This gets generalized, informally, into the claim that any two sufficiently like-minded reasoners — two people who "think alike," or even two separately trained models — have a rational basis for cooperating, with no repeated play or enforcement mechanism required. I think this generalization smuggles in far more than it earns. In the formal program-equilibrium literature, getting a genuine, non-question-begging guarantee of mutual cooperation (e.g., via provability-logic constructions like FairBot, which cooperates only once it has proven that the other side cooperates) requires mutual source-code transparency and a shared formal proof system, not mere similarity of thinking style. Please argue that informal, everyday appeals to superrationality between merely like-minded — as opposed to mutually transparent — agents are not actually rationally warranted, engaging with the strongest objection a defender of the informal argument could raise.
