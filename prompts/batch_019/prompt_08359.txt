The question, worth 20 points, was:

"Explain the demandingness objection to consequentialism, using an example. Then present and evaluate ONE consequentialist strategy for responding to the objection. (300–400 words.)"

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The demandingness objection holds that consequentialist theories, because they require agents to do whatever will bring about the best available outcome, end up requiring far more self-sacrifice than ordinary morality expects of us. Peter Singer's shallow pond case makes the worry vivid: if you would ruin an expensive pair of shoes to pull a drowning child out of a pond, the same reasoning seems to show that you should give away most of your discretionary income to organizations that save lives efficiently, since that money will do far more good there than sitting in your bank account. Because there is always someone whose situation could be improved more by your money or time than your own life is improved by keeping it, strict maximizing consequentialism appears to demand that agents devote nearly everything to helping others, leaving little room for personal projects, relationships, or rest. It also seems to abolish the category of the supererogatory: acts that used to count as generous and optional become strictly obligatory.

One influential reply is Peter Railton's satisficing consequentialism. On this view, an act counts as morally right as long as it brings about an outcome that is good enough, rather than the single best outcome achievable, so agents are permitted to stop well short of the optimum provided they remain reliably disposed to give appropriate weight to others' good. Railton argues that the fully rational consequentialist need not be a single-minded pursuer of the good in every choice, and that building this kind of psychological slack into an agent's character is itself what a sensible consequentialism recommends, since agents who are constantly calculating and self-denying tend to produce worse outcomes over a lifetime than agents who are allowed some stability in their own commitments and relationships.

This looks like a genuinely successful reply. By relocating the demand from each individual act to the general shape of an agent's life and character, Railton's view shows that consequentialism, properly understood, was never really as demanding as the objection assumes; the shallow pond case only seems damning if we ignore the difference between a rare emergency and a standing policy."
