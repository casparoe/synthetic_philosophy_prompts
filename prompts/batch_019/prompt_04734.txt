In my philosophy elective, we just spent a week on Descartes, and I keep thinking about a piece of his argument our teacher never really unpacked: in Part V of the Discourse on the Method, and again in his letters to the Marquess of Newcastle and to Henry More, Descartes doesn't just assert what's sometimes called the bête-machine, or beast-machine, doctrine -- he gives two fairly precise tests for telling a genuine rational soul apart from a mere mechanism. The first is linguistic: a real thinking being can use words or signs flexibly, to respond appropriately to almost anything said in its presence, whereas even a "talking" animal like a parrot or a magpie is just triggering a fixed response to a particular cue. The second is behavioral versatility: a machine, or an animal, might be built to outperform any human at one or two tasks, but it could never act appropriately across the whole range of situations that life throws up, the way reason lets even a fairly dim-witted person do, because that takes understanding rather than a repertoire of dispositions. Descartes uses both tests to deny animals thought and an immortal soul, even while granting -- this part I'm less sure I have right -- that they are organized bodies capable of at least some sensation.

What I can't stop wondering is how Descartes would hold up this same two-part test against a handful of things he obviously couldn't have known about: Alex the African grey parrot, who learned to label objects, colors, shapes, and quantities and to answer novel questions about combinations of them rather than just repeating sounds; Betty the New Caledonian crow, who spontaneously bent a straight wire into a hook to fish a food bucket out of a tube she had never seen solved that way before; Kanzi the bonobo and Koko the gorilla, whose lexigram and sign-language use is still disputed as either genuine syntax or elaborate conditioning; elephants and magpies that pass the mirror self-recognition test; and the 2012 Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, in which a group of neuroscientists announced that the neural basis for conscious experience is present in birds, mammals, and even octopuses.

Could you go through those cases one at a time and tell me, using Descartes' own two criteria rather than a general "science has moved on" gesture, which ones he could plausibly wave away as sophisticated instinct or mechanism, and which ones -- if any -- would actually force him to give ground? I'm especially interested in whether the language test survives contact with Alex and the ape-language cases better or worse than the versatility test survives contact with Betty the crow, and in whether the Cambridge Declaration's claims about animal consciousness are even answering the question Descartes cared about, or a different question he would say is being conflated with it. My friends and I disagree about whether he would stubbornly hold the line or find some principled way to concede ground without giving up substance dualism altogether, so I would really appreciate an actual verdict rather than an "it's complicated" shrug.
