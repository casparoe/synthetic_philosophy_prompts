So my cousin and I inherited a small rental property jointly from our grandmother, and working out the buyout has turned into this drawn-out back-and-forth: at every stage, either of us could take a smaller guaranteed payout and end things right now, or push for one more round of "let's actually get it appraised properly first," which nets more money for both of us if we both stick with it, but leaves whoever proposes the extra round exposed if the other one just lowballs and bails at the next step instead. A friend of mine who's finishing an econ PhD took one look at how I was describing this and went "congrats, that's a centipede game, and the rational move is technically to defect on move one," which sent me down a rabbit hole on backward induction that I can't quite shake.

Here's the part that's actually bugging me and that I havent seen explained well anywhere: the argument for defecting immediately seems to require reasoning about what a rational player would do at, like, round 40 of the game -- but if everyone really is rational and everyone knows it, round 40 is never supposed to be reached in the first place. So how is "what a rational player does at round 40" even a coherent thing to reason about, if reaching round 40 already falsifies the assumption the whole argument depends on? I saw a reference to an actual back-and-forth between Aumann and Stalnaker over pretty much this exact issue -- something about whether "common knowledge of rationality" even makes sense once your asking counterfactually about branches that aren't supposed to happen -- but the papers themselves were way over my head. Could you actually walk me through what that disagreement comes down to, in plain language but not dumbed down, and tell me honestly whether anyone thinks this paradox is actually resolved at this point, or whether "just defect immediately" is still treated as the "rational" answer even though nobody can fully justify the reasoning that supposedly gets you there?
