Below is an exam question from a course on classical Indian philosophy, together with a student's answer.

Question: Nyāya epistemology recognizes four pramāṇas: pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda. (a) Give the five members (avayava) of a Nyāya inference, illustrating each with the standard smoke-and-fire example, and state what must hold of the reason (hetu) for the inference to count as sound. (b) Using the standard gavaya example, explain precisely what cognition upamāna is supposed to deliver, and why Nyāya treats it as a pramāṇa in its own right rather than as a special case of perception, inference, or testimony.

Student's answer: Nyāya's inference (anumāna) is built out of five members: the pratijñā, or thesis ("the hill has fire"); the hetu, or reason ("because it has smoke"); the udāharaṇa, an example embodying the general rule ("wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen"); the upanaya, which applies that rule to the case at hand ("this hill has smoke, which is invariably attended by fire"); and the nigamana, which restates the thesis as now established ("therefore, the hill has fire"). For the inference to be sound, the hetu must stand to the sādhya, the property to be proved, in a relation of vyāpti, or invariable concomitance: wherever there is smoke, there must be fire, without exception. Nyāya establishes this relation inductively, by observing a sufficient number of cases in which smoke and fire occur together and finding no case in which smoke occurs without fire; once enough such positive instances have accumulated and no counter-instance has turned up, the generalization is taken to be secured. This is also why Naiyāyikas devote so much attention to cataloguing the hetvābhāsas, the various ways a proposed reason can fail to meet this standard.

Upamāna is introduced through the case of a forest-dweller who has never seen a gavaya. Told by a forester that "a gavaya is an animal like a cow," he later comes across an animal of that description in the forest and, recalling the forester's remark, recognizes for the first time that the animal before him is a gavaya. This piece of knowledge cannot be credited to perception, since he has never perceived anything under the description "gavaya" before and so has no perceptual concept ready to apply; it cannot be credited to inference, since there is no general rule from which the conclusion is deduced; and it cannot be credited to testimony by itself, since the forester's remark named no particular animal. Because the recognition depends on noticing a similarity and draws on memory, perception, and testimony together without being fully reducible to any one of them, Nyāya concludes that comparison must be admitted as a fourth, independent pramāṇa.
