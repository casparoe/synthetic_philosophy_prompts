Q4. Molinism claims to secure both libertarian human freedom and meticulous divine providence via "middle knowledge."
(a) [8 pts] Explain where middle knowledge fits among God's three logical moments of knowledge, and what a counterfactual of creaturely freedom is.
(b) [7 pts] State the grounding objection to middle knowledge in your own words, noting who's generally credited with pressing it and who Molina himself was originally arguing against.
(c) [5 pts] Molina's own proposed answer to the truthmaker problem appeals to what's often called "supercomprehension." Explain the idea and say whether you think it succeeds.

Student's answer:

Molinism divides God's knowledge into three logical moments. First, natural knowledge covers all necessary truths, including every possible world God could create. Second, middle knowledge covers all the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: for any possible free creature C in any possible circumstance S, it tells God what C would freely do if placed in S. This is contingent (things could've gone the other way) but still logically prior to God's free decision about which world to actualize — that's what lets God pick a world where creatures freely do what he wants without forcing them. Third, after the decree, free knowledge covers what will actually happen, now fixed because God has picked one world out of all the feasible ones. Molina worked this whole apparatus out in the sixteenth century specifically to answer Calvin's doctrine of unconditional predestination, since Calvinists said that if God foreordains everything, humans can't really be free, and Molina wanted God to be fully in control of history while creatures stay genuinely free.

The classic problem is the grounding objection, first pressed by Robert Adams. Counterfactuals of freedom are supposed to be contingently true, but if I would freely do X in circumstance S, what fixes that as true rather than the opposite? Nothing about S necessitates it, or my choice wouldn't be free — and since God already knows via middle knowledge exactly what I'd do before I'm even created, it's hard to see how I'm genuinely able to do otherwise once the circumstance actually arrives. So the counterfactuals seem to lack a truthmaker, and my freedom looks fixed in advance regardless.

Molina's answer is that God's knowledge of these counterfactuals is grounded in his "supercomprehension" of each possible creature's nature: God understands a possible free creature so perfectly that he just sees, from that nature alone, what it would do in any circumstance — similar to how someone who understood you completely might predict your choices. I don't think this works. If the creature's nature doesn't necessitate the choice (and it can't, or the choice wouldn't be free), then no amount of comprehending the nature, however perfectly, should tell you which way an undetermined choice would go. Supercomprehension just relabels the problem instead of solving it.
