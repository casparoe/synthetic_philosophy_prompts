I'm writing a piece on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and the historical starting point that keeps coming up in the secondary literature is a dense passage from Kant's general introduction to *The Metaphysics of Morals* (1797). Here it is in William Hastie's 1887 translation, where the Latin tags make it read more like a legal brief than a work of philosophy:

"A collision of duties or obligations (collisio officiorum s. obligationum) would be the result of such a relation between them that the one would annul the other, in whole or in part. Duty and obligation, however, are conceptions which express the objective practical necessity of certain actions, and two opposite rules cannot be objective and necessary at the same time; for if it is a duty to act according to one of them, it is not only no duty to act according to an opposite rule, but to do so would even be contrary to duty. Hence a collision of duties and obligations is entirely inconceivable (obligationes non colliduntur). There may, however, be two grounds of obligation (rationes obligandi), connected with an individual under a rule prescribed for himself, and yet neither the one nor the other may be sufficient to constitute an actual obligation (rationes obligandi non obligantes); and in that case the one of them is not a duty. If two such grounds of obligation are actually in collision with each other, practical philosophy does not say that the stronger obligation is to keep the upper hand (fortior obligatio vincit), but that the stronger ground of obligation is to maintain its place (fortior obligandi ratio vincit)."

What I can't work out is what, exactly, is supposed to survive on the losing side of one of these collisions. Kant insists duties themselves never conflict, only the "grounds" that would generate them, and once the stronger ground wins, acting on the other rule is "contrary to duty," not merely regrettable. Does his apparatus leave any room for something like what later writers call moral residue — a lingering obligation to apologize, compensate, or feel remorse toward whichever claim lost out — or is the defeated ground meant to disappear without a trace once the "stronger ground" has done its work? And more basically, is the distinction between an obligation and a mere ground of obligation doing real philosophical work here, or is it essentially a redescription that lets Kant deny dilemmas by definitional fiat while leaving the underlying practical tension exactly where defenders of real dilemmas locate it?
