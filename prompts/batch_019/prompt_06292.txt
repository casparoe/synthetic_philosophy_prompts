I am preparing a graduate seminar on eighteenth-century political philosophy, and this week's session on Book IV of Smith's *Wealth of Nations* — particularly the long chapter "Of Colonies" — produced a sharper disagreement among my students than I had anticipated, one that a colleague and I have kept arguing about by email ever since. Several students were struck by how unsparing Smith is toward the mercantile system: his argument that monopoly trading companies and restricted colonial markets chiefly enrich a narrow class of merchants and manufacturers while imposing real costs, in taxation and foregone trade, on the broader public of the mother country, and his blunt remarks on the violence European powers visited on the native inhabitants of the Americas and, especially, the East Indies. Other students pointed to the closing pages of that same chapter, written amid the crisis with the American colonies, where Smith does not simply call for independence but weighs it against a scheme of full incorporating union, with American representation in Parliament in proportion to taxation, and seems to worry less that empire was wrong to want than that Britain's rulers had let themselves indulge in a dream of empire disproportionate to the nation's real resources.

I would like you to write an essay that settles this disagreement, or at least argues persuasively for one side of it. The question is whether Smith's treatment of colonies amounts to a principled critique of empire as such, or only to a critique of the "mercantile system" — exclusive companies, trade monopolies, and the fiscal-military apparatus needed to sustain them — which would leave room for him to endorse some reformed, more voluntary and commercially integrated union between Britain and its colonies. In building your case, engage with the specifics: his analysis of who actually profits from colonial monopoly and who pays for it; his comments on the treatment of indigenous peoples under European rule; the concrete remedies he proposes for the American crisis and his own assessment of their political feasibility; and how his four-stages account of societal development, and his ranking of societies within it, bears on whether his position can fairly be called anti-imperialist in any thoroughgoing sense. Please don't hedge your way to a conclusion that the matter is simply ambiguous: weigh the textual evidence on both sides and defend a specific, arguable verdict about how Smith's stance is best characterized.
