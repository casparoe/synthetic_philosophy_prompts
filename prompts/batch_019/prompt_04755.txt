I've been reporting a piece on how philosophers who died just before the recent explosion of animal-cognition research might have reacted to it, and Robert Nozick keeps coming back to mind. In a footnote in "Anarchy, State, and Utopia," he breaks with the tidy picture you'd expect from a rights-based libertarian and takes animal suffering seriously on its own terms, openly wondering whether something as trivial as the pleasure of taste could really outweigh the pain involved in producing a piece of meat. He sketches a split framework that commentators have since glossed as roughly "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" — aggregative cost-benefit reasoning for creatures without rights, his own side-constraint-based rights for persons — but he admits he has no principled way to say exactly where the line between the two groups falls, especially once you bring in humans who lack whatever capacities are doing the work of justifying it.

What revived this for me is the fight over octopus farming, which only really took off two decades after Nozick's death. A Spanish seafood company's plan to open the world's first commercial octopus farm drew sustained pushback from cognitive scientists pointing to octopuses' problem-solving, apparent tool use, and other signs of a complex inner life, and the backlash helped push states like California and Washington to ban octopus farming outright, around the same time the UK moved to legally recognize cephalopods as sentient beings. It's almost a designed test case for the line Nozick's footnote left dangling: an animal that is nothing like a human, but also nothing like the cows and chickens his libertarian contemporaries mostly had in mind.

I'd like you to reconstruct, as rigorously as you can from Nozick's actual arguments rather than generic animal-ethics talking points, how he would likely have worked through this case. Does the octopus's apparent sophistication give it a real claim to something closer to his side-constraint treatment, or would he have held the line that only capacities much closer to full personhood — language, long-term planning, moral agency — qualify? And however he'd have resolved that, how do you think he'd have reconciled it with his broader hostility to state paternalism: would he have welcomed the farming bans as the state finally taking a genuine moral cost seriously, or would he have viewed them as exactly the sort of coercive overreach his politics existed to oppose?
