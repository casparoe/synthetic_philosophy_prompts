Newton's bucket argument (from the Scholium to the Principia) is supposed to show that rotation, and therefore space itself, is absolute rather than merely relative to other bodies. In the experiment, a bucket of water is set spinning: at first the bucket moves relative to the water but the water's surface stays flat, and later the water spins along with the bucket, so there's no more relative motion between them, but now the surface is concave, climbing up the sides. Since the concavity doesn't track the relative motion between the water and the bucket, Newton concludes that the water's true rotation must be motion relative to space itself, not relative to any other body. A nice companion piece is Mach's two-globes case: two globes connected by a cord, spinning in an otherwise empty universe, would still show tension in the cord, revealing true rotation even though there's nothing else around for them to be rotating relative to.

Leibniz's response was relational: he denied that space is a substance over and above bodies and their relations, arguing via the principle of sufficient reason that if space were absolute, God would have had no reason to create the material universe in one location or orientation rather than a uniformly shifted or rotated version of the same universe, since the two would be completely indistinguishable from each other. Since God always acts for a reason, there's no fact of the matter about which "version" was created, so space can't be an independent substance in the first place. Mach pushed this further and argued that what really explains the water's concavity or the cord's tension isn't absolute space but rotation relative to the average distribution of all the other matter in the universe, the "fixed stars" — so inertial effects are really just interactions with distant matter, an idea that later inspired Einstein while he was building general relativity.

The hole argument (Earman and Norton) tries to show that manifold substantivalism, the view that spacetime points exist as a manifold independently of the metric and matter, leads to an unacceptable indeterminism once you take general covariance seriously. Because Einstein's equations are invariant under diffeomorphisms, you can take a solution and apply a "hole diffeomorphism" that's the identity everywhere except inside some small region H, producing a distinct mathematical solution that agrees perfectly with the original everywhere outside H. If spacetime points have their identities independently of the metric, these count as two distinct physical possibilities that agree on everything up to the boundary of H but disagree inside it, so even total knowledge of the state outside H fails to determine what happens inside — a radical failure of determinism that seems too cheap to be real. Sophisticated substantivalism tries to block this by denying haecceitism about spacetime points: it says the two diffeomorphic models don't represent distinct possibilities at all, just the same possibility described in two different ways, since the points have no "thisness" over and above the qualitative role they play in the model. But once you give up the idea that the points are anything over and above the properties and relations instantiated at them, there's no real difference left between the substantivalist and the relationist — sophisticated substantivalism is really just relationism dressed up in substantivalist language.
