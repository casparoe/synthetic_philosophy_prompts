In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception, we have been grappling with a methodological worry pressed most forcefully by Firestone and Scholl's 2016 critique of "top-down" effects: many textbook cases of cognitive penetration fail to distinguish a genuine alteration of perceptual phenomenology from mundane confounds such as shifts of attention, post-perceptual judgment, contamination by memory, response or criterion bias, and the slide between "seeing" and merely "seeing as." With that worry in view, I'd like you to rank the following seven classic cases from most to least convincing as evidence that a cognitive state (a belief, desire, emotion, or piece of stored knowledge) alters the content of perceptual experience itself, rather than something downstream of it.

1. Delk and Fillenbaum: cardboard cutouts shaped like a heart or an apple are judged redder than a neutral shape presented in the identical physical hue.
2. Bruner and Goodman: poor children overestimate the size of coins more than wealthier children do, and more than either group overestimates same-sized plain discs.
3. Hansen, Olkkonen, Walter, and Gegenfurtner: an achromatic, banana-shaped patch looks faintly yellow, apparently owing to the "memory color" of bananas.
4. Bhalla and Proffitt: hills look steeper to perceivers who are fatigued, elderly, unfit, or burdened with a heavy backpack than to perceivers who are not.
5. Balcetis and Dunning: an ambiguous figure is resolved more often in favor of whichever interpretation is paired with a desirable outcome for the viewer.
6. Siegel's "Angry Jill": coming to believe, on independent grounds, that Jill is angry makes her otherwise neutral expression visually seem angry.
7. Perceptual-expertise cases (wine tasters, radiologists): experts report simply seeing a feature that untrained novices cannot see at all, rather than judging more accurately on the basis of an identical experience.
