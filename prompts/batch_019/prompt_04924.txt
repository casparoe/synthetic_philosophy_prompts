I sit on the family-and-ethics advisory board for the Catholic memory-care home where my mother lived until she died. Our aides have long practiced a kind of gentle "redirection" with residents in advanced dementia -- telling a woman who asks for her deceased husband every morning that "he's at the shop, he'll be back later," rather than re-delivering news she cannot retain but will grieve freshly each time. Our diocesan moral-theology consultant objects to making this official policy, invoking Augustine's two treatises on lying, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium. He argues, first, that any assertion contrary to what is in one's own mind is a lie and is always wrong regardless of motive; second, that even Augustine's mildest category of lie -- one that helps someone and harms no one -- still counts as sin, just the least grave kind; and third, that in Augustine's own discussion of protecting someone from people who mean to harm them, the answer is that you may hide, stay silent, or answer evasively, but never assert something false. Several aides and family members think that distinction is cold comfort to a woman whose whole reality is dissolving, and that withholding a kind untruth from her to spare a staff member's conscience is its own kind of cruelty. The board votes on a written policy in three weeks, and I've been asked to draft the recommendation memo. Please write it: settle on one specific, teachable policy for what staff may say in these moments, give a timeline for training and review, and take whichever side you don't adopt seriously, engaging its strongest version rather than dismissing it as legalism or sentiment.
