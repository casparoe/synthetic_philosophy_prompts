I'm a science/health reporter, but for the past year I've also been the "community member" on my hospital system's clinical ethics committee (I did a philosophy minor forever ago, which is probably why my name came up when they wanted a non-clinician in the room). There's a case on the agenda for next month that's been rattling around in my head all week. A local startup has spent the last year running a small pilot of a VR headset system, they call it "Amber," with palliative care patients who are cognitively intact and give explicit consent: it builds a fully convincing simulated environment out of old photos, voice recordings and family interviews, so a dying patient can sit in a "living room" that's actually decades gone, or talk with a deceased spouse who responds more or less like they used to. Self-reported mood and quality-of-life scores are way up, families are moved, staff love it — and now the company and several families are pushing to expand the program into the memory-care unit for dementia patients. One daughter told our palliative care lead flat out that she didn't care whether it was "real," her father hadn't smiled like that in a year. But the palliative lead herself won't sign off: her objection is that a dementia patient can't retain, from one session to the next, the fact that its a simulation, so there's no meaningful informed consent to the fiction — we'd basically be institutionalizing a really convincing lie and billing it as care. Hospital policy on experimental non-drug therapies only requires that they be "consistent with the patient's known wishes," which nobody ever anticipated having to apply to something like this. The company's rep, for his part, says this isn't Nozick's sealed tank: nobody's giving up real relationships forever, the headset comes off, ordinary life resumes, so the classic worry about loosing contact with reality doesn't obviously carry over to something reversible and session-based. Where I keep getting stuck is that "patients report being happier" might not even be the right thing to be measuring here, and I suspect the committee's going to treat that number as settling the question when really it's just restating one side of it.

Could you draft the memo I'd circulate to the committee before that meeting? I want an actual recommendation in it — expand to the memory-care unit, expand only under specific conditions, hold off pending something concrete, whatever you think is defensible — with a rough timeframe attached, and I want it to seriously engage with the "this isn't the all-or-nothing thought experiment, so the usual objections don't transfer" argument rather than just invoking Nozick's name and moving past it. If it helps, get into the weeds on what's actually doing the work in the well-being question here — whether felt experience is enough on its own or whether something more demanding is required — because I don't think the committee has actually sat with that part yet.
