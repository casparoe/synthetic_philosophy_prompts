I'm a senior in high school working on an independent-study project for my philosophy elective that combines my two biggest interests, philosophy of mind and machine learning, and my teacher has let me spend the last few weeks reading through some of the public write-ups on mechanistic interpretability -- the material on superposition and sparse autoencoders -- alongside a few classic texts on what it takes for something to count as a "representation." I keep running into a disagreement that neither literature really settles on its own, and I think I would understand it far better if I could see it argued out loud between two people who actually hold the opposing views, rather than just reading each side's position stated in isolation.

On one side is a view I've started calling, in my notes, "interpretability realism" (I'm not sure if there's an official term for it): when a sparse autoencoder finds a direction in a model's activations that fires across many different contexts sharing some recognizable theme, and clamping that direction up or down changes the model's output in exactly the way you'd predict if the direction "meant" that theme -- the way Anthropic's demo model started bringing up the Golden Gate Bridge in almost every reply once that feature was turned up -- that looks like genuine evidence the network represents the concept, on more or less the same grounds we believe in unobservable things like electrons because positing them lets us predict and manipulate the world. On the other side is a more deflationary view: increasing the autoencoder's dictionary size keeps causing a given feature to split into finer and finer sub-features, which suggests there is no privileged level at which "the" representations live, only levels that are more or less useful for a given purpose; and even setting that worry aside, being a causal lever that reliably changes the output doesn't obviously add up to representation in the fuller sense philosophers usually intend -- a car's throttle position is a causal lever on the engine's behavior too, and nobody wants to say the throttle "represents" how fast the driver wants to go.

Could you write me a dialogue between a defender of each view? I'd like it to read like two people who respect each other's competence -- maybe two friends from the same reading group, or two researchers on a panel -- genuinely trying to change each other's minds, so that each of them has to respond to the specific point the other just made rather than simply restating their opening position. In particular, I'd like the realist to have a substantive answer to the dictionary-size/feature-splitting worry, and the skeptic to have a substantive answer to the clamping evidence, and I'd like the dialogue to end without it being obvious which side is supposed to have won.
