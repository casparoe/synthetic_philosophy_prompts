This semester I'm taking my school's philosophy elective, and we're in the unit on social contract theory. Our teacher only assigned the "greatest hits" from Hobbes's Leviathan -- the chapter on the state of nature and the one where the sovereign gets instituted -- but I got curious and kept reading on my own. I noticed Hobbes keeps coming back to geometry as though it's the one subject that got knowledge completely right, and I found two passages that seem to explain this in different, maybe even conflicting, ways. When I asked my teacher about it, she said it was a good catch but we needed to move on to Locke, so I'm hoping you can help me actually sort it out.

The first passage is from Chapter IV, "Of Speech":

"Seeing then that truth consisteth in the right ordering of names in our affirmations, a man that seeketh precise truth had need to remember what every name he uses stands for, and to place it accordingly; or else he will find himself entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twigs, the more he struggles the more belimed. And therefore in geometry, which is the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind, men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations, they call definitions, and place them in the beginning of their reckoning."

The second is from Chapter XI, "Of the Difference of Manners":

"For the doctrine of right and wrong is perpetually disputed, both by the pen and the sword; whereas the doctrine of lines and figures is not so, because men care not, in that subject, what be truth, as a thing that crosses no man's ambition, profit, or lust. For I doubt not but if it had been a thing contrary to any man's right of dominion, or to the interest of men that have dominion, that the three angles of a triangle should be equal to two angles of a square, that doctrine should have been, if not disputed, yet by the burning of all books of geometry, suppressed, as far as he whom it concerned was able."

Here's what I can't untangle. The first passage makes geometry's certainty sound like a matter of method: it starts from careful, explicitly agreed-upon definitions, and any field could in principle do the same. The second makes it sound like geometry is undisputed only because nobody's power or profit happens to hang on the answer, which has nothing to do with method and would imply that even a sloppily defined claim could become "geometry-like" simply by being harmless to everyone's interests. So first, are these two explanations for geometry's certainty actually compatible, or is Hobbes running together two different theories without noticing? Second, I also read that Hobbes spent years insisting he had squared the circle and kept getting publicly and rather embarrassingly refuted by the mathematician John Wallis; on the "method" story that shouldn't have been able to happen to someone using geometry's own tools, so does that whole episode count as evidence for the second, interest-based explanation instead? Third, since Leviathan itself proceeds by defining words like "person," "right," and "commonwealth" as carefully as Euclid defines "triangle," is Hobbes implicitly betting that political science can win geometry's certainty through that same method -- and if the second passage is the correct account of why geometry is certain, wouldn't that bet already be lost, given that politics obviously does cross people's ambition and profit? I'd like you to work through these three questions in order rather than giving me a general overview of Hobbes on geometry.
