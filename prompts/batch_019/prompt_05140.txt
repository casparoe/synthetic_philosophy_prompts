I work as a software engineer, and I've become somewhat preoccupied with how casually people in my field throw around the phrase "paradigm shift" -- the move from monoliths to microservices, from on-prem infrastructure to the cloud, and, most recently, from rule-based/symbolic AI to deep learning, all get described that way in blog posts and conference talks. Curious where the phrase actually came from, I read Kuhn's "The Structure of Scientific Revolutions" over a couple of long flights, and I now suspect the tech-industry usage has drifted quite far from what Kuhn was actually arguing, especially around incommensurability: his claim that successive paradigms aren't just competing theories but involve changed standards for what counts as a good explanation, and sometimes even changed meanings for the terms both sides think they share.

I'd like you to compare Kuhn's actual, technical notion of a paradigm shift and incommensurability with the much looser sense in which "paradigm shift" gets used in tech and business writing. Concretely: is the popular usage basically Kuhn's idea just applied to smaller, more frequent changes, or is it missing something conceptually essential to what Kuhn meant -- for instance, the discontinuity in shared standards of evaluation, the change in meaning of key terms across the divide, or his claim that there's no paradigm-independent way to adjudicate between the old view and the new one? Then, applying whatever criteria you settle on, I'd like your actual verdict on a case I keep going back and forth on: does the shift from symbolic, rule-based AI to deep learning genuinely satisfy Kuhn's criteria for a paradigm shift involving real incommensurability, or is it better described as a major technological change that happens to get called one? Please commit to a definite answer and defend it rather than hedging down the middle.
