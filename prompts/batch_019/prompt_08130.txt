A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about what Newton's bucket experiment actually establishes. I took the concave shape of the spinning water as evidence that a body's true state of motion cannot be reduced to its motion relative to other particular bodies, which pushes us toward substantivalism about space. They agreed that relative motion to the bucket, the room, or the earth cannot do the explanatory work, but denied that this forces substantivalism on us; they first invoked Leibniz's objections to Newton, then, when I noted that those concern whether the whole universe could have been shifted to a different place in space rather than whether rotation can be relativized, switched to Mach's proposal that inertial effects depend on the overall distribution of matter in the universe. We could not agree on whether that last move counts as a genuinely relationist alternative or is simply a substantival fact wearing a disguise. Could you compare substantivalism and relationism specifically as responses to the bucket, rather than to Newton's other arguments for absolute space, and assess whether Leibniz's actual objections, or Mach's proposal, really accounts for the water's behavior without covertly reintroducing something space-like?
