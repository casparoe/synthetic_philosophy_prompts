I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about liberal justifications for border control, and Michael Dummett has become one of my central case studies. Over the last month I've been working through *On Immigration and Refugees* (2001) alongside some of the pamphlets and articles he produced out of his decades of activism with the Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants.

What I find hard to pin down is how far his framework is meant to generalize. He never denies that a state has some prima facie right to control immigration, yet almost everything he actually says about British practice is devoted to showing how that supposedly neutral right was exercised in bad faith: his discussion of the 1971 Immigration Act's "patriality" rule, which granted right of abode to Commonwealth citizens with a UK-born parent or grandparent while excluding others with equally deep Commonwealth ties, reads as an argument that a facially colour-blind legal category was constructed to produce a racially selective result. His treatment of refugees, by contrast, is far less hedged, coming close to treating the duty to admit people fleeing persecution as overriding the ordinary discretion he grants states elsewhere. I raised this in my department's reading group last week, and we ended up disagreeing about whether these are really two applications of one consistent principle or just two separate intuitions he never fully reconciled.

Dummett died in December 2011, before a run of developments that seem almost tailor-made to test this framework: the "hostile environment" policy rolled out in Britain from 2012, the 2018 Windrush scandal in which long-settled Commonwealth-born residents were detained, denied services, or threatened with deportation partly because the Home Office had itself destroyed the landing-card records that would have proven their status, the ending of EU free movement after Brexit in favour of a nominally nationality-blind points-based system, and the continuing fight over Channel small-boat crossings and offshore removal schemes such as the Rwanda plan. Reconstructing his actual arguments as closely as you can, rather than the reaction a generically sympathetic liberal philosopher might have, how do you think Dummett would have judged each of these episodes? I'm especially curious whether he would have read Windrush as simply the same danger he diagnosed in the 1970s recurring in administrative disguise, and whether a formally equal, nationality-blind system would have struck him as genuine progress on the discrimination he spent his career opposing, or as a subtler way of reproducing it.
