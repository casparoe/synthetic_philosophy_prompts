My grandpa passed away about a month ago and it's brought up some stuff in the family I wasnt expecting. He had a big cardiac arrest, spent a few days on a ventilator, and once it was clear he wasn't coming back we agreed to withdraw support. He'd always been signed up as an organ donor, and because he wasn't brain dead (the transplant coordinator was pretty upfront that he didn't meet the criteria), they did what's apparently called "donation after circulatory death" instead — pulled the vent, waited for his heart to stop, waited about five more minutes, then called it and took him to the OR for the kidneys and liver.

At the reception after the funeral my aunt and uncle got into it over this, and it's still going in the family group chat. Roughly:

(1) My aunt said five minutes isn't long enough — she'd read that hearts have restarted on their own after longer gaps than that — and that it feels like they took his organs before he was actually gone.
(2) My uncle said that's not the point: nobody was ever going to shock him or start CPR no matter what happened, we'd already agreed to that, so once his heart stopped it was never coming back — and that's all "permanent" has to mean.
(3) She said that's a little too convenient: if "permanent" just means "we decided not to intervene," then he didn't become dead because of anything happening in his body, he became dead because of a decision made in a waiting room, which seems backwards.
(4) He said she didn't have this reaction two years ago when Grandma was declared brain dead, and that's also just doctors agreeing a line's been crossed after running some tests, so why does a decision disqualify it now and not then.
(5) She said those aren't the same kind of "gone" — with Grandma the whole brain, brainstem included, was confirmed destroyed for good; with Grandpa the coordinator told us straight out he didn't meet that bar, he just wasn't going to get better.
(6) He said fine, but by that logic every circulatory-death donation anywhere is premature, since none of those patients are brain dead by definition, so is she saying the whole practice shouldn't happen, or just that this one felt too fast?

Neither of them has backed down. Can you go through this turn by turn and say who's actually got the better argument at each point, not just who "wins" overall — and call out anything you think they're both missing?
