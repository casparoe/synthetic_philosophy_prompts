I'm one of two student representatives on my high school's Commencement Committee, which also includes the principal, two teachers, and a member of the alumni association. This year's leading candidate for graduation speaker is an alum: a retired general who, as a young officer, ordered an airstrike on an insurgent logistics hub that he knew, at that hour, also served as a crowded market. He has always defended the choice on the grounds that the shock of the civilian toll broke local support for the insurgency and ended the war years earlier than continued fighting would have. In my ethics elective, we just read Elizabeth Anscombe's pamphlet opposing Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree: she argued that deliberately killing civilians to force a surrender is murder no matter how the arithmetic of lives saved comes out, and that this differs entirely from merely foreseeing civilian deaths as a side effect of hitting a legitimate target. Some committee members find that distinction decisive against inviting him; others think it's an impossible standard to hold a wartime commander to, and that declining on those grounds would just be moralizing with hindsight. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to our next meeting, recommending whether we extend the invitation, decline, or find some middle path, and make sure it takes the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than just dismissing it.
