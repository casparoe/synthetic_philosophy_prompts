In my AP Government class, we spent a week on Article V, and my teacher justified how hard the Constitution is to amend with the Ulysses story: the framers supposedly did to future majorities what Odysseus did to himself before sailing past the Sirens, tying them down in advance so a later, tempted version of "us" couldn't undo what a clearer-headed version had decided was essential. I found this convincing until I looked into which parts of the Constitution are actually unamendable and discovered that Article V itself permanently protects one clause: no state can be deprived of its equal vote in the Senate without its own consent, no matter how many other people later decide that's unfair. Digging further into what I gather is called "precommitment" in political theory (there's apparently a whole book on it by Jon Elster), I found that Germany's postwar constitution goes even further, permanently forbidding any amendment to human dignity and a few other core principles, precisely because Weimar's constitution had no such lock and could be dismantled through amendments that were technically legal.

What's been bothering me since is a letter Thomas Jefferson wrote to James Madison arguing, in effect, that the earth belongs to the living rather than the dead, and that no generation has the right to bind the next with laws or constitutions lasting longer than a single lifespan, which he calculated at about nineteen years. That cuts right against the Ulysses comparison: Odysseus binds a version of himself he will fully become again once the danger passes, with his own advance consent and his own later relief, while an entrenched constitutional clause binds people who were not yet born when it was written and never agreed to anything. On the other hand, I've also seen it argued that the mast did not just restrain Odysseus but enabled him to do something otherwise impossible, hear the Sirens and live, and that constitutional constraints like independent courts or regular elections might work the same way, making democracy possible rather than simply limiting it. I cannot tell whether that rescues the analogy or just relocates the problem.

So instead of a general gesture at "the analogy is imperfect," I would like you to work through the comparison in real detail: in which specific respects does constitutional precommitment, such as an entrenched or practically unamendable clause, genuinely function like Ulysses binding himself, and in which specific respects is it doing something different in kind, particularly regarding the same-agent-and-consent feature and the enabling-versus-restricting distinction? Please close with your own verdict on whether that difference should make us more suspicious of hard-to-amend constitutional provisions than the Sirens story usually implies, rather than settling for "both sides have a point."
