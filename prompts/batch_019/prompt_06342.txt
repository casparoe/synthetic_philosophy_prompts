In what is often called Parfit's Hitchhiker case, a traveler dying in the desert can be rescued only by a driver who refuses to set off unless he is already confident that she will pay a promised fee once they reach town — at which point, with the ride secured and no further dealings between them, paying seems to do her no good at all. Write an essay that takes and defends a position on the following question: does this case establish that genuine rationality can require being disposed to perform an act that remains, considered on its own, irrational, so that something worth calling "rational irrationality" is a real phenomenon? Or does it instead show that assessing the payment in isolation is simply the wrong standard of rational choice — so that, on a McClennen-style resolute-choice criterion, a Gauthier-style disposition-based criterion, or some non-causal decision rule, paying is the rational act outright, with no irrationality left to explain away? Engage directly with causal decision theory's case for holding that paying fails to maximize expected utility at the moment of choice, and say explicitly whether you take this dispute to be, at bottom, the same disagreement that divides one-boxers from two-boxers over Newcomb's Problem, or whether the two cases actually pull apart, and why. A qualified verdict (for instance, one that distinguishes cases by how reliable the driver's foresight is) is acceptable; simply cataloguing the positions without adjudicating between them is not.
