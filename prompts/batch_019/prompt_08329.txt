The question, worth 20 points, was: "State the Condorcet Jury Theorem and the conditions under which it holds. Then explain what the theorem is and is not entitled to tell us about the wisdom of enlarging real deliberative bodies — juries, committees, electorates — engaging with at least one serious objection to its relevance for actual institutions."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"The Condorcet Jury Theorem describes a group of voters facing a single binary choice — for instance, whether a defendant is guilty or not guilty — where each voter judges correctly with some fixed probability p, and does so independently of every other voter. If p is greater than one-half for each voter, then as the size of the group increases, the probability that a majority of the group reaches the correct verdict rises and tends toward one; if p is less than one-half, the same logic works in reverse, and enlarging the group drives the probability of a correct majority toward zero instead. This is part of why the theorem has been so attractive to epistemic democrats such as List and Goodin, who have used it to argue that, given the right conditions, large and inclusive bodies can be better than small expert ones at tracking the truth.

Of the theorem's conditions, independence is doing most of the work, and it is meant to guarantee that each juror reaches a verdict impartially: on the merits of the case, rather than being swayed by how others in the room are already leaning or by some personal stake in the result. This condition is hard to secure outside the mathematics. Jurors who deliberate before voting, who share a social background, or who have been exposed to the same pretrial publicity, will tend to make correlated rather than independent errors, and once errors are correlated in this way the theorem's reassuring convergence result no longer applies — a large committee that is biased in the same direction is no more reliable than one such voter alone, and can be less so.

A further complication comes from Condorcet's own work on majority rule more broadly. He showed that when a group chooses among three or more options by pairwise majority votes, the resulting collective ranking can cycle, preferring A to B and B to C and yet also C to A, even when each voter's individual preferences are entirely consistent. This voting paradox counsels real caution about treating the Jury Theorem's convergence result as a general vindication of enlarging deliberative bodies: the very majoritarian machinery that tracks the truth so elegantly in the two-option case can, once a body faces more than two options, fail to settle on any stable verdict at all, which is a poor advertisement for simply making committees or electorates bigger.

Taken together, then, the theorem supports a narrow and conditional argument for epistemic democracy — a good case for larger juries only where competence and independence can actually be assumed — rather than a general brief for the wisdom of crowds."
