I'm a PhD student in economics focusing on mechanism design, and outside my program I sit on the five-person governance council of Alder Row, a 26-member worker-owned cooperative that does contract data analysis for outside clients. We've always settled contested strategic questions through open discussion followed by a simple majority vote, but a newer member with a background in crypto governance has proposed something more ambitious for a defined class of decisions: a futarchy mechanism in the spirit of Robin Hanson's old proposal that institutions should vote on values but bet on beliefs. Concretely, the council would still vote on which outcome measure counts as "success," but the actual choice between rival policies would be settled by internal conditional markets, denominated in co-op credits: whichever policy's market prices in the higher expected value of that measure is the one we adopt automatically.

The proposal has an immediate test case. We're deciding what to do with this year's $85,000 surplus: one faction wants to hire a fourth full-time coordinator to relieve everyone's administrative load, another wants to pay it out as a patronage dividend. Under the proposed mechanism, members would trade in two conditional markets, "coordinator hired" and "dividend paid," each settling eight months out against the quarterly retention-and-satisfaction index we already collect, with whichever policy's market closes higher determining the outcome. Advocates argue this would surface things nobody says aloud in a room of 26 people -- who's actually close to quitting, who resents the workload -- far better than another round of discussion could. Our treasurer, who has clearly done some reading on this, isn't opposed to prediction markets generally but thinks this particular use is reckless: with at most two dozen possible traders there isn't enough depth for arbitrage to correct a motivated member who leans on a price; the retention index is a loose enough proxy for what we actually value that people will trade, and afterward behave, so as to move the number rather than improve the thing it's supposed to track; and anyone who loses a bet has some ability to make their prediction come true after the fact -- for instance, by quietly checking out if "coordinator hired" wins, so the index dips and their wager against it looks vindicated in hindsight.

We meet in three weeks, and if the council approves this mechanism for the surplus decision it will also become our standing procedure for anything over $20,000 -- which is presumably why, when the proposal came up, the rest of the council nominated me, of all people, to draft the discussion memo the whole cooperative will read beforehand (payback, I suspect, for a field-exam note on decision markets I made everyone sit through last spring). Please write that memo. It should give a clear recommendation -- adopt, reject, or adopt with specific modifications -- along with a concrete implementation timeline, and it should engage the treasurer's objections seriously enough that she could respond to them point by point, rather than resolving the disagreement by simply asserting one side is right.
