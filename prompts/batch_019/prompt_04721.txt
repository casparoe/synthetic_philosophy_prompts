I have been researching a piece on the philosophy and physics of symmetry, and two thought experiments keep striking me as strange cousins of one another. The first is Max Black's 1952 argument against the identity of indiscernibles: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, alike in every intrinsic and relational property, yet unmistakably two things rather than one. The second is Kant's much older discussion of "incongruent counterparts": his observation that a left hand and a right hand share identical internal relations among their parts — the same lengths, the same angles — yet cannot be superimposed on one another, a puzzle he first used as evidence against a purely relational theory of space before later repurposing it within his critical philosophy. Both seem to trade on the idea that a complete inventory of an object's qualitative and relational properties can leave something important undetermined: individuality in Black's case, orientation or handedness in Kant's. I would like your help thinking this through: are the two arguments really doing the same philosophical work, or does the parallel break down on closer inspection? And of the two, which do you find the more persuasive challenge to its target — the identity of indiscernibles for Black, relationalism about space for Kant?
