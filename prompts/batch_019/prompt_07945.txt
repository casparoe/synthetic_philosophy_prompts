Okay, this is a pretty specific request but here goes. My friend Priya just hit her FI number after almost a decade grinding at a quant fund, and she's actually doing it: quitting at 33, no consulting gig lined up, no "advisor" title, just done. There's a leaving party for her next weekend and her partner roped a few of us into giving toasts, and because I'm the resident econ PhD student in the friend group I got voted "the one who has to say something that isn't just cheers." The annoying part is the room is going to split right down the middle: half Priyas old trading-floor coworkers, who are all quietly running their own FIRE spreadsheets and would clock a Keynes reference instantly, and half her mom and college friends who just want to hear that she's happy and not secretly having a breakdown.

Here's why I can't stop thinking about Keynes specifically. We read "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" in a history-of-economic-thought elective two years ago, and his whole bet in there is that the "economic problem" gets basically solved within about a hundred years, everyone ends up on something like a fifteen-hour week, and the real difficulty stops being scarcity and becomes figuring out how to "live wisely and agreeably and well" once you don't actually have to work anymore. A hundred years out from 1930 lands close enough to right now that it's definately an insane coincidence for a party happening this year. But he's not smug about any of it - there's a whole bit about how we've been trained for generations to strive and not enjoy, and how even people who've always had money and leisure mostly haven't handled it well. Then a few years later, in a much more personal essay called "My Early Beliefs," he looked back at himself and his whole Bloomsbury crowd as young men who thought Moore's "good states of mind" - love, beauty, that kind of thing - were basically the entire content of a good life, and as an older man walked a lot of that back, something like civilisation being a thin, precarious crust over something a lot less pretty underneath. So Priya isn't just retiring early, she's personally arriving, right on Keynes's own hundred-year timeline, at the exact question he thought humanity would eventually have to face and wasn't sure it was actually ready for.

Can you write the toast? I don't want the lazy version where the joke is just "lol Keynes said fifteen hours and was so wrong" - every finance-econ person in that room has already made that joke to Priya's face at least once. I want it to use both essays to say something genuinely true, and a little unresolved, about what she's walking into: celebratory, with a real laugh or two, but not allowed to pretend the leisure question is easy just because the money question got solved for her personally. It has to land the same for her mom as for the guy who sat next to her on the trading desk, run about three minutes read out loud, and not tip over into a lecture at any point.
