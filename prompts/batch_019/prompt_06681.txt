I have been working through Jeff McMahan's revisionist critique of the traditional "moral equality of combatants" for a seminar on the ethics of war, and one move in the dialectic keeps bothering me. Against the traditional independence thesis — the view, associated with Michael Walzer, that jus in bello is independent of jus ad bellum, so that combatants fighting for an unjust cause are not murderers provided they observe the rules of war — McMahan argues that liability to lethal defensive force tracks responsibility for posing an unjust threat. On his view, unjust combatants are liable to be killed by their just adversaries, since fighting for a cause that lacks just cause makes them responsible for an unjust threat, while just combatants pose no such threat and so remain non-liable, retaining their right not to be killed even by unjust combatants who are merely obeying orders. The obvious objection is epistemic: individual soldiers are almost never in a position to determine, from the inside, whether their war satisfies jus ad bellum, so it seems both unfair and practically unworkable to make the permissibility of their fighting hinge on a fact they cannot access. McMahan's standard reply is to grant that this shows unjust combatants are typically excused for fighting while denying that it justifies their fighting: on his view, a reasonable but false belief that one is permitted to kill does not make the killing itself permissible.

I would like you to push back specifically on that excuse/justification distinction, rather than on McMahan's account of liability more generally. Please construct the strongest argument you can that the epistemic situation actually facing ordinary soldiers — asymmetric access to intelligence, dependence on a chain of command, severe time pressure, and the unreliability of unaided individual judgment about matters of state that even experts dispute — does not merely excuse unjust combatants but can render their fighting objectively permissible, so that something close to the moral equality of combatants is recoverable from within McMahan's own liability-based framework, rather than defended only on the separate, more institutional ground that the law of war must remain usable independently of the "deep morality" of any given conflict. I suspect some version of this move has been tried in the broader literature on acting permissibly under uncertainty, but please work the argument out for yourself rather than summarizing what you take existing proposals to say, and be explicit about exactly where and why it blocks the inference from "reasonably believed permissible" to "merely excused."
