Two weeks ago, in the emergency department, I discharged a patient after concluding, from the full clinical picture, that his atypical chest pain was not cardiac. He turned out to be fine, but at the following morbidity-and-mortality review a colleague raised a newly circulated internal audit: physicians in our department who describe themselves as "clinically confident" about presentations like his are wrong almost as often as those who describe themselves as unsure. I have no particular reason to doubt the audit's methodology, or to think I am somehow exempt from its finding. And yet my recollection of that specific case still seems, sign by sign, to support the call I made. Trying to work out what position this leaves me in, I have started reading about "higher-order evidence" and "epistemic akrasia," but I would rather understand the actual problem than just pick up the vocabulary.

Write an essay on whether it can be rational to go on holding a first-order belief while also believing, on solid higher-order grounds, that beliefs formed the way this one was are generally unreliable. Weigh the case for the Enkratic Principle, which treats that combination as a form of irrationality structurally reminiscent of Moore's paradox, against responses sometimes called "level-splitting" views, which try to permit exactly this combination when the higher-order evidence is misleading. Do not settle for "it depends": defend a specific verdict, and state clearly what it implies for a case structured like mine.
