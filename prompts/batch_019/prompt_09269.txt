The question was:

"Dretske's early informational theory of content runs into the disjunction problem. (a) Briefly explain the disjunction problem. (b) Explain how a teleosemantic theory like Millikan's is supposed to solve it by appealing to biological/proper function. (c) Some critics think a structurally similar indeterminacy problem re-emerges even inside teleosemantics itself. Explain this problem using the frog/fly case, and say briefly whether you think Millikan has the resources to block it. [20 points]"

And here's the students answer, word for word:

"(a) On a purely informational or causal theory, a state R represents whatever reliably causes it. But R is often caused by more than one thing — e.g. a 'square' detector might sometimes fire because of a trapezoid seen at an angle that projects a square-ish retinal image. Since R correlates with both squares and these trapezoids, the informational theory seems forced to say R's content is really the disjunction 'square-or-trapezoid-at-an-angle,' in which case the trapezoid case can never count as a misrepresentation — it's just another correct case of detecting the disjunctive property. That seems wrong, since intuitively the system is making a mistake, not representing some weird disjunctive property.

(b) Teleosemantics fixes this by looking at the proper function of the mechanism that produces R — what it was selected to do, because of the fitness payoff that behavior produced historically. If the mechanism was selected because R-caused-by-squares led to successful outcomes, then R's content is fixed as 'square,' full stop, and the trapezoid cases count as misfires relative to that function rather than as evidence of a disjunctive content. This restores a real gap between content and mere cause, which is exactly what you need for misrepresentation to even be possible.

(c) The frog/fly case pushes on the same soft spot from another angle. A frog's optic tectum fires in response to small dark moving convex things, and the frog snaps its tongue in response, which is how it normally eats. The problem is that this is really just Davidson's Swampman worry again: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate frog that popped into existence by freak accident would have neurons firing in exactly the same pattern, but since it has no selection history at all, teleosemantics has to say its tongue-snap detector represents nothing whatsoever — which shows that appealing to history can never really pin down a determinate content, since the history-less case is left completely undetermined. Millikan basically has no way to block this, since she can't consistently appeal to history to fix content in one breath and then admit that history doesn't settle it in the next. Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory was actually developed by Millikan and her allies as a patch for exactly this gap — it makes the trapezoid-detections asymmetrically dependent on the square-detections in a way that doesn't need any selection history to do the work, which is a cleverer fix than most people give teleosemantics credit for."
