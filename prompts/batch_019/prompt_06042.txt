For a dissertation chapter on objective chance, I have been examining whether the propensity interpretation of probability can survive Humphreys's paradox. In brief: propensities are supposed to ground directed, causal conditional probabilities such as P(effect | cause), but the probability calculus lets us derive an "inverse" quantity, P(cause | effect), from any such propensity via Bayes' theorem; if that inverse quantity must likewise count as a propensity, we seem committed to the effect having a backward-in-time causal tendency to produce its own cause. Please argue that a propensity theorist can accept the paradox's premises about causal asymmetry without abandoning the propensity interpretation, by explaining why not every quantity the calculus generates from a genuine propensity must itself be a propensity, and what status the derived inverse quantity should have instead.
