I have been thinking about Toby Ord's notion of moral trade: the idea that two people who disagree deeply about ethics can nonetheless strike a mutually beneficial bargain, on the model of comparative advantage. Each party does a bit more of what the other values, in exchange for the other doing likewise, so that both end up ahead by their own lights without either conceding the underlying moral disagreement. I want to press on a distinction that I think Ord's framing tends to obscure, and I have sketched the argument below rather than writing it up in full. I would value your comments before I try to turn it into a full post for the EA Forum.

The argument, in outline: genuine moral trade is essentially interpersonal. It requires two parties with distinct, fixed evaluative standpoints, each willing to act somewhat against their own values in one respect in return for the other doing likewise in a respect they care about more. This is structurally just like ordinary trade between people with different tastes or endowments: the gains are real because there are two separate ledgers being reconciled.

Contrast this with "moral offsetting": a single person, uncertain between moral views rather than committed to some rival value, eats meat but funds animal-welfare charities, or flies but pays for carbon removal, to offset the wrong they might be doing. I want to argue that this is not trade in any interesting sense. There is no counterparty, no second ledger, and no distinct interest being reconciled; it is simply a restatement of maximizing expected choiceworthiness under moral uncertainty, dressed in the language of exchange. This relabeling is not innocent: it borrows legitimacy from the genuinely two-sided case, and it leaves the offsetting agent's self-assessment far more exposed to motivated reasoning than a real trade would be, since no independent party is checking whether the "deal" is actually favorable.

Does this interpersonal/intrapersonal distinction hold up, or is there a respectable way to cash out offsetting as real trade—say, by treating one's own credences, sub-agents, or time-slices as trading partners? Is there existing work on moral uncertainty or moral trade that already makes or rebuts this point and that I should be engaging with? And, independent of whether the argument is correct, does it strike you as an interesting wedge into the topic, or more a distinction without a difference?
