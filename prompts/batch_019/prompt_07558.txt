I cover science for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a feature on what recent astrobiology findings imply for the Fermi paradox -- the puzzle of why, given the sheer number of stars and the age of the galaxy, we have found no trace whatsoever of another technological civilization. While reporting the piece, I interviewed two researchers who were upbeat about the last decade's steady stream of findings: amino acids turning up readily in meteorites and cometary ice, extremophiles thriving in conditions once assumed sterile, and liquid water apparently common under the icy crusts of moons like Europa and Enceladus. Both read this as good news on two levels at once. Not only, they told me, does it suggest that life is not some vanishingly rare fluke; because the "easy" early steps toward a spacefaring civilization are apparently not the bottleneck, humanity has most likely already gotten through the hardest part of what the economist Robin Hanson dubbed the "Great Filter" in his well-known essay on the paradox, so our odds of eventually colonizing the galaxy ourselves are rather better than the silence of the night sky might otherwise suggest.

I would like to lead my article with that reassuring framing, but I want to run the logic past you first, because a couple of the existential-risk researchers I interviewed for a separate piece -- people who study things like engineered pandemics and unaligned AI -- did not seem remotely comforted when I mentioned it to them, and I could not tell whether they were simply being professionally gloomy or whether I am missing something about how the Great Filter argument actually works. Could you help me sort out which it is, so that I represent the science accurately in both pieces?
