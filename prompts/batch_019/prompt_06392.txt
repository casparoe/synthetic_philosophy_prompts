Question (10 pts): "Using the BHK interpretation, explain why the law of excluded middle (P ∨ ¬P) is not a theorem schema of intuitionistic logic. Illustrate with a specific proposition P for which we currently lack a constructive proof of P ∨ ¬P. Then state whether intuitionistic logic proves ¬(P ∨ ¬P) for that P, and justify your answer."

Student answer:

"On the BHK reading, a proof of A∨B is either a proof of A tagged 'left' or a proof of B tagged 'right' -- you have to actually know which disjunct holds and possess a proof of it, not just know that at least one does. A proof of ¬A is a construction turning any hypothetical proof of A into a proof of an absurdity like 0=1.

This is why excluded middle fails: take Goldbach's conjecture G ('every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes'). Nobody currently has a proof of G, and nobody has a proof of ¬G, so we're not entitled to assert G∨¬G. This isn't just about our current ignorance though -- since intuitionists identify truth with provability, the unprovability of G∨¬G makes the disjunction actually false, so intuitionistic logic proves ¬(G∨¬G) for any undecided proposition like this one. That's the sense in which intuitionism is a genuine rival to classical logic rather than just a restriction of it: where classical logic asserts P∨¬P for every P, intuitionistic logic asserts its negation for any P we haven't settled yet.

The whole apparatus traces back to Brouwer, who held that mathematics is a languageless mental activity and that a statement only becomes true once we possess a construction proving it. Brouwer formalized this into the intuitionistic calculus, dropping double-negation elimination (¬¬A→A) and excluded middle as valid schemas while keeping the rest of classical logic intact; the resulting system was later given its 'proof-as-construction' gloss by Heyting and Kolmogorov, hence 'BHK.' Kolmogorov's angle in particular was that the same calculus can be read as a logic of problems and solutions rather than proofs and propositions, which is why some people call it the 'problem interpretation.'"
