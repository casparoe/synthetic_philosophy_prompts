I'm a philosophy PhD student currently auditing a seminar on classical Indian epistemology (my own work is in mainstream analytic epistemology), and I've run into a debate that the seminar treats as settled background but that I don't yet have a real feel for. The Naiyāyikas hold that the validity (prāmāṇya) of a cognition is always established *parataḥ* — extrinsically, by some further cognition, typically an inference from the cognition's practical success (*pravṛtti-sāmarthya*) or from the ascertained absence of defects in whatever produced it — while Kumārila Bhaṭṭa and the Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsakas hold that validity is *svataḥ* — intrinsic to the cognition from the moment it arises, so that only invalidity, when it occurs, needs certifying by something extra. Everyone in the room treats the Nyāya position as the more sophisticated, externalist-friendly one, and I can repeat that back, but when I press on the obvious worry I don't get much beyond a shrug toward Gaṅgeśa's name: if every cognition's validity needs certifying by some further cognition, and that certifying cognition would itself seem to need certifying, doesn't the whole thing either regress forever or bottom out in something simply taken as self-certifying — which would just relocate the three horns of Agrippa's trilemma into Sanskrit and smuggle Kumārila's view back in through the side door?

Could you actually walk me through the structure of the Nyāya response to this regress worry? I gather it turns on distinguishing how a cognition's validity is produced (*utpatti*) from how that validity comes to be known (*jñapti*), and that it presumably also connects to the independent Naiyāyika view that cognitions in general are not self-revealing and require a further cognition, an *anuvyavasāya*, just to be grasped at all — but I would rather have the actual gears of the argument than the two labels I've collected. I'm especially interested in whether the regress is stopped in a principled way or merely relocated one level up, and in what, on the Nyāya side, was supposed to be so costly about Kumārila's simpler picture that it justified taking on all this extra machinery, given that Naiyāyikas elsewhere are not shy about invoking economy (*lāghava*) against their opponents.
