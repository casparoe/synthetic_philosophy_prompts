The question read:

"One well-known objection to Integrated Information Theory (IIT) notes that a very simple physical system — for instance, a large, sparsely connected network of elementary logic gates, wired so that each gate's state depends only on a handful of its neighbours, of the kind used to build robust error-correcting codes — can turn out, on IIT's own formalism, to have an enormous amount of integrated information (Φ), and hence, by IIT's lights, a correspondingly high degree of consciousness, even though the system is doing nothing that looks remotely interesting from the outside.
(a) Explain why the exclusion postulate is what forces us to evaluate Φ at the level of the grid itself (or some particular subset of it), rather than at the level of a single gate, or of the whole room the machine sits in.
(b) State what the force of the objection is supposed to be, and say who first pressed it against IIT, and in what form.
(c) IIT is often advertised as fixing consciousness by a system's intrinsic causal structure rather than by the function it computes. Explain what this contrast is supposed to mean, and say whether the grid example helps or hurts IIT's claim to be a genuinely non-functionalist theory."

The student's answer was:

"(a) The exclusion postulate says that, of all the overlapping candidate sets of elements one could draw a boundary around — a single gate, a pair of gates, the whole grid, the grid plus the table it sits on — only one actually specifies a subject of experience: the one whose Φ is locally maximal. A lone gate has no internal cause-effect structure to integrate, so its Φ is zero. Extending the boundary out into the room does not help either, since the extra elements (the table, the air) are barely coupled to the grid's dynamics, and a partition that simply cuts them off loses almost no cause-effect information; so Φ cannot be increased, and is usually reduced, by including them. The maximum therefore sits somewhere in the grid itself, which is why IIT identifies the grid, and not some larger or smaller assembly, as the relevant complex.

(b) The objection is due to Scott Aaronson, who constructed grids of exactly this kind and showed that, once you actually run IIT's own calculation on them, the resulting Φ comes out to be vanishingly small: because every gate only ever talks to its nearest neighbours, information never really gets the chance to integrate across the whole system, so effects on one side of the grid barely reach the other side. Aaronson's point was that this shows Φ fails to track the sort of global binding IIT's own postulates are supposed to guarantee, since a system that is only ever locally connected turns out, sensibly enough, to score low on a measure that is meant to reward exactly that kind of widespread interdependence.

(c) The causal-structure/function contrast marks the difference between two ways of individuating a mind. Functionalism says two systems are experientially alike whenever they share the same input-output profile, however that profile happens to be physically implemented, so a digital simulation of a brain would be conscious in just the way the brain is, provided it reproduced the same behavior. IIT is usually introduced as denying exactly this and tying consciousness to the causal powers of the specific physical mechanism instead. But the grid case shows the distinction collapses under pressure: since IIT grounds the presence and quality of experience entirely in Φ, and Φ is just a further quantity computed from the system's organization, IIT is best described as a fairly literal restatement of functionalism, just with 'integrated information' substituted for 'functional role' as the property whose sameness across physically different systems guarantees sameness of experience."
