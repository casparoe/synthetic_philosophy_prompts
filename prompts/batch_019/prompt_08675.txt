My first editor, Alan, died Tuesday at 84. He spent his twenties and thirties covering wars and famines before switching to the science and ideas beat, where he trained me and where I inherited his old job. Somewhere along the way he also became a genuinely serious amateur Leibniz scholar -- not the Pangloss cartoon, the actual Theodicy -- with two things he'd correct you on at the bar without fail. One: "best possible world" doesn't mean happiest or least suffering, it means something like maximum variety of phenomena under the simplest possible laws, which he found beautiful and I still find a little cold. Two: he couldnt stand "everything happens for a reason," because his whole point was that it didn't have to happen this way -- the world's contingent, other worlds were genuinely possible, this one just wasn't ruled out. He also wasn't a believer, which made it stranger that he still needed "best" to mean something with no one left to do the choosing, and he died before I got him to fully explain that part.

I'm giving the eulogy Saturday, maybe six or seven minutes. Most of the room just wants permission to cry and to hear he was a great editor; four or five rows are people from the philosophy seminar he audited after retiring, and they'll absolutely clock it if I flatten his real view into a Pangloss joke or a "reason for everything" platitude. Can you write it -- something that gets his actual position right, including how a guy who spent his twenties covering famines could hold it without the whole thing sounding obscene, but that still works as a eulogy and not a seminar?
