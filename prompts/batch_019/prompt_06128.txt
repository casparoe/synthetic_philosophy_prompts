I'm a retired biochemist (spent almost forty years on cell signalling and metabolism, of all things) who has, in retirement, finally gotten round to reading all the philosphy I skipped as an undergrad. My little reading group just worked through Cicero's De Senectute, and it's stuck with me more than I expected going in. Cato spends most of the dialogue knocking down the four standard complaints against old age -- that it pulls you out of active life, weakens the body, strips away pleasure, and sits too close to death -- and his answers all lean on the same basic move: old age trades strength and appetite for judgement and authority, and it actually frees you from what he (channelling that Sophocles anecdote, I think, the one about escaping a "savage master") calls the tyranny of the passions. Then there's the ending, which I keep coming back to: death is either nothing to worry about, if the soul dies with the body, or something good, if it survives, and either way a sensible old man should leave life the way a satisfied guest leaves a banquet, or an actor exits before the last, most tedious act -- ripe fruit falls on its own, it doesn't need to be torn off the branch.

So here's my actual question, and its got a bit of a personal edge to it given my own age: what do you reckon Cato/Cicero would make of the current push, in my old field of all places, to treat aging itself as a fixable engineering problem rather than a stage of life you're meant to get through with the right attitude -- the whole senolytics-and-reprogramming crowd (Sinclair's lab, outfits like Altos Labs) who are quite explicitly trying to reverse cellular aging rather than just help people bear it gracefully. Would he see that project as basically continuous with his own logic -- reason and art perfecting nature, the way farming already "improves" on the wild, so why not old age too -- or would he see it as exactly the vice he warns against, old men grasping for one more act instead of leaving the party while they're still enjoying it? I'd like an answer that actually works from the specific moves in his argument -- the four complaints, the bit on the passions, the banquet/play/fruit imagery -- rather than a generic "the Romans were big on accepting fate" answer, which is the bit I could have guessed myself.
