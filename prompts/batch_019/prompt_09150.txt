For the past few months I've been part of a small reading group working through Hegel's Philosophy of Right, and the session on sovereignty and the relations between states ended in a long-running argument between two of the regulars, Marcus and Sofia, that neither would let go of over drinks afterward. Marcus's opening claim was that Hegel doesn't merely tolerate war as a regrettable necessity but treats it as positively good for a people's ethical life. He pointed to the passage where Hegel says that a lasting, let alone "perpetual," peace would let a nation's ethical life stagnate and turn corrupt, much as a sea needs storms to keep from growing foul, and argued that this language of health and corruption is doing real evaluative work, not merely descriptive work — indeed, he added, it's exactly the sort of passage later critics such as Popper seized on to paint Hegel as an apologist for state violence. He also noted that Hegel goes out of his way to reject Kant's proposal for a federation of states to arbitrate disputes and approximate perpetual peace; if Hegel's real point were just that force is the last resort once everything else fails, Marcus argued, he would have no principled reason to be hostile to projects aimed at making that last resort rarer over time, any more than we abandon efforts to reduce domestic crime because we accept it can never be eliminated entirely.

Sofia's response was that Marcus was running together two different arguments. One is structural: because there is no common judge standing over sovereign states the way courts stand over individual citizens, an unresolved conflict between two fully sovereign wills has, by definition, no arbiter left but force — a claim about what sovereignty and adjudication require, not a value judgment in favor of war. The other is the organicist language of health and corruption, which she read as concerning the relation between individuals and the state's ethical substance — the point being that private ends like property and personal security must show themselves as finite and subordinate — rather than as a claim that violence itself is good. On Kant, she argued that Hegel's objection isn't "don't bother reducing war" but something narrower: a league of states is only ever as binding as the particular sovereign wills that compose it, so it cannot be a genuine common sovereign in the way a state is sovereign over its own citizens — a claim about what such a federation can and cannot guarantee, not a complaint about the goal itself. She also pointed out that Hegel insists war between "civilized" states be conducted with real restraint — sparing domestic institutions, family life, and envoys, and keeping the possibility of peace alive throughout — which sits oddly with reading him as simply cheering on conflict.

Marcus granted the point about restraint but didn't think it settled anything: a practice can be hedged with considerable restraint, he said, and still be held to be good rather than merely permissible, and the stagnation metaphor is explicitly a health-versus-corruption contrast, which should trouble us regardless of how the resulting wars are conducted. From there the two of them went another couple of rounds — Sofia maintaining that the organicist language does less independent work than Marcus thinks once it's read against the "no common judge" argument, Marcus maintaining that she was explaining away language Hegel had no need to use if he meant only what she says he meant — without either side budging.

I'd like a genuine adjudication of this rather than a verdict for one side. Could you go through the exchange move by move — the stagnation passage, the "no common judge" argument, the response to Kant, and the significance of restraint in war — and say who has the better of it at each step, and why?
