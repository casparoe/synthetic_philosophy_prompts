So I've been re-reading Jaynes' *Probability Theory* (again) and got nerd-sniped by Bertrand's paradox for like the third time this year. Quick recap in case its useful context: you ask for the probability that a "random" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and depending on whether you randomize over the two endpoints, over a radius-and-a-point-on-it, or over where the midpoint lands in the disk, you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 respectively. Jaynes' famous move in "The Well-Posed Problem" is that since the problem as stated never tells you the circle's size or position, any legitimate answer has to stay invariant under translating, rotating, and rescaling the whole setup -- and he claims this uniquely picks out the "random radius" method (1/2), which he backed up empirically by literally chucking straws onto circles chalked on a floor and getting frequencies that matched. First time I read it I found this so satisfying, like the principle of indifference had been rescued from itself just by being more careful about what exactly you're being indifferent between.

Except then I fell down a rabbit hole and found a rebuttal (Drory, maybe? 2015-ish, might be misremembering the name) arguing that Jaynes' invariance requirements don't actually force a unique answer at all -- you can apparently cook up other sampling procedures that satisfy the exact same rotation/translation/scale invariance and still land you on 1/3 or 1/4, which if true would mean the well-posing move never actually got rid of the arbitrariness, it just buried it one level down, in the choice of which procedure counts as the honest implementation of the symmetry. So now I genuinely can't tell if Jaynes solved anything or if the principle of indifference is exactly as sunk as it was before Bertrand's paradox.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people hashing this out? I'd like it set with them actually re-running some version of the straw-drop experiment on an office floor while they argue, so there's a shared physical thing for them to point at and disagree about the meaning of. One should be a committed Jaynesian who thinks the invariance argument genuinely dissolves the paradox and has a real, substantive comeback to the "other procedures satisfy the same invariances" objection -- not just a restatement of the original argument -- and the other should think the whole well-posing strategy is a kind of shell game, but has to actually grapple with why the straw-drop experiment keeps spitting out 1/2 instead of just waving it away as irrelevant. I want them evenly matched, with real back-and-forth where each one responds to what the other just said rather than talking past each other or one of them folding too easily. Doesn't need to be super long, a couple pages of back-and-forth is plenty, and it's totally fine if they don't land on a clean resolution at the end -- as far as I can tell the actual literature hasn't either, so forcing a tidy handshake would feel fake.
