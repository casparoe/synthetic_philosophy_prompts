Three weeks ago I came down with a bout of labyrinthitis, and for the better part of a week I had a strange, low-grade vertigo that waxed and waned continuously rather than switching on and off. What I found genuinely unsettling, once the acute phase passed, was realizing that there had been no instant during the illness at which I could truthfully say "now I know I feel dizzy" as opposed to "a moment ago I didn't" — at any two points close enough together in time, my confidence about my own state felt identical, even though somewhere between clearly fine and clearly dizzy the fact must have changed. When I described this to a housemate who is finishing a PhD in epistemology, she told me I'd basically stumbled onto the phenomenon behind Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument in Knowledge and Its Limits, and that the very same style of reasoning is supposed to show that the KK principle — that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p — fails for ordinary epistemic agents like us.

I've since read a couple of secondary discussions of the argument and can follow it in outline: something about a margin-for-error constraint on knowledge, applied to a sequence of cases running from a point where a condition (feeling cold, feeling dizzy, whatever) obviously holds to a point where it obviously fails to hold, with neighboring cases in the sequence too similar for the subject to discriminate between them. But I keep losing the thread on the mechanics: I can't reconstruct in my own head exactly how the margin-for-error premise and the luminosity assumption combine, step by step, to generate a contradiction. There's also a wrinkle none of the summaries I've found addresses head-on: Williamson is equally well known for arguing that ordinary vague predicates like "heap" or "thin" have sharp but unknowable boundaries, so why doesn't he take the same line here and simply say that "feeling dizzy" has a sharp, unknowable cutoff somewhere along my sequence of cases, while still maintaining that whenever I do feel dizzy, I thereby know it? Why does the argument instead force him to give up luminosity itself, rather than just adding one more unknowable boundary to his list?

Since the KK-principle failure is the part that matters most for a seminar paper I'm now writing, I'd really appreciate it if you could spell out exactly how the general anti-luminosity reasoning gets redeployed against the specific condition "knowing that p," so that it ends up undermining Kp → KKp rather than merely some unrelated introspective condition — and then tell me which premise in that redeployed version (the margin-for-error principle itself, the assumption that the relevant cases form a suitably fine-grained and cognitively indiscriminable chain, or something else) strikes you as most vulnerable, and how defenders of the KK principle have actually tried to attack it in the literature.
