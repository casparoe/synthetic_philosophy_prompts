(b) What exactly does the proof show to be "trivial," and why was this treated as a serious problem for theories of conditionals rather than a curiosity? (c) Briefly describe one way philosophers have tried to respond to, or live with, the result."

And here's the student's answer, typos and all:

"The thesis under attack is Adams' Thesis, which says the probability of an indicative conditional 'if A, B' is always identical to the conditional probability, i.e. P(A → B) = P(B|A) whenever P(A) > 0. Lewis's proof works by taking a single credence function P and conditionalizing it on B and on not-B. If we update P on B, then A → B is guaranteed true given A and B (since A → B just says B given A), so the updated probability of A → B is 1. If we update P on not-B, A → B is guarenteed false in the same way, so its updated probability is 0. Crucially, the Equation has to keep holding after we update too, since its supposed to hold for any rational credence function, including P-conditional-on-B and P-conditional-on-not-B. Averaging the two updated probabilities by the law of total probability, weighting by P(B), gives P(A → B) = P(B). Combined with the original equation this forces P(B|A) = P(B) — so A and B come out probabilistically independent, for basically any A, B and any P you like. Since real people's credences obviously don't treat every pair of propositions as independent, something has to give. That's the sense in which P is shown to be trivial: almost no interesting probability function can satisfy the Equation across the board. This mattered so much because Adams' Thesis was supposed to back up the idea that indicative conditionals are ordinary propositions that combine with other propositions via the normal probability calculus, the same way Stalnaker's possible-worlds account needed them to; Lewis's proof shows no such proposition can do that job except in freak cases. Lewis's own verdict was that indicative conditionals must not have truth conditions at all — they're not really true or false, just more or less assertible depending on the conditional probability — a position that's now called the 'no truth value' view. A rival, more conservative response is to keep truth conditions but argue the Equation was never a good semantic constraint in the first place, since 'assertability tracks conditional probability' can instead be explained pragmatically, e.g. via a conventional implicature of robustness, without requiring the conditional's actual probability to equal the conditional probability."
