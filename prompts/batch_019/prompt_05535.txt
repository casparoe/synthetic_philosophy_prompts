Over the last several months I coordinated a project to compile a public dataset of water-quality readings from a few thousand monitoring sites, with every individual measurement checked against the original lab records before being entered. When the dataset was released, I added a short note to the documentation acknowledging that, simply given the number of entries involved, it almost certainly contains at least one transcription error somewhere, even though I have no idea which reading it would be and no particular reason to doubt any specific one. A friend of mine who studied logic in graduate school seized on this over dinner last week: he argued that since I was individually confident, of every single reading, that it had been correctly transcribed, I was thereby committed to being confident that the readings, taken together, had all been correctly transcribed, since that combined claim asserts nothing beyond what each individual claim already asserts on its own. On his view, my disclaimer meant either that I secretly doubted some particular entry, in which case I should say which one and correct it, or that I was simply holding inconsistent beliefs.

I felt strongly that both attitudes were perfectly reasonable to hold at once, but after a long back-and-forth neither of us managed to convince the other, and the disagreement has stayed with me since. What complicates matters further is that a philosophy course I took years ago left me with a hazy memory of a supposedly similar case involving a large raffle: one can apparently be confident, of each individual ticket, that it will lose, while being equally confident that some ticket will win, and I recall this being presented as a situation in which ordinary standards of rational belief run into real difficulty. My friend maintained that the raffle case and my dataset case are, in whatever respect either is problematic, "exactly the same problem." I am not sure that is right, though I could not say precisely why the two cases might differ, if indeed they do.

I would like a considered verdict rather than a restatement of either of our positions: was my friend correct that I was holding inconsistent beliefs, or is there a principled way to defend holding both the belief that each entry is accurate and the belief that the dataset as a whole is not error-free? And is the comparison to the raffle actually apt, or does it paper over some real difference between the two situations that matters to how each should be resolved?
