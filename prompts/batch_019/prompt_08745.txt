At the small developer-tools company where I work, we recently made the source code for our core product publicly visible for the first time, and it has turned into a surprisingly heated internal argument that's been running for weeks. The new license lets anyone read the code, modify it for their own use, and even redistribute their changes, but it forbids repackaging it as a competing hosted service — essentially the same move several well-known infrastructure and database companies have made in recent years after watching big cloud providers resell their software without contributing anything back. About half my team wants the launch announcement and README to call this "open source," since the code is genuinely sitting out in the open and anyone can send us a pull request. The other half, mostly people who have worked on real open-source projects before, say that's misleading, because the license fails a couple of the criteria in the Open Source Initiative's official definition — specifically the ones about not discriminating against particular fields of endeavor or classes of user. What makes the argument strange is that nobody disputes any fact about what the license actually permits; both camps describe its terms identically. They only disagree about whether those terms add up to something that earns the label.

I've somehow ended up responsible for drafting the final wording, mainly because I don't have a strong enough stake for either side to resent me for it, but I have no background in philosophy and I'm not sure how to settle this beyond picking a side and absorbing the complaints afterward. It occurs to me that since nobody involved is confused or mistaken about any actual fact, more arguing seems unlikely to change anyone's mind — but I can't tell whether that means the disagreement isn't a real one, or whether one side is still simply correct and the other simply wrong. Is there a principled way to work out whether two people who agree on every fact but disagree about what to call the result are disagreeing about anything at all, and if it turns out they aren't, does that mean it genuinely doesn't matter which way we write the announcement?
