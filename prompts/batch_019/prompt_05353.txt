I am a retired physicist who only came to philosophy in earnest after closing the lab for good, and for the last few months a small reading group of fellow retirees has been working through Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics alongside Bart Schultz's biography Henry Sidgwick: Eye of the Universe. What has genuinely surprised me is not the ethics but the second career it seems to have launched: for nearly twenty years Sidgwick was a leading, meticulous, and largely disappointed investigator of ghosts, mediums, and telepathy, serving as the Society for Psychical Research's founding president from its creation in 1882, alongside Frederic Myers and Edmund Gurney. Most of what I have read treats this as an odd, faintly embarrassing footnote to "the real philosophy," best explained away by Victorian spiritualist fashion or personal grief. Having spent my own career designing controls against exactly the kind of self-deception mediums traded on, I find I disagree, and I have written up my reasoning as a short set of notes for the group. I would be grateful if you would referee them for me, the way I used to referee manuscripts for journals: major concerns first, then minor ones, and please do not spare me on either the history or the philosophy.

My argument runs as follows. In the concluding chapter of the Methods, Sidgwick confesses that he cannot, from purely secular premises, show that the standpoint of Rational Benevolence always trumps that of Rational Egoism: an agent facing a genuine choice between a personal loss and a greater good for others seems, on his own analysis, to have equally rational grounds for acting from either standpoint. He calls this the dualism of practical reason and treats it as a genuine crack running through the foundations of ethics, not a puzzle to be shrugged off. The only repair he can see is a further, quite unphilosophical fact: that the universe is arranged, by God or by the structure of a future life, so that virtue and self-interest ultimately coincide. Since Sidgwick had already resigned his Trinity fellowship in 1869 rather than affirm doctrines he no longer believed, that fact was not something he could simply postulate on authority; it had to be something he could investigate. That, on my reading, is exactly what the psychical research was: not a retreat from the argument of the Methods but its forced continuation, an attempt to settle empirically the one question his ethics could not settle a priori. It is telling that the Society's own house style, under Sidgwick, was fiercely self-critical rather than credulous: it was the Society's own further inquiries that established the Creery sisters had been using a signalling code, and its large Census of Hallucinations was designed precisely to test whether apparitions coincided with real deaths more often than chance allows. Sidgwick never got the coincidence he needed and, so far as I can tell, died still genuinely unsure; the more famous "cross-correspondences," which many people vaguely credit to him, only began appearing after his death in 1900.

What I cannot decide is what to make of the negative result. If the dualism can only be closed by an empirical discovery, and twenty years of the most careful investigation then available found nothing, is intellectual honesty not better served by concluding that the dualism is simply real and unrepaired, rather than by continuing to treat it as an open research question? I do not know whether Sidgwick drew that conclusion himself, or should have. I would also like to know whether I have the history right in enough of its details — the 1869 resignation, the 1882 presidency, the Creery and cross-correspondence episodes — and whether my central claim, that the psychical research is continuous with rather than a lapse from the ethics, is a minority position, a majority position, or simply a confusion on my part. Please be as candid as you would be with a colleague's draft.
