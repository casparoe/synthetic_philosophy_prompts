In my physics class this year, right after we finished the unit on thermodynamics and entropy, my teacher mentioned, almost as a throwaway comment, that a lot of philosophers of physics don't actually think the increase of entropy explains why time has a direction; they think it just relocates the mystery rather than solving it. That comment has stuck with me for weeks. Reading further on my own, I learned that the fundamental laws of motion are time-symmetric (nothing in them singles out a "forward" direction), and that Loschmidt and Zermelo both raised serious objections to Boltzmann's original argument that entropy has to increase. I also came across one of Boltzmann's own responses to that problem: that we should expect to find ourselves near a rare, temporary statistical dip in an otherwise high-entropy universe, with entropy rising in both directions away from that dip. That seems to lead to the strange conclusion that "the future" just means "whichever direction entropy happens to increase from where you're standing," with no further fact about which way time really runs. I brought this up with a friend from my math team on the bus home from practice, and we've been arguing about it ever since without either of us budging.

Could you write the argument out as a dialogue between the two of us, so I can finally see the strongest version of both positions laid out side by side? One character should hold the view I've been leaning toward: that the direction of time is nothing more than the direction in which entropy happens to increase, so that every other temporal asymmetry we care about (memory, causation, the difference between affecting the future and being affected by the past) is downstream of the entropy gradient, and there is no additional fact about which way time flows once that gradient and the special low-entropy boundary condition behind it are fixed. The other character should hold my friend's view: that time has its own direction, built into the world independently of thermodynamics, and entropy merely tends to increase toward that already-fixed future because of the special boundary condition, so that even a brief statistical dip in which entropy decreased for a while would not mean time had run backward for its duration, which is exactly what my view seems forced to say. I want the two of them to press each other on the hard parts rather than just restating their own side: my view needs to explain why a rare recurrence shouldn't count as a local time-reversal, and my friend's view needs to explain what could possibly fix one direction as "the" future if literally every observable asymmetry tracks the entropy gradient perfectly. It doesn't have to be long (a page or so is fine), but please don't let either character fold just to wrap things up neatly, since neither my friend nor I have.
