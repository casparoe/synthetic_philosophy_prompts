Quick bit of context: I run a Tuesday evening reading group with my grad students on moral psychology, and last week's session was on the situationist critique of virtue ethics -- Doris's chapter on Milgram and the Darley & Batson "Good Samaritan" seminarians study, plus Harman's fundamental attribution error paper. I've taught this material more times than I can count, but I dont think I've ever had students turn the Doris-vs-Kamtekar debate into an actual personal argument before, which is what happened with two of my students, Owen and Kate, who kept going after the group broke up and apparently continued over beers. I only got it secondhand from both of them the next day, so I've tried to reconstruct the moves fairly rather than however each of them wants to remember "winning."

(1) Owen opened with: the data settle it. Milgram got something like two-thirds of ordinary volunteers to run the shock generator all the way up despite the "victim" screaming, and Darley & Batson found that whether a seminary student was in a hurry predicted whether he'd stop for a man slumped in a doorway far better than whether he'd just been assigned to prepare a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan itself. Throw in Mischel's old finding that cross-situational consistency in behavior rarely gets above a correlation of about 0.3, and you get: the robust, reliably-manifesting traits virtue ethics wants us to cultivate -- honest, compassionate, brave across the board -- just aren't there to cultivate. (2) Kate said this only refutes a "globalist" picture of character that Aristotle never held anyway: he thought full virtue was rare and that most people sit somewhere between akrasia and vice, so it's no surprise a bunch of seminarians or undergrads don't display robust virtue under pressure -- the studies are testing ordinary people for a property the theory never predicted they'd have. (3) Owen said that's a hollow victory: if virtue is that rare, virtue ethics can't deliver the practical guidance it's famous for, since we have no reliable test for whether character education ever produces it, whereas we do have good evidence that just changing the situation (less rushed, different framing) reliably changes behavior -- so surviving as a claim about a rare ideal isnt the same as surviving as useful advice for the rest of us. (4) Kate replied that this conflates two different questions -- what's worth becoming versus what's currently the most efficient lever for shifting population behavior -- and added, following Kamtekar, that you can't even describe the experiments without character-vocabulary creeping back in: Milgram's obedient subjects are most naturally read as displaying a vice, excessive deference against their own better judgment, not as evidence that trait-talk is empty. (5) Owen shot back that this redescription move is too cheap, because it's unfalsifiable -- you can always relabel whatever happened as "revealing a trait" after the fact, with no advance way of saying which trait fires in which situation, so nothing was actually predicted or explained.

Neither of them budged and I honestly think they're each partly right, which isn't much use to either of them. Could you referee this properly -- go move by move, rate each one from -2 (clearly wrong) to +2 (clearly right), and say briefly why? I'm especially curious whether Kate's move (2) is a principled restriction on the theory's scope or a rescue that ends up costing more than it saves by move (3), and whether Owen's unfalsifiability charge in (5) actually sticks against Kamtekar-style redescription or proves too much (since plenty of respectable theories redescribe evidence in light of themselves). If there's something the literature already handles that neither of them reached for, flag it too. End with a one-line verdict: on balance, who's closer to right, Owen or Kate?
