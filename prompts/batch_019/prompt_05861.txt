I used to work IT support at a mid-sized company, and about a year ago we had a minor data breach that traced back to a security patch that had been sitting unapplied for months. In the postmortem meeting, half the room kept insisting that not installing the patch was "the cause" of the breach, since the specific hole the attackers used obviously wouldn't have existed if we had installed it, while the other half argued that the real cause was the attacker, and that calling an absence of action a "cause" didn't really make sense — nothing happened, so how could nothing be a cause of anything? I couldn't let the question drop afterward, especially once I noticed how strange things get if you take the first view seriously: our whole security team could have applied that patch, so could the vendor who was slow to release it, and so, technically, could I, if I had stayed late one night with admin access for no particular reason. It feels absurd to call all of those people causes of the breach, yet I can't find a non-arbitrary place to stop the list once "it wouldn't have happened otherwise" is allowed to count as enough for causation.

Reading around on my own since then, I've gathered that this connects to a genuine, longstanding disagreement among people who think seriously about causation in general, quite apart from my particular example. Some hold that a cause has to be linked to its effect by an actual physical process of some kind, which would mean an absence can never really be a cause of anything — at best a convenient stand-in we allow ourselves in everyday talk. Others hold that causation is fundamentally just a matter of counterfactual dependence: if the effect wouldn't have happened without the "absence," that's enough to make it a cause, full stop, regardless of any physical process connecting the two.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two people who hold these opposing views and are equally sharp, using the data-breach case as their running example. Have the "physical process" side explain why treating an absence as a genuine cause is a mistake, and have the "counterfactual dependence" side push back and defend the idea, with both of them honestly grappling with two complications rather than talking past each other: first, the problem I ran into above, that counterfactual dependence seems to turn far too many people into simultaneous "causes" of the same breach; and second, a case where the attackers actually had a second, unrelated vulnerability ready as a backup, so that the breach would have happened at almost the same time even if that particular patch had been installed. I want genuine back-and-forth, with each person responding to what the other just said rather than restating their opening position, and I don't want either side to obviously win by the end — if anything, it would be interesting if they ended up figuring out whether they really disagree about the facts of the case, or only about how they're willing to use the word "cause."
