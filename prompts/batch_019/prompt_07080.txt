I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist (forty-one years in the lab before I finally hung up my coat), and lately I've fallen into a philosophy rabbit hole via a "philosophy for curious retirees" reading group at my local library. Two weeks ago we got onto causation, specifically something called "causation by omission," and its been nagging at me since, because causation is a concept I thought I had cold after four decades of physics.

Here's my hangup, in my own words before I went looking for the fancy version of it: in physics a cause is always some kind of transfer — energy, momentum, a force doing work, a bond breaking. There's an actual physical process connecting cause and effect, which I gather is basically the intuition some philosophers built into "conserved-quantity" theories of causation. But an omission is, by definition, the absence of any such process. Take the standard example the books use: a lifeguard sees someone struggling and just doesn't move. Nothing physical passes between the lifeguard sitting still and the drowning; the drowning proceeds by ordinary physiology exactly as it would on an empty beach with no lifeguard at all. So in what sense did the not-jumping-in cause the death, rather than merely being a circumstance in which some other, perfectly ordinary causal process ran its course unimpeded? And if inaction counts as a cause simply because, counterfactually, things would've gone differently had the lifeguard acted, then why isn't every other able-bodied adult in the world who also failed to jump in equally a cause of the death? Somebody has to explain why we pin it on the lifeguard and nobody else, and I honestly can't tell whether that "somebody" is pointing at a further fact about the world's causal structure or just a fact about who we happen to hold to a duty.

From some reading around, I gather this is a real fork in the metaphysics of causation: counterfactual theorists (Lewis is the name that keeps coming up) are willing to just count omissions as genuine causes, hard-nosed process theorists resist that and reach for weaker labels like "quasi-causation" instead, and at least one writer I ran into thinks the "which omission counts" question gets settled by social norms and expectations rather than by anything in the world's causal structure itself. What I'd like from you is an essay that actually adjudicates between these rather then surveying them politely. Take the physical-process objection to omissive causation seriously, take the "why the lifeguard and not everyone else" problem seriously as a cost for the counterfactual side, show how each camp responds to the other's strongest argument, and then commit to a real verdict on whether omissions can ever genuinely be causes. Something like "omissions are causes, but only relative to a contextually fixed norm, and that's not a scandal" counts as an answer; "reasonable philosophers disagree" does not.
