I'm a senior in our Educational Studies program, and this semester's capstone seminar has centered almost entirely on Alfred North Whitehead's *The Aims of Education*, especially the essay "The Rhythm of Education" and its account of the three recurring stages of learning: the stage of romance, in which a subject first seizes the imagination through sheer curiosity and undisciplined exploration; the stage of precision, in which that excitement is narrowed into exact technique and disciplined method; and the stage of generalization, in which the learner returns to freedom, now able to wield what was mastered. My cohort recently voted for me to give the student address at our program's own diploma ceremony next month, a smaller and more intimate event than the university-wide commencement, held in our department's building, where roughly half the folding chairs will hold parents, siblings, and partners who have never read a word of philosophy of education, and the other half will hold faculty and classmates who spent the semester next to me arguing over how seriously to take Whitehead's claims.

One of those arguments is the reason I'm writing to you. In seminar last week, a classmate insisted that Whitehead's whole framework is basically incompatible with any ceremony built around grades and diplomas, given how openly suspicious he was of external examinations and of treating a credential as the point of an education; I haven't been able to shake the thought since, especially given that I am about to stand up at an event whose entire purpose is handing exactly such credentials to people who just survived four years of exactly that kind of testing. I don't want the speech to quietly drop this tension to keep the mood comfortable, and I don't want it to turn preachy and scold the room for caring about the diploma either. I'd like it to find something true and genuinely warm to say — something a thinker this wary of "inert ideas" and formal certification could actually have endorsed — while still landing for the parents in the back who mostly just want to feel proud of their kids.

Could you draft that address for me? I'm picturing about five minutes at the lectern, in three short movements that correspond to romance, precision, and generalization as they actually played out over our four years, rather than Whitehead's name dropped once for decoration before the speech reverts to generic commencement uplift. Please also steer clear of the two moves I keep finding in sample commencement speeches online — the "follow your passion" platitude and the extended metaphor about journeys — since both are close to the kind of inert, pre-packaged idea he spent his career warning teachers against.
