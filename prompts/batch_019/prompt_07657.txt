I'm a software engineer, and most of my friends are too, so a lot of our socializing doubles as amateur forecasting. Two of them, Naomi and Dev, have argued for months over the odds that a cryptographically relevant quantum computer -- one able to break RSA-2048 -- exists within twenty years: Naomi, who migrates systems to post-quantum algorithms for a living, says 10%; Dev, who works at a quantum computing startup, says 55%. A few weekends ago they spent nearly three hours walking each other through their full reasoning, and neither number moved much. Afterward they argued about the disagreement itself:

(1) Dev said that staying apart is now the irrational part: Aumann's agreement theorem says two Bayesians who share a prior can't knowingly keep disagreeing once their posteriors are common knowledge, so someone here must be reasoning badly.

(2) Naomi said the theorem only bites if they share a prior, and there's no reason to think they do: five years of cryptographic paranoia and five years of qubit optimism give them different starting pictures of the world, and that's not a sign of bad reasoning, just of being different people.

(3) Dev said that's too quick: a prior is whatever's left after subtracting each person's specific evidence, so two rational people should be able to trade all of it and converge; treating "who I am" as a permanent excuse to disagree is exactly what the common-prior idea, going back to Harsanyi, says you don't get for free.

(4) Naomi said that reverses the burden of proof: a single prior every rational person must land on is a substantive, contested claim, not something Bayesian coherence alone gives you, and most economists who invoke Aumann's result treat the common prior as a modeling convenience, not a law of rationality. She added that even granting a shared prior, one conversation almost certainly doesn't produce the strict, mutual common knowledge of exact posteriors the theorem needs -- so nothing here proves irrationality on either side.

I was there for the walk home and, with no philosophy background, couldn't tell you who came out ahead. Could you adjudicate move by move?
