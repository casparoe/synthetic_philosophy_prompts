I spent my working life as an observational astronomer (mostly minor planets and variable stars), and since retiring I've been trying to fill the gaps in my philosophy education by reading some of the founding texts myself rather then just the summaries. A friend from my old department keeps bringing up "Frege's puzzle" over dinner, always via the tired morning-star/evening-star example, and it needles me a bit, because in my own field "two names, one object" was a constant headache - minor planets got independently discovered and provisionally designated by different observatories all the time, and it sometimes took years of orbit-fitting before anyone could show that, say, 1898 DQ and 1901 GV were the same rock. So rather than read the famous 1892 "On Sense and Reference" paper again, I went back to what Frege wrote about identity thirteen years earlier, in section 8 of the Begriffsschrift, back before he had the sense/reference distinction to work with.

Here's my own rough patchwork translation of the bit that's got me stuck (my German is rusty, so I've cobbled this together from a couple of different editions, typos included):

"Identity of content differs from conditionality and negation in that it relates to names, not to contents. Elsewhere a sign is merely a proxy for its content, so that any combination into which signs enter expresses only a relation among the contents they stand for. But the moment two signs are joined by the sign of identity of content, they abruptly stand for themselves, since what is thereby asserted is exactly the circumstance that two names share one content. So introducing a sign for identity of content forces a kind of split into the meaning of every sign, which now stands sometimes for its content, sometimes for itself. If a content could only ever be picked out in a single way, this would all be pointless - a sign for identity of content would express nothing worth judging. It only earns its keep because a content can be fully determined in more than one way, and it can be a real piece of knowledge, not a triviality, that these different routes converge on one and the same thing." (He follows this with an example about a geometrical point that gets picked out via two different constructions, which I'll admit I didn't fully follow, so feel free to skip past that bit if its not central.)

What I can't get straight in my head is this: Frege seems to be saying that a true statement of identity is ultimately a claim about our *names* - that two labels happen to latch onto the same thing - rather than a claim about the world itself. But that doesn't match my own experience at all: working out that two "different" minor planets were actually one object felt exactly like an astronomical discovery, about orbits and rocks, not a discovery about paperwork or notation. So did Frege really mean to reduce identity claims to claims about our labeling conventions, and if so, how is that not immediately self-undermining (surely which symbols we happen to use is itself arbitrary, so how could a fact about them be the substantial thing we're learning)? And is this "names, not contents" idea the very view he attacks in the opening pages of "On Sense and Reference," or is the actual relationship between the two texts messier than a simple reversal? I'd love a patient, detailed walkthrough rather than the one-line summary I keep getting from people.
