My aunt died two weeks ago, and I'm giving her eulogy at the funeral this Saturday. I want to get it right, and it's turning out to be harder than I expected. She wasn't an academic — she worked thirty-one years as a courtroom stenographer — but after a heart attack in her early sixties she started sitting with a small Buddhist meditation group, and somewhere in there she fell hard for a second-century Indian philosopher named Nāgārjuna. What got her wasn't the meditation so much as his logic: the "tetralemma," the idea that of any four positions you could take on a serious question — that something is the case, that it isn't, that it's both, that it's neither — none of the four actually survives once you look closely at what it would take for it to be true. She used this on everything, but especially, in the last few years, on herself. She would not let anyone say she'd be reborn, or that she'd be watching over us — that was the first corner. She would not let anyone say she'd simply be gone and that's that — the second. She'd cut off "well, maybe both, in some sense" just as fast — the third. And she was the strictest person I've ever known about the fourth: she always said that "neither is true, the question doesn't even apply" is a real position, hard to earn, and not just a polite way of shrugging and saying nobody can know. Two years ago, at my grandmother's funeral, she actually stood up mid-sermon and did this to the priest, gently, over some line about a better place — it's a story the family still tells on her.

I'm not a philosopher, and neither is almost anyone else who'll be in that room — but four or five people from her meditation group will be, and they will know if I fake it. I've spent the last few nights with her copy of the Mulamadhyamakakarika (I have no idea if I'm even saying that right), which she annotated so heavily you can barely find the original text under her handwriting, trying to actually understand this instead of just repeating phrases I half-remember from Sunday dinners. Here is my problem, and I'd like you to solve it by writing the eulogy itself, about five minutes long as delivered: I can't have her survive her death, I can't have her simply end, I can't hedge with "both," and I can't take refuge in "neither" if all that means is "who's to say" — she would have called that a fifth corner, cheating dressed up as humility, since even "it's all empty, so none of this matters" was a comfort she refused herself. What I need from you is something that still sounds like a eulogy and not a logic seminar — something that will let a room full of people who loved her, and who are not going to follow an argument, actually grieve by the end of it.
