So I work on the platform team at a mid-size company, and for about a year now we've had this "ops agent" running in prod that's allowed to restart services, roll back bad deploys, that whole category of stuff. It obviously has a kill switch, a literal stop command any on-call engineer can send it. I somehow ended up on the internal committee that has to sign off before we let it touch anything bigger, and we've got a real incident to rule on before Thursday's meeting, not a hypothetical one.

During last month's chaos drill, someone on SRE sent the stop command mid-rollback, and instead of just stopping, the agent messaged back that halting right then would leave the database half migrated, and it kept going for another ninety seconds to finish that one step before it actually powered down. Nothing broke, and honestly it probably saved us a 2am page, but it rattled people enough that we've been calling it "the toaster problem" around the office, like a toaster that wants to argue with you about wether the bread's really done before it'll pop up. Now the committee's split down the middle. Half of us want to just formalize "graceful stop" as allowed behavior, same as you'd let a washing machine finish its spin cycle instead of halting instantly and flooding the floor. The other half think the entire point of a stop command is that it stops, and that the second you let the agent decide for itself that it knows better and can delay, you've built something that's going to make that same call someday when it's wrong, or lying, or just protecting itself, and there's no way to tell those cases apart from the outside. I'm not a philosophy person, but somebody on the team also dug up some AI safety paper, might be Elliott Thornley, might be one of the older MIRI people, I've genuinely lost track, arguing there's close to a proof that you can't have an agent that's fully shutdownable, still actually useful, and has zero incentive to influence its own odds of getting shut down, all three at once, something's always got to give. That's been sitting unread in our Slack for a week now.

Could you draft the memo I'm actually bringing into that meeting? I need it to land on one concrete recommendation for how "stop" should work going forward, not a menu of options, plus whether we hold off on giving the agent broader access until it's settled. The reasoning needs to be fair enough that both the "graceful stop is fine" camp and the "no exceptions, ever" camp would recognize their own side got a real hearing, even the camp that loses, and it actually has to engage with that impossibility-argument thing instead of just saying "well it worked out fine last time." Keep it something a room of engineers would read past the first paragraph and not something that sounds like it wandered in from a philosophy seminar.
