Over the past few months I've been reading about philosophy of mind on my own, since I'm hoping to study computer science and cognitive science in college, and one paper that really stuck with me is Stevan Harnad's 1990 piece on what he called the "symbol grounding problem." As I understood it, his worry was that a purely symbolic system, one where every symbol is defined only in terms of other symbols inside that same system, can never actually connect any of its symbols to the real world; he compares it to trying to learn Chinese using nothing but a Chinese-Chinese dictionary, where you can hop from definition to definition forever without ever cashing any of it out in something you already understand. His proposed fix was that at least some basic symbols need to be anchored to the world through the senses, via feature-detectors built up from direct sensorimotor experience, before more complicated symbols built out of them can count as meaning anything at all.

I brought this up on my school's debate team while we were prepping for a round on AI regulation, and I argued that this problem seems basically solved by now, since current models like GPT-4o or Gemini are "multimodal": they're not just trained on text but also take in images, audio, and video, so such a system can supposedly link the word "apple" directly to the pixels of a photographed apple instead of just to other words. One of my teammates said that this isn't really the kind of grounding Harnad had in mind and that multimodal training might not solve anything at all, but she couldn't quite explain why not, and it seemed to me like she was just being contrarian for the sake of winning the argument. Was she onto something real here, and if so, what exactly would still be missing from a multimodal model that Harnad's argument requires? Or is it fair to say that feeding these systems pictures and sound alongside words does resolve the problem in the sense he actually cared about?
