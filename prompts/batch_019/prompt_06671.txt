A while back I fell down a rabbit hole reading about ranked-choice voting reforms that were being debated for my city council elections, which is how I first came across Arrow's impossibility theorem: the result that once you fix a handful of quite modest-sounding conditions on how individual rankings should be combined into a single group ranking, no rule can satisfy all of them at once except one that simply lets a single person's preferences dictate the outcome. I found that unsettling in a way that stuck with me. More recently, while digging through some old philosophy and economics forum threads on utilitarianism, I kept running into mentions of something called "Harsanyi's aggregation theorem," usually brought up as a kind of counterpoint: if you instead let individuals have preferences over gambles rather than just fixed outcomes, and require the group's preferences to agree with everyone whenever everyone already agrees among themselves (a Pareto condition), you apparently get a clean positive result instead, with the group's own preferences representable as a weighted sum of everybody's individual utility functions. A few posters described this as Harsanyi finding a loophole around Arrow, or even as rescuing utilitarianism from the wreckage Arrow supposedly left behind.

That way of putting it has always struck me as a little too convenient, like winning a game by quietly changing its rules rather than beating your opponent at the original one. I'd like you to lay out carefully what is and isn't actually going on here. What, specifically, is it about Harsanyi's setup that allows him to reach a positive aggregation result where Arrow could not, given that both theorems appear, on the surface, to be about the same basic problem of turning many people's preferences into one social preference? Is it accurate to describe Harsanyi's theorem as "getting around" or "solving" the problem Arrow posed, or does that framing quietly assume the two theorems are competing answers to the same question when they are actually not? And even taken entirely on its own terms, does Harsanyi's theorem get you all the way to classical utilitarianism, where everybody's utility counts equally, or only to a looser family of weighted-utilitarian rules - and if it is the latter, what further assumption would be needed to close that gap? I would like a careful, step-by-step answer rather than a high-level gloss, and I would also appreciate it if you would end with your own judgment on which of the two theorems, if either, gives us better grounds for treating a group of people as though it had one coherent set of preferences of its own.
