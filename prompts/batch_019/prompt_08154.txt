I'm writing my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy on Henry Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, focusing on what he calls the dualism of practical reason: his admission that reason alone cannot show a fully rational egoist why the impartial demands of duty should ever outweigh self-interest. While researching, I learned that Sidgwick spent much of his adult life on a project that looks, at first glance, completely unrelated: he helped found the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, served as its first president, and personally investigated mediums and séances for decades, partly in the hope of finding empirical evidence bearing on personal immortality. My supervisor thinks this biographical detail doesn't really belong in a philosophy essay, but I suspect it matters more than that. Please describe the most important implications of Sidgwick's psychical research for how I should read his own unresolved verdict on the dualism at the end of the book, and specifically whether his decades of chasing empirical evidence for an afterlife suggest that he already suspected no purely philosophical argument could close the gap between duty and self-interest on its own.
