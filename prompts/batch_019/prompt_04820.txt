So I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole a while back after some commenter on an ACX open thread (or maybe it was a LessWrong comment, I don't fully remember) mentioned, almost as an aside, that George Berkeley — yes, the "esse est percipi" guy, the one everyone cites for the tree-falling-in-the-forest thing — spent alot of his later career writing about Irish monetary policy of all things. Turns out he wrote this whole tract called The Querist, structured as hundreds of numbered rhetorical questions (very weird format, but that's apparently just how he liked to argue), where he keeps circling back to the idea that money isn't wealth in itself, just a "ticket" or "counter" for reckoning and transferring power over other people's labor — and that Ireland's chronic coin shortage could be solved by a national bank issuing paper credit, since what actually drives prosperity is industry and circulation, not how much gold happens to be sitting in the country.

Here's the complication though: more than a decade before that, he'd written a much more moralizing pamphlet in the wake of the South Sea Bubble, basically blaming the crash on speculation, luxury, and a general collapse of public virtue. I might be fuzzy on some specifics since this is all filtered through a comment thread I read once, but I think the broad shape is right.

I keep trying to figure out how those two strands of Berkeley would react to crypto and I honestly can't decide. Does the Querist-era guy look at Bitcoin and go "sure, obviously, tickets and counters all the way down, I called this in 1735, the metal never mattered"? Or does the South-Sea-Bubble guy look at an unbacked, ungoverned, wildly speculative private currency with no national bank anywhere near it and see exactly the sort of scheme he warned Britain about? I don't want the cute one-liner answer where idealist-philosopher-therefore-obviously-loves-fake-internet-money — I want you to actually weigh the two strands of his own economic writing against each other and tell me which one wins and why, and ideally say something about where he'd land on central bank digital currencies too, since those seem like the more obvious modern descendant of the "national bank issuing tickets" idea than Bitcoin ever was. Also, separately from your actual answer: do you think this is a genuinely underdetermined question given what we know of his views, or is there a real fact of the matter here that a careful enough reading of his economic writing would settle?
