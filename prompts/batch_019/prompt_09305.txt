Here's the question they were given:

Causation by omission. Consider the claim "The gardener's failure to water the roses caused them to die."
(a) Explain how a counterfactual theory of causation, like Lewis's, would evaluate this claim, and say whether the failure to water comes out as a genuine cause on that view. State the general form of the theory you're relying on.
(b) Process/production theorists such as Phil Dowe deny that omissions can be genuine causes. Explain the reasoning behind that denial, and how such theorists try to account for why omission-talk still feels natural to us.
(c) A classic worry, the "problem of profuse omissions," points out that the roses' death also counterfactually depends on the Queen of England's failure to water them, my failure to water them, and so on. Explain this worry in your own words and say briefly whether you think it's a serious problem for the counterfactual theory.

And here's the students answer, typos and all:

(a) On a counterfactual theory of causation like Lewis's, C causes E just in case E counterfactually depends on C: if C hadn't happened, E wouldn't have happened either (holding the laws fixed and looking at the closest possible worlds). Formally, Lewis's theory says a cause is an insufficient but non-redundant part of a condition that is itself unnecessary but sufficient for the effect. Applied to the roses: if the gardener had watered them, they wouldn't have died, so the roses' death counterfactually depends on the failure to water them, and the omission comes out as a genuine cause. This is actually a spot where Lewis bites a bullet, since in his early work he was suspicious of treating "absences" as events, but by the time of essays like "Void and Object" he's willing to say omissions can be relata of the causal relation, because all that really matters for his theory is the pattern of dependence, not whether there's a genuine "thing" doing the causing.

(b) Process theorists like Dowe think that's the wrong verdict. On Dowe's conserved quantity theory, a causal process is a world-line of an object that carries a conserved quantity like energy or momentum, and causation happens when two such processes intersect and exchange some of that quantity. Since an omission isn't a process at all -- there's no object and no line to trace -- Dowe's theory can actually be extended to cover it directly: the missing watering is treated as a process transferring a zero magnitude of the relevant conserved quantity, which is what lets Dowe count it as a real, if limiting, case of physical causation instead of a merely apparent one. So on his view we don't have to give up the intuitive claim that the failure to water is a genuine cause, we just have to reinterpret what "transfer" means.

(c) The profuse omissions worry is that the counterfactual theory seems to overgenerate causes: the roses' death also depends counterfactually on the Queen of England not watering them, or on me not watering them, and so on for indefinitely many people who did nothing. But intuitively only the gardener's failure counts as the cause. I think this is a real cost for the counterfactual theory but not a fatal one -- Lewis can say all of those omissions are causes in the strict metaphysical sense, and that picking out the gardener's failure as the one worth mentioning is a seperate, pragmatic matter about normal conditions and background expectations, not a fact about causation itself. That divide between what's a cause and what's worth mentioning is basically Ned Hall's point that there are two different things we might mean by "cause," dependence and production, and a lot of this debate is really a disagreement about which concept is more central.
