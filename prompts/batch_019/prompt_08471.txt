I'm a PhD student in the history of economic thought, and I've spent the last few weeks buried in Book IV of the Wealth of Nations for a dissertation chapter on Smith's critique of the mercantile system. What strikes me is how much more textured his views on empire are than the "free trade good, tariffs bad" caricature I absorbed as an undergraduate. He is scathing about the East India Company's drift from a trading concern into the de facto sovereign of Bengal, arguing that a company of merchants makes an especially poor ruler because its directors' interest lies in extracting short-run profit rather than in the long-run prosperity of the people they govern. He also treats the whole system of colonial trade monopoly as a scheme that serves a narrow class of merchants and manufacturers at the expense of the wider public, who pay higher prices and foot the bill for colonial wars. And rather than simply cheerleading for empire, he seriously entertains two alternatives for Britain and its American colonies: voluntary separation, or a genuine parliamentary union in which colonial representation would be set in proportion to each colony's contribution to tax revenue - a scheme under which he predicts the empire's political center of gravity would eventually have to shift across the Atlantic as the colonies grew to outweigh the mother country.

A friend of mine in international relations and I have a running disagreement about whether any of this machinery bears on present-day accusations that Chinese overseas infrastructure lending under the Belt and Road Initiative amounts to a new "debt-trap diplomacy" - saddling smaller, poorer states with loans they cannot service and then converting the resulting leverage into long-term control of strategic assets, the standard example being Sri Lanka's 99-year lease of Hambantota Port to a Chinese state-owned firm after its government could no longer manage the debt. She thinks the parallel to the East India Company's creeping takeover of Bengal is close to exact; I'm skeptical, partly because Belt and Road lending isn't bundled with a formal trade monopoly or a company fielding its own army, and partly because the borrowing states remain juridically sovereign in a way Bengal never was under Company rule.

Rather than just gesturing at Smith's general enthusiasm for free trade, I'd like you to reconstruct, as precisely as you can from his actual arguments about colonial monopoly, chartered companies, and the balance of costs and benefits of empire, how he would likely referee this disagreement. Would he see Belt and Road-style lending as recognizably the same species of problem he diagnosed in the East India Company and in Britain's own colonial trade restrictions, or would he insist on a sharper distinction than my friend allows? And either way, what would his diagnosis imply about what the borrowing states, or the lending power, ought to do differently?
