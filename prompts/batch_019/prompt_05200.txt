I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole reading old "meaning of life" essays for a philosophy reading group I'm part of, and I keep coming back to Frank Ramsey's "Epilogue" (the closing chapter of his Foundations of Mathematics essay collection), where he lays out his own personal, pretty irreligious outlook on life -- funny enough given his brother literally ended up Archbishop of Canterbury. There's this bit where he says he pictures the world in perspective rather than like a scale model: people and the things we care about looming large in the foreground, the stars shrunk down to something like the size of threepenny bits, because he just flatly refuses to feel humbled by how physically big the universe is. It reminded me a lot of Thomas Nagel's essay "The Absurd," which also shoots down the standard "we're tiny specks doomed to vanish" argument for why life is absurd, on the grounds that if we were bigger, or lived forever, the exact same puzzle would just show back up in a different form -- so size and duration can't really be doing the work. Except Nagel still ends up concluding life IS absurd, just for a totally different reason (something about the clash between how seriously we take our own lives from the inside and our weird capacity to step outside and view them as arbitrary), whereas Ramsey comes across as basically at peace with everything, not absurdist at all, just cheerfully human-scaled about the whole thing.

So could you compare these two views for me in some depth? I'm mainly curious whether Ramsey and Nagel are actually making the same underlying move when they each dismiss the "we're small and we die" argument, and only diverge afterwards, or whether they're rejecting it for different reasons right from the start. And then, since I honestly can't decide for myself: whose overall response to cosmic-insignificance worries do you think actually holds up better -- Ramsey's shrug or Nagel's irony? Don't just lay out both sides neutrally, actually pick one and defend it.
