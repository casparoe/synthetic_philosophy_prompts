I'm working on a longer piece about whether large language models actually understand anything they're saying, or whether "understanding" is even the right concept to reach for with them, and it sent me down a rabbit hole into some old philosophy of language stuff — specifically Donald Davidson's idea of "radical interpretation." The setup is basically: drop a field linguist in front of a speaker with zero shared history, no dictionary, no bilingual helper, nothing to go on but the noises the speaker makes and the situations they make them in. Davidson's claim is that you can still, in principle, work out what the speaker means and believes, but only by leaning on a "principle of charity" — you assume they're mostly rational and mostly saying true things — and you have to solve for their beliefs and their word-meanings together, as a package, since you can't read either one off the evidence on its own. The part that really got me is his wierder follow-on move, that this basically rules out a truly alien way of thinking: if something's different enough from us that no amount of charitable interpreting turns up real overlap in truth and rationality, Davidson says there's no "mind" or "language" there to speak of, we just haven't hit on the right interpretation yet — massive, systematic error isn't something you can pin on someone, its evidence that you mistranslated them.

Anyway, I've read so many summaries of this by now that it's turned into mush in my head, and I'd rather see it actually working, dramatized, than explained at me one more time. Could you write me a short story — first contact with aliens, a newly switched-on AI, a diplomat stuck with a machine that refuses to use human words, whatever setting works best, something in the spirit of the linguist sections of a good first-contact story — where a character has to do this kind of radical interpretation cold, on a genuinely unfamiliar "speaker," and where the real guts of the problem are what the plot actually hinges on: the charity assumption doing load-bearing work, the belief/meaning package deal, the live chance that two totally different interpretations fit the evidence equally well with nothing left over to decide between them, and that uncomfortable idea that "too alien to interpret" might just mean "not a mind at all." I don't want Davidson name-dropped in one line of dialogue and left at that, and please don't let it resolve into a neat "we cracked the code" ending — I want it to actually sit with the uncomfortable part of the theory instead of resolving it away.
