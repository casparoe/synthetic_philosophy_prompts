I spent my career as an entomologist working on parasitoid wasps, and only in retirement have I begun reading moral philosophy in earnest. This combination has left me stuck on a question Darwin lacked the vocabulary to even pose. In his correspondence with Asa Gray, Darwin famously confessed that he could not square a benevolent, designing God with the existence of the Ichneumonidae, whose larvae consume caterpillars alive from within, and he took the sheer scale of suffering built into predation and parasitism as genuine evidence against providential design, consistent with his broader insistence that natural selection was an unplanned, undirected process. I'd like you to work out, from Darwin's actual documented views rather than his general reputation, how he would have responded to negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics — the position that reducing suffering deserves priority, possibly even lexical priority, over promoting happiness — and, in particular, to the conclusion some suffering-focused thinkers now draw from it: that humans should deliberately intervene in wild ecosystems, through fertility control, habitat modification, or similar means, to reduce the enormous suffering wild animals inflict on one another. Would his anguish over the Ichneumonidae have pushed him toward endorsing such interventions, or would his caution as a naturalist, and his sense of nature as an interdependent system not to be lightly tampered with, have made him resist that conclusion? Please give a definite answer rather than a noncommittal survey of possibilities.
