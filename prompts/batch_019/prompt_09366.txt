The following is from the Note and Addition to §324 of Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* (1820), in the section on the state's external sovereignty, immediately after Hegel has argued that individuals have a duty to sacrifice property and even life for the state's independence (S. W. Dyde translation, 1896):

"War is not to be regarded as an absolute evil. It is not a merely external accident, having its accidental ground in the passions of powerful individuals or nations, in acts of injustice, or in anything which ought not to be. Accident befalls that which is by nature accidental, and this fate is a necessity. ... It is necessary that what is finite, such as life and property, should have its contingent nature exposed, since contingency is inherent in the conception of the finite. ... Necessity becomes in this way exalted to the work of freedom, and becomes a force which is ethical. ... [F]inite pursuits are rendered unstable, and the ethical health of peoples is preserved. Just as the movement of the ocean prevents the corruption which would be the result of perpetual calm, so by war people escape the corruption which would be occasioned by a continuous or eternal peace."

Addition: "...the state is individual, and in individuality negation is essentially implied. Although a number of states may make themselves into a family, the union, because it is an individuality, must create an opposition, and so beget an enemy."

Two distinct arguments seem to be at work here. The Note grounds the necessity of war in "the conception of the finite" itself: because contingency belongs to the very concept of anything finite, that contingency must eventually be made actual rather than merely conceded in the abstract. The Addition, arguing against Kant's proposed league of states, instead appeals to a claim about individuality as such — that "in individuality negation is essentially implied" — so that even a federation of states, being itself an individual, must generate an opposition and "beget an enemy." That second claim looks like a straightforward application of Hegel's general logical doctrine that a determinate singular thing achieves its determinacy only by excluding some other, a doctrine that should hold of a world-federation exactly as much as of any single state. Yet elsewhere in the book Hegel treats the analogous problem among persons — who are likewise individuated only through mutually exclusive claims in Abstract Right — as resolved rather than perpetuated once those persons are made subject to a common sovereign. So is Hegel entitled to treat the impossibility of perpetual peace as following from the logic of individuality as such, or is the real work in the Addition being done by the much more mundane, broadly Hobbesian fact that states, unlike persons, answer to no common power? And if it's the latter, does anything in Hegel's own framework rule out a genuine world-state eventually doing for the community of nations what the state already does for the individuals within it?
