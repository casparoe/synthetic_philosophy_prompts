I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, and Good's theorem came up in our decision theory reading group this week in a way that left me wanting more than I currently have. As I understand it, I. J. Good showed in a short 1967 paper, "On the Principle of Total Evidence," that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can never expect, ex ante, that cost-free information will leave them worse off than deciding without it, provided they update correctly and remain free to act on whatever posterior they end up with.

I can follow the standard proof: the value of acting optimally on a given belief state is a convex function of that belief, since it is the upper envelope of the functions -- one per available action -- that are linear in the probabilities, and so, by Jensen's inequality, the expectation taken before the experiment of this convex function evaluated at the (random) posterior is at least as large as the same function evaluated at the prior, given that posteriors average back to the prior. I gather this is closely related to, but not quite the same as, Blackwell's theorem on comparing statistical experiments. What I don't yet have is a way of narrating any of this to a first-year without putting a single inequality on the board.

Could you help me build that intuitive, gut-level version -- something that makes it feel almost obvious that the value of information cannot be negative, tied to the fact that one always retains the option of ignoring what one learns? And, separately, I would like help pinning down exactly which premise of Good's setup is doing the work in the cases where the "information can't hurt" slogan is famously said to fail: strategic settings in which revealing information to, or about, other agents can destroy gains from trade or bargaining surplus; cases where merely knowing something carries direct utility or disutility apart from any decision it informs; and cases of imperfect self-command, where an agent cannot be trusted to actually use new information the way their earlier self would have wanted them to. A friend in my cohort thinks these are three genuinely distinct failure modes, while I suspect they all cash out as violations of the same background assumption in Good's argument. I would be curious which of us is closer to right.
