Popular discussions of Bell's theorem typically compress the result into a slogan: quantum mechanics is "nonlocal," or entangled particles exhibit "spooky action at a distance." This gets the moral roughly right but hides the logical structure that makes the theorem philosophically interesting, since deriving a Bell inequality does not rest on a single "locality" premise but on several independent assumptions that can each be denied separately. Following a decomposition due to Jarrett and Shimony, the usual locality condition splits into parameter independence (the statistics of the outcome on one wing of the experiment do not depend on the distant measurement setting) and outcome independence (the statistics of the outcome on one wing do not depend on the distant outcome, conditional on the complete state of the particle pair); a further, often tacit, premise is measurement independence, sometimes called the "free choice" assumption (the complete state of the particle pair is statistically independent of the settings the experimenters will later choose). Every interpretive strategy on the market can be classified by which of these it sacrifices: orthodox quantum mechanics, taking the quantum state itself as the complete description, keeps parameter independence (consistent with the no-signaling theorem) but violates outcome independence; Bohmian mechanics keeps outcome independence but builds an explicit, faster-than-light dependence on the distant setting into the guidance equation, violating parameter independence; superdeterminists deny measurement independence, holding that correlations between experimenters' choices and particle states trace back to a shared causal past; and many-worlds proponents contend that once one drops the assumption that a measurement yields a single, determinate outcome, the remaining unitary dynamics can be told as an entirely local story.

The loophole-free Bell tests of 2015 closed off the two main experimental objections (the locality and detection loopholes), so the choice among these strategies is no longer plausibly a matter of waiting for better data; it is a live interpretive question. Each option has a real cost. Superdeterminism seems to undercut the statistical reasoning that all of experimental science, not just this family of experiments, depends on. Bohmian nonlocality sits uneasily with the relativistic prohibition on preferred frames of simultaneity, even though the theory reproduces the empirical predictions of orthodox quantum mechanics exactly. Many-worlds buys locality at the price of a much larger ontology, and critics contend that its story about probability and self-location remains unfinished business. And declining to add anything to orthodox quantum mechanics leaves outcome independence violated without any deeper explanation of why the world behaves that way.

Write an essay that weighs these four strategies against one another and defends a specific verdict about what Bell's theorem, together with its experimental confirmation, actually establishes about locality as a feature of the physical world. Do not settle for an even-handed survey: after laying out what each option costs and buys, commit to a position, whether that is "the theorem shows that locality, in a well-defined sense, must be abandoned," a defense of one particular escape route as preferable to the others, or some other precisely stated and argued verdict.
