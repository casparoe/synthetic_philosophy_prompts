I work as a backend engineer, and a few months ago a small reading group at my company -- mostly other engineers, plus a friend who is finishing a physics PhD -- worked through parts of Tim Maudlin's book *Philosophy of Physics: Space and Time*. The chapter that has stuck with me since is the one on the "hole argument," John Earman and John Norton's argument against spacetime substantivalism. As I understand the setup: you take a solution of Einstein's field equations, pick a matter-free "hole" region, and apply a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere outside the hole but smoothly scrambles things inside it; dragging the metric field along this map gives you a second solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole but disagrees with it on the metric inside. If spacetime points are genuine individuals whose identity doesn't depend on which fields they happen to carry, the two models seem to describe two distinct possible worlds that are indistinguishable from outside the hole -- so the field equations plus a complete specification of matter fail to fix what happens inside it. That struck me as a strange price to pay just for believing spacetime points are real, freestanding things.

What I've never managed to pin down is how the two standard substantivalist replies to this actually differ from each other. Maudlin's own response, which I've since chased down in a couple of secondary sources since the book itself doesn't dwell on it, is to say that a spacetime point has its metrical properties essentially, so there is no possible world in which that very point carries the other metric -- the "second" model was never an alternative fate for the same points to begin with. But there's a rival substantivalist line from Jeremy Butterfield and Carolyn Brighouse that instead borrows David Lewis's counterpart theory to deny that spacetime points have well-defined identities across models at all, so again the two diffeomorphic models were never rival descriptions of the same points. Confusingly, both of these get filed under the label "sophisticated substantivalism" in everything I've read, which makes me suspect they're doing the same job with different machinery. My instinct as a programmer is that they're actually quite different moves: one sounds like saying an object's identity is baked into its state, so it can't survive being re-described with different state at all; the other sounds more like admitting there was never a stable pointer to the object in the first place, only a bundle of structural roles that different models happen to fill in different ways.

Could you lay out precisely how these two responses differ in what they concede to Earman and Norton and what they manage to block, ideally by identifying exactly which assumption of the hole argument each one gives up? And once that's clear, which of the two do you think is the more defensible way for a substantivalist to resist the indeterminism conclusion, and why? I would also genuinely like to know whether my identity-baked-into-state-versus-no-stable-pointer analogy tracks the real philosophical difference between the two positions, or whether it's obscuring something important.
