Roughly, it went like this. (1) Wes opened by saying the bombing is basically a textbook double-effect case: the operative intention was to knock out Japan's war production and force a fast surrender, which on net saves lives once you count the alternative (invasion, or a starvation blockade), and the huge civilian death toll was foreseen but not intended, just a horrible side effect of hitting industrial targets that happened to sit inside cities. (2) Naomi said that's not what double effect licenses, and it's not even the argument Anscombe is making - the deaths weren't a side effect at all, they were the mechanism: the whole reason to hit a live, intact city instead of, say, the demonstration option that was actually floated internally beforehand and rejected, was to produce a body count and a shock big enough to break the Japanese government's will. If mass death is how the plan works, its the means, not a side effect, and double effect never gets off the ground - by Anscombe's lights that's just murder in service of a policy. (3) Wes came back with: you can always redescribe an intention narrowly enough to dodge that move - his "operative intention" was just "destroy this HQ, destroy this production," full stop, and the shock is a further foreseen-but-unintended consequence of doing that suddenly and totally. Doesn't that trick work for literally any double-effect case, which would make the whole doctrine worthless as an actual constraint rather than a license to call anything a side effect? (4) Naomi granted that's a real problem for double effect in general, but said you're not stuck accepting any redescription at face value - you ask whether the plan would even make sense, would even have been chosen, without the mass civilian destruction, and the actual targeting criteria (cities were picked partly for not having been firebombed yet already, so the damage would be dramatic and legible) are evidence that visible mass destruction was load-bearing for the theory of the case, not incidental to it. (5) Wes conceded the history probably cuts her way, but said that only shows double effect doesn't apply here, not that the bombing was wrong - you can still run a plain lesser-evil argument, seperate from double effect entirely, that a terrible act was justified because it prevented a worse one, so all Naomi's actually done is close off one defense of Truman, not prove Anscombe's actual conclusion. (6) Naomi said that's exactly where Anscombe stops being subtle: she thinks intentionally killing innocents as a means is absolutely off the table no matter how the arithmetic comes out, and thought a philosophical culture willing to wave that away "if the sums are big enough" had gone rotten in a specific way - which is apparently also why she's the one credited with sticking the label "consequentialism" onto that whole style of reasoning in the first place, as an insult.
