In my school's philosophy of religion elective, we recently spent a couple of weeks on Augustine's account of evil: his claim that evil is not a substance in its own right but a privation of the good, and his related view that both moral and natural evil trace back to the Fall of Adam and Eve. Our teacher walked us through the Genesis passage where the ground itself is cursed on account of Adam's sin, together with Paul's remark in Romans that the whole created order was subjected to futility and is waiting to be freed from decay, to illustrate just how far Augustine and the tradition following him were willing to go: on this view, physical nature itself became corrupted by human sin, not merely human souls.

A few of us got stuck on this after class, because that same week in AP Biology we had been covering the fossil record, and it is fairly uncontroversial at this point that predation, parasitism, disease, and mass extinction go back many hundreds of millions of years, long before anything resembling a historical Adam and Eve could have existed to sin and set this corruption in motion. In other words, all the "natural evil" Augustine wants to explain as a consequence of the Fall appears to have been firmly in place well before there was any human wrongdoing for it to be a consequence of. I would be curious to know how you think Augustine himself, reasoning only from commitments he actually held (the privation theory, his sometimes surprisingly non-literal readings of Genesis, and whatever else was genuinely available to him) rather than from anything a modern theologian might say on his behalf, would try to respond if this timeline were put to him directly. Does his framework contain any real resources for absorbing this problem, or does the fossil record simply break the Fall-based explanation of natural evil beyond repair?
