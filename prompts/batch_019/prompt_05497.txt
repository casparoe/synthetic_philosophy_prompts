This term's History and Philosophy of Science seminar paired Galileo's 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina with Bellarmine's 1615 reply to Foscarini. In our discussion section, two classmates, Marcus and Elena, ended up arguing about what Galileo was actually claiming.

Marcus said Galileo's real position is a proto-"non-overlapping magisteria": scripture teaches salvation, not astronomy, so any passage that sounds like a claim about nature was never really asserting a physical fact -- it's accommodated language for ordinary readers who couldn't follow a demonstration. Scripture and natural philosophy can't conflict, on this view, because they were never about the same subject matter.

Elena disagreed: Galileo insists scripture is wholly true even where it touches the physical world; the accommodation is in the phrasing, not the content. That's why he leans so heavily on Augustine, whose rule is not "scripture doesn't address nature" but "when a demonstrated truth seems to conflict with a scriptural passage, we've misread the passage." Nature and scripture are both true and both about the same world.

Marcus replied that this only relocates the problem: if interpretation always bends to whatever's been "demonstrated," scripture never actually gets the last word on any factual question, which is functionally identical to having no jurisdiction over nature at all -- "harmony" is just a nicer name for "separate spheres."

Elena answered that it isn't functionally identical, because the bending is gated behind a demanding, rarely-satisfied condition: genuine demonstration, not conjecture. That, she said, is exactly why Bellarmine's caution to Foscarini wasn't a rejection of Galileo's principle but an application of it -- Bellarmine would have granted the reinterpretation too; he just didn't think Copernicus had met the bar yet.

Marcus's comeback was that Galileo pushed heliocentrism well before anyone had anything resembling proof -- no detected stellar parallax, no adequate physics of a moving earth, a tidal argument that turned out to be wrong -- so his own "wait for genuine demonstration" rule was one he wasn't prepared to follow himself.

Elena's response was that overreaching on the evidence and abandoning the principle are different failures: a judge who wrongly convicts someone hasn't thereby rejected the "beyond reasonable doubt" standard.

Neither had budged by the time section ended. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move -- who has the better of it at each step, not just overall -- and say what, if anything, both of them are missing about Galileo's actual position?
