And here's the answer one student turned in (anonymized):

"Gödel's argument works with a handful of primitives. A property is 'positive' if it's the kind of property a maximally perfect being would have (Gödel leaves this mostly undefined, treating it as a theological primitive). Something is 'God-like' if it has, as essential properties, exactly the positive ones and none of the negative ones. An essence of x is a property that x has and that necessitates every other property x has. And x has 'necessary existence' if its essence is necessarily exemplified by something.

The axioms are: (1) for every property, either it or its negation is positive, but never both; (2) any property that's necessarily entailed by a positive property is also positive; (3) being God-like is itself a positive property; (4) positive properties are positive as a matter of necessity, not just as it happens; and (5) existence is a positive property, which is really just Gödel restating Descartes' old premise that existence is a perfection in more careful modal dress.

From (3) and a lemma that positive properties are self-consistent (i.e. possibly instantiated), we get that it's possible that a God-like being exists. Axioms (1) and (2) then let you show that if a being is God-like, God-likeness is that being's essence — because for every property, the being either has it essentially (if positive) or lacks it essentially (if the negation is positive), so nothing about it is left contingent. Since necessary existence is among the positive properties, it belongs to the essence of any God-like being, which by definition means that being's essence is necessarily exemplified. Putting the possibility premise together with this and running it through S5 (roughly: if it's possible that something necessarily exists, then it necessarily exists) gets you the conclusion: necessarily, a God-like being exists.

The best-known objection here isn't Kant's, since Gödel isn't smuggling existence in as a first-order predicate the way Anselm and Descartes do — the sharper problem is the 'modal collapse' objection, first pressed by Alvin Plantinga. Because axiom (1) assigns every property or its negation to the being's essence, and that essence is now guaranteed to be necessarily exemplified, every property the world actually happens to have turns out to be built into something that had to exist all along. Trace this through and it looks like every truth, not just facts about God, ends up necessary — contingency disappears from the picture entirely, which is a strange result for a proof to deliver, since it erases the very modal distinctions the argument leans on to get off the ground. Defenders like Anderson have tried to rescue the argument by redefining 'positive' more restrictively so this doesn't fall out, but the fix can feel more like a patch than a principled reply. On balance I don't think the objection shows the argument is invalid, but it makes swallowing the premises a lot more costly than they first look."
