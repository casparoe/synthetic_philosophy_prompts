For a piece on protracted corporate disputes, I've been following a legal fight between two former business partners over control of a small manufacturing company. What began as a disagreement over a $300,000 buyout offer has, after five years of litigation, cost both sides a combined $4 million in legal fees, with no resolution in sight. Off the record, each side admits the original stakes stopped mattering long ago: neither can bear letting the other be seen to have won after so much has already been spent, and each is still waiting for the other to crack first. Their own lawyers, meanwhile, insist that stopping now would be the truly foolish move, given how much has already been committed. I suspect this fits a broader pattern rather than simple stubbornness. Is there ever a genuinely rational reason to keep pouring resources into a fight once its costs have dwarfed the original prize, or does the logic of such situations guarantee an irrational outcome no matter how sound each individual decision looks at the time? And what, practically, would it take to break the cycle once it is underway?
