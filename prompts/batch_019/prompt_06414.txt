Hey -- I'm prepping a short talk for our department's history-of-philosophy reading group next month (it's a pretty informal series, mostly grad students plus a couple of retired colleagues who show up for the biscuits), and I want to sanity-check the central move before I turn it into slides. I'm not a Sidgwick specialist -- I mostly teach the survey course and got pulled into this while prepping a lecture -- so its entirely possible I'm about to say something embarrassing in front of the one person in the room whose actually read the Memoir cover to cover.

The standard line on Sidgwick's involvement with the Society for Psychical Research is that it's basically a footnote: eminent utilitarian has an odd Victorian hobby, chases mediums around on the side, mostly irrelevant to the actual philosophy. My notes argue against that. The claim is that the SPR work is a continuation, by other means, of unfinished business at the end of the Methods of Ethics. He gets to the end of the book, lays out the dualism of practical reason -- egoism and universal hedonism each look fully rational from the inside, and he can't find a proof that ranks one over the other -- and says the only thing that could really close that gap, rather than just leave us living with it, is some further fact about the world: a moral order in which duty and self-interest actually coincide, which is basically a Kantian postulate move done in Sidgwick's own more empiricist idiom. Only he'd already resigned his fellowship back in 1869 over not being able to honestly assent to doctrine, so just helping himself to that fact on faith isn't really available to him anymore. So he spends the rest of his life trying to get the same fact a different way: as actual evidence, via the SPR, that consciousness survives death and that the universe has the kind of moral structure his ethics needs so that practical reason doesn't just bottom out in two equally rational but incompatible demands. On this reading the ghost stuff isn't a hobby running alongside the epistemology, it's the same rigor aimed at the one question his official philosophy left dangling.

Does that basically hold up, or am I forcing a tidier story onto two interests that were really more separate than that? I'd like comments on whether this is roughly the right way to read the connection, and if so whether the notes overstate or understate it; any complications from Sidgwick's own writing (or the secondary literature on him) that you think I should flag before I say this out loud to people who'd know; and, separately from the accuracy question, whether you think this is actually an interesting enough point to build twenty minutes of talk around or whether it's old news to anyone who already knows the Sidgwick literature.
