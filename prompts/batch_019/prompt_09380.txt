Two of my grad students got into a proper argument after our virtue ethics seminar last week and I still haven't managed to settle it in my own head. (1) Leah said the Darley & Batson "Good Samaritan" study basically sinks Aristotelian virtue ethics: if whether a seminary student stops to help a groaning stranger in a doorway comes down to whether he'd just been told he's five minutes late, rather than to any stable trait of compassion, then "compassion" isn't the robust, cross-situational disposition virtue ethics needs it to be. (2) Owen said that's no suprise to an Aristotelian, since Aristotle already held that full virtue is rare and takes years of habituation -- most people are merely continent or incontinent at best, so of course a bunch of twentysomethings in a hurry aren't paragons of compassion. (3) Leah shot back that this move is suspiciously convenient: if no amount of behavioral evidence could ever count against the existence of stable character, then the theory's been innoculated against disconfirmation entirely. (4) Owen answered that it's not ad hoc, since the rarity claim is sitting right there in the Nicomachean Ethics centuries before anyone ran an experiment, and anyway the real Aristotelian claim was never about behavioral consistency but about practical wisdom picking out the salient features of a situation -- so the hurry manipulation just shows most people lack phronesis, not that stable traits are a myth. (5) Leah's last word: fine, but if virtue takes that much, almost nobody qualifies, so what use is the theory to the rest of us actually trying to act well?

Go through this move by move -- who's got the better of it at each stage, not just an overall winner -- and tell me what you think both of them are still missing.
