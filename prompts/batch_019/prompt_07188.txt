A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about what emotions actually are. She was defending the view that an emotion like fear is at bottom an evaluative judgment (roughly, judging that something is dangerous); I was defending the view that it's at bottom a feeling of bodily states. I cited William James's 1884 essay "What Is an Emotion?" in my own support, since I remembered it as the classic statement that the feeling of bodily change simply is the emotion. Rereading the relevant passage this morning, though, I'm no longer sure it does the work I want it to. Here it is:

"Our natural way of thinking about these standard emotions is that the mental perception of some fact excites the mental affection called the emotion, and that this latter state of mind gives rise to the bodily expression. My thesis on the contrary is that the bodily changes follow directly the PERCEPTION of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur IS the emotion. Common sense says, we lose our fortune, are sorry and weep; we meet a bear, are frightened and run; we are insulted by a rival, are angry and strike. The hypothesis here to be defended says that this order of sequence is incorrect, that the one mental state is not immediately induced by the other, that the bodily manifestations must first be interposed between, and that the more rational statement is that we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful, as the case may be. Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

"I now proceed to urge the vital point of my whole theory, which is this. If we fancy some strong emotion, and then try to abstract from our consciousness of it all the feelings of its characteristic bodily symptoms, we find we have nothing left behind, no 'mind-stuff' out of which the emotion can be constituted, and that a cold and neutral state of intellectual perception is all that remains. ... It is the purely speculative one of subtracting certain elements of feeling from an emotional state supposed to exist in its fulness, and saying what the residual elements are. ... What kind of an emotion of fear would be left, if the feelings neither of quickened heart-beats nor of shallow breathing, neither of trembling lips nor of weakened limbs, neither of goose-flesh nor of visceral stirrings, were present, it is quite impossible to think. Can one fancy the state of rage and picture no ebullition of it in the chest, no flushing of the face, no dilatation of the nostrils, no clenching of the teeth, no impulse to vigorous action, but in their stead limp muscles, calm breathing, and a placid face? The present writer, for one, certainly cannot. The rage is as completely evaporated as the sensation of its so-called manifestations, and the only thing that can possibly be supposed to take its place is some cold-blooded and dispassionate judicial sentence, confined entirely to the intellectual realm, to the effect that a certain person or persons merit chastisement for their sins. ... A purely disembodied human emotion is a nonentity. ... emotion dissociated from all bodily feeling is inconceivable."

Three things I'd like help untangling, rather than the standard textbook gloss on the James–Lange theory:

1. When James says the feeling of bodily changes "IS" the emotion, is that a strict identity claim, or something weaker — e.g., that the feeling is merely a necessary constituent? Does the answer matter for whether this is really a "feeling" theory of emotion, as opposed to a "perception" theory, given that what's felt is itself described as a perception of bodily changes?
2. Is the subtraction argument in the second paragraph actual evidence for his thesis, or does it just assume what's in question — that once the bodily feelings are stripped away, the remaining cognitive appraisal is merely fainter, rather than a different kind of state altogether (i.e., not fear at all, just a judgment)? My friend could grant that the residue is "cold" and still deny this shows that feeling, rather than something like urgency or full-blooded assent, is what made the original state emotional.
3. Does anything here let James explain why the resulting bodily commotion counts as fear of the bear specifically, rather than a bodily upheaval that merely happens to coincide with seeing one? If not, is that a point in my friend's favor, since judgments have objects built into them and raw feelings arguably don't?

Did either of us actually have James on our side, or does this passage leave the dispute between a feeling theory and a judgment theory of emotion genuinely open?
