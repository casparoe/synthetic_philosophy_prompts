I'm two years into a novel set in a fictional cotton-mill town in the Blackstone Valley, loosely inspired by the one my grandmother grew up in, though every character and event in it is invented. The book hinges on a chapter in which my protagonist, the town's bank president, declines to renew the mill's operating loan during one bad winter, and the mill shuts within the month, taking the town's only real employer down with it. Everyone in my writing group who has read the draft wants him to answer for this; they talk about him as though he'd pushed someone off a roof, even though all he actually did was decline to sign a piece of paper.

That reaction is exactly what I was aiming for, dramatically speaking. But a friend who is finishing a doctorate in economic history read the same chapters and told me, kindly but firmly, that I'm cheating: cotton mills across New England were failing that same winter regardless of what any individual lender decided, undercut by cheaper non-union mills in the South and a credit contraction no local banker could have offset on his own, so "my" mill was almost certainly finished within a year or two no matter what he decided that winter. She thinks that by hanging the town's ruin on one man's signature, I'm handing readers a comforting, false picture of how that kind of collapse actually happens, and letting them aim a very personal verdict at someone who, on her account, barely moved the outcome at all.

I can't just dismiss this, because she's obviously right that no single decision caused the regional collapse of an entire industry. But her argument unsettles me from the other direction too: if it's enough to show that his choice made no real difference, the same reasoning seems like it would excuse almost anyone's choices during almost any large historical disaster, which would mean nobody is ever quite responsible for what happens in hard times — and that feels like too easy an exit, for him and for history generally. Before I go back to the manuscript, I'd like help thinking this through properly. Is she right that his decision didn't matter in the way my draft assumes? Is there a flaw in how she moves from "the industry's decline was bigger than any one man" to "so this particular choice made no difference and deserves no blame"? And if both things can be true at once, what would actually writing that look like on the page?
