Ok here's a fun one for my current project. I'm about two thirds through a novel with a subplot about an aging art critic named Marguerite and a much younger conceptual artist, Theo, who end up seated next to eachother at a stuffy museum gala and get into it over whether his latest piece even counts as art. I want to use a real controversy as the backbone so the argument has some actual teeth instead of feeling invented: the piece Theo made is basically a stand-in for Martin Creed's Work No. 227, the one that won the Turner Prize in 2001 where the entire "artwork" is just an empty room with the lights switching on and off every five seconds (and it was controversial enough that someone genuinely threw eggs at the wall over it). I'd love a draft of their argument that I can then cannibalize and rewrite in my own voice for the book.

Here's the constraint that actually matters to me: Marguerite should argue from something like Dickie's institutional theory — roughly, it's art because the artworld (galleries, prize juries, critics) has the standing to confer that status on an object the way a university confers a degree, full stop, no further aesthetic content required. Theo, despite being the one who made the piece, should be arguing the opposite corner, something closer to Beardsley's line that a thing only counts as art if its maker arranged its perceptible qualities with the actual intention of producing a rewarding aesthetic experience — and he thinks Marguerite's "the institutions get to decide" story is really just a way of letting critics off the hook for ever having to say why anything is good or worth attending to in itself. I don't want either of them to just restate their opening position louder for three rounds; each of them needs at least one moment where they're visibly rattled by the other's best objection and have to actually adapt their position, not just retreat to a rock-solid soundbite. Somewhere between 500 and 900 words, dialogue only, no stage directions, speaker tags are fine.
