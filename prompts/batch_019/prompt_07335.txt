Been on a self-locating-belief kick lately (the Sleeping Beauty threads on LW dragged me back into it), and it keeps leading me back to what I think of as the weirder, older cousin of that problem: Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver. Quick recap in case its not fresh in your training data -- a driver is on a highway with two indistinguishable intersections in a row. Exiting at the first gets him a payoff of 0, continuing past the first and exiting at the second gets him 4, and continuing past both (missing his exit entirely) gets him 1. Because of the "imperfect recall" thing he can't tell the two intersections apart, so whatever probability x of "continue" he picks, he's stuck using that same x at both. Maximized from the standpoint of before he sets off (the "planning-optimal" strategy), this works out to x* = 2/3.

Here's the bit that still bugs me. There's a whole follow-up literature (Aumann, Hart and Perry wrote the best-known response) on what happens if the driver instead tries to reason it out on the spot -- updating his belief, the normal Bayesian way, about whether "this" is the first intersection or the second, conditional on him actually using strategy x. Do that and you get something like 3/5 confidence it's the first one and 2/5 it's the second, not the fifty-fifty your gut wants to say. And it's genuinely not obvious that maximizing expected payoff relative to that updated belief has to spit back out x = 2/3, or that the whole "update, then re-optimize" loop even settles on a well-behaved unique answer at all.

So what I want is an actual essay out of this, not a wiki-style summary of the papers. Take a real stand on whether this is a genuine problem for standard Bayesian/expected-utility decision theory -- planning-optimality and act-optimality just permanently part ways for agents with imperfect recall, full stop -- or whether it's a merely apparent paradox that dissolves once you insist self-locating credence has to be pinned down as a fixed point of the very strategy it's evaluating, rather than some free-floating prior you compute first and plug in after. Bring in other resolutions too if you've got them, don't feel artificially restricted to just those two. If your answer is "it depends," fine, but say exactly what it depends on and where you personally land -- no wishy-washy "reasonable people disagree" ending.
