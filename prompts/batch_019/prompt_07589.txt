I've spent the last couple of weeks working through Dana Scott's tidied-up presentation of Gödel's ontological argument for a philosophy of religion seminar, and I keep running into the same difficulty that apparently stopped several commentators before me: the "modal collapse" objection usually traced back to Howard Sobel. I can follow the individual moves of the proof well enough — that positive properties are closed under necessary implication, that being God-like is itself positive, that God-likeness is thereby an essence of anything that instantiates it, that necessary existence is positive too, and that S5 then lets a bare possibility be pushed all the way to necessity. I can even reconstruct, step by step, the further derivation showing that once an arbitrary actually-true proposition is allowed to count as a trivial property of the God-like individual, the whole apparatus forces that proposition to be necessarily true — and since the proposition was arbitrary, this holds for every truth whatsoever, so that contingency collapses entirely.

What I'm missing is a gut-level sense of why this has to happen, rather than a symbol-by-symbol confirmation that it does. If you had to point to the single piece of machinery in the axioms that is really doing the damage, as opposed to treating collapse as an unlucky quirk of this particular derivation, what would you say it is? And, at that same intuitive level rather than in fully formal terms, do you think C. Anthony Anderson's proposed emendations to the axioms — restricting what can count as a positive property and revising how essences are defined — genuinely remove that piece of machinery, or do they mainly relocate the problem somewhere it is harder to notice? I would rather come away with a clear picture of the underlying culprit than with another derivation to check line by line.
