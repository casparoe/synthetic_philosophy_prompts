I've been reporting a piece on the ethics of online pile-ons -- cases where a company reverses a decision, or a private citizen loses a job, after a wave of collective online pressure that no single person appears to have organized. While background-reading for it, I ended up refereeing an argument between two academics I'd interviewed separately for the piece: a historian of nineteenth-century print culture (call her Ana) and a Kierkegaard specialist (call him Erik). Both had independently brought up Kierkegaard's account of "the public" and "leveling," from the section of "Two Ages" usually excerpted in English as "The Present Age," so once I mentioned that to each of them, they asked to be put on a call together. I mostly just listened.

(1) Ana opened by arguing that Kierkegaard's account is close to a perfect description of a pile-on: "the public," on his view, is an abstraction with no concrete members, produced by the press, and "leveling" is exactly the process by which real damage is done to an individual while responsibility dissolves into that abstraction -- nobody in particular did it, yet it happened. (2) Erik objected that this elides the feature Kierkegaard cared about most: the public, for him, was constitutively passive, made up of readers scattered alone with their newspapers, never actually assembling, substituting endless talk and comparison for decisive action -- whereas a pile-on is the public actually doing something, with real, traceable consequences, so calling it "leveling" mislabels a rare case of collective efficacy as an instance of the very inertia Kierkegaard thought defined the age. (3) Ana countered that Kierkegaard's own biography cuts against Erik here: he was hounded into becoming a laughingstock on the streets of Copenhagen by a public whipped up by a satirical paper called The Corsair, so his own framework must already allow for the public to produce real effects; what makes something "leveling" rather than ordinary collective action, she said, is only that the effect arrives with authorship so distributed that no single participant has to own it, which is exactly the structure of a like, a share, or a quote-post. (4) Erik's rejoinder was that the Corsair affair actually cuts the other way: the damage there had an identifiable author -- the paper's editor and its cartoonist -- and the public's role was closer to an audience's, receiving and circulating and laughing along, than to a co-author's; a pile-on collapses that three-part structure, since the people laughing and the people striking are the same distributed set, and it's a real question whether Kierkegaard's concept was built to survive that collapse or simply stops applying once it happens. Neither of them budged, and the call ended without a resolution.

I should mention that I already knew the broad comparison between Kierkegaard's public and social media wasn't original to either of them -- versions of Ana's opening move have been made in print before -- so what I'm actually interested in is how much of it holds up once Erik starts pressing on the details. Could you go through the four numbered moves above and say, for each one, who has the better of the argument and why, rather than just picking an overall winner? I'm especially interested in move (4): is Erik's distinction between an audience and a co-author doing real conceptual work, or is it more of a redescription? And is there a consideration neither of them raised that you think should actually settle the question?
