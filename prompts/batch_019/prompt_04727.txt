As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I've been working through a few old, public-domain translations of classical philosophical texts in my spare time, mostly to get a feel for how a fairly rigorous non-Western epistemology carves up "valid sources of knowledge" -- something that struck me as loosely analogous to deciding which input channels a system is allowed to trust before it treats a claim as verified. I got curious about the Nyāya school's list of four pramāṇas (perception, inference, comparison, and testimony) from a couple of survey articles, but those summaries move very quickly over "comparison" (upamāna), so I tracked down the 1913 Satis Chandra Vidyabhusana translation of Gautama's Nyāya Sūtras (published in the "Sacred Books of the Hindus" series) on archive.org and read the sūtra and commentary on it directly. The prose is dense and the argument moves fast, and there's one step in it I can't reconstruct on my own.

Here is the passage -- sūtra 6 of Book I, Chapter I, together with the commentary that follows it immediately, coming right after the earlier sūtra that lists the four pramāṇas:

"Comparison is the knowledge of a thing through its similarity to another thing previously well known.

A man hearing from a forester that a bos gavaeus is like a cow resorts to a forest where he sees an animal like a cow. Having recollected what he heard he institutes a comparison, by which he arrives at the conviction that the animal which he sees is bos gavaeus. This is knowledge derived through comparison. Some hold that comparison is not a separate means of knowledge, for when one notices the likeness of a cow in a strange animal one really performs an act of perception. In reply it is urged that we cannot deny comparison as a separate means of knowledge, for how does otherwise the name bos gavaeus signify the general notion of the animal called bos gavaeus. That the name bos gavaeus signifies one and all members of the bos gavaeus class is not a result of perception but the consequence of a distinct knowledge called comparison."

I can follow the story about the traveler and the forester well enough, and the objection is intuitive: surely noticing "that cow-like animal in front of me resembles a cow" is just ordinary perception, so why add a whole extra item to the list? What defeats me is the reply. It seems to turn on some claim about how the word "bos gavaeus" comes to stand for an entire class of animals, and it insists this is "not a result of perception" -- but I can't tell what work that claim is doing, or why perceiving the resemblance plus simply being told the word by the forester (testimony) wouldn't already cover it, without needing to mint a fourth, independent source of knowledge. Could you unpack exactly what the commentator's counter-argument is claiming, and explain why -- if it's a good argument -- a general term coming to apply to a whole class of things is supposed to resist explanation by perception and testimony working together? Plain-language help would be appreciated, since my grasp of the technical vocabulary doesn't go much beyond what I've picked up skimming secondary sources.
