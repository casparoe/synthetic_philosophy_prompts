I'm taking a seminar this term on classical Islamic philosophy, and this week's unit centered on Avicenna's philosophy of mind, in particular the "flying man" (sometimes "suspended man") argument from the psychological portion of his Kitab al-Shifa (The Healing). A few of us stayed after section arguing about what the thought experiment actually establishes and never quite resolved it, which is a problem, since it's going to be the centerpiece of a short paper I owe next week. I'd rather work through it properly than fall back on the one-line gloss — "Avicenna anticipated Descartes' cogito" — that seems to be everywhere in the secondary reading I've found so far.

Here is the argument as I have it in my notes; I don't have the reading in front of me, so treat this as a reconstruction rather than a verbatim quotation, though I've tried to keep the moves intact. Avicenna asks us to imagine a fully grown, rational person created all at once, suspended in air or a void so that he touches no solid body, with his limbs spread apart so that they do not even touch one another, and with his sight veiled so that he perceives nothing whatsoever of the external world. His claim is that such a person, in that very state, would still affirm his own existence — what the Arabic tradition calls his anniyya, roughly his individual selfhood — without any hesitation, while affirming nothing at all about the existence of his hand, his heart, his brain, or any body or bodily extension, since none of that has been delivered to him in this condition. From this he concludes that the self we are aware of in this immediate way is not the body, and that our awareness of self does not depend on, or pass through, any awareness of body.

What I can't pin down is how big a gap there is between the two halves of that conclusion. The claim that immediate self-awareness doesn't require awareness of body looks like a fairly modest, epistemic point about what a certain kind of introspective act does and doesn't presuppose. But Avicenna, and most of what I've read about him, treats the thought experiment as establishing something stronger and more metaphysical: that the self really is other than the body, a substance in its own right. That looks like exactly the move Descartes gets pressed on in the Sixth Meditation — that being aware of, or able to conceive, A apart from B doesn't by itself show A and B are really distinct substances. Our professor mentioned in passing that Michael Marmura reads the argument more conservatively, as establishing self-awareness or self-intellection specifically rather than full substance dualism, but we ran out of time before getting into why. Could you help me think through, first, whether Avicenna's argument is vulnerable to the same "aware-apart-therefore-really-distinct" objection Descartes's is, and whether anything in his own conceptual toolkit — his handling of essential versus accidental attributes, or his talk of positing or supposing rather than simply conceiving — gives him a way to resist that objection that Descartes lacks; and second, whether Marmura's more conservative reading can really be squared with the fact that Avicenna, at least as I've seen the argument described, goes out of his way to deny that the flying man would affirm any specific organ, which already sounds like more than a merely epistemic conclusion?
