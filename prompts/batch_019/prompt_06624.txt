(a) [10 pts] Reconstruct the chain of reasoning from 'possibly, a God-like being exists' to 'necessarily, a God-like being exists,' being explicit about which modal principle does the crucial work of converting the possibility claim into a necessity claim.
(b) [15 pts] Critics say that Gödel's axioms entail 'modal collapse.' Explain precisely what this means, why it is a distinct and more serious problem than the standard parody objections raised against ontological arguments since Anselm's time, and who is generally credited with first raising it."

And here is the student's answer, in full:

"(a) The argument rests on three pieces of machinery: a positive property (Pos), the definition of 'God-like' as having exactly the positive properties as essential properties, and the notion of an essence as a property that necessarily entails all of a thing's other properties. From the axioms that a property and its negation cannot both be positive, and that positive properties are closed under strict implication, Gödel derives that being God-like is itself a consistent property -- possibly exemplified. He then shows that if something is God-like, God-likeness is its essence, and, because necessary existence is stipulated to be a positive property, anything God-like has necessary existence, which just means that every essence of it is necessarily exemplified. Putting the pieces together: if a God-like being exists at all, it necessarily exists. Since this conditional is itself derivable from the axioms alone, it holds necessarily, and the modal principle that lets us convert 'possibly, necessarily a God-like being exists' into 'necessarily, a God-like being exists' is the Barcan formula, which allows the possibility and necessity operators to commute with the existential quantifier so that the two nested modalities collapse into one.

(b) 'Modal collapse' names the discovery that Gödel's axioms secure far more than God's necessary existence: they entail that every actually true proposition is a necessarily true proposition, so that there turn out to be no genuinely contingent truths at all. This is a much deeper problem than the parody objections that go back to Gaunilo's 'perfect island,' which only show that the same pattern of inference is reusable for silly conclusions if you grant an analogous possibility premise, and which you can always resist simply by denying that the substitute premise is as plausible as the theological one. Modal collapse can't be dodged that way, because it isn't generated by substituting some other property into the argument; it's generated because Gödel's definition of an essence lets any actually true proposition p get smuggled in, via the property 'being such that p,' as one of a God-like being's essential properties, so that the very axioms meant only to secure God's necessary existence end up necessitating p as well, for any true p whatsoever. Alvin Plantinga was the first to flag this problem in print, and he took it to be decisive grounds for preferring his own, differently axiomatized modal argument to Gödel's."
