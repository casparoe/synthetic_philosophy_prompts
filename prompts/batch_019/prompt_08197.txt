Van Inwagen's Special Composition Question (SCQ) asks: given any objects — the xs — under what conditions do they compose something, i.e., under what conditions is there a further object y that has each of the xs as a part, such that no two of the xs share a part, and every part of y shares a part with at least one of the xs? Van Inwagen argues that answers to the SCQ fall into three broad camps. Universalists, such as David Lewis, hold that composition is unrestricted: any two or more objects whatsoever compose some further object. Nihilists hold that composition never occurs, so that the only objects that exist are mereological simples. "Moderate" answers instead try to restrict composition using everyday criteria such as contact, fastening, or being suitably stuck together, and these come closest to common sense.

Van Inwagen rejects all three in favor of his own restricted answer: some objects compose a further object if and only if their activity constitutes a life. Because only organisms are unified by a life in this sense, the only composite objects that strictly exist are living things; there are no tables, rocks, or stars, only simples "arranged tablewise," "rockwise," and so on, which lets van Inwagen paraphrase ordinary talk about artifacts without being ontologically committed to their existence. This is, in effect, the view Ned Markosian later calls "Brutal Composition": rather than stating necessary and sufficient conditions for composition in more basic terms, van Inwagen simply treats facts about when a life, and hence a composite object, occurs as metaphysically primitive.

The most serious problem facing any restricted answer, including van Inwagen's, is vagueness: whether some activity counts as constituting a life admits of borderline cases (a virus, the exact moment of an organism's death), so composition would sometimes be indeterminate. Van Inwagen handles this by treating the vagueness as merely epistemic: there is always a sharp fact about whether composition occurs; we simply cannot always know where the cutoff falls, so no genuinely indeterminate objects, or indeterminate counts of objects, are ever required.
