I recently agreed to serve a two-year term on my physics department's small internal "frontier science" fund -- committee work I took on mainly because I had the lightest teaching load in the room that semester -- and I am now mildly regretting it, because the case in front of us this cycle has turned out to be far more philosophically loaded than I expected. The fund exists to give modest seed money to theoretical proposals too speculative for the usual federal panels, and the most contested application, submitted jointly by two well-regarded colleagues in our theory group, asks for support to develop landscape-style multiverse model-building: the argument, roughly, is that even though individual "pocket universes" realizing different vacua are themselves unobservable, the statistical structure of the landscape as a whole yields testable-in-principle constraints on the constants we actually measure in ours.

Two senior members of the committee want to reject the proposal outright, on the grounds that a research program which cannot in practice be falsified is not physics, whatever else it might be, and they have been invoking Popper by name in every meeting. The proposers, unsurprisingly, feel they are being held to a standard that philosophers of science themselves moved past decades ago, and have started firing back with Lakatos and Duhem, which has done far more to raise the temperature of our discussions than to resolve anything. As the token experimentalist on an otherwise theory-heavy committee, and someone with no formal training in philosophy of science, I don't trust myself to referee this by intuition alone.

I would like a memo I can circulate before Thursday's meeting, and defend afterward, that actually engages the substance rather than just trading names -- something I could attach to an email, not a philosophy paper. It should state a clear recommendation (fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions and a review timeline) and defend it in terms the proposers could actually respond to. Specifically, I'd like it to address: (1) whether the Duhem-Quine point -- that no single hypothesis is ever tested in isolation, so a failed prediction can always be pinned on some auxiliary assumption instead -- actually helps the proposal's case here, or is beside the point; (2) what it would concretely take, given the actual content of this proposal rather than in the abstract, for the program to count as "progressive" rather than "degenerating" in Lakatos's sense; and (3) whether Laudan was basically right that no principled demarcation line has ever been successfully drawn, and if so, what that leaves a funding committee doing when it worries aloud about "is this really science." Please also lay out the strongest version of whichever side's case the memo ends up not endorsing, since I will have to face that half of the room once the decision is announced, and would rather not be blindsided by the best objection to my own recommendation during the meeting itself.
