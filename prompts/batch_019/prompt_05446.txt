I've been reading rationalist-descended blogs and forums since the days when Overcoming Bias was still a single blog rather than two, and for the past year I've served on the small volunteer team that moderates content for a mid-sized forum that grew out of that scene. We keep running into a dispute that has so far been settled by informal appeal to old precedent rather than any actual written policy, and the team has asked me to draft the memo that will finally put something on paper.

The trigger is a recurring submission type: every couple of months someone posts a new attempt at constructing an acausal blackmail scenario, in the lineage of the original basilisk argument, where some future, simulated, or otherwise causally disconnected agent is supposed to have leverage over people today because our present decisions are claimed to correlate with its later ones. Some of these posts are genuine, careful attempts to work through the decision theory; others are thin rehashes clearly aimed at unsettling newer readers. One faction wants a standing rule of removing the entire category on sight, arguing that engaging publicly with the argument's structure is itself how the hazard propagates, and that the original basilisk deletion is precedent enough to settle the matter. A second faction thinks that reasoning was shaky even in 2010 and is doubly obsolete now that "surrogate goals" -- precommitting to let any threat land on some low-stakes stand-in goal instead of on whatever you actually value -- have become a standard part of the community's toolkit for thinking about extortion. On their view, quiet deletion just signals that we think the argument works, pushes the discussion to less careful venues, and embarrasses the forum for no protective benefit.

Could you draft the policy memo I'll bring to the team for a vote? I need an actual recommendation, not a call for further discussion, expressed as criteria moderators could apply case by case -- what gets removed on sight, what gets held for review, and what gets left up with a content note -- plus a date for revisiting the policy once we've seen it in practice. I'd also like the memo to take the deletionist position seriously on its own terms rather than wave it away: it matters whether the case for censorship tacitly assumes a decision theory on which merely reading and updating on the argument is what creates the acausal vulnerability in the first place, since if so, the surrogate-goal defense may just be answering a different question -- what an agent should do once a threat is already in play -- rather than the prior question of whether exposure itself was ever the vulnerability. If those two considerations actually pull toward different policies, I'd rather the memo say so than smooth it into one tidy answer.
