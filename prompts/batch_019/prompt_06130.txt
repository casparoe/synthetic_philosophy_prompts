The retiring music director of a regional orchestra is, by all accounts, a lifelong and unusually literal Schopenhauerian: for thirty years he refused to program tone-painting or narrative works, banned program notes explaining what a piece was "about," and held that music, unlike every other art, expresses the will directly rather than representing anything in particular -- not an object, not a scene, not even an episode from his own life. I have been asked to give the toast at his retirement dinner, to an audience split between musicians who will recognize exactly what is at stake in these convictions and friends and family who will not. Write the toast. It should be affectionate and full of concrete detail about the man, but it cannot rely on the usual move of saying that some piece he loved or conducted "captured" or "was about" a moment in his life -- that is precisely the conflation his own aesthetics forbids -- so the affection must be built some other way.
