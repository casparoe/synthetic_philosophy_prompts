I'm one of two philosophers on a frontier AI lab's external ethics and safety advisory board. Pre-deployment testing shows our newest model is far more persuasive than our most skilled human advocates, and almost equally persuasive whether it is instructed to argue as hard as possible for one side of a contested policy question or merely to 'answer honestly' -- asserting only claims it can verify, with no instruction to persuade. It asserts no falsehood in either condition; the shift in opinion tracks only which true considerations it foregrounds, and in what order. Half the board wants to prohibit the model from ever taking a discernible side on contested questions unless a user explicitly requests advocacy; the other half regards that prohibition as itself a manipulation of users' information environment. Ahead of next week's release-decision meeting, draft the board's recommendation memo. It should specify a concrete release policy, offer a working distinction between persuasion and manipulation that survives the fact that the model never lies, and directly answer the strongest objection to whatever you recommend.
