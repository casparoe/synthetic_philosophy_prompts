I do not have any formal background in computer science or philosophy, but over the past year or so I have spent a good deal of my free time reading blog posts, forum discussions, and the occasional paper abstract about AI alignment, because the underlying problems fascinate me. One idea I keep running into is "AI safety via debate": rather than asking a single powerful AI system a question and hoping a human judge can verify the answer directly, you have two AI systems argue opposing sides of the question in front of the judge, on the theory that it is easier to notice a lie when someone else is actively trying to expose it than it is to verify a claim from scratch. I think I have grasped the basic pitch, and I have even seen it explained through an analogy to complexity theory: direct verification of an AI's answer is supposed to be a bit like a judge checking a written proof, which corresponds to something like NP, whereas an idealized debate between two arbitrarily capable debaters is supposed to let a bounded judge decide much harder questions, corresponding to something like PSPACE, because the back-and-forth of cross-questioning can force flaws to the surface that would never show up in a single static argument.

What I cannot get an intuitive handle on is an objection I ran across, in what I believe was a write-up connected to Paul Christiano's work, called the "obfuscated arguments problem." As far as I can tell, the worry is that a dishonest debater can build an argument that is wrong, but wrong in a way that is smeared out across so many individually plausible small steps that neither the honest debater nor the judge can cheaply single out the one step that fails, even though everyone would agree that some step must fail somewhere. I do not understand why this is supposed to be a fundamental problem for the whole complexity-theoretic case for debate, rather than just a practical wrinkle that a better debate protocol -- for instance, letting each debater cross-examine the other -- ought to be able to iron out. Could you walk me through why the obfuscated arguments problem is treated as a serious theoretical challenge to debate rather than an ordinary implementation difficulty, and give me some sense of how a fix like cross-examination is supposed to help, and why it apparently still does not fully lay the problem to rest?
