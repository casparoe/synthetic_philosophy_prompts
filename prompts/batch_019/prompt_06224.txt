Okay so this has been rattling around in my head for a few weeks after I went down a youtube-lecture rabbit hole. There's a philosopher, L.A. Paul, who wrote this whole book around an idea she calls "transformative experience," and her big illustration is asking whether it even makes sense to decide to become a vampire (like, immortal, blood-drinking, totally different senses and values) by doing normal cost-benefit, expected-value type reasoning. Her point is that you can't really do that, because becoming a vampire is "epistemically" transformative (you literally can't know what it's like to experience the world as a vampire until after you already are one) and also "personally" transformative (your values themselves shift once you've transformed, so the "you" who'd be doing the evaluating afterward isn't really the same "you" doing the deciding beforehand). She says ordinary stuff like deciding to have a kid has basically the same structure. Her way out, if i remember right, is that instead of calculating expected value over an outcome you literally cannot picture, you should decide based on whether you place a high value on "revelation" itself - on finding out big new things about yourself and the world, whatever they turn out to be.

This got me thinking about Mill, weirdly, because of that bit in Utilitarianism where he says the only way to know if one pleasure is genuinely "higher" than another is to ask people who've experienced both, and that these "competent judges" would never willingly trade down even if the lower pleasure is supposedly more intense (his whole "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied" line). It feels like Mill ran into basically the same wall Paul is describing, over a hundred years earlier, and his fix - defer to whoever has actually lived both sides - is kind of the same move as Paul's "revelation" idea, just dressed up differently. So here's what I'm actually curious about: if you resurrected Mill and walked him through Paul's argument, what do you think he'd say, and why, given everything else he believed (his empiricism, his weird "proof" of the principle of utility, his stuff about individuality and "experiments in living" from On Liberty)? Would he resist the idea that expected-value reasoning just breaks down for big life choices, or would he see it as basically confirming what he already thought? And do you think his "competent judges" fix would actually hold up equally well for both of Paul's examples - or would it work fine for something like parenthood, where plenty of people have lived on both sides of the decision, but completely collapse for the vampire case, where by definition nobodys ever tried both sides (seeing as vampires aren't, you know, real)? If it collapses for one but not the other, does that mean the vampire example is kind of a red herring, or is it pointing at something that secretly undermines the parenthood case too?
