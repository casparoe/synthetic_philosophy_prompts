I'm the elected worker rep on the joint health and safety committee at the plant I work at (small auto parts manufacturer, nothing fancy), and we've got a genuine fight brewing after an incident last month. One of the stamping machines caught fire. When the investigation team went through it they found two seperate problems that were both live at the exact moment it happened: a technician had manually bypassed the safety interlock earlier that shift, totally against protocol, and completely unrelated to that, a coolant hose turned out to have a manufacturing defect that our outside engineer says would've made the machine overheat and catch fire regardless, interlock or no interlock. He was pretty blunt that either problem alone, that day, under those conditions, would've been enough by itself to start the fire.

So now about half the committee is arguing that, strictly speaking, you cant say the bypass "caused" the fire, since it would've happened anyway because of the hose, and you can't blame the hose either by the same logic, since it would've happened anyway because of the bypass. Their conclusion is we shouldn't name either one as "the cause" in the corrective action report, which happens to also mean no real discipline for the tech and no formal claim against the hose supplier. That conclusion bugs me but I couldn't say exactly why, so I've spent a few weeks after work reading up on theories of causation on my own (never studied any philosophy, I just got kind of obsessed) - I got through Lewis's counterfactual idea about causes, and started poking at this "interventionist" way of thinking that people trace to Woodward and to Judea Pearl's structural equation stuff, which honestly seems built for exactly this kind of case, but I honestly can't tell if I'm applying any of it right or just picking whichever theory tells me what I already wanted to hear.

We owe management and our insurance carrier a finalized corrective action report by the end of the month, and I want to bring something concrete to Tuesday's meeting instead of just my gut feeling. Could you write the short memo I'd actually hand out to the committee: it needs to land on one clear recommendation for how the report should describe causal responsibility (tech gets disciplined, supplier gets a claim filed, both, or neither, and why), and it has to actually engage with the "you can't blame either one" argument rather than just declare it wrong - and if there's a genuine fairness problem with disciplining the tech as hard as we would've if he'd been the only cause, given the fire was going to happen anyway, I want that taken seriously too, not waved off.
