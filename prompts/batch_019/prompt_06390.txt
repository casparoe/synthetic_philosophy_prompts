I've spent the past few weeks sitting in on a friend's graduate seminar on philosophy of language while I circle a new project, and one session has stayed with me more than the others: Donald Davidson's account of radical interpretation. What I find dramatically rich about it is the bind it puts an interpreter in. Imagine someone with no dictionary, no bilingual informant, and no independent window onto what another speaker believes, who must nonetheless work out simultaneously what that speaker means by her sentences and what she believes, using nothing but patterns of assent and dissent to sentences under observed circumstances. The whole enterprise is held together by what Davidson called the principle of charity: the working assumption that the speaker is, on balance, both logically coherent and largely right about the world she's reacting to. What makes this more than a dry methodological point is that charity can pull in two directions at once. Faced with an apparently false or irrational-seeming utterance, the interpreter can either preserve the speaker's rationality by revising the translation, or preserve the literal translation by attributing to her a real error, and nothing in the method itself says which cost is cheaper to pay.

I'd like you to write a short story, somewhere in the 1,500-2,500 word range, that dramatizes this predicament from the inside rather than explaining it from the outside. Choose whatever premise puts an interpreter in genuine, radical difficulty: a field linguist working with an isolated community, a signals analyst poring over an intercepted transmission with no key, a clinician trying to make sense of a patient whose speech has drifted from any recognizable use, a researcher attempting to converse with a mind built on entirely unfamiliar principles. I'd rather you avoid a straightforward "first contact with aliens" setup unless you can make it feel genuinely unfamiliar, since that framing tends to flatten this particular problem into generic mystery-solving. Whatever premise you choose, I want the story to turn on one concrete, consequential moment: the interpreter has to decide, right there, whether to charge the person or system being interpreted with a strange false belief, or to revise the reading of what they said or did in order to keep them rational and coherent, and I want that decision to cost someone something real, not just an intellectual puzzle solved or left open. If you can also gesture, lightly, at Davidson's later idea that objective thought itself depends on a kind of triangulation between two minds and a shared world, such that radical interpretation is ultimately about whether two things can be said to mean anything at all in each other's company, so much the better.

One last request: I'd like the story to hold up perfectly well for a reader who has never heard of Davidson, but I'd also like a reader who has to feel a small shock of recognition partway through, once they realize exactly which piece of philosophical machinery the plot is built on. Please just give me the story itself, without any framing note or title card that spells out the philosophy in advance.
