I sit on the discretionary grants committee of a small philanthropic fund that mostly supports fairly conventional global-health work, and this week I have to write the recommendation on a proposal that reproduces, almost too neatly, an argument I had with a friend just last weekend. She works in quantitative trading and took the hard line on Pascal's mugging: any decision procedure built on subjective expected utility is committed, by its own logic, to paying up whenever the product of stated probability and claimed payoff comes out positive, no matter how small the probability or how outlandish the payoff, and refusing once the numbers get uncomfortable is not a principled objection but a failure of nerve. I pushed back with something like Bostrom's leverage penalty -- the more good a claim says you personally are positioned to do, the more it should be discounted, since almost no one is ever actually in a position to swing outcomes for billions of people -- but she kept asking why the discount should track the size of the claim rather than just being a story we tell ourselves to avoid an uncomfortable conclusion, and I didn't have a clean answer. We left it there.

Now the committee is facing a live version of the same problem. A two-person team has applied for a grant that would use roughly 80 percent of this year's discretionary budget to build a wastewater-surveillance protocol meant to catch a specific class of engineered pathogen before it can spread widely enough to end technological civilization. Their own written estimate puts the probability that the protocol is ever the decisive factor in averting such a release at around one in several million, but they argue -- and our technical reviewer confirms the arithmetic checks out once you accept the scale of what's at stake -- that the expected value of preventing that outcome is so large that this single proposal's expected value exceeds every other application combined, including our recurring bed-net and cash-transfer grants, whose effects are far more certain but smaller by many orders of magnitude. Two committee members want to fund it on exactly those grounds and call the rest of us timid; three others want to decline, but for different and not fully compatible reasons -- one repeats my leverage-penalty intuition, one simply says "we don't fund lottery tickets," and one worries aloud that we are all dressing up squeamishness as rigor. I have been asked to draft the memo the committee will actually vote on Thursday.

Could you write that memo? It should recommend a specific, actionable disposition for the proposal -- fund in full, fund partially, decline, or defer pending some named further step -- together with the reasoning behind it, and it should set out a general criterion the committee could apply to future proposals with this same tiny-probability, enormous-payoff structure, so that we stop improvising case by case. Whichever way you come down, please give a genuinely fair hearing to the strongest version of the losing argument rather than dissolving it into a slogan: both "the expected value swamps everything else, and declining to act on your own numbers once they get large is not rationality, it's superstition" and "a policy of paying out to whoever names the largest number gets exploited without limit" deserve serious engagement before the committee will trust the recommendation.
