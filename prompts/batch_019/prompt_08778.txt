Quick philosophy-of-language question for a piece I'm noodling on about the history of visual communication. Otto Neurath and Wittgenstein were both kicking around Vienna in the 1920s and both got fixated on "pictures" as a way of explaining how representation works -- Wittgenstein's picture theory of meaning in the Tractatus, and then a few years later Neurath's Isotype project, literally designing a picture language for statistics and public education. I keep assuming these must be related somehow but I suspect they're actually doing pretty different things with the word "picture."

Can you compare the two for me? Specifically: what makes something a "picture" in each account, and how does it manage to represent what it represents? Does Neurath's practical picture-language work basically vindicate Wittgenstein's earlier idea, or does it quietly abandon it (I recall reading the two of them didn't get on personally either, for waht it's worth)? And if you had to bet on one of these two notions of "picture" as more defensible today, which one wins?
