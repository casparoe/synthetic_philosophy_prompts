I'm a PhD student in evolutionary biology working on a genus of small plethodontid salamanders confined to isolated springs and seeps in a karst region of the state, and because almost no one else here works on this group, my advisor arranged for me to sit as the taxon specialist on the state wildlife agency's Rare Species Scientific Advisory Committee, which reviews classification questions ahead of formal listing decisions. We currently have to settle the status of a population discovered three years ago in a single spring complex roughly 40 km from the nearest population of its closest relative, a widespread and secure species. The isolated population is diagnosably distinct in coloration and several morphometric traits, and its mitochondrial DNA forms a clade that is reciprocally monophyletic relative to the parent species; but genome-wide nuclear data show a low, nonzero signal of historical introgression, and when we have brought individuals from both populations together in controlled enclosures they interbreed readily and produce viable, fertile offspring. So far as anyone can tell, nothing intrinsic keeps the two populations apart, only the accident of the dry terrain between their springs.

The committee has split largely along the lines of which species concept each member is applying, often without quite naming it that way. Several members think that because nothing prevents interbreeding, treating the spring population as a separate species is indefensible, and that it should be filed as a subspecies or "population of concern." Others think that a diagnosable, independently evolving lineage is exactly what species status should track, whatever its interbreeding potential, and that demanding proof of intrinsic reproductive isolation would strip species status from many uncontroversial allopatric species. A third faction, including our chair, wants to sidestep the dispute entirely by calling it "merely a matter of definition" and defaulting to an administrative "species of concern" designation that carries no independent listing eligibility. The stakes are not merely theoretical: a regional utility's groundwater withdrawal permit hearing is six weeks away, and whether the spring population is independently listable, rather than protected only derivatively through its widespread relative, will likely decide whether the hearing considers impacts to it at all.

Could you draft the short memo I will circulate to the committee before our next meeting? It should recommend a concrete classification decision, or a concrete process for reaching one, on a timeline tied to the permit hearing; propose a criterion the committee could apply consistently to future cases of this kind; and take the "this is merely definitional" objection seriously as a real consideration rather than waving it away, since I suspect it is not simply wrong even though I don't think it excuses the committee from making a call.
