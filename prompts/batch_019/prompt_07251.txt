Next week our department is hosting a job-talk candidate who defends a Parfitian thesis sometimes called "rational irrationality": in cases with the structure of Parfit's Hitchhiker — you must actually pay the driver once you're safely in town, where paying secures nothing further for you — it can be rational to make yourself disposed to do something that, performed at that moment, is irrational. Her stock response to the charge that this is incoherent is to deny what decision theorists call the separability of choices: she insists the puzzle only looks paradoxical if you grade each act in isolation from the plan it belongs to. Please draft eight to ten hiring-committee questions, each with a follow-up keyed to her likely answer, that push past that response rather than let her simply restate it. Include at least one question on what her position buys us that Gauthier's constrained maximization and McClennen's resolute choice do not, since both already avoid ever calling the momentary act "irrational"; one on whether the argument survives once the driver is merely a reliable predictor rather than a perfect one; and a closing question on what, by her own lights, would count as a genuine counterexample to her view.
