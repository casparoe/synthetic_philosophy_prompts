I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week that started, weirdly, from an argument at work about whether our internal "code freeze" policy even counts as a real rule, since half the team follows a written doc that flatly contradicts what our director actually enforces in practice. A coworker who did philosophy in undergrad said this was basically a baby version of the Hart vs. Fuller debate from legal philosophy, so I went and read some summaries (I'm just a backend engineer, no formal philosophy training, so bear with me if I butcher this).

From what I can piece together: Hart says a rule counts as "law" just because it was made through the correct process (passed by the right people via what he calls the "rule of recognition"), regardless of whether its fair or morally decent — law and morality are seperate questions, full stop. Fuller pushes back and says that's not enough, because for a system of rules to count as law at all (rather than just organized coercion wearing a label) it has to satisfy a checklist he calls the "inner morality of law" — stuff like being public, general, not self-contradictory, not changing every five minutes, actually possible to comply with, and matching what officials in practice actually do. The example everyone always drags out for this is the WWII "grudge informer" cases in Germany, where postwar courts had to decide if Nazi-era statutes that let people legally denounce each other still counted as real law.

Since I already know the grudge-informer example cold at this point, can you give me a totally different example — set in something modern like corporate policy, an app's terms of service, or an internal engineering standard, not a courtroom — of a rule that would come out fully "valid law" under Hart's positivist test but would flunk several of Fuller's specific principles at once? And then actually walk through how a strict Hartian and a strict Fullerian would argue it out over whether that rule is really binding "law" in the full sense, rather than just giving me the "law vs. morality" bumper-sticker version I already got from the summaries.
