I was reporting on a small AI-safety workshop, for a piece on decision theory in that world, and at dinner afterward two of the researchers ended up re-litigating Roko's basilisk with far more rigor than the internet meme usually gets. Both work on decision theory professionally but asked not to be named, so I'll call them Naomi and Callum.

Naomi's opening move: anyone who accepts timeless or updateless decision theory strongly enough to endorse one-boxing on Newcomb's problem has already conceded that present choices can be correlated with, and in the relevant sense influence, what a distant optimizing agent decides. Having conceded that, she said, you can't wave the basilisk away as a category error -- you have to meet it on its own terms.

Callum's reply: meeting it on its own terms is exactly what sinks it. An updateless agent fixes its whole policy in advance, and never yielding to threats dominates sometimes yielding, because only the former removes any incentive for a sufficiently rational future mind to issue the threat at all. A mind smart enough to run this argument gains nothing from actually punishing anyone, so it wouldn't precommit to doing so.

Naomi granted that for threats improvised by the AI itself, but said the real worry was always different: the punishment scheme could be designed by present-day people, before any AI exists, specifically to bind their creation and motivate today's donors -- an ordinary commitment device between agents who already exist, not opportunistic extortion from a hypothetical mind, so "don't negotiate with blackmailers" doesn't obviously transfer.

Callum's last move: that version isn't really Roko's basilisk anymore -- it's a claim about specific living people's intentions, to be settled by ordinary evidence rather than acausal reasoning -- and in any case, for every scenario in which failing to help gets punished there's an equally coherent one in which helping out of fear rather than genuine conviction gets punished instead. Absent evidence favoring one over its mirror image, he said, both deserve the same negligible weight.

Neither budged, and I didn't referee it at the table. Could you go through their four moves and say who has the better of each one and why, note anything both of them seem to be taking for granted that shouldn't be, and close with your own verdict on whether anything resembling Roko's basilisk survives this exchange as a genuine decision-theoretic problem, rather than a Pascal's mugging in different dress?
