In my philosophy class we recently finished Berkeley's Principles of Human Knowledge, and as a side reading our teacher assigned a few sections of his later work The Querist, where he describes money as a kind of "ticket" or "counter" for conveying the power to command goods, rather than something valuable in itself. It struck me that this is really just his immaterialism applied to economics: since Berkeley already denies that objects have any existence or nature independent of being perceived, it shouldn't matter whether currency is backed by gold or is simply printed paper, because either way its value is just as "unreal" as matter itself. Doesn't this mean that, on Berkeley's own principles, a government could print as much money as it wanted without any real economic consequences? I'd like to use this connection in my term paper, so I want to make sure I'm not misreading him first.
