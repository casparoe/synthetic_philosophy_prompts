I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been working through a discrete math textbook in the evenings for fun, and I recently hit the section on Zorn's Lemma: the statement that any partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound must contain a maximal element. I found the mechanics manageable enough -- I worked through the standard argument that every vector space has a basis, even an infinite-dimensional one, and a couple of the usual algebra applications, like the existence of maximal ideals. What stopped me was a footnote mentioning, almost as an aside, that Zorn's Lemma, the Axiom of Choice, and the Well-Ordering Theorem are all provably equivalent to one another, given the rest of the usual Zermelo-Fraenkel (ZF) axioms of set theory.

That equivalence bothers me, because the three statements do not feel remotely equivalent. The Axiom of Choice -- roughly, that you can always pick one element out of each set in an arbitrary collection of nonempty sets -- seems almost too obvious to be worth stating. The Well-Ordering Theorem, which says that every set, including the real numbers, can be arranged so that every nonempty subset of it has a smallest element, strikes me as closer to obviously false: as far as I know, nobody has ever actually written down a well-ordering of the reals, or has any real idea what one would look like. Zorn's Lemma sits in the middle somewhere -- I don't have a gut reaction to it in either direction, true or false; it just feels like machinery you invoke. When I mentioned this to a friend a couple of years into a math PhD, she said the reaction was basically universal among people meeting this material for the first time, and left it there, which did not really resolve anything for me.

I have followed the proof that each of the three statements implies the other two, and I don't doubt that they are logically equivalent. What I would like is an explanation pitched at intuition rather than formal derivation: why would logically equivalent statements provoke such different reactions in anyone encountering them, and is that purely an artifact of human psychology, or is it tracking something real about the differences between these three formulations -- something to do with constructiveness, perhaps, or with what each version forces you to visualize directly versus lets you quietly avoid visualizing? I suspect this is really a question in the philosophy of mathematics rather than in mathematics itself, which puts it even further outside what I know, but it's the thing I'm actually stuck on.
