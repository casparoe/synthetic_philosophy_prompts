I've been reading LessWrong and the wider rationalist blogosphere for close to a decade, and a few of us who met through those circles have since started a small in-person group that mostly works through problems in decision theory and formal epistemology. At a recent session, one member who is finishing a doctorate in probability theory presented a betting game as a thought experiment, and it has since split the group into three camps that I haven't been able to adjudicate between.

Here is the game. You flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time; since each flip is independent and fair, the probability that the first head occurs on flip number n is (1/2)^n. If the first head lands on an odd-numbered flip n, the house pays you 2^n/n dollars; if it lands on an even-numbered flip n, you pay the house 2^n/n dollars instead. So getting heads on flip 1 nets you $2, on flip 2 costs you $2, on flip 3 nets $2.67, on flip 4 costs $4, and so on, with both the prizes and the penalties growing quickly, offset by rapidly shrinking probabilities. The obvious move is to find the expected value by multiplying each payoff by its probability and summing over n = 1, 2, 3, .... Do that, in that order, and you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2, a little under seventy cents, and a few of us took that as proof the game is a mildly favorable bet. Then someone objected that summing "in order of n" is only one choice among many: because the sum of the absolute values of the terms (1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ...) diverges, the very same terms can be legitimately regrouped and reordered - two winning outcomes for every losing one, say, or countless other patterns - to produce a different total, including totals that run off to positive or negative infinity. Nothing about how the game is specified tells you which ordering is the "correct" one for adding up the terms.

This has hardened into three positions nobody will budge from. One camp maintains that the roughly-seventy-cent figure just is the value of the game, and that the reordering business is a mathematical curiosity that shouldn't move anyone's answer. A second camp holds that the game simply has no well-defined expected value, full stop, and that we have to live with that. A third camp accepts the second camp's diagnosis but wants to draw a much larger lesson from it about the limits of expected-value maximization as a general theory of rational choice under uncertainty. For what it's worth, I already know the standard reply to the St. Petersburg paradox about bounded utility functions and diminishing marginal value of money, and I don't think it actually resolves what's bothering us, since our disagreement is about whether the expectation is even a well-defined number in the first place, not about whether an infinite expectation should be discounted. So: if a friend offered, tonight, to actually play this game with you for real cash - flip a real coin, settle up however many flips it takes - how should you reason about whether to accept? And do any of my group's three camps actually have this right?
