I don't have any formal training in history or law, but over the past year I've gotten pretty absorbed in reading Frederick Douglass on my own, starting with his autobiography (a friend's recommendation) and then moving on to collections of his speeches and newspaper editorials. One thing that keeps coming up is his early alliance with William Lloyd Garrison: Douglass stood behind Garrison's famous description of the Constitution as "a covenant with death and an agreement with hell," and for years he argued that a document which explicitly protected the slave trade for twenty years and contained both the three-fifths clause and the fugitive slave clause could not honestly be called anything other than a pro-slavery charter wearing the language of liberty as a disguise.

That seems to me like about as credible a verdict as anyone could hope to get on the Constitution's original character, since it's coming from a man who had actually escaped slavery and had no reason to flatter the document. So I'm genuinely puzzled by why so much civil rights rhetoric ever since — Martin Luther King's description of the Constitution as a "promissory note" owed to Black Americans is the clearest example — keeps treating the same document as something fundamentally redeemable, as though its promises of liberty and equality just need to be finally cashed in rather than recognized as false advertising from the start. Doesn't building a whole strategy of reform around "fidelity to the Constitution" amount to quietly setting aside Douglass's own hard-won judgment on the matter, in favor of something more rhetorically convenient?
