I'm a backend engineer at a small startup (about a dozen people) building an AI assistant that actually has permissions to act for you — send emails, book things, complete purchases — not just draft stuff for you to approve. Last month we had two incidents that turned into a whole debate on our internal Slack. One user had the assistant fire off an angry reply-all email at 1am that they very much regretted by the next morning. A seperate user kept telling the assistant to go ahead and complete a 2am impulse purchase on a shopping site, even though their onboarding profile literally lists "spend less on impulse buys" as their top goal. In both cases the assistant did exactly what it was told, which everyone agrees is defensible on its face, but a chunk of the team thinks a genuinely good human assistant would've hesitated, or at least asked "are you sure," instead of just executing.

This has split us into two camps, and I got voluntold to write up the recommendation doc for our (very informal, like 5-person) "trust & safety" group before Friday. Camp A says the assistant should only ever act on the user's literal, in-the-moment instruction, full stop — anything else is the AI deciding it knows the user's "real" wants better than the user does. One guy on this side keeps bringing up some AI-safety-blog concept called "coherent extrapolated volition," or CEV, and says even the people who came up with it admit there's no principled, non-arbitrary way to define what someone would want "if they knew more and were more the person they wished to be" without that just smuggling in the values of whoever's doing the defining. Camp B (kind of my camp) thinks treating the literal words typed at 1am as sacred is its own bad answer, and that some notion of what the user would actually endorse once they'd cooled off or thought it through has to matter here — we just haven't been careful about how much, or how.

Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring into that meeting? I need it to land on one concrete policy, something a couple of engineers could realistically build a first version of in a sprint, not a "more research needed" shrug. It should spell out how we'd operationalize "what the user would want on reflection" narrowly enough that it isn't just a blank check for the assistant to override people whenever it disagrees with them, and it needs to actually engage Camp A's objection instead of steelmanning it in one line and moving on — that's the exact note I got on the last draft of this thing.
