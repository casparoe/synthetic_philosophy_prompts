I've been working through the politics of the "left" Vienna Circle for my dissertation, and Carnap poses a puzzle I can't quite resolve. In his Intellectual Autobiography he describes himself as broadly sympathetic to Otto Neurath's socialism, and the 1929 manifesto he co-authored with Neurath and Hahn explicitly ties the scientific world-conception to contemporary movements for reorganizing economic and social life. Neurath, for his part, spent years arguing against Mises and later Hayek that a planned economy could dispense with market prices altogether, coordinating production and needs directly "in kind." Contemporary heirs to that argument now point to the scale of data and computation available to large logistics and technology firms and ask whether this finally supplies the informational resources Mises and Hayek insisted no central planner could possess.

Drawing on Carnap's actual philosophical commitments—his verificationist, fallibilist view of what makes a question empirical; his treatment of value statements as expressions of attitude rather than truth-apt claims; and his principle of tolerance toward competing linguistic frameworks, adjudicated by fruitfulness rather than correctness—how do you think he would have reacted to this computational revival of Neurath's side of the calculation debate? Would his official philosophy have required him to keep the empirical question of planning's tractability and the normative question of whether to plan sharply apart, or do you think his personal sympathies for Neurath's project would have bled into how he framed the "empirical" question itself?
