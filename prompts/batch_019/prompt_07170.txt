I teach a seminar on constitutional theory, and we recently spent a session on Jon Elster's *Ulysses and the Sirens*, in particular its central image: a constitution, on this reading, functions like the ropes with which Odysseus has his crew bind him to the mast, a device by which a sovereign people ties its own hands against passions, panics, or factional temptations that it can anticipate in the abstract but knows it will not resist in the moment. A few students pressed on a disanalogy I found genuinely hard to answer: Odysseus is one continuous person who consents to, and later is identical with, the man straining against his bonds, whereas a constitutional generation binds a great many people who never agreed to anything at all — citizens not yet born, about whose interests and convictions the founders can only speculate.

That disagreement is more or less the one Jefferson and Madison had in 1789. Jefferson, reasoning from Buffon's mortality tables, concluded that no law or constitution should validly bind anyone for more than about nineteen years, on the principle that the earth belongs, as he put it, "in usufruct to the living." Madison thought this was close to unworkable, and that a people can rationally value being bound by a wise prior commitment, in much the way a psychiatric patient's "Ulysses contract" — authorizing others to override her future refusal of treatment during a crisis she can already foresee — serves her own interests rather than overriding them. What I can't quite settle, and what I'd like my students to sit with, is when a precommitment of this kind is a gift to the future and when it is simply the dead hand of the past.

For a short fiction exercise I'm adding to the course, I'd like to give students a piece that makes this tension vivid rather than presenting it as a quarrel between two dead Virginians — something like the way Asimov's robot stories make the Three Laws feel like live dilemmas rather than a dry list of rules. Could you write a short story, on the order of 1000 to 1500 words, set at the founding of some polity — a breakaway colony, a newly independent island state, or a more speculative arrangement, whichever setting serves the idea best — in which one framer argues for entrenching a particular provision so deeply that no future majority, however overwhelming, will ever be permitted to touch it? I want the philosophical stakes to emerge through plot and character rather than through speeches: what the provision is meant to guard against, and what it costs the generations who inherit it without having agreed to any of it themselves.
