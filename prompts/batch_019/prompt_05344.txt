My younger sister is a behavioral ecologist who has spent most of her career thinking about costly signaling, and in three weeks she is getting married; because I am the family's professional writer, I have inherited the toast. This is a more mixed room than any I have faced at a book event: half the guests are her colleagues and former labmates, who will recognize the handicap principle the moment I gesture at it and will absolutely notice if I get Zahavi wrong or reach for the easy peacock-tail example (I have been warned, half-jokingly, that they will heckle if I do); the other half are aunts, college friends, and people from the groom's side who have never heard the phrase "costly signal" and would be lost, and faintly bored, if I tried to teach it to them from first principles.

The complication I actually want the toast to work through, rather than smooth over, is this: my sister and her fiancé are almost defiantly unextravagant. There was no grand proposal, the ring was inexpensive, and the wedding itself is a backyard affair on a folding-chair budget. Read naively, the handicap principle seems to say that a signal is credible in proportion to how wasteful and expensive it is, which would make their whole courtship look, on paper, like weak evidence of anything. I don't think that is actually what the theory implies once you bring in the finer distinctions -- say, the difference between a Zahavian handicap and a hard-to-fake index signal, or the old rivalry between honest-signaling accounts and Fisherian runaway explanations of ornamentation, or simply the difference between cost paid in money and cost paid in the much less fakeable currency of time -- and I would like the toast to actually make that argument: correctly enough that the biologists in the room feel the logic click into place rather than wince, and warmly enough that everyone else just experiences it as a love story. If it helps, the one true detail I keep returning to is that for three years, before they lived in the same city, the groom drove four hours each way most weekends, in every season, without ever once framing it as a grand gesture; I suspect that is a better example than anything with feathers.

Could you write this toast? Aim for something that reads aloud in about five minutes, built around one sustained thread rather than a string of separate jokes, and please keep it a toast rather than a lecture -- the theory should be doing quiet structural work underneath the sentences, not sitting on top of them being explained.
