I'm working on a piece about philosophers who theorized about the body while their own bodies were falling apart, and Nietzsche is the obvious centerpiece. In Ecce Homo he gets weirdly specific about diet and climate -- rants about German cuisine wrecking the "German spirit," trashes beer and heavy meals, has strong opinons on tea vs coffee, and basically says the right climate and diet are a precondition for having good ideas at all. Meanwhile the guy had crushing migraines, near-blindness and stomach problems for pretty much his entire adult life, kept moving around Europe chasing a climate that would fix him, and never really got better before he finally collapsed in 1889. So which is it -- does his own miserable, lived experience basically debunk his physiological theorizing, or does the "great health" idea from The Gay Science (the notion that real insight has to be earned through repeated sickness and convalescence, not just handed to you by stable good health) mean the contradiction is actually the whole point, not a strike against him? Write an essay that takes this seriously, weighs both readings, and lands on an actual verdict -- not a wishy-washy "he's complicated" ending.
