Since retiring after some four decades as a field ecologist, I have been filling the time once spent on grant applications and fieldwork logistics with a rather more leisurely education in political philosophy and the theory of collective choice. It was in the course of this reading that I first met Robin Hanson's proposal for "futarchy": rather than have a legislature or committee decide both what to value and which policy will best achieve it, one should have people vote on a single, agreed measure of welfare, and then let a conditional speculative market, populated by people staking real money on the outcome, decide which policy is expected to maximize that measure.

The idea has recently taken on a very concrete form for me, because I now sit on the board of a small foundation that distributes something like half a million dollars a year to early-career researchers in my former field. At our last meeting, one of the younger trustees, an enthusiastic reader of the same literature that first brought futarchy to my attention, proposed that we abandon our usual practice of panel deliberation followed by a vote and instead run, for each cycle's handful of proposals, a conditional market in which a pool of researchers from the field would bet on a composite five-year "success score" (built from citation counts, independent replications, and a retrospective expert survey) for each proposal, conditional on its being funded; we would then simply fund whichever proposal the market priced highest. I found the suggestion unexpectedly difficult to dismiss, yet I could not say with any precision why it still troubled me, beyond a suspicion that shrinking Hanson's scheme down from a whole polity to a dozen people who routinely referee one another's papers might change its character rather more than its proponents seem to assume.

I would very much value your help thinking this through. Could you set out what you take to be the most important implications of adopting a futarchy-style, conditional-market mechanism for a small, recurring, closed-community decision of exactly this sort, rather than for the large, anonymous polities Hanson mainly had in mind? I am especially curious whether the objections one usually meets in the literature, such as thin and easily manipulated markets, the difficulty of compressing something like "good science" into a single scalar target, and the supposedly clean separation between voting on values and betting on beliefs, become more severe at so small a scale, less severe, or simply turn into different problems altogether.
