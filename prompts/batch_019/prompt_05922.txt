I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel where the back half hinges on one chapter: my main character, Deirdre, coming to terms with the fact that she may have stopped loving her husband. My writing group keeps saying the moment feels arbitrary right now, like she just wakes up one day and decides its true, and I think what's missing is some actual structure underneath it rather than vibes. I sat in on a friend's epistemology seminar a while back for research on a different project, and she mentioned this idea of "luminosity" — basically the claim that if you're in a mental state you should automatically be in a position to know you're in it — plus something called the KK principle, that knowing a thing requires also knowing that you know it. Apparently theres a well-known argument (I want to say it's from a guy named Williamson?) that both of these break down in gradual, sorites-y cases, the standard example being someone slowly feeling less and less cold as a room warms up, where there's no single moment they could point to and say "now I know I've stopped feeling cold."

That example's a bit dry for fiction purposes, so here's what I'm hoping you can help with: could you come up with two different scenarios for me — one that's really just about the luminosity failure (does Deirdre feel something right now, love or otherwise, and can she reliably tell?), and a second, more specific one that's about the KK failure specifically — a case where she does, in some real sense, know she's in a certain state, but doesn't know that she knows it, or wrongly feels certain that she does. I'd like both to be rooted in something other than a physical sensation like temperature — memory, self-deception, or a slow emotional drift over years would be more useful to me than a sorites of blankets — and I'd really appreciate it if you could walk through exactly how each one maps onto the margin-for-error logic, so I'm not just faking the philosophy for the sake of a nice scene.
