This semester I'm taking a philosophy of language seminar, and we've spent almost three weeks on Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rules and private language: the paradox that no fact about a person's past behavior, dispositions, or introspectible mental life can settle whether they've ever meant 'plus' rather than some deviant function that agrees with plus on every case they've actually computed and diverges later, and the 'skeptical solution' Kripke offers instead, on which meaning is secured not by any private fact but by a community's practice of endorsing and correcting one another's usage. It's the most genuinely disorienting thing we've read all year, and I keep finding myself trying to apply it to things it probably wasn't meant for.

Which brings me to my actual problem: my older sister is getting married next month, and as her only sibling I've been asked to give the toast at the reception. She met her fiancé during her own philosophy PhD, so a good third of the room will be philosophers of language who will immediately recognize any reference to Kripke, while the rest of the guests -- our parents, his family, people we grew up with -- have never heard of the rule-following paradox and shouldn't have to in order to enjoy the toast. I'd like you to write it for me: about five minutes long, warm rather than lecture-like, built around a genuinely good 'quus'-style joke about deviant rule-following as applied to a wedding vow. But I don't want it to just land the joke and move on -- I'd like Kripke's skeptical solution to do real work in the toast's actual point about what does and doesn't secure a promise like 'till death do us part' across fifty years of circumstances that nobody in the room can currently foresee, so that it ends up somewhere true and moving about marriage for the whole room, and not just a clever aside for the philosophers.
