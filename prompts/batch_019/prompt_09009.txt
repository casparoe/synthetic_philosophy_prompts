The dollar auction (Shubik's game where the top two bidders both have to pay up but only the highest bidder actually gets the dollar) always gets trotted out as basically THE illustration of escalation of commitment. But when psychologists actually explain escalation of commitment -- Staw's self-justification story, or Whyte's prospect-theory spin where it's really about dodging a 'certain loss' by gambling on a bigger one -- they're usually talking about a lone decision-maker sinking more money into their own failing project, no rival bidder needed at all. So: compare the dollar auction to the broader escalation-of-commitment phenomenon. Are the two actually driven by the same underlying mechanism, or is the auction doing something different -- some game theorists even argue the rising bids there can be locally "rational" once your in second place, which doesn't obviously carry over to Staw's cases? Where exactly do they come apart, and does the dollar auction actually teach us anything about the real-world cases it's constantly used to illustrate?
