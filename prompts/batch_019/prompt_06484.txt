So I'm about a third of the way into a novel set during the Late Bronze Age collapse -- that stretch around 1200 BCE where the Mycenaean palaces burn down, the Hittite empire just sort of dissolves, Ugarit's last letters home are basically the king begging his neighbors for help because the enemy's ships have already been sighted, and then the correspondence just stops, and Egypt only barely holds off the Sea Peoples under Ramesses III. I've got three rotating POV characters (a Hittite scribe, a Mycenaean widow, an Egyptian marine) and the collapse happens to them through a string of very specific bad decisions, bad harvests, and bad luck. A friend of mine who's ABD in ancient history keeps telling me this doesn't actually explain the collapse, it just dramatizes it -- that a real explanation would look more like the "systems collapse" stuff archaeologists talk about (these palace economies were so interdependent that one shock anywhere cascaded into failure everywhere) or the paleoclimate papers about a multi-decade drought, something general enough that it would've produced roughly the same result no matter which particular scribes and widows happened to be standing around. This turned into a whole argument about whether my kind of explanation is even a real one, and apparently there's a whole philosophy-of-history literature about exactly this fight -- she kept namedropping Hempel, and then some people who argued back at him, Dray and Danto i think, maybe Gallie too, but she was fuzzy on the details and so am I.

Can you write me the argument itself, properly, since neither of us actually knows it well enough? Two people, at the bar after a conference panel on the collapse, a couple drinks in. One of them is a hardline covering-law type: nothing is really explained unless it can be cashed out, even loosely or statistically, as an instance of some general law, so "systems collapse" or the drought papers are the only things in the room that count as explanations, and a narrative like mine is just description wearing an explanation's clothes. The other is a narrativist, in that Dray/Danto/Gallie vein, who thinks that whole demand is a category mistake, and that laying out how the specific sequence -- drought, then raids, then famine, then migration, then the palace economies going dark one by one -- hangs together as a story is itself the explanation, no law required. Im trying to work out which one I actually believe before I decide how much of a unifying mechanism my own book needs underneath the plot, so I need real back-and-forth: make the covering-law person force the narrativist to say what's doing the explaining if not some regularity, and make the narrativist force the covering-law person to admit "systems collapse" is either too vague to have predicted any of this in advance or is just a narrative wearing a lab coat. Neither one should fold, and neither should be the strawman -- I want both of them at full strength.
