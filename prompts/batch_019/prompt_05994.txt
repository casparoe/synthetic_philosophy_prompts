I spend most of my working life writing Haskell, and I tinker with Scheme on weekends for fun. A while back I fell down a rabbit hole reading about the Curry-Howard correspondence: the idea that types are propositions and well-typed programs are proofs of them. That half of the story felt almost obvious once it clicked — implication lines up with function types, conjunction with pairs, disjunction with tagged unions, and plain typed lambda calculus lines up neatly with intuitionistic natural deduction. What threw me was the extension to classical logic. As far as I can tell, if you want a closed term of the type corresponding to Peirce's Law, ((P -> Q) -> P) -> P, or something that behaves like a witness for double-negation elimination (¬¬P -> P), ordinary lambda calculus can't give you one — you need to add a control operator like call/cc. I've used call/cc and exception-style escapes in real code, so I understand perfectly well what it does mechanically: it reifies "the rest of the computation" as a value you can invoke later, letting a piece of code jump back to an earlier point and overwrite the answer it had already committed to there.

What I don't have is any intuition for why that particular operational trick is the computational shadow of classical reasoning specifically, rather than of some other control-flow effect entirely. I raised this with a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD, and her answer — roughly, that a non-constructive proof doesn't need to know in advance which disjunct of a disjunction is true, only to be able to correct itself once it finds out — sounded right, but it fell apart as soon as I tried to restate it in terms of what call/cc actually does to the call stack at runtime. I understand, at a surface level, why intuitionists refuse double-negation elimination: a proof of P ∨ ¬P is supposed to tell you which disjunct holds, and a proof of ¬¬P is supposed to be convertible into an actual proof of P, and classical logic just helps itself to both without doing that work. What I can't get to click is the bridge from that proof-theoretic complaint to the specific runtime behavior of a control operator.

Could you walk me through, at an intuitive rather than a fully formalized level, why "accepting excluded middle / double-negation elimination" cashes out computationally as "being able to abandon a computation partway through and resume an earlier point with a new value," as opposed to some other computational effect entirely — so that the correspondence, which I believe is originally due to Timothy Griffin, stops feeling like a neat historical accident and starts feeling like it had to come out that way?
