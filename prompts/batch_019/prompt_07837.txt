Three weeks ago I started my first job, as the junior half of a two-person fact-checking department at a mid-sized newsmagazine, shadowing a woman who had run that desk for nearly thirty years. She died suddenly last weekend, and because I was the newest hire — and, it turns out, the only other person in the building who had actually read the two essays she kept taped inside her desk drawer, Hannah Arendt's "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics: Reflections on the Pentagon Papers" — I have been asked to say a few words at Friday's memorial. Several colleagues who worked alongside her for decades, and who have heard her deliver some version of this material to every new hire, will be sitting next to her family and oldest friends, most of whom have never encountered Arendt and mainly knew her as someone constitutionally incapable of letting a wrong date go uncorrected.

What I don't want to write is the eulogy that two of her former colleagues have already, gently, warned me off: the one that calls her a guardian of truth or describes her career as a long fight against lies. Apparently this phrasing genuinely irritated her, because she thought it inverted Arendt's argument in two specific ways. First, Arendt's point in "Truth and Politics" is that factual truth is fragile precisely because it depends on a whole web of witnesses, records, and institutions, not on any individual's heroism — a single honest person standing alone secures nothing. Second, she didn't think Arendt was most worried about the old-fashioned, targeted lie, the kind of concealment aimed at a specific rival who might half-suspect the truth anyway; what alarmed Arendt in the Pentagon Papers essay was the newer, image-driven kind of lying that tries to redescribe an entire situation at once for a domestic audience, even when the people it was ostensibly meant to fool — in that case, the Vietnamese — already knew the truth on the ground better than anyone in Washington. Her own daily work of checking one small, unglamorous fact against a primary source was, by her own account, aimed at that second, more diffuse danger, not at catching individual liars in some dramatic confrontation.

Could you write this eulogy? It should run about five minutes read aloud, needs to reward the colleagues who will recognize exactly what she meant without turning into a seminar on Arendt, and has to remain warm and legible to relatives who couldn't place the name Hannah Arendt if asked. I'd rather it build outward from one small, concrete scene of her at work — checking a date, a spelling, a quotation against a source — than announce its philosophical point directly, and whatever else it does, it should not call her a guardian or a warrior of the truth.
