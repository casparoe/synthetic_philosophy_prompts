I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I recently wrote a short simulation of Bertrand's paradox: I generated random chords of a circle three different ways -- by sampling two uniform points on the circumference, by sampling a uniform point along a fixed radius, and by sampling a uniform point inside the disk -- and got three different empirical estimates (roughly 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4) for the probability that a chord is longer than the side of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle. Reading further, I found that Edwin Jaynes claimed to resolve the paradox by requiring the answer to be invariant under translations, rotations, and rescalings of the circle, a requirement that he argues singles out the second construction and its answer of 1/2 as objectively correct. Please argue that Jaynes's invariance solution does not actually rescue the principle of indifference here, but merely relocates the arbitrariness that Bertrand's paradox exposes: that demanding invariance under one particular transformation group rather than another, equally "ignorant" one is itself a substantive assumption about how the chord is physically generated, rather than a conclusion that ignorance alone forces on us.
