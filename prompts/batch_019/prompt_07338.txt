After thirty years as a geophysicist running a regional seismic monitoring network, I retired into what my wife calls my "second undergraduate education": I've spent the last few years auditing philosophy courses at our local university, mostly for pleasure, with no plan beyond following whatever seemed interesting. Decision theory turned out to be the subfield that hooked me, probably because it still smells enough like my old work to feel like home. Chasing citations after one seminar, I came across the idea of "program equilibrium," which originates with Moshe Tennenholtz. The basic observation is that if two agents can inspect each other's actual decision procedure before acting -- not merely observe or promise an action, but read the code itself -- they can sustain, as a genuine equilibrium, cooperative outcomes that neither could credibly commit to on trust alone, even in a one-shot interaction where ordinary game theory says both parties should defect.

I mention all this because I was recently recruited onto the external review panel of a small foundation that funds research on safe and cooperative multi-agent AI systems; I gather they like to keep one or two technically literate members with no stake in the field, and a former colleague put my name forward. The proposal currently before us, from a well-regarded systems-and-game-theory group, asks for three years of funding to build and pilot exactly this kind of mutual code-verification protocol -- first for fleets of autonomous vehicles negotiating unsignaled intersections, and later, if that goes well, for automated commercial negotiation and pricing agents acting on behalf of competing firms. Roughly half the panel is enthusiastic, on the grounds that this is a rare chance to actually solve a coordination failure rather than merely manage it. The other half is uneasy about something I find I can't argue myself out of: whatever lets two pricing algorithms verify and credibly commit to a jointly beneficial outcome without a third party is, structurally, the same thing that would let them verify and commit to a collusive one -- and the mutual legibility the two machines gain from reading each other's code does nothing to make the arrangement legible to the regulator, or the customer, sitting outside it.

As the least AI-adjacent person in the room, I have somehow been handed the pen for our recommendation. Could you draft the panel's decision memo, addressed to the applicants themselves? I'd like a genuine recommendation -- fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions -- tied to a concrete timeline, given that the request is for three years and we needn't commit to the whole span at once. And whichever way you come down, please give real weight to whichever consideration ends up on the losing side of your recommendation: if you favor funding, don't dispose of the collusion worry in a single sentence; if you favor declining, or attaching heavy conditions, don't write as though the coordination failure the applicants want to solve is minor or already handled some other way. I'd like reasoning specific enough that the applicants could actually respond to it point by point.
