Three weeks ago, a close friend of mine was driving home after a couple of glasses of wine — she has always insisted, and I believe her, that she felt entirely in control of the car — when a cyclist without any lights swerved into her lane and she could not brake in time. He was seriously injured. I have driven home in a nearly identical state more times than I would like to admit, and nothing has ever gone wrong. Since the accident, several mutual friends have started treating her quite differently: one recently described her to my face as someone who "could have killed a person," while nobody has ever said anything remotely like that to me, even though, so far as either of us can tell, we behaved in exactly the same way and simply ended up on opposite sides of an outcome that neither of us controlled.

Trying to make sense of this, I have been reading Bernard Williams's and Thomas Nagel's essays on "moral luck," and I now understand why they treat this as a genuine paradox rather than a puzzle with a tidy solution. We officially subscribe to something like Nagel's "control principle" — the idea that people should not be praised or blamed for what is not ultimately up to them — yet almost none of our actual moral judgments respect it once you look closely: not just the resultant luck in my friend's case, but circumstantial luck (whether you are even placed in a situation that tests your character), constitutive luck (the temperament and capacities you happen to have been dealt), and, in the background, the causal luck of being the product of a history you never chose. Nagel himself seems to treat the natural endpoint of this reasoning — that the space left over for genuinely autonomous, luck-free agency shrinks almost to nothing — as an uncomfortable conclusion to be resisted or contained, rather than accepted outright.

What I would like from you is an argument for the opposite reaction: that we should bite the bullet all the way down. Specifically, I would like you to argue for the position that there is no principled place to stop the erosion once resultant luck is granted to be morally arbitrary — that circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck are implicated by exactly the same reasoning, so that a consistent application of the control principle really does dissolve the ordinary notion of a responsible agent, and that this should be accepted as the correct conclusion rather than treated as a reductio of the principle itself. I would especially like the argument to engage with, and try to defuse, the natural objection that a morality with no room left for genuine desert is either incoherent or practically unlivable.
