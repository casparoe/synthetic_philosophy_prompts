Last Tuesday an electrician rewired my kitchen with GFCI outlets. While explaining why the old wiring had been dangerous, he described how the breaker senses a ground fault and cuts the current within a fraction of a second, fast enough that a person completing the circuit is never actually shocked. It took me a moment to place why this sounded so familiar: it is structurally identical to C. B. Martin's "electro-fink" example from the philosophical literature on dispositions, which I had not thought about since a metaphysics seminar years ago. I have been re-reading around the topic since, and I would like to see the resulting debate played out properly rather than just summarized.

Write a dialogue between two metaphysicians who specialize in dispositions, matched in sophistication so that neither position wins by default. One defends a broadly Lewisian "reformed" conditional analysis, on which finks are handled by tying the disposition to an intrinsic causal basis retained through the stimulus, with masks and antidotes handled by further qualification of the same strategy. The other is a dispositional essentialist who holds that this patching cannot go on indefinitely, and who presses the case of a putatively fundamental physical disposition, such as electric charge, that appears to have no distinct categorical basis for the reformed analysis to invoke. Have the Lewisian give a substantive reply to that specific case rather than restating the general strategy, and let the essentialist push back on the reply in turn, ending before either side concedes.
