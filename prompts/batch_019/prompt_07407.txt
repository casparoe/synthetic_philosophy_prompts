I spent my career as an experimental physicist, where almost every number I ever reported came with an error bar attached. Since retiring, I have finally had time to read philosophy properly, which is how I ended up, somewhat to my own surprise, working through a logic textbook at the urging of a granddaughter who is finishing a doctorate in set theory. Mathematics had always been the one part of the intellectual landscape I treated as a genuine exception to my professional habit of doubting everything: once something was proved, I assumed the matter was closed. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem has rather spoiled that comfortable picture. As I understand it, no consistent formal system strong enough to encode ordinary arithmetic can prove its own consistency from the inside, so not even Peano arithmetic can vouch for itself; and a proof that does establish PA's consistency, such as Gentzen's, has to step outside PA and invoke transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀, which seems to relocate the problem of what to trust rather than dissolve it.

What I cannot get an intuitive grip on is what this actually leaves us with. If a non-circular, from-the-inside proof of consistency is ruled out in principle, what exactly grounds working mathematicians' evident confidence that arithmetic, or even something as rich as ZFC, is not secretly inconsistent — is it, at bottom, just several generations' worth of failure to find a contradiction, dressed up as something more principled? And why does that confidence come in visible degrees rather than being all-or-nothing? Ordinary arithmetic is treated as close to sacrosanct, while the consistency of stronger axioms, such as the large cardinal axioms, is openly discussed as a live risk and even ranked in something like a hierarchy of trust. History suggests such caution is not merely paranoid: Frege took his logical axioms for arithmetic to be self-evident right up until Russell's letter revealed a contradiction hiding inside them. I would very much like an intuitive, non-technical account of what is actually going on here, epistemically, and of whether the Frege episode is a genuine cautionary precedent for today's foundational axioms or a fundamentally different kind of situation.
