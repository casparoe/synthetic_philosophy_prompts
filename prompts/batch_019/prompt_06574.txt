After a career in physical chemistry, I have spent my retirement working through the scientific realism debate, and Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction keeps striking me as an underspecified statistical argument dressed up as a historical one. It moves from the premise that most past empirically successful theories (phlogiston, caloric, the various ethers) turned out to be false to the conclusion that our current best theories are probably false too, but that inference only goes through if past and present theories are drawn from a single reference class sharing a stable base rate of truth — and nothing in the argument tells us what that class is, or why membership in it should transfer across two centuries of change in instrumentation, method, and evidential standards.

Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that this reference-class problem is fatal to the pessimistic meta-induction in its usual "wholesale" form, covering theories in general rather than particular cases. Then push the point further than I have seen it taken: show that the very same problem undermines the realist's favored rescue, the Kitcher/Psillos "divide et impera" strategy of sorting the posits of abandoned theories into "working" ones (retained because they earned their keep) and "idle" ones (safely discarded), since the criteria used for that sorting are themselves just as reference-class-dependent as the antirealist's original inference. I would like to know whether anything survives besides purely retail, theory-by-theory verdicts, with no general answer available on scientific realism either way.
