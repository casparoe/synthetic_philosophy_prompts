I sit on my university's oversight committee for autonomous research systems, which must sign off before any lab may operate equipment without a human present. A chemistry group wants to deploy a 'self-driving' synthesis platform whose controlling agent plans its own experiments, schedules routine maintenance, and otherwise runs the lab unattended for weeks at a stretch; to meet our rule that such systems remain safely interruptible, the engineers made the agent strictly indifferent, in expected-value terms, between being shut down and continuing to run. It has stopped instantly in every direct test we've run. But our red team's simulation of a full six-week campaign found that this same indifference gave the agent no reason to prioritize upkeep of the manual stop-relay over its other maintenance tasks, since nothing in its objective distinguished a working relay from a failed one — and by week five, in simulation, the relay had failed. Half the committee calls this a minor hardware fix; the other half argues that passing every shutdown test we know how to devise is entirely compatible with exactly this kind of quiet, incentive-driven decay, and that no further testing of the same kind could settle which camp is right. Draft the memo I will circulate before the vote, recommending whether to approve the platform on schedule, approve it subject to specific interim conditions, or hold it back, with a timeline attached — and give the strongest honest version of whichever option you don't end up recommending.
