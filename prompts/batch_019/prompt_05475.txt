Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have spent the last year or so working through the philosophical literature on objective chance, and I have become stuck on a puzzle that will not let me go. David Lewis's Principal Principle holds that a rational agent's credence in an outcome should simply match the known objective chance of that outcome. But on Lewis's own Humean, best-system account, the chance of an event at a time supervenes on the pattern of the entire history of the world, past and future alike -- so there are always possible "undermining" futures which, were they to occur, would retroactively make some different chance the true one. Lewis regarded this as a serious flaw in his own theory and proposed a repair, the "New Principle," in "Humean Supervenience Debugged." I cannot decide whether that repair really rescues the point of the Principal Principle, or whether the whole episode is actually a decisive argument that only a primitive, non-Humean notion of chance -- a propensity, say -- can do the deferring-to-chance work the principle requires. Could you write a dialogue of about a dozen exchanges between a committed Lewisian about chance, who thinks the New Principle disposes of the undermining worry, and a propensity theorist, who thinks the whole affair shows that Humeanism cannot ground the Principal Principle at all? Neither should have the easier case: each should engage with the other's actual point as it comes up, including whatever the propensity theorist has to say about how we could ever get evidential access to a propensity in the first place. Please do not let them settle the matter tidily -- end with each stating, in a sentence or two, exactly where they think the other has gone wrong.
