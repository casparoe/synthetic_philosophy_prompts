Me and my friend Theo got into it pretty hard last night over this decision theory puzzle called "Death in Damascus" — he'd come across it for a logic class and brought it up over drinks, and we ended up going back and forth for like an hour without ever agreeing on what the actual puzzle even is. His version of the setup: some decision theorists (Gibbard and Harper, I think, back in the 70s) took the old fable where a guy spots Death in the market and flees to another city, and turned it into a real decision problem — Death is a perfect predictor, so whichever city you pick, Damascus or Aleppo, Death has already headed there. Theo's read was that this basically just proves fatalism, that your "choice" never mattered in the first place. I kept arguing thats not even the interesting bit — the real paradox is supposed to be that there's no stable choice available at all: whichever city you settle on, the act of settling on it seems to make the other one look like it would've been safer, so you can never land on a decision you wouldnt instantly want to reverse. Apparently even flipping a coin only half fixes this, since a good enough predictor could predict the coin too. Neither of us walked away sure who was right, and honestly I'm not sure either of us really has a grip on what the paradox even is.

Anyway — could you write me a short story out of this? Not a retelling of the old flee-the-city fable, since that's not really the point anymore; I want something that actually makes a reader feel the specific paradox we were stuck on (no choice you can make peace with, rather than just "fate wins regardless"). It doesn't need to be set in ancient Damascus, and Death doesn't need to show up as a literal robed figure if you've got a better vehicle for the same idea — think of how Ted Chiang uses the actual plot of Story of Your Life to do real philosophical work on determinism instead of just gesturing at it. Something in that spirit would be perfect.
