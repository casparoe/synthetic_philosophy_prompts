My school has a small philosophy elective that works through primary texts instead of textbook summaries, and this month we've been sitting with the part of Kierkegaard's Two Ages that's usually excerpted in English as "The Present Age." I hadn't expected to find it so relevant. Kierkegaard contrasts a passionate "age of revolution," where people actually commit to causes and to each other, with what he calls the present age: reflective, cautious, and passionless, where conviction dissolves into endless talk and comparison instead of action. What's really stuck with me is his account of "the public" and "leveling" (a translator's footnote gave the Danish as nivellering): the press, he argues, calls "the public" into existence as an abstraction made of no one in particular, which somehow has strong opinions and real power to ruin a person, while no individual member of it ever has to own the verdict. He treats envy, not open hostility, as the quiet engine underneath this leveling — a resentment of anyone who stands out that drags everyone down to the same flattened level instead of raising anyone up.

I'd like you to write a short story, roughly 1,000–1,400 words, that dramatizes this dynamic in a present-day high school, without ever naming Kierkegaard, quoting him, or having a character explain the theory out loud. Here's a seed in case it helps: an anonymous app tied to the school's Wi-Fi (so a post can only come from someone physically on campus, never from a named person) starts amplifying an exaggerated, half-true story about one particular student, and within a day almost everyone seems to have a strong opinion about her, even though hardly anyone posting actually knows her. I'd like the plot to genuinely turn on at least three pieces of the idea above: the public's power despite no one in it taking responsibility, the flattening of a specific, complicated person into a cartoon everyone can agree on, and envy hiding underneath the pile-on's self-righteous tone.

Please also include one character who attempts the kind of exposed, named, particular stand that Kierkegaard treats as the only real counterweight to leveling, and commit to a real ending about whether that stand works or gets absorbed by the crowd rather than leaving it ambiguous. My honest guess, given how bleak the essay is, is that Kierkegaard would bet on the second outcome, and I'd rather the story earn its ending than drift toward whichever one is more comforting. (A friend of mine insists this whole essay is just describing group chats and social media a century early, so if the mechanics end up feeling like an update rather than a costume drama, that would be the best version of this.)
