question I gave them: In "Should the Numbers Count?" Taurek denies that we're morally required to save the greater number of people rather than the smaller number, in a case where we can only save one group or the other and there's no other morally relevant difference between them (e.g. a scarce drug that can save one stranger or save a different, larger group of strangers, but not both). Explain the main argument he gives for this conclusion, and explain the most important objection to it.

their answer: Taurek's target is the assumption that if you can only save one group, you're required to save whichever one is bigger. He asks us to imagine a scarce drug that's enough to either treat one person or a different group of five people, and most people's first reaction is that you obviously treat the five, since five deaths are worse than one. Taurek's move is to say that this "five is worse than one" talk quietly treats the five deaths as if they all happen to one big combined sufferer who racks up five losses, but that's not actually whats going on - each of the five only loses their own single life, exactly like the one person only loses their own single life. Since there's nobody for whom the outcome is five times as bad, you can't add the losses up across different people the way you could add up losses to the same person over time. Because each person's claim to be saved is seperate and equally strong, Taurek thinks the fair way to resolve it is a coin flip, and to reflect the fact that more people are counting on it going their way, he weights the flip 5:1 toward the larger group rather than a straight fifty-fifty. He concludes that it would be wrong to just save the five outright, since that gives the one person's claim no chance at all, whereas the weighted coin still respects it.

The main objection, usually credited to Parfit, is that Taurek's view has to say we should be just as willing to flip a coin between saving 1 person and saving 1,000,000, which strikes most people as a much harder bullet to bite than the original 1-vs-5 case, and that this suggests something's gone wrong with the argument even if the 1-vs-5 case seems fine on its own.
