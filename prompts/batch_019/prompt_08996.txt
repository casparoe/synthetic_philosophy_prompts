I'm taking AP Seminar this year, and for our current unit we're each drafting individual research reports on misinformation and media literacy. During one of our peer-review sessions, two people in my group, Owen and Sofia, went off-topic into a genuine argument about how to think about conspiracy theories in general, and it's been stuck in my head since, because I don't think either of them was simply wrong.

(1) Owen opened by saying it's built into the structure of a conspiracy theory that it's unfalsifiable: any inconvenient evidence just gets absorbed as further proof of the cover-up, so the belief is insulated from disconfirmation by design, and that alone is reason to be suspicious of the whole category. (2) Sofia answered that this can't be the full story, since the Watergate break-in, the CIA's MKUltra program, and the tobacco industry's internal memos about cancer risk were all real coordinated cover-ups that were dismissed as paranoid theorizing before they were confirmed, so you can't define the category in a way that makes believing in a conspiracy automatically irrational when some conspiracy theories were simply true. (3) Owen replied that Sofia was equivocating between "conspiracies," which obviously happen, and "conspiracy theories" as a specific style of reasoning that treats official denials as further confirmation and reaches for hidden coordination over coincidence or incompetence -- and that Watergate was actually confirmed through exactly the conventional means (subpoenas, whistleblowers, dogged reporting) that committed conspiracy theorists usually distrust. (4) Sofia said that move is circular: if every case that turns out true gets quietly relabeled "journalism" while "conspiracy theory" is reserved for the false ones, the category will look irrational by definition, and that isn't an argument so much as deciding the answer in advance -- the honest position, she said, is to judge each specific claim on its own evidence instead of adopting any blanket attitude toward the category. (5) Owen's last word was that this doesn't settle whether we can still criticize a reasoning habit: someone who chronically over-detects hidden intentional coordination and treats every denial as confirmation is reasoning badly even on the rare occasion they happen to be right, the way a stopped clock being correct twice a day doesn't vindicate the clock.

Neither of them backed down, and we had to get back to peer-reviewing outlines before it went any further. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each one, who has the better of it and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether Owen and Sofia are actually talking past each other here -- whether one of them is answering a question about what makes a particular belief justified and the other is answering a question about what makes a general habit of reasoning reliable -- and if so, whether that means they could both be right about different things at once.
