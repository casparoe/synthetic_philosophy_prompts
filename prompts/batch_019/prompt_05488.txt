After forty years as a condensed-matter physicist, I've spent a good part of my retirement finally working through the philosophy I never had time for during my career, and this term that has included auditing a small seminar on twentieth-century philosophy of religion at the university near where I now live. The instructor, aware of my background, has taken to letting me mark a couple of the weekly short-answer submissions alongside her, mostly so that I get some practice thinking like a philosopher rather than a physicist. This week's question concerned Kurt Gödel, a figure I first encountered decades ago through the incompleteness theorems, long before I learned that he had also spent real energy, in private correspondence, defending an unusual philosophical case for life after death.

The question set to the class was: "In letters written late in life to a family member who did not share his views, Gödel offered an argument for the persistence of the person after death that appeals neither to scripture nor to any established religious doctrine. Reconstruct the premises and conclusion of this argument in your own words, state which premise is doing the essential work, and briefly assess whether that premise ought to persuade someone who does not already accept it." Below is one student's answer. I have been asked to mark it out of 20, with 5 points allotted to each of: accurate reconstruction of the argument's context and content, correct identification of the load-bearing premise, quality of the philosophical assessment, and overall precision of the philosophical detail throughout.

Student's answer: "Gödel makes his case in a series of letters written in the early 1960s to his mother, who had her own doubts about whether anything of a person survives death. His reasoning is not theological in the ordinary sense - he never appeals to scripture or revelation - but rests on his broader conviction, which he described under the heading of 'rationalistic optimism,' that the world is fundamentally rational: nothing that happens in it is arbitrary or without a reason, in a spirit reminiscent of Leibniz.

"From this he draws a striking inference. The human brain, he points out, is an organ of enormous and mostly untapped capacity: a person's intellectual and moral development is, at the moment of death, almost always still radically incomplete, with the overwhelming majority of the brain's potential never exercised. If this life were the only one available to us, that fact would make the world thoroughly irrational - as pointless as building an elaborate piece of machinery and switching it off before it performs almost any of the operations it was designed to perform. Since, on the rationality premise, the world cannot be pointless in this way, Gödel concludes that some form of continued existence must be available in which this development is carried further.

"The premise doing the real work, then, is not any particular claim about brains, but the global claim that the world is thoroughly rational and that nothing in it occurs in vain - in effect the same modal move he later made fully explicit and symbolic in the ontological proof, where the mere possibility of a maximally perfect being is shown, via the modal axioms he adopts, to entail that being's necessary existence. Gödel is really running the identical inference in prose: because a rational afterlife is 'possible' and the world is maximally rational, it must be actual.

"I think this premise is the argument's weak point. It amounts to a strong version of the principle of sufficient reason, and anyone not already inclined to think the universe is generally purposive can simply deny that undeveloped potential counts as evidence against a world's rationality: plenty of natural processes - a seed that never germinates, a species that goes extinct partway through evolving some complex trait - end without fulfilling their apparent 'potential,' and we don't ordinarily take this as evidence that the world itself is irrational. So the argument only persuades someone who already grants a background commitment to providence that a skeptic, such as Gödel's own mother, evidently did not share."
