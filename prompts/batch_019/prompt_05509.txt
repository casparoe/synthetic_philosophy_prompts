I recently began work as a graduate research assistant in a decision theory lab, and for our first reading group of the term we were assigned Gregory Kavka's 1983 paper on the toxin puzzle, as a warm-up before we move into the literature on commitment devices in behavioral economics (Ulysses contracts, stickK-style forfeiture bets, golden handcuffs, and the like). For anyone unfamiliar with the puzzle: an eccentric billionaire tells you that if, at midnight tonight, you form the intention to drink a vial of toxin at noon tomorrow, a million dollars will appear in your account by morning; the toxin will only make you painfully ill for a day and have no lasting effects, and you need not actually drink it to collect the money, only to intend to, since the billionaire has some reliable way of telling whether you've genuinely formed the intention. Kavka's own conclusion is that no rational person can succeed in forming this intention, because by midnight you already know that once tomorrow comes — with the money already won or lost on the strength of your intention alone — you will have no remaining reason to actually drink the toxin, so whatever state you talk yourself into at midnight cannot be a genuine intention to do something you know, even as you form it, that you will not and should not carry out.

Most of my cohort treated this as more or less settling the matter, taking it to expose a real limit on what a rational, self-aware agent can intend. I came away unpersuaded, largely because the same reasoning seems to prove far too much about perfectly ordinary cases of promising, dieting, and precommitment. Before our next meeting I'd like to have the strongest version of the opposing side on hand, since nobody in the group could reconstruct it properly from memory. Specifically, I'd like you to argue for the "resolute choice" response to the puzzle: that it reveals no genuine limit on rational intention-formation at all, but rather the failure of the standard "sophisticated," act-by-act picture of practical rationality, on which every choice must be re-justified from scratch at the moment of action — and that a rational planner can, and in a case like this should, follow through on a previously adopted plan even when an isolated recalculation at the moment of action would counsel otherwise. Please build the case as strongly as you can, and make sure to engage directly with the most obvious objection to resolute choice: that it looks suspiciously self-serving, since it seems to let you brand any policy that is advantageous-to-have-committed-to as "rational" by fiat — and explain why that charge does or doesn't actually stick.
