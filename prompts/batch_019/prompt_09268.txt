This term I'm taking a jurisprudence seminar that has spent several weeks on the disagreement between judges who hold that a statute means only what its enacted text says, and judges who think a statute should be read in light of the purpose it was meant to serve, even when that reading sits uneasily with the most natural sense of the words. I hadn't expected this debate to feel practically pressing, but a dispute that came up in my university climbing club two weeks ago turns out to raise exactly the same issue, and I would rather work out the reasoning properly than just split the difference.

Our club's constitution, written when the club was founded about fifteen years ago and barely amended since, says the emergency fund "may be used to reimburse members for medical costs arising from injuries sustained while hiking, climbing, skiing, or engaging in other similar outdoor pursuits organized by the club." Two weeks ago, a member broke her wrist when the club's rented van skidded on ice on the way to a climbing trip, well before anyone had put on a harness. About half the executive committee say the fund plainly doesn't apply: being driven somewhere is not hiking, climbing, or skiing; "other similar outdoor pursuits" shouldn't be stretched to cover a van ride; and if we start making exceptions for anything merely connected to a trip, we lose any principled way of saying what falls outside the clause at all (what about someone injured buying gear for the trip, or at the planning meeting beforehand?). The other half point out that transportation is treated as part of "the trip" for every other purpose - attendance records, trip insurance, pre-trip briefings - and that refusing reimbursement here defeats the obvious point of having an emergency fund in the first place. Matters got more complicated when an alumna on our advisory board turned up an old email thread from when the clause was drafted, in which the founding members explicitly said they wanted the fund to cover "anything that goes wrong getting people into the backcountry and back," language that sounds like it was meant to include travel. Some committee members think that settles the matter; others insist we should interpret the clause the club actually adopted rather than a decade-old email nobody voted on, and worry that leaning on it now sets an awkward precedent for how future disputes get resolved.

As treasurer, I have to help draft the committee's recommendation, and I would like it to rest on more than instinct. How should a case like this actually be resolved, and is there a principled basis for deciding how much weight that old email thread deserves against the wording the club formally adopted - or does this disagreement simply reflect two legitimate but genuinely incompatible things we might want a rule like this to do?
