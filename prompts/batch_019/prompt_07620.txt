For a novel I am drafting, I need a scene set at a conference hotel bar, where two former graduate-school classmates who have not spoken in a decade run into each other and fall back into an old argument. Both became epistemologists: Judith has spent her career defending foundationalism, while Aaron is known for defending infinitism — the view, developed above all by Peter Klein, that a belief can be justified by an endless, non-repeating chain of reasons rather than by grounding out in something basic. Write their exchange as a dialogue that turns on the real technical dispute rather than a summary of positions. Judith should press the charge that an infinite chain of reasons can never do justificatory work because it is never completed; Aaron should press the charge that a basic belief is arbitrary the moment it is asked to stop the regress without a reason of its own, and should meet the standard finite-mind objection to infinitism by distinguishing reasons one currently holds from reasons merely available to one. They may dismiss coherentism along the way, but the real weight of the scene should fall on the foundationalist/infinitist dispute. Neither philosopher should be left without a serious rejoinder, and a reader who knows the literature should not be able to guess in advance which side the author favors.
