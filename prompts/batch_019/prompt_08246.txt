I've been reporting on why an AI system's answer can feel epistemically cheap even when it happens to be correct, as opposed to one that is right because the process behind it was sound. That question sent me back to the passage in Plato's Meno that first raises the issue of why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief. Socrates answers that a true opinion becomes knowledge only once it is tied down by an account of its cause; otherwise, like Daedalus's animated statues, it wanders off. This is sometimes read as an ancient ancestor of reliabilism, on which a belief counts as knowledge when it is produced by a reliable process. But reliabilism is widely thought to run into the "swamping problem": once a belief is already true, the further fact that it was produced reliably seems to add nothing to its value, any more than a good cup of coffee is improved by having come from a dependable machine. Please argue that Plato's tethering solution falls to exactly this objection. In particular, address the most natural rejoinder — that "giving an account of the cause" is a condition on understanding rather than on causal reliability, and so escapes the swamping problem in a way bare reliabilism does not — and explain specifically why that rejoinder fails.
