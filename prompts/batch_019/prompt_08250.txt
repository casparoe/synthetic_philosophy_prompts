I've been down a rabbit hole this week reading about Popper's notion of verisimilitude (the idea that a false theory can still be "closer to the truth" than another false theory -- Newtonian mechanics vs. Aristotelian physics is the classic example) for a philosophy of science reading group a few of us started. What's kind of wild is that Popper's own formal definition, built out of "truth content" and "falsity content," turned out to be busted: Tichý and Miller independantly showed (1974, if I've got the year right) that on Popper's own definition a false theory can never have more verisimilitude than another false theory, which kind of guts the whole project since basically every interesting scientific theory is, strictly speaking, false. Since then there's been a whole cottage industry trying to rescue the concept in pretty different directions: similarity/"likeness" measures over possible worlds (which then run into their own headaches about being language-dependent, kind of like Goodman's grue problem), structuralist "nomic truth approximation," refined content-based measures that try to avoid the original Tichý-Miller collapse without going full similarity-theoretic, and Bayesian "estimated" verisimilitude that weights truthlikeness by how well-supported a theory is by the evidence.

For next week's meeting I want to bring a tier list of these repair projects, ranked by how successful you think they actually are at giving a technically workable AND philosophically well-motivated account of truthlikeness -- not just "does it dodge the known technical problems" but "does it actually capture the pretheoretic idea of one false theory being closer to the truth than another."
