I'm writing a column on how contemporary arguments against free will echo older philosophical debates, and I'd like to test one pairing in detail. Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" holds that ultimate moral responsibility is impossible. You act as you do because of the way you are; to be truly responsible for your actions, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are. But establishing that would require having already existed, with some prior set of values, in order to have chosen your present character — which only pushes the question back a step. The regress that results can be halted only by the impossible demand of being causa sui.

Now suppose this argument were put to Chrysippus, the Stoic philosopher who built the school's defense of moral responsibility around a theory of assent. His compatibilism turns on distinguishing an "auxiliary and proximate" cause — the external impression that strikes the mind — from a "principal" cause, namely the mind's own settled nature, which governs whether it assents. This is the point of his well-known cylinder analogy, and it is what lets him describe assent as "up to us" (eph' hēmin) even within a fully fated universe. Reconstruct, in some detail, the response Chrysippus would most plausibly give to Strawson's regress. Then assess it: does his apparatus actually escape the regress, or does it just relocate the question to who, or what, is responsible for the "nature" doing the principal causing?
