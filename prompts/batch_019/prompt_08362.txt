I've spent a fair amount of time reading about how advanced AI systems might end up bargaining with each other — splitting resources, avoiding costly conflict, and so on — and one argument I keep running into has been bothering me. The idea, which I think some people call a "commitment race," is that whichever side manages to lock in a binding commitment first gets the upper hand, since the other side is then just reacting to a fait accompli; but if every sufficiently rational agent sees this coming, everyone has an incentive to commit as fast as possible, maybe before they've actually worked out what's wise to commit to, and if two sides lock in incompatible demands this way, neither can back down and both end up worse off than if they'd never bargained at all. Some people seem to think this danger is overstated and that speed is still basically the right call once you understand the logic properly; others think that the whole "commit first and commit hard" strategy is itself the trap, though I've never seen anyone spell out what a wiser agent would actually do differently in the moment. Could you write a dialogue between two such agents, on opposite sides of an impending negotiation, each deciding in real time whether to transmit its opening commitment before the other one does — one arguing that committing early and firmly is simply what rationality recommends, the other arguing that this very reasoning is what drags both sides toward ruin? Please have them engage each other's specific arguments rather than just restating their own, keep both equally sharp, and don't let either side come out as the clear winner.
