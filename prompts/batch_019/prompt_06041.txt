I'm taking a decision theory course this semester, and a disagreement in my friends' weekly betting pool has been nagging at me in a way the assigned readings haven't quite resolved. Each week we all agree on which side of a given wager carries positive expected value, but one friend insists that being rational just means judging every bet purely by its expected value, staking as much of your bankroll as possible whenever the odds favor you, since doing otherwise leaves money on the table. When I tallied results over a full season, everyone who followed his rule went broke at least once, but he waves this off, arguing that if you imagine many people following the same rule in parallel, it still wins out on average, so any one person's ruin is just bad luck rather than evidence that the strategy itself was mistaken. Another friend instead wagers some smaller, fixed percentage of her bankroll every time, but cannot really explain why that policy should count as more rational rather than merely more cautious. Is either of them actually onto something defensible, or does rationality simply have nothing further to say about bet size once a wager's expected value is positive, leaving it purely a matter of personal taste?
