Since retiring from a career in observational astronomy, I have spent much of my time working through the philosophy of science literature, and I have grown especially interested in inference to the best explanation (IBE). At a reunion dinner last month, two old colleagues of mine fell into a genuine argument about what the nineteenth-century hunt for hidden planets shows about IBE's reliability, and I would like an outside verdict on their exchange.

(1) Owen argued that Le Verrier's reasoning behind Neptune and his reasoning behind Vulcan were the very same piece of inference to the best explanation, applied to two different orbital anomalies: an unseen planet is the hypothesis that would best explain the residual motion, so infer that it exists. Since one inference succeeded and the other failed for reasons nobody could have known in advance, he took this as a clean historical vindication of van Fraassen's "best of a bad lot" worry — IBE at most ranks the explanations we happen to have thought up, with no assurance that the true one is among them.

(2) Marta objected that the two cases were not actually symmetric at the time: Le Verrier's predicted position for Neptune was precise enough that Galle found it within about a degree on the first night he looked, whereas Vulcan rested on a handful of unconfirmed sightings that no two observers could ever agree on. That difference, she said, was available before the fact, not only in hindsight.

(3) Owen replied that this concedes his point rather than answering it: if what separated the two cases was a precise, independently checkable prediction, then the real work was being done by ordinary hypothetico-deductive confirmation, not by any distinctively explanatory virtue such as simplicity or unification. IBE, on his view, is getting credit here for a success that actually belongs to prediction-testing.

(4) Marta answered that this contrast is artificial: a novel, precise prediction that pans out is not a rival to explanatory goodness but part of what made the Neptune hypothesis the better explanation in the first place, on any reasonable account of explanatory virtue. Separating "genuine" confirmation from explanatoriness in this way, she said, just smuggles the desired conclusion into the definitions.

Neither would budge. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and adjudicate each on its own merits, rather than simply declaring an overall winner?
