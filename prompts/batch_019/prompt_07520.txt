My doctoral advisor died three weeks ago. As her last PhD student, I have been asked to deliver the eulogy at her memorial, which will draw roughly equal numbers of logicians and philosophers of mind who worked alongside her for decades, and family and old friends who have never heard of a Gödel sentence. She spent her career defending the Lucas–Penrose argument in its hardest form — not the popular 1961 version, but Penrose's later attempt, in Shadows of the Mind, to answer the standard objection that one can never be sure one's own axioms are consistent, by treating mathematical insight as something seen rather than derived. She was careful, in life, never to let this shade into cheap comfort: "her work lives on" would have struck her as a mechanist's consolation prize, since it makes her a program still running, and "she's at peace, watching over us" would have struck her as a dualism she never held. I want the eulogy to draw its comfort from the actual shape of her own view instead of smuggling in either of those moves — something like: no eulogy, being a finite, justified selection of true things about a person, can be complete with respect to her, and she would have insisted that this is not a defect in the eulogy but a fact about people in general. Please write this eulogy. It should run six to seven minutes, be warm rather than lecture-like, and move the people in the room who loved her without giving the colleagues listening closely any reason to think I got the philosophy wrong.
