I spent the better part of four decades as an experimental physicist before retiring, and one of the more disorienting pleasures of retirement has been sitting down with philosophy properly rather than just borrowing bits of it at dinner parties. Over the winter I worked through Wittgenstein's On Certainty with a small discussion group that meets monthly at our local library, and it left me rattled in a way few books have: the suggestion that a proposition like "I have two hands," or "the earth has existed for a long time," is not something I know in any ordinary sense, but rather one of the "hinges" that must stay put before anything as ordinary as knowing or doubting can get started, struck me as either a profound diagnosis of something I had half-noticed in my own field, or a piece of philosophical sleight of hand -- I honestly cannot yet tell which. The group has arranged a recorded conversation with a philosopher who has published extensively in defence of a contemporary hinge epistemology of exactly this sort, and I have been asked to draft the questions, on the theory that decades of refereeing grant applications have left me suited to interrogation.

Could you draft eight to ten questions that go beyond inviting her to restate the position? I would like at least one question on the sheer incredulity a newcomer feels at being told that something as mundane as "I have two hands" is not, strictly, known; one on the charge that the view tips into relativism once we grant, as Wittgenstein's own remarks about differing "world-pictures" seem to invite, that a community whose hinges include oracles or omens would be just as entitled to its certainties as we are to ours; one on why Moore's proof of an external world -- valid, and built from a premise that looks perfectly known -- fails to transmit that knowledge to its conclusion, and whether her diagnosis requires rejecting the closure principle for knowledge outright or only some restricted version of it; and one drawn from my own field: plenty of what functioned exactly like a hinge in the history of physics -- the absoluteness of simultaneity, say, which nobody argued for and which doubting would have made ordinary laboratory practice unintelligible -- turned out later to be simply false, so I would like her pressed on whether "hinge" designates anything more principled than "currently unquestioned," and whether the view can avoid collapsing into a sociological claim about entrenchment dressed up as epistemology. For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she seems most likely to give, so the conversation does not stall out on the standard reply.
