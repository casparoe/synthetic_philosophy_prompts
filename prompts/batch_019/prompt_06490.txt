My father died two weeks ago, after spending the last twenty-five years of his ministry as an increasingly committed process theologian. Having done doctoral work in theology in his forties, he arrived, by way of Whitehead and Hartshorne and later figures like David Ray Griffin, at a theodicy in which God's power is persuasive rather than coercive: God did not, and metaphysically could not, simply reach into the world and stop the cancer that killed him, because every actual occasion -- every event, every creature -- retains some irreducible measure of self-determination that even God cannot override; God could only offer possibilities and suffer alongside whatever resulted. This cost him some standing with his bishop and with parishioners who wanted a more straightforwardly omnipotent God, and he never recanted it. Nearer the end, he asked, quite explicitly, that whoever gave his eulogy not quietly smuggle back in the consolations he had spent his career arguing against: no hidden divine plan, no God who simply chose not to intervene, nothing implying that a rescue had been on offer and withheld.

I teach philosophy of religion myself, which is exactly why he trusted me with this and exactly why I am anxious about getting it right. I will be delivering the eulogy this Saturday to a room that will be perhaps two-thirds congregation expecting ordinary comfort and one-third former students and fellow clergy who will notice immediately if I flatten his actual view into something easier. Could you write it for me -- roughly five minutes -- staying strictly faithful to what his view does and does not permit one to say about why God did not save him, while still finding, ideally from within the view itself rather than smuggled in from outside it, something real for the room to hold on to? I keep wondering whether there is something to be made of the process-theology idea of 'objective immortality' -- that nothing of value is simply lost, but is instead taken up everlastingly into God's own experience of the world -- though I would rather you found the right thread yourself than have me hand you one ready-made.
