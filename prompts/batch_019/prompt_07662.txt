So this happened in my philosophy program's reading group last week and I still can't decide who came out ahead. We'd just finished going through the fine-tuning argument, and two people (not me) got into a real back-and-forth over whether the multiverse reply actually defuses it. Roughly how it went:

1. Dan opened with the standard "you can't just shrug this off" move: sure, we could only ever observe a universe whose constants permit life, but that selection effect doesn't make the fine-tuning any less in need of explanation. He brought up Leslie's firing squad case: fifty sharpshooters all miss you, and "well obviously I wouldnt be here to notice otherwise" isn't an explanation of why they missed, it's just a fact about who's left to ask the question.

2. Mei said the firing squad case smuggles in exactly what's in dispute: there's one you and one squad. The real question in cosmology is whether there's one "trial" or a huge ensemble of them, and if it's the latter, some fraction will have life-permitting constants, so asking "why this one" is like being handed a specific raffle ticket and being stunned its number came up.

3. Dan said that move -- positing extra unseen universes just because the one we see is improbable -- is pretty much what Ian Hacking called the inverse gambler's fallacy: you don't get to infer "there were probably many other rolls" just from the rarity of the roll in front of you, and cosmologists reaching for a big ensemble to explain away fine-tuning are running the same bad inference on universes instead of dice.

4. Mei said that's not a clean transfer of the fallacy: Hacking's dice-watcher has no reason to think their looking at the dice is connected to the outcome, but in the fine-tuning case there's a built-in link between observers and outcomes, since we can only be around to ask the question in universes that permit life. So the two cases share "rare outcome plus a hypothesized ensemble" but not the structure that actually makes it a fallacy.

5. Dan granted the selection effect is real but said it cuts both ways once you're careful: whether our existence actually favors the ensemble hypothesis depends on stuff like the self-sampling vs self-indication assumption, and on some ways of setting up the reference class the multiverse doesn't come out better supported than a single fine-tuned universe at all. So Mei's "of course some ticket wins" picture is quietly assuming an answer to a question that's still wide open.

6. Mei said fair, but that cuts against Dan too: if the reference-class stuff is genuinely that unsettled, the original "this is astronomically improbable, so it demands a design explanation" step is in the same trouble, since it also needs a fixed, non-arbitrary probability measure over possible constants that nobody involved has actually defended.

I couldn't tell whos got the better of this by the end. Can you go through moves 1 through 6 and say who's ahead at each step, rather than just handing out an overall winner? Also flag it if you think they're both resting on something shakier than either seems to realize.
