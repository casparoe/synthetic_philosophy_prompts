I spent about forty years as a bench pharmacologist before I retired, and mostly to stay useful I still sit on my university's IRB. We've got a psilocybin-for-treatment-resistant-depression protocol up for review that's given me more trouble than any grant application ever did. Buried in the informed consent section is what the PI's team keeps calling, only half-jokingly, a "Ulysses clause": while sober and competent, participants agree in writing that if they ask the session facilitators to stop during the dosing session itself, because the experience has gotten frightening or overwhelming, the facilitators should (absent an objective medical emergency) talk them through it rather than end the session, on the grounds that acute in-session distress is common, often resolves into the most therapeutically important part of the whole experience, and doesn't reflect the persons stable, reflective wishes.

Two of my fellow board members want to reject this outright because it seems to gut the plain old right to withdraw from research at any time, no reasons required, which I always understood to be about as close to sacred as research ethics gets. I've been reading Elster's stuff on precommitment lately (my daughter's a philosophy postdoc and keeps handing me sequels), and I can see the Odysseus-tied-to-the-mast shape of this plain as day. But it feels like a harder case than Odysseus, because with Odysseus we're pretty confident the lashed-to-the-mast self is the sane one -- here I honestly don't know whether the tripping participant asking to stop is the one talking nonsense, or whether the sober self who signed the consent form is the one who didn't understand what they were agreeing to. There's also a bit in Elster about constitutions binding future parliaments that keeps nagging at me, like it's the same puzzle wearing a different coat. Could you draft the memo I need to bring to next week's board meeting? It should land on approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, take the withdrawal-rights objection seriously rather than wave it off, and give the board something concrete to vote on with a timeline.
