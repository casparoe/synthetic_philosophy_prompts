In a graduate reading group on self-knowledge, we've spent the last couple of weeks working through Timothy Williamson's argument against luminosity in Knowledge and Its Limits: the thesis that no non-trivial mental condition -- one that can obtain in some cases and fail to obtain in others -- is "luminous," where a condition is luminous just in case whenever it obtains, you're thereby in a position to know that it does. The engine of the argument is a margin-for-error principle: because our powers of discrimination and introspection are never infinitely fine-grained, knowing that a condition holds in a given case requires that it also hold in every case close enough to be indiscriminable from that one -- otherwise the very same process that produced your true belief would, in a neighboring case, produce a false belief that feels no different from the inside, which makes the original belief true only by luck. Run that principle across a long sequence of cases sliding gradually from a clear instance of the condition to a clear non-instance, each barely distinguishable from its neighbor, and mathematical induction forces the condition to hold at every step, including the far end where it plainly doesn't. Williamson takes the resulting absurdity to sink not just naive Cartesian self-transparency but the unrestricted KK principle as well (if you know that P, you're in a position to know that you know P), since "one knows that P" is itself just another condition of the relevant kind.

I'd like a dialogue between two epistemologists who've both worked through the argument in detail and come out in different places. One should hold that the argument is basically sound and be willing to accept its full cost, including the failure of KK for perfectly ordinary introspective states. The other should try to carve out some restricted luminosity, arguing that at least certain occurrent phenomenal states have no hidden fact that could float free of how things seem to the subject -- so there's no real gap for a margin of error to open up -- and that the argument only really threatens the classificatory judgments layered on top of experience (applying some predicate like "cold" or "painful"), not the bare phenomenal fact itself. Have the first philosopher press on whether that distinction actually earns its keep, given that even registering that a phenomenal state is occurring at all seems to involve a fallible cognitive act of its own, and have both characters keep responding to whatever the other has just said rather than restating their opening positions. Bring the exchange to a close with the two of them agreeing on exactly which single premise -- the margin-for-error principle itself, or the claim that introspection is relevantly like ordinary perceptual discrimination -- the disagreement really turns on, even if they still can't agree on which one ought to be given up.
