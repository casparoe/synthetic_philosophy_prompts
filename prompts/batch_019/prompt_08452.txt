My formal-verification work has given me a decent working grasp of first-order logic and model theory, and that's how I fell down a rabbit hole into Skolem's paradox. I understand the basic mechanics: the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that if ZFC has a model at all, it has a countable one, yet from the inside that same model still "believes" the reals are uncountable, exactly as ZFC proves. Every explanation I've found resolves this by noting that the bijection witnessing the model's countability lives outside the model rather than inside it — but that feels like it relocates the puzzle rather than dissolving it, since "uncountable" seems like it should be an absolute property, not one that fluctuates depending on which model you happen to be inside. Could you go beyond that standard hand-wave and explain what it actually means for cardinality to be "non-absolute" here, why this doesn't quietly trivialize first-order axiomatizations of set theory, and whether Skolem's own conclusion — that "set" and its cardinality are therefore only ever relative notions — still has serious defenders today, or whether it has been fully supplanted by the more deflationary view that this is just a symptom of first-order logic's inability to pin down an "intended" model?
