In the last decade or so of his life, Mill grew markedly more sympathetic to socialism than he had been earlier in his career, a shift visible both in the unfinished "Chapters on Socialism" (published posthumously in 1879) and in the successive revisions he made to the Principles of Political Economy. Yet his sympathy was selective. He judged Fourier's scheme the most carefully worked out of the socialist proposals then on offer, precisely because it did not divide output equally: it apportioned the product among labor, capital, and talent while still guaranteeing every member a subsistence minimum, which Mill thought preserved incentive far better than the strict egalitarianism he associated with communist schemes such as Owen's or Cabet's. At the same time, his economics remained deeply preoccupied with population: he consistently argued that transferring income to the poor without a corresponding change in reproductive habits would simply be absorbed by an increase in numbers, leaving wages no higher than before, and he was generally wary, in his writing on poor relief and on individuality alike, of arrangements that dulled the incentive to self-exertion.

Given these specific and partly conflicting commitments, how do you think Mill would respond to contemporary proposals for a universal basic income, especially versions justified by the claim that automation will structurally reduce the demand for human labor? Would he treat UBI as the natural heir to the Fourierist scheme he found most defensible, or would his views on population and character weigh decisively against it? Ground your answer in the specific tensions internal to his position rather than in generic Millian liberalism.
