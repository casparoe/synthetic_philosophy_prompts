Two colleagues of mine spent a department dinner arguing about a proposal that a visiting speaker had defended earlier that day: enlarging criminal trial juries and dropping the unanimity requirement, in order to make verdicts more reliable. Dana, who works on philosophy of law, defended the proposal; Robert, who works on social epistemology, opposed it. As best I can reconstruct it, the exchange went as follows.

(1) Dana argued that the Condorcet Jury Theorem settles the matter: if each juror is even slightly more likely than not to identify the correct verdict, and jurors judge independently, then the probability that a majority reaches the correct verdict approaches 1 as the jury grows, so a larger jury can only help.

(2) Robert objected that real jurors are not independent in the theorem's sense. They deliberate, they anchor on whoever speaks first or most confidently, and they are exposed to the same evidence and courtroom rhetoric, so their votes are correlated, and nothing in the theorem guarantees that correlated voters behave like independent ones.

(3) Dana granted that independence is idealized, but replied that correlation only slows the rate at which accuracy improves with size; it does not reverse the direction of the effect, so a bigger jury should still do at least as well, on average, as a smaller one.

(4) Robert raised a further worry: the theorem assumes jurors vote their sincere private judgment, but actual jurors reason strategically about whether their vote will be decisive. Once that is built in, he argued, sincere voting is no longer obviously the rational strategy, and under a strict unanimity rule in particular, this can make larger juries convict more innocent defendants rather than fewer, which is exactly backwards from what the appeal to the theorem had promised.

(5) Dana answered that this is an objection to unanimity as a decision rule, not to jury size as such: pair a larger jury with a majority or supermajority rule instead, and Robert's worry no longer applies, while the benefits of scale remain.

(6) Robert closed by saying this concedes his real point: whether enlarging juries helps now turns entirely on which rule one pairs it with, and on empirical assumptions about jurors' independence that nobody at dinner had actually established, so invoking the theorem to settle the proposal, for it or against it, was never doing the work either side thought it was.
