I've been chewing on the Löbian obstacle from the old MIRI "tiling agents" stuff for an AI safety reading group I'm in (I think the actual paper is Yudkowsky and Herreshoff, something like "Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI with Stable Utility Functions"). The obstacle as I understand it: if agent A0 wants to fully trust a successor A1 that reasons with the same proof system -- i.e. A0's theory can prove "whenever A1 proves X, X is true" as a general schema -- then Löb's theorem lets A0 turn that into a proof of X itself, for X's that have no business being provable, which is obviously bad news. The paper's own point is that the "obvious" patch, only ever trust a successor that's strictly weaker than you, dodges the Löb problem but also rules out real self-improvement, so most of the paper is about doing better than that with some trick called parametric polymorphism (if I'm remembering the name right).

Anyway, in my reading group there's this one guy who keeps insisting the whole obstacle is just an artifact of using formal provability as your notion of "trust," and that it evaporates once you let the agent reason probabilistically about its successor instead -- like with reflective oracles, or just a Bayesian prior over what the successor will conclude -- since you're no longer inside a system where Löb's theorem even applies. I'm pretty skeptical of this. My hunch is that swapping probability for proof just relabels the same wall, because you still can't get a non-circular reason to put high credence in "my successor's confident conclusions are reliable" no matter what formalism you're using, so somthing extensionally Löb-shaped should come back and bite you regardless of whether you're doing logic or probability. Could you write out the strongest version of my side of this argument -- that the Löbian obstacle (or a real structural cousin of it) survives the move from proof to probability, and isn't some special defect of first-order logic in particular? I'd like it to actually engage with how reflective oracles are supposed to dodge the usual diagonalization move, rather than just asserting that "probability fixes self-reference" and leaving it at that.
