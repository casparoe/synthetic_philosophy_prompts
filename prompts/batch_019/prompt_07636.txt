I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and next week I'm interviewing a philosopher for a Q&A on animal sentience. She has spent years developing a precautionary, marker-based framework for deciding which animals qualify as "sentience candidates" -- creatures for whom evidence such as nociceptors, flexible avoidance learning, motivational trade-offs, and responses to analgesics makes sentience a live enough possibility to warrant proportionate protection, even absent a settled theory of consciousness. On her view, this already covers decapod crustaceans and, more controversially, some insects.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the framework. Include at least one question on whether a "theory-light," markers-only approach can avoid smuggling in a substantive theory of consciousness through the choice of markers themselves; one on whether the asymmetry argument for precaution -- that false negatives are morally worse than false positives -- ignores the real costs of over-attribution, such as diverted resources and diluted concern for clear-cut cases; and one that presses specifically on insects, given how minimal and differently organized their nervous systems are compared with those of vertebrates. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Close the list with a question about what evidence would lead her to drop a species from her list of candidates.
