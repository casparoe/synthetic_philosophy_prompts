Theories of causation disagree sharply about omissions: does a lifeguard's failure to notice a struggling swimmer, or a gardener's failure to water a neighbor's plants, count as a genuine cause of the resulting harm, or is it merely something we call a "cause" in loose, everyday speech? Compare a counterfactual-dependence account of causation, of the kind associated with David Lewis, with a physical-process or conserved-quantity account, of the kind associated with Phil Dowe, on this question. Focus especially on the selection problem: in any such case, indefinitely many other omissions were equally sufficient for the outcome (any of dozens of passersby could have warned the swimmer), yet we single out only one or two of these non-events as responsible. Which theory gives the more principled treatment of this selectivity, and where, if at all, does the other have to reach beyond its own official resources to accommodate it? Justify your answer.
