I co-run a pretty small philosophy podcast (we record it in my friend's basement, a few hundred listeners on a good week), and next month's guest is a philosopher of religion who defends a strongly Wittgensteinian line on religious belief -- basically that when someone says 'God exists' or 'there will be a Last Judgement,' they aren't making the kind of empirical claim that could turn out true or false the way 'there's a chair in the room' does, but expressing a picture that regulates their whole life, and that this belongs to its own language-game that ordinary factual reasoning doesn't really have a grip on. I've done enough reading around this to know it gets called 'Wittgensteinian fideism' by critics going back to Kai Nielsen, and that the label itself is a whole fight, so I really don't want the interview to just be her restating the view nicely for forty minutes while I nod along.

Can you draft me 8-10 interview questions that actually press on this, each with a short note on the smooth reply she'll probably give (something in the neighborhood of 'you're imposing the grammar of empirical belief onto religious belief') and a sharper follow up that doesn't let her retreat to that same move again? I want at least one question on the fideism/relativism worry -- that insulating religious language this way seems to make it impossible to say a language-game is ever confused, or that two people (two religions, or a believer and an atheist) are really disagreeing rather then just talking past each other -- and one on whether the whole picture is even faithful to what an ordinary devout person thinks they're doing, since most believers would say they mean something like a literal historical or future fact, not just a life-orienting picture, so isn't she quietly changing the subject rather than defending them. I'd also like one question going after the textual basis itself: this 'view' is basically stitched together out of a set of 1938 lecture notes somebody else took down, not something Wittgenstein wrote up and endorsed as a general theory, so how much of what she's defending is actually his versus a later, tidier construction. End the list with a question asking what, if anything, could show this whole approach is wrong -- i.e. that religious and secular claims really are in rational competition after all.
