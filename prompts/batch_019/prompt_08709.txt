I'm taking a seminar this semester on theories of consciousness, and we recently spent two weeks on Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory. What has stuck with me isn't the slogan "consciousness is integrated information" so much as one specific, slightly perverse consequence of how Φ is actually defined: because it measures how much a system's causal structure is irreducible to that of its parts, rather than anything about input-output behavior, IIT is committed to cases where behavior and consciousness come apart entirely. A comparison that comes up constantly in the IIT literature — a single photodiode versus a digital camera sensor — is the clearest case in point: the camera, wired as thousands of independent photodiodes, discriminates far more states than the single diode does, yet because its elements don't causally constrain one another, IIT denies the assembly any more integration than its parts already have on their own — it never adds up to a single point of view, however much raw information it processes. Likewise, a purely feedforward network can reproduce a recurrent network's input-output mapping exactly and still be assigned zero Φ. A friend in my seminar and I got into a long argument this week about whether this makes the theory admirably rigorous or just untethered from anything we'd ordinarily mean by "aware," and I haven't been able to let it go.

I'm picturing something closer in spirit to Greg Egan's Permutation City than to a conventional robot-uprising story: the unease should come from taking IIT's actual technical commitments seriously and following them out to their most uncomfortable conclusion, not from a generic sentient-AI premise grafted on afterward. I'll leave setting, plot, and characters entirely up to you, but I'd like the ending to sit with that discomfort rather than resolve it neatly.
