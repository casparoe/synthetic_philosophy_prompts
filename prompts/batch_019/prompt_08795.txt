I'm a PhD student in linguistics currently TAing a graduate seminar on the semantics-pragmatics interface, and this week's unit covers the derogatory content of slurs. The assigned readings do a reasonable job cataloguing the standard positions — encoded, content-based accounts; conventional-implicature or presuppositional treatments; and various pragmatic stories built around signaling or prohibition — but laid out as a taxonomy, the debate tends to flatten into a list of who holds which label. My students come away able to name the positions without much feel for what is actually doing the argumentative work, which is the part I'd like this week's handout to fix.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of language, meeting for coffee during a workshop break, who hold genuinely opposed views on this question? One should maintain that a slur's derogatory force is part of its conventional linguistic meaning — encoded at the level of the lexical entry, whether as ordinary descriptive content, a dedicated non-at-issue or expressive dimension, or something in between (broadly in the spirit of Hom's combinatorial externalism, or Potts-style treatments of conventional implicature extended to expressives) — so that a competent speaker grasps that content simply by knowing the word, independently of how it is used on any given occasion. The other should maintain that the derogatory effect is no part of what the word means at all, but is instead generated on the fly by general-purpose pragmatic mechanisms — a standing social prohibition on the term itself, or Gricean reasoning about why a speaker reached for a marked word when a neutral synonym was available (broadly in the spirit of Anderson and Lepore's prohibitionism, or Bolinger's signaling account). I'd like the two of them to get into real difficulty over two pieces of data that, as I understand it, any adequate account has to say something about: first, that the offensiveness of a slur typically survives when the word is embedded under negation, in a question, or in the antecedent of a conditional, even where the containing sentence does not assert that anyone falls under the term; second, that the very same word can lose its derogatory force when used by in-group speakers in reclaimed or appropriated contexts. Rather than letting either character simply assert that their framework handles these facts, I would like them to work through the actual mechanics and press each other on the cases where those mechanics start to look strained.

A couple of stylistic requests. Please skip the throat-clearing survey of the field and drop the two characters straight into the disagreement, the way people argue at the second coffee break of a workshop rather than in a lecture hall. Neither side should be easier to argue for than the other, and the dialogue need not resolve into a winner — a sharpened, still-live disagreement is a perfectly good place to end. Finally, to keep things from being needlessly graphic, please do not use an actual slur anywhere in the dialogue; a placeholder term or an invented, mildly pejorative word will serve the philosophical point just as well.
