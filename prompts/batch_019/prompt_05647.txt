I've been drafting notes toward a short paper on the epistemology of disagreement, and before I develop the argument any further I would appreciate your candid assessment of whether its central move is sound or simply confused. The topic is the common prior assumption (CPA): the requirement, built into Harsanyi's framework for games of incomplete information and central to Aumann's agreement theorem, that any two Bayesian agents' differing beliefs about some event must be traceable entirely to differences in the private information they have received, rather than to differences in the prior probabilities they assigned before receiving any information at all. Philosophers who invoke Aumann's theorem in debates about the rationality of disagreement typically treat CPA as an innocuous rationality constraint — something like a requirement that agents not build arbitrary, self-serving biases into their starting points — and I want to test a suspicion that this understates how peculiar an assumption it actually is.

The suspicion arises from thinking about type spaces. A given profile of belief hierarchies — each agent's belief about the underlying state of the world, her belief about the other agents' beliefs about that state, her belief about their beliefs about her beliefs, and so on upward — can be represented by more than one type space: there is the canonical or "universal" space, in which a type simply is its own hierarchy, but there are also smaller, "redundant" type spaces in which two distinct types happen to generate exactly the same hierarchy of beliefs. My understanding, which I would like checked, is that whether a common prior exists is sensitive to this choice of representation, even though the hierarchies themselves — what the agents actually believe, at every order — do not change across representations. If that is correct, then CPA is not, at bottom, a constraint on what agents believe, which is presumably what an epistemic rationality constraint should govern, but a further and somewhat arbitrary fact about which of several equally faithful models one happens to have chosen. I have a vague memory of something like this point figuring in the Gul-Aumann exchange in Econometrica in the late 1990s, though I would not trust my recollection of either side's argument without rereading it.

What I would find most useful is comment on three things: first, whether I have the technical point about redundant types and representation-sensitivity right, or whether there is some fact about belief hierarchies that rules out the apparent arbitrariness I am describing; second, whether, supposing the point is technically correct, it really does undercut the epistemic uses to which Aumann's theorem is typically put, or whether there is some way of reformulating CPA directly in terms of hierarchies rather than type spaces that would rescue it; and third, more bluntly, whether this whole line of thought is already old news in the philosophical literature on disagreement, in which case I would rather find that out now than after writing forty more pages.
