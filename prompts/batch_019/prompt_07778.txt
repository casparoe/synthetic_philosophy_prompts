So this happened in my study group for philosophy of mind (we're in the middle of the functionalism/multiple realizability unit) and I haven't been able to let it go. We'd just gone over the classic Putnam/Fodor argument against type-identity theory — pain can't be C-fiber firing because octopuses and humans are both capable of being in pain despite not sharing the relevant physical state — plus Kim's reply to it, plus a follow-up paper our professor assigned that pushes back on Kim (I think it was Bechtel and Mundale? not 100% sure I'm remembering the names right). Two people in the group, Nadia and Colin, ended up in a genuine back-and-forth about it for like twenty minutes and I still can't decide who came out ahead.

Nadia opened with the textbook move: since pain is realized by totally different physical states across species (octopuses dont even have a centralized brain the way vertebrates do), pain can't be identical to any single physical type, so type-identity theory is finished and something functional has to be doing the real definitional work instead. Colin countered with Kim's line: fine, give up on one universal law tying "pain-in-general" to one physical kind, but that only pushes you toward "local" or species-specific reductions — human pain is human physical state P1, octopus pain is octopus physical state P2, and so on — each one a perfectly good reductive identity, so physicalism comes out fine without needing functionalism at all. Nadia's comeback was that this just relocates the problem: if the only reason we group P1 and P2 together as "pain" in the first place is that they play the same causal role (caused by damage, produces withdrawal/avoidance behavior, etc), then functionalism is quietly doing all the unifying work anyway, and Kim's solution is basically functionalism wearing a physicalist trench coat. Colin then brought up the assigned pushback paper: neuroscientists typically identify "the same" mental state across species using functional or behavioral criteria to begin with, so it's a little rich to then act surprised the physical realizers don't line up — maybe multiple realizability is partly an artifact of comparing mismatched grains of description rather than a real discovery about the world. Nadia's final point was that this proves too much: if any functional similarity used to pick out "the same" state can always be dismissed as question-begging, then nothing could ever count as genuine multiple realizability, and at some point the sheer anatomical gulf between a mammalian cortex and an octopus's decentralized, arm-heavy nervous system has to actually mean something.

Neither of them gave any ground and I was honestly to behind on the reading to referee it live.
