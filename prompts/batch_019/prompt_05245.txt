For an independent research paper on the philosophy of science, I've been reading about Karl Popper's complicated relationship with Darwinism, and I've gotten stuck on a question I can't find a good answer to. In the 1960s and 70s, Popper argued that natural selection was "almost tautological" and better described as a "metaphysical research programme" than a genuinely testable scientific theory. Yet in a lecture published in 1978, "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind," he announced that he had changed his mind, calling it a recantation, and argued that natural selection does after all generate risky, testable predictions. Popper died in 1994, two years before Michael Behe's "Darwin's Black Box" popularized "irreducible complexity" as a supposed empirical challenge to gradual natural selection; separately, other intelligent-design and creationist writers have long supported such challenges by quoting Popper's own earlier tautology remark, rarely mentioning that he had already recanted it. If Popper had lived to see Behe's specific argument and this recurring use of his own words, do you think he would have treated irreducible complexity as a legitimate falsification-style test that natural selection might fail, or would he have dismissed it for essentially the same reasons that led him to recant the tautology charge in the first place? Please reconstruct the reasoning you think Popper himself would have used to reach his verdict, not just state the verdict.
