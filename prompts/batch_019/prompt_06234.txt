I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on the semantics and pragmatics of conditionals, and in the last session we worked through David Lewis's 1976 triviality proof against what's usually called Adams' Thesis (sometimes just "the Equation" in this literature): the claim that the probability of an indicative conditional "If A, then C" should equal the conditional probability P(C|A), rather than being the ordinary probability of some proposition "A > C" being true at a possible world. Lewis showed that, given only fairly modest assumptions about how a rational agent's credences should evolve on receiving new evidence, almost no probability function can satisfy that equation across the board for every choice of A and C — enforcing it in full generality forces rational credence to collapse into something degenerate and trivial. I followed the proof itself well enough, but the seminar broke up in an argument about what to actually conclude from it, and I'd like to see that argument staged properly rather than just gestured at.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of language who stay behind after the seminar to keep arguing at the whiteboard. One is a committed truth-conditional theorist about indicative conditionals — the kind of position defended, in different ways, by Stalnaker's possible-worlds semantics and by Jackson's material-conditional account — who holds that "if"-sentences express ordinary truth-apt propositions, and who responds to Lewis's proof by restricting or explaining away Adams' Thesis rather than giving up truth conditions. The other is a committed defender of the Equation, in the spirit of Adams or Edgington, who holds that it is basically correct as a description of our conditional credences and concludes instead that indicative conditionals must not express truth-apt propositions in the first place. You can assume both characters already accept the proof and don't need to re-derive it; the dialogue should instead dramatize what each side is committing itself to once the dust settles.

Have the two of them press each other on the specific difficulties their opponent's camp is independently known for, rather than just restating their own view. Each should offer the best available reply on behalf of their own position before the other pushes back again. Neither side should be easier to argue for than the other, and the exchange should contain genuine back-and-forth rather than two monologues. You don't have to bring them to agreement, but by the end, each should have to explicitly own the specific piece of explanatory debt that their own position still carries.
