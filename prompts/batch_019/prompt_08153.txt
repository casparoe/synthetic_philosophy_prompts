I have no academic background in philosophy, but for a while now I have been working through teleosemantics on my own as an approach to naturalizing mental content — the idea that a mental state represents whatever it does because of the function it was selected to perform. What I keep getting stuck on is the disjunction problem. Take the standard example of the frog's "bug detector": it fires at flies, but lab frogs will also snap at BB pellets flicked across their visual field, and throughout the frog's evolutionary history, being a small dark moving object and being food were essentially the same property. So when Millikan says the mechanism's Normal function is what fixes its content, I cannot see why that content comes out as "fly" rather than "small dark moving thing" or "food," given that all three properties were equally present whenever the trait was actually being selected for. I understand the general appeal to selection history in the abstract, but I cannot reconstruct, step by step, how it is supposed to single out one of these as the real content instead of leaving things stuck in a disjunction. Could you walk me through the mechanics of how a proponent like Millikan thinks the appeal to Normal conditions, together with what the downstream "consumer" systems need, actually breaks that tie — and then explain, in equally concrete terms, why critics such as Fodor do not think the maneuver removes the indeterminacy? I want to come away with a feel for exactly where the disagreement bites, not just a list of competing position labels.
