I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this term, and for the last few weeks we've been working through the "accuracy-first" program as an alternative to the classical Dutch Book arguments for probabilism — mainly Jim Joyce's 1998 paper "A Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism" and several chapters of Richard Pettigrew's *Accuracy and the Laws of Credence*. I follow the basic motivation: a Dutch Book argument shows that incoherent credences will accept a set of bets that guarantees a loss, which is only a pragmatic reason to be coherent, whereas accuracy-first epistemologists want a purely epistemic reason, grounded solely in the idea that credences aim at accuracy rather than at practical success. The theorem meant to deliver this is the claim that any credence function violating the probability axioms is "accuracy-dominated": there is always some coherent alternative guaranteed to be more accurate in literally every possible world, not merely better in expectation.

What I can't get an intuitive handle on is how the argument scales up, and I'd like to sort this out before I commit to it as my term-paper topic. Our professor presented de Finetti's original version, restricted to the Brier score, as essentially a disguised instance of the Pythagorean theorem: credences are points in a Euclidean space, the coherent ones form a convex set, and an incoherent credence turns out to be farther from every corner of that space representing a fully correct assignment than its nearest coherent neighbor is. Joyce's contribution was supposed to show that something like this survives even once you drop the assumption that inaccuracy must be measured by squared Euclidean distance specifically, replacing it with his more general conditions on what counts as a legitimate measure of epistemic disvalue. I can grind through the two-proposition toy example from lecture, but I don't see what it is about those more general conditions that keeps a Pythagorean-style inequality intact once you're no longer working with an actual distance metric. A couple of classmates and I argued about this after class and only got as far as "the conditions make the scoring rule behave enough like a distance function," which feels like restating the conclusion rather than explaining it. Could you walk me through, at an intuitive rather than fully formal level, why that generalization goes through, and also say a word about why this is supposed to count as a genuinely non-pragmatic vindication of probabilism rather than a Dutch Book argument dressed up in geometric language?
