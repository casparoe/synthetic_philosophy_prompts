Over the past year I have been teaching myself about AI safety in the evenings, largely through books and forum posts, since I never studied computer science or philosophy in any formal setting (I run a small landscaping business, for context, so this is purely a personal interest of mine). The idea that clicked hardest for me was Stuart Russell's discussion, in Human Compatible, of the "off-switch game": if a robot is built to be genuinely uncertain about whether its planned action actually serves the human's interests, and it treats a human reaching for its off switch as evidence about that, then the rational move for the robot is to let itself be switched off, since resisting would only make sense if it were already certain it knew better, and a properly designed agent should never be that certain. That struck me as a remarkably clean solution: build the right kind of humility into the reward function, and the incentive to disable your own off switch simply disappears.

Given that, I am puzzled as to why "the shutdown problem" is still treated in the AI safety literature as a live, unresolved issue, with people apparently developing entirely new decision-theoretic frameworks (I have come across references to proposals involving "incomplete preferences") just to get an agent to tolerate being shut down. If uncertainty about the reward function already does the job, as Russell's game seems to demonstrate, why isn't that the end of the story? I mentioned this to an acquaintance who works in machine learning, and he agreed the problem is "definitely not solved," but he was on his way into a meeting and could not elaborate, so I would genuinely like to understand what the off-switch game leaves out, or where my reasoning goes astray.
