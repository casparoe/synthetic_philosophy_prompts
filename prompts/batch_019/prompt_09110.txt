I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on social epistemology, with a research interest in how institutions aggregate dispersed information -- which is presumably why I ended up on the nine-member allocations subcommittee of my university's Graduate Student Senate. Each spring the subcommittee awards a single $40,000 "Graduate Life Innovation Grant" to one proposal from a student-submitted shortlist; this year it has come down to a subsidized on-campus childcare co-op and an expanded mental-health telehealth stipend, and both camps have lobbied hard. A fellow senator, who has been reading about Robin Hanson's futarchy proposal and its recent small-scale outings on Optimism and Solana's MetaDAO, wants us to settle the tie not by our usual closed-door vote but by a decision market: the full senate would first vote on a welfare metric (he's proposing a composite built from items already on our annual wellbeing survey), and we would then open two conditional markets -- one that resolves on that metric if childcare receives the grant, one that resolves on it if telehealth does -- denominated in bookstore-credit tokens, since university policy forbids anything resembling real-money gambling among students. Whichever conditional market is trading higher after two weeks would determine which proposal receives the funds.

Reactions on the subcommittee split roughly down the middle. Some members are genuinely excited, on the grounds that this would surface information dispersed among our roughly 900 graduate students in a way our nine-person vote never could. Others think it's an elegant solution to a problem we don't have, and worry that a market this thin, with stakes this low, run among a population as small and socially entangled as ours, will end up rewarding whichever faction organizes best rather than whichever proposal actually serves the metric. As the subcommittee's resident philosophy-of-markets person, I've been asked to draft the decision memo we'll circulate before the full senate meeting next Tuesday.

Could you write that memo? It should close with one clear recommendation -- run the market as proposed, run a modified or smaller pilot version, or decline and keep the ordinary vote -- attached to a concrete timeline for what happens next either way. I'd like it to take the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't land on seriously, and to engage with the actual mechanics on the table -- the wellbeing-survey metric, the conditional-market structure, the token stand-in for real money, the size of our trading population -- rather than restating the generic "markets versus deliberation" talking points you'd find in any op-ed on the subject.
