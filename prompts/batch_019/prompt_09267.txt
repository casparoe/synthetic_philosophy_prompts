Okay, something about information cascades has been bugging me and I don't think I actually have a gears-level model of it. In the classic sequential herding setup -- the Bikhchandani/Hirshleifer/Welch style models, where people arrive one at a time, each gets a noisy private signal, each sees the full history of what everyone before them chose (but not their signals or how confident they were), and then acts -- a cascade can form and rope in a huge number of people, but it's also described as really fragile: one more piece of public info can flip the whole crowd even after hundreds of people have piled on, as if all those choices were worth almost nothing evidentially. That seems backwards on first pass -- shouldn't 300 people picking option A be much stronger evidence for A than 3 people picking it, no matter how the whole thing got started? Can you walk through the actual mechanism by which the cascade stops accumulating evidence once its running, and explain why this isn't a bias or mistake by anyone involved -- i.e. how every single person in that crowd of 300 can be a perfectly rational bayesian updater and the aggregate result is still this thin, brittle thing that one contrary data point can knock over?
