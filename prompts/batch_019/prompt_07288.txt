After our department's colloquium on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, two of my postdoctoral researchers, Dev and Renata, stayed behind and fell into an exchange I have not been able to stop turning over. (1) Dev defended illusionism: there is no intrinsic, ineffable phenomenal quality to pain or to the redness of red, only an introspective mechanism that represents experience as having such qualities — inaccurately, in the way a visual illusion represents two equal lines as unequal. (2) Renata objected that this cannot be the whole story: you can be wrong about the external world, but not about how things merely seem to you, and that this sort of certainty about how things seem is exactly the datum illusionism denies. (3) Dev replied that the sense of certainty is itself just another output of the same introspective system, so if that system can misrepresent first-order experience, there is no principled reason to exempt its second-order verdict that "this is definitely how it feels" — insisting otherwise simply re-asserts phenomenal realism rather than arguing for it. (4) Renata answered that this move is too cheap: if any testimony about how things seem can be waved away as a further possible misrepresentation, then no experience could ever count as evidence against illusionism, which makes the view unfalsifiable by the very data it is supposed to explain. (5) Dev's last word was that illusionism is not insulated from all disconfirmation, only from this one kind of testimonial evidence — it still owes, and can in principle be held to, independent predictions about the cognitive mechanisms that generate the introspective "illusion" in the first place. Neither conceded a thing. Please go through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall, and close by identifying what you think both Dev and Renata are missing.
