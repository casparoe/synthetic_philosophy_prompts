As a longtime reader of LessWrong and adjacent blogs, I keep coming back to a connection I haven't seen anyone spell out. Yudkowsky's old sequence post "No Universally Compelling Arguments" argues that no fixed sequence of words could move every possible mind toward a given conclusion, since persuasion works only by engaging cognitive machinery the listener already happens to have. This strikes me as, in substance, a generalization of Bernard Williams's internalism about practical reasons to the space of all possible minds: for Williams, the statement "A has a reason to X" is true only if X-ing would serve some element already present in A's subjective motivational set S, reached by a sound deliberative route from S. I would like you to argue for the externalist side against this shared assumption. Specifically, make the strongest case you can that Williams's own concession—that a "sound deliberative route" may include coming to appreciate one's situation more clearly, and not merely narrow means-end calculation—already commits him to reasons that do not bottom out in the agent's antecedent S, so that his official rejection of external reason statements as false or unintelligible does not actually succeed.
