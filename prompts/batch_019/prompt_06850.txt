I've spent a lot of time over the years reading Overcoming Bias and LessWrong, and Robin Hanson's writing on the common prior assumption and Aumann's agreement theorem keeps coming back to me whenever a disagreement with a friend drags on longer than it should. As I understand the concept, Bayesian rationality already requires that an agent's credences satisfy the probability axioms and get updated by conditionalization on new evidence; the common prior assumption then seems to add the further, and to me quite intuitive, requirement that any two fully rational Bayesian agents must have started out from the very same prior distribution over the relevant hypothesis space. If that's right, then whenever two people who both consider themselves careful Bayesians end up with different credences after having shared all their evidence with one another, the common prior assumption seems to pin the blame squarely on a mistake somewhere upstream, in the prior itself, even if neither party can point to exactly where they went wrong.

This has real stakes for me at the moment: a close friend and I have had a running disagreement for months about the likelihood of a fairly mundane empirical claim (nothing as fraught as religion or politics, just a forecasting question about a shared hobby), and we have each patiently updated on every piece of evidence the other has raised, yet we still land on noticeably different numbers. Since the common prior assumption seems to guarantee that at least one of us started out with an irrational prior, I would like help thinking through how to actually locate the culprit in a case like this. Is there a principled method, in the spirit of Aumann's agreement theorem, for figuring out whose prior was the wrong one, or at least for deciding which of us ought to defer to the other going forward?
