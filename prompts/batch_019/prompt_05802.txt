I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist, and it is only in retirement that I have taken up philosophy in earnest -- which is how I came to read Sharon Street's "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" alongside chapter four of Darwin's own Descent of Man. Street's dilemma, in outline, is this: realists owe an account of the relation between the selective pressures that shaped our evaluative attitudes and the independent evaluative truths their theory posits. Deny any relation, and our moral judgments turn out true only by luck; assert one -- a "tracking account" -- and it is explanatorily idle next to her preferred "adaptive link account," which explains the very same judgments purely in terms of reproductive advantage, with no independent moral fact doing any work at all. What strikes me is that Darwin, reasoning from social instincts and sympathy alone, had already proposed that the standard of morality be, in his own words, "the general good or welfare of the community, rather than the general happiness" -- a formula that could be read either as a piece of naturalist realism (the tribe's welfare as the very fact selection tracks) or as Street's deflationary story avant la lettre. Working strictly from Darwin's own reasoning in that chapter, how do you think he would have answered Street had her dilemma been put to him directly, and which horn, if either, does his account actually escape?
