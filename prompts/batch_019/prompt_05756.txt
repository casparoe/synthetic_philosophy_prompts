been on a bit of a Sidgwick kick lately (fell down the rabbit hole after a Parfit tangent — i think it was him who said Methods of Ethics is basically the best book on ethics ever written). the thing that's been bugging me is the psychical research stuff: the guy co-founded the Society for Psychical Research, served as its president more than once, and spent something like two decades personally investigating mediums, telepathy claims, apparitions, etc., mostly turning up fraud or nothing. every intellectual history i've read treats this as a slightly embarrassing Victorian eccentricity, kept nicely seperate from "serious" Sidgwick the ethicist.

i want you to argue against that framing. specifically, make the case that the psychical research wasn't a hobby bolted onto his philosophy but was in some sense forced on him by it — by the "dualism of practical reason" from Methods of Ethics, i.e. the worry that egoism and utilitarianism are both rationally self-evident yet can conflict in what they demand, and that only some further fact about the universe (god, immortality, w/e) could stop practical reason from just contradicting itself. give me the strongest version you can of the claim that going out and empirically hunting for evidence of an afterlife/moral order was actually a rational, maybe even inevitable, move for someone with his level of intellectual honesty — not a lapse in rigor but an application of it. bonus points if you deal with the obvious "isn't this just motivated reasoning" objection along the way.
