Two weeks ago I did my first sensory-deprivation float session — floating in body-temperature salt water, in total darkness and silence — and at some point I lost any clear sense of where my body ended, so that the only thing I still seemed able to affirm was that "I" was there at all. Trying to make sense of that afterward, I came across Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment: a person created fully formed in mid-air, blindfolded, limbs spread so as to touch nothing, who nonetheless affirms his own existence while affirming nothing about his body. It struck me as eerily close to what I'd felt, so I brought it up over dinner with two friends, Nadia and Omar, and it turned into a real argument about what the thought experiment actually proves.

(1) Nadia said it's essentially a knock-down proof that the self is an immaterial substance distinct from the body: since the flying man affirms his own existence while affirming nothing about any bodily organ, self-awareness must be independent of the body, so the self isn't the body — Descartes' cogito, six centuries early, and arguably cleaner.

(2) Omar objected that this slides from an epistemic fact to a metaphysical one: at most the scenario shows you can be aware of yourself without being aware of your body, which no more shows the self is a different substance than being unsure whether Clark Kent is Superman shows they're different people.

(3) Nadia replied that this is unfair to Avicenna's actual argument: in the Shifa, the flying man appears alongside his other arguments for the soul's immateriality, as one plank in a cumulative case, so objecting that it fails "as a stand-alone proof" misdescribes the job it was meant to do.

(4) Omar answered that intent doesn't rescue a bad inference — if the scenario, taken on its own, only licenses the weaker conclusion that self-awareness doesn't depend on body-awareness, that's all it contributes to the cumulative case; and that's also why much of the specialist literature (Marmura among others) reads the flying man mainly as an argument about the immediacy of self-awareness, not as a self-sufficient dualist proof.
