Two friends of mine, Leah and Marcus (both stats postdocs, not philosophers), had this argument at dinner and it's been stuck in my head since -- I'm doing my quals on social epistemology so I can't help reading it as a peer-disagreement case. Before a big replication project's results came out, they'd each independently forecast how many of 30 pre-registered studies would replicate, using the same public info. Leah said 13, Marcus said 21. What actually happened isn't the point; the fight was about what they should've done once they compared notes, before either knew who was closer.

Marcus: we were obviously peers here -- same info, same effort, I trust her forecasting as much as my own -- so once we disagreed we should've both moved to around 17, not stuck with our own number.

Leah: no, because my 13 wasnt a gut number -- I'd gone through all 30 studies individually, weighting by sample size and effect size. That's a checkable reason my estimate was better-supported, not just a feeling.

Marcus: everyone feels that way about their own process in the moment. If "I was careful" counted as a tiebreaker, nobody would ever have to budge on a disagreement, since both sides always feel that.

Leah: sure, but you don't get to assume we were equally careful either -- you called us "basically peers" before I'd told you anything about my method. Once I describe it, you can't wave it away as "that's what everyone says" -- you have to check it.

Marcus: fine, but how do I check it without redoing the whole study-by-study argument myself, which is exactly the dispute we're stepping back from? If judging whose method was better requires settling the first-order question anyway, "zoom out and compare methods" was never really zooming out.

They left it there. Can you go through this move by move, say who's got the better of it at each step (not just who wins overall), and flag any move that was available to either of them but that neither one made?
