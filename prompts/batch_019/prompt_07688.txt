I'd heard somewhere that Richard Price only got around to publishing Bayes' essay on inverse probability partly because he wanted a formal weapon against Hume's miracles argument, and now that's stuck in my head and I keep wondering whether Hume's own argument is secretly doing something Bayesian, if that even makes sense to ask about a 1748 text.

The bit that's got me stuck is the paragraph where he lays out his famous maxim:

"The plain consequence is (and it is a general maxim worthy of our attention), 'That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact, which it endeavours to establish; and even in that case, there is a mutual destruction of arguments, and the superior only gives us an assurance suitable to that degree of force, which remains, after deducting the inferior.' When any one tells me, that he saw a dead man restored to life, I immediately consider with myself, whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened. I weigh the one miracle against the other; and according to the superiority, which I discover, I pronounce my decision, and always reject the greater miracle."

What I can't work out is how literally to take the "weighing" and "deducting the inferior" language. Is he describing something close to an actual probability calculation, comparing the strength of two competing improbabilities and taking the difference, or is that too generous and its really just a colorful way of saying "go with whichever story is more probable"? And either way, doesn't this sit oddly next to the much more famous, stronger-sounding claim (that testimony alone could basically never be sufficient to establish a miracle) that everyone quotes from earlier in the same Part? It reads to me like there's an a priori argument and a seperate in-practice weighing argument tangled together in this one passage, and I can't tell if that's a real interpretive issue people have raised or if I'm just not reading it carefully enough. Could you walk through what's actually going on logically in this specific paragraph, and how (or whether) it's meant to hang together with all the empirical stuff he piles on afterward in Part II?
