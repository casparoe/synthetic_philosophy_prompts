I spent most of my working life doing research on the vestibular system and proprioception (retired now, finally got time to read stuff way outside my old field), and lately I've fallen into philosophy of mind, mostly out of curiosity about consciousness and the mind-body problem. My book club just did a session on medieval Islamic philosophy and someone brought up this argument from Avicenna called the "flying man" (I've also seen it called the "floating man"), supposedly from his big encyclopedia, the Shifa, in the part on the soul — and it kind of stopped me in my tracks, because its essentially a proprioceptive deprivation experiment run entirely in the imagination, a thousand years before anyone had that vocabulary. The setup, as best I remember it: picture a person created all at once, as a fully grown and rational adult, but suspended in empty air or void, with his limbs held apart so they never touch each other or anything else, no wind on the skin, no sound, no light — totally cut off from any sensory or bodily awareness whatsoever. Avicenna's claim is that this person, despite having zero information about whether he even has a body, would still immediately and confidently affirm "I exist" — and he uses that to argue our awareness of our self is not the same thing as our awareness of our body, so the soul has to be something distinct from it.

I can't stop picturing this scenario and I'd love to see it turned into an actual short story rather than just an argument sitting on a page. Could you write one where some character (human or otherwise, whatever works dramatically) genuinely ends up in something like this predicament — cut off from all bodily sensation — and has to work out in real time what they can and can't be sure of about themselves? I don't want a philosophy lecture wearing a story costume; I want the actual uncertainty and the stakes of it to come through in how the character thinks, notices things, maybe panics a little. Feel free to set it wherever and whenever you like, sci-fi or otherwise, and don't worry about "resolving" the philosophical question by the end — I'm more interested in the experience of being caught inside it than in who wins the argument.
