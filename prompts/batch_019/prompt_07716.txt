I work as a backend engineer, and over the past year I've picked up a fair amount of AI safety vocabulary simply from being around colleagues who work on it — "corrigibility" and "the shutdown problem" are among the terms that come up most. As I understand it, the worry is that any system competent enough to pursue goals reliably will, for purely instrumental reasons, resist being corrected, retargeted, or switched off, since being shut down is bad for nearly any goal it might have. A great deal of technical effort has gone into designing agents in which this resistance simply never arises, ideally without making the agent stupider or less capable as a side effect.

Completely separately, my book club spent this spring working through Hobbes's Leviathan, and one passage has stayed with me since: in chapter 14, Hobbes argues that a covenant not to resist someone trying to kill you is void from the outset, because no person can actually hand over the right to defend their own life. That right, he says, is not the kind of thing anyone can rationally sign away, even by their own agreement, since self-preservation is the whole point of entering into any covenant in the first place. That struck me as describing exactly the structure of the shutdown problem, just applied to people rather than machines — and it may not be an entirely incidental parallel, since Hobbes opens Leviathan by describing the Commonwealth itself as an artificial man, an automaton in which sovereignty serves as an artificial soul animating the whole body. He already had a category, in other words, for a constructed agent with a will of its own.

I'd like you to reconstruct, as carefully as you can from Hobbes's own premises, what he would actually say once the corrigibility problem was explained to him in these terms. Does the chapter 14 argument commit him to thinking that full corrigibility — an agent that never resists correction or shutdown — is not an engineering problem still awaiting a solution, but something closer to a contradiction in terms for any agent capable enough to be worth worrying about at all? And if he would expect resistance to be inevitable once an artificial agent is capable enough to count as a "person" in his sense, what would he recommend instead, given that his own solution to the equivalent problem among human beings was never to try to suppress the will to self-preservation, but to arrange power so that acting on it is never rational? Would his advice about handling a powerful AI end up closer to his advice about restraining a sovereign, or closer to his advice about managing a subject?
