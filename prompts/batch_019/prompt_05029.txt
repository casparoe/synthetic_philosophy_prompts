I spent my working life as a research chemist, and now that I'm retired I've got way more time to read the philosophy I always meant to get around to. A few weeks back I went down a rabbit hole on philosophy of religion and came across David Lewis's little essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" -- the one where he tries to work out whether it even makes logical sense to say Christ was punished "in place of" sinners. The way I understood his argument, the crux is a comparison with debt: if I owe you fifty bucks, you don't care who hands you the money, the debt's just gone once its paid. But punishment seems totally different -- if some random guy goes to prison for a robbery someone else committed, nobody thinks justice got served, we think an innocent man got railroaded and the actual robber is still walking free. Lewis spends most of the essay trying to pin down exactly why those two cases feel so different, and I came away thinking his tentative answer turns on whether punishment is essentially about the guilty party personally bearing a cost, versus something more like a debt owed to an aggrieved party (God) who, as the one who's owed, might have the standing to accept a substitute if he wants to.

Obviously Lewis died in 2001, so he never got to weigh in on what came after. I've since learned that William Lane Craig wrote a whole book defending penal substitution more or less head-on against Lewis's kind of worry, leaning on legal ideas like vicarious liability and imputed guilt, while Eleonore Stump went the opposite direction and argued that analytic theology should just drop the whole penal, transactional picture of the atonement in favor of a more Thomistic story built around love and union rather than guilt and debt. I brought this up with an old labmate of mine over email (he's a molecular biologist with zero theology background, but he loves this kind of argument) and we just ended up arguing in circles about which way Lewis would have jumped. So I figured I'd ask you instead: going by the actual moves Lewis makes in his essay -- his instincts about desert, about what makes punishment different from debt, about who has standing to forgive -- do you think he'd have found Craig's rescue attempt convincing, or been more sympathetic to Stump's move to just abandon the penal framework? Or do you reckon he'd have said neither one actually gets past the problem he raised in the first place?
