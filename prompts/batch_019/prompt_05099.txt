So I'm hashing out a chapter on higher-order evidence and want a sanity check on an argument before I commit to it in writing. Conciliationists (equal-weight-view style) say that once you find out a genuine epistemic peer disagrees with you about p, your rationally required to shift your credence toward theirs. Thing is, "how should you respond to peer disagreement" is itself a contested question in epistemology, and there's no shortage of smart, well-informed steadfasters who reject conciliationism outright. So by conciliationism's own lights, shouldn't its proponents drastically lower their confidence in conciliationism the second they notice that disagreement? Elga's fix in his "how to disagree about how to disagree" paper is to treat your own standards for handling disagreement as a kind of fixed point that isn't itself up for conciliatory revision — but that move looks suspiciously like the exact dogmatic entrenchment conciliationism is supposed to rule out when, say, a flat-earther just refuses to budge. Can you argue that conciliationism is genuinely self-undermining in a way that Elga-style fixed-point maneuvers can't actually rescue it from? If you've got space, a paragraph on whether steadfastness escapes an analogous charge would be a nice bonus, but that's not the main ask.
