I'm taking a seminar this term on theories of causation, and we've just moved from Lewis's counterfactual analysis to Woodward's interventionist account. As I understand it, Woodward's central improvement on Lewis is to replace the rather mysterious appeal to similarity among possible worlds with the more concrete idea of what would happen under a hypothetical experimental intervention on the cause variable. But if a genuine intervention has to be something that could actually be carried out on a real system, this seems to imply that causal claims about things nobody could ever experiment on—say, the initial conditions of the universe, why the dinosaurs went extinct, or the causes of a past epidemic—turn out not to be genuine causal claims at all, or at best a much shakier sort of causal claim than the ones we can verify in a lab. Given how much of science and history rests on causal claims of exactly this sort—ones that could never be tested by an actual intervention—why should interventionism count as progress over the counterfactual theory rather than a step backward?
