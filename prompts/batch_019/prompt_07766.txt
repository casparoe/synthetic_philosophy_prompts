So this happened in my epistemology reading group like two weeks ago and I still can't decide who won. We're doing a unit on Wittgenstein's On Certainty this semester, plus some of the secondary lit around it (Pritchard's stuff on hinge commitments not being beliefs, a bit of Wright, some Coliva), and two people in the group -- I'll call them Deb and Marcus so I don't get in trouble -- ended up in a proper argument about whether Wittgenstein actually answers Moore's "here is a hand" business or just quietly changes the subject. I was supposed to be drafting my own seminar paper this week but now I can't stop chewing on their exchange instead of writing it, so here's as much of it as I could reconstruct afterward:

(1) Deb opened by saying the whole hinge move is a bait-and-switch: Wittgenstein grants that Moore can't really be wrong about "here is a hand," but then says that's not knowledge, it's just a hinge our practices turn on -- so instead of refuting the skeptic he's conceding our whole belief system rests on something unjustified and telling us not to worry about it. Same conclusion as the skeptic, just better PR.

(2) Marcus said that gets the dialectic backwards. The skeptic's argument only bites if "knowing p" always means having ruled out alternatives on the basis of evidence, and the entire point of calling something a hinge is that it isn't in the market for justification to begin with -- like asking what justifies the rule that bishops move diagonally. Once hinges are constitutive of the practice of giving and asking for reasons, rather than an output of that practice, "how do you know your hinges are correct" isn't a question we've failed to answer, it's a question that doesn't even have the right grammar.

(3) Deb replied that this is too clean, because Wittgenstein's own examples don't cooperate -- things like "the earth has existed for a long time" or "I am now in England" are exactly the sort of thing people normally support with evidence and would give up if the evidence went the other way, and Wittgenstein himself says the line between rule-like and empirical propositions shifts and isnt sharp (the riverbed passage). So either ordinary hinges are just extremely well-confirmed empirical beliefs, in which case they're not exempt from justification after all and the skeptic's back in business for them, or Marcus owes us a principled line between the two kinds, which nobody in the text ever actually draws.

(4) Marcus said that's exactly why people like Coliva split hinges into two tiers -- a small set of genuinely basic ones (there's a world, my faculties are roughly reliable) that are preconditions for any inquiry at all and aren't candidates for evidence either way, plus a much bigger set of ordinary, context-relative hinges that behave just like Deb says: fixed within a given inquiry, but revisable from outside it. That's not a patch, he said, it's literally Wittgenstein's own river image -- the bed is mostly rock, but partly sand that shifts.

(5) Deb said fine, but now all the real philosophical weight has just moved onto that small set of basic hinges, and for those Wright is at least honest in a way Wittgenstein isn't: Wright says flat out we have no evidence for them, can't get any, and we're "entitled" to help ourselves anyway. That's not dissolving the skeptical problem, she said, that's admitting it's real and then shrugging at it. Marcus didn't really have a comback for that one, and neither did I.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and adjudicate them one at a time -- not just an overall winner, but who's got the better of each specific exchange and what the other person is missing at that step? Then give me a one-line final verdict: Deb, Marcus, or genuine tie. And since neither of us could settle (5) that night, one more thing -- is that actually a live, unresolved soft spot in entitlement-style responses to hinge skepticism, or is there some standard move in the literature that Deb, Marcus, and I are all just missing?
