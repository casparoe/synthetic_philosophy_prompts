Here's the problem from the set: "The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem implies that if ZFC is consistent, it has a countable model M. But ZFC proves that P(ω) is uncountable (this is Cantor's theorem). (a) Explain what is going on with P(ω) inside M, and why this isn't an outright contradiction. (b) Is Cantor's Theorem true or false 'inside' M? Explain. (c) Textbooks often say Skolem's Paradox shows that cardinality notions like 'countable' aren't absolute between models. Explain what 'absolute' means here and how this resolves the puzzle. (d) Is this the same underlying phenomenon as ZFC's failure to be categorical, or as Gödel's incompleteness theorems? Explain the relationship, if any."

And here's Priya's answer: "(a) By downward Löwenheim–Skolem, since ZFC's language is countable, if ZFC has an infinite model it has a countable model M. Since ZFC proves 'P(ω) is uncountable,' M satisfies this sentence too — there's some element of M that M thinks is P(ω), and M thinks there's no bijection between it and ω. But M itself only has countably many elements total, so from outside M, the object M calls 'P(ω)' is really just a countable set. It doesn't contain a bijection between itself and ω as one of its elements, that's all. So M isn't wrong so much as it's missing a piece of information (the bijection) that exists in principle once you step outside it. This isn't a real contradiction because M is still being completely consistent by its own lights — it correctly reports that none of the functions it has access to happen to be a bijection.
(b) Since Cantor's Theorem doesn't actually hold without qualification once you look at M from outside, I'd say it's only true 'relatively' inside M — what M certifies as uncountable turns out, from the standpoint of the background universe, to actually be countable, so in a sense the theorem fails to hold absolutely even though it's a theorem of ZFC.
(c) 'Absolute' means a property comes out the same whether you evaluate it inside a model or from a bigger surrounding universe. Countability isn't absolute: whether x counts as countable depends on which bijections happen to be available wherever you're evaluating it, so a set can be uncountable in a smaller model and countable in a bigger one that just has more functions lying around. That's really the whole resolution — once 'countable' is relative to the model rather than an intrinsic feature of the set, there's no contradiction, just a relativity.
(d) I think this is basically the same underlying issue as Gödel's incompleteness theorem — both are results showing first-order theories, even nice ones like ZFC, can't fully pin down the structures they're supposed to describe: incompleteness shows there will always be true sentences ZFC can't prove, and Skolem's Paradox/Löwenheim–Skolem show ZFC can't even pin down which model it's talking about, so non-categoricity is really just incompleteness showing up at the level of models instead of proofs."
