So I've been reading old philosophy essays for fun lately and stumbled on this pamphlet Elizabeth Anscombe wrote in 1956 trashing Oxford for giving Truman an honorary degree — it's literally called "Mr. Truman's Degree," and her whole point is that Hiroshima and Nagasaki were murder, not just a regrettable wartime necessity. I brought it up at a bbq and it kicked off a whole argument between my brother Greg and his girlfriend Noor while I just sat there watching.

Greg: Dropping the bombs was the right call — it ended the war fast and avoided an invasion that would've killed way more people on both sides, so net more lives were saved.

Noor: Doesn't matter how many lives were saved later, deliberately incinerating something like a hundred thousand civilians to make a point to the Japanese government is murder, full stop.

Greg: But every bombing campaign in that war killed civilians as a side effect too, so what's actually different here, is it just the body count?

Noor: It's not the number, it's that with a normal raid the dead civilians are a side effect of hitting a factory, but with Hiroshima the civilian deaths WERE the plan — kill enough people fast enough that the emperor surrenders. That's basically Anscombe's whole point, and it's why she'd call one murder and not the other even if the same number of people end up dead.

Greg: That's a distinction without a difference. The guys planning factory raids knew for a fact civilians would die too, "foreseen" vs "intended" is just something you tell yourself after to feel better — and isn't Anscombe just a devout catholic assuming an absolute rule against killing innocents and working backwards from there? That's not an argument, that's restating the rule louder.

Noor: It's not just a religious thing though, people who aren't catholic at all still think there's a difference between accepting a death as a cost and using a death as your actual method — like nobody thinks it's fine to execute a random innocent person to calm down a riot, even if it saves more lives than the riot would've cost. Greg just doesn't like that the same logic applies to Truman.

Nobody backed down, and we were a few drinks in by that point. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of each point, not just who wins overall? And also, does either of them actually end up drifting away from what Anscombe herself argued?
