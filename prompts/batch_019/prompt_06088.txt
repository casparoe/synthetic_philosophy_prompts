hey, so for my metaphysics seminar we've got a short paper due on bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and before i write up actual prose i sketched out my argument and wanted your take on it. mainly i want comments on whether i'm actually getting the philosophy right, since this stuff is easy to mangle. here's the rough sketch:

- bradley's move (from appearance and reality): if a and b stand in relation R, and R is some further "thing" over and above a and b, then we need another relation R1 to tie R to a, and R2 to tie R to b. but R1 and R2 are themselves relations, so they need their own further ties, and so on forever. bradley takes this to show relations can't be ultimately real, which feeds into his case for the Absolute (reality is one undifferentiated whole, relations and distinctness are just "appearance").

- russell's response is basically to accept the regress but deny it's vicious - a relation can just relate its terms directly ("external relations"), and the fact that you can always generate a further relational fact about how R relates to a doesn't mean the original fact needs that further fact to already be true. so it's a regress, but a harmless one, kind of like how "P is true" being true doesn't require "'P is true' is true" to already hold first.

- this connects to russell's seperate puzzle in the principles of mathematics about the unity of the proposition: why does "socrates is wise" express one unified, truth-evaluable thing rather than just being a list of two items (socrates, wisdom)? russell thought it had to do with the relation "actually relating" its terms rather than just being named/mentioned, but never really cashed out what that difference consists in.

- armstrong tries to block the regress by saying states of affairs are fundamental, not built by combining independently existing particulars and universals via some further glue relation - the particular and universal are abstracted FROM the state of affairs rather than combined INTO it, so nothing further is needed to tie them together. i think this is what people call the "non-relational tie" move, though i might be mixing up armstrong and bergmann on that exact phrase.

my main worry, which i want your honest opinion on: doesn't this just rename the problem instead of solving it? saying the tie is "non-relational" and "fundamental" doesn't explain how two supposedly distinct things end up necessarily unified, it just stipulates that they are. is that objection actually fair, or is there real explanatory work being done that i'm not giving armstrong credit for?
