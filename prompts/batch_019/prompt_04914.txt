In their celebrated 1958 exchange in the Harvard Law Review, H. L. A. Hart argued that a rule's status as law is conceptually independent of its moral merit, while Lon Fuller replied that law possesses an "inner morality" of its own: eight procedural desiderata (generality, public promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, freedom from contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between the rules as announced and as actually administered by officials) that any system of rules must satisfy to a reasonable degree merely to count as law, regardless of whether its content is just. Fuller illustrated these requirements with the parable of King Rex, whose successive attempts at legal reform collapse one after another, each time because he violates a different one of the eight principles, until his subjects are left with nothing that could properly be called law, only a series of unpredictable exercises of power. Hart, reviewing Fuller's book some years later, accepted that most working legal systems display these features but denied that this makes them a "morality" in any interesting sense, comparing Fuller's principles instead to the practical know-how involved in poisoning someone efficiently: a purposive craft has its own internal standards of success, but satisfying them is not thereby a moral achievement.

This came up recently in a jurisprudence seminar I'm taking, when we were asked to consider how the debate might extend to two contemporary developments in automated law that obviously postdate both men. The first is the use of opaque machine-learning risk-assessment tools in bail, parole, and sentencing decisions, whose internal scoring logic is not fully articulable even by the engineers who built them. The second is the rise of self-executing "smart contracts," which apply their terms mechanically and without any possibility of discretionary non-enforcement once deployed — an extreme realization of the old "code is law" idea. Strikingly, these two cases seem to pull on Fuller's eight desiderata in almost opposite directions: opaque scoring systems arguably offend clarity and promulgation, since those subject to them cannot know the actual rule being applied to them, whereas smart contracts achieve congruence between announced rule and official action almost too perfectly, apparently at the cost of the space for reciprocity and discretionary judgment that Fuller thought was essential to law as opposed to mere management. How do you think Fuller would assess each of these two cases against his eight principles, and would he conclude that either, or both, fail to count as law in his sense, rather than merely being bad or unwise law? And how would Hart, holding fast to his separation thesis, most plausibly resist whatever verdict Fuller reaches — would he insist, much as he did regarding the validity of wicked Nazi-era statutes, that these are simply separate questions about a rule-system's design and efficacy, with no real bearing on legal validity as such?
