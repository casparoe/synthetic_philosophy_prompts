Had a long, kind of pointless arguement with my friend Dee last night about whether 'disease' is just a biological fact or whether its shot through with values, and neither of us gave any ground. She's fully on the Boorse train: disease is just statistically subnormal functioning relative to your species/age/sex reference class, no values required, end of story. I kept pushing on menopause — it's the loss of a function (ovulation) that used to work fine, but Boorse has to call it healthy because it's "normal for the reference class" of women that age, which felt to me like he was just choosing how to slice up the population so the answer comes out non-disease, not discovering some value-free fact about bodies. Then I brought up how longevity scientists are actually lobbying right now to get aging itself classified as a disease, purely so anti-aging drugs have an official condition to treat — which seems like proof that disease categories get drawn where it's useful, not where biology insists. Dee thinks all of this is still fine for naturalism if you're careful about it. Can you write an essay that actually settles this: lay out naturalism (Boorse specifically, including how and why the reference-class move is supposed to work) versus normativism or hybrid views (pull in Nordenfelt or Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, your call), use the menopause/aging reference-class issue as the real test case instead of something generic like homosexuality-in-the-DSM, and land on an actual verdict — 'naturalism is right about the biology but illness/treatment-worthiness is separately normative' counts as a verdict, 'it's complicated' doesn't.
