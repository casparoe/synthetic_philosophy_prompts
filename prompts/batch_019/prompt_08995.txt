I'm plotting out a historical novel set in the 1690s where the main character is a governess hired to tutor the two sons of a minor gentry family who are obsessed with all the "new" ideas about child-rearing coming out of London. So naturally I've fallen down a research hole on Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education (cold baths, loose shoes, no beating the children, all that) to get her methods and voice right, and something's been nagging at me that I can't quite shake, even after asking a history-nerd friend of mine about it over drinks last week.

Locke is basically THE tabula rasa guy -- no innate ideas, mind is blank paper at birth, everything comes from experience and habit. But then in the education book he keeps talking as if kids already show up with a fixed "temper" or bent of mind that a tutor has to work around rather than write on from scratch, like some children are just naturally stubborn, or timid, or curious, and no amount of habituation is fully going to overwrite that. That sounds alot like innate character sneaking back in through the servants' entrance right after he'd kicked innate ideas out the front door.

Could you write me a proper essay on this -- is it a genuine contradiction in Locke's thinking, or is there a decent way to reconcile "no innate ideas/knowledge" with "innate temperament" so he gets to keep both? Go through the strongest version of each side, but actually commit to an answer at the end instead of a wishy-washy "it's complicated" -- I want to know who wins so I can decide whether to have my governess character wrestle with this exact tension on the page, maybe arguing with the lady of the house about whether her youngest boy is just "born difficult" or being raised wrong.
