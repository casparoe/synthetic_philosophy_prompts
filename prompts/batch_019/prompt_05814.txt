I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist, and like most people trained in that world I absorbed Gödel's incompleteness theorems as a kind of totem long before I understood what they actually said. Since retiring I've had time to read around the man himself rather than just the theorems, and I was genuinely surprised to find, mainly through Hao Wang's recorded conversations with him and the letters to his mother that have since been published, that Gödel held a perfectly sincere belief in a personal afterlife, and thought he had something more than a consoling hope for it. The argument, as far as I can reconstruct it, runs roughly: the world is rationally constructed, in the strong Leibnizian sense that nothing occurs, and nothing is built, without a sufficient reason; it would be senseless, in exactly that sense, for something as elaborate as the human brain to be developed over a lifetime only to be used, at best, for some small fraction of what it's capable of before being switched off for good; so if the world really is rational, there has to be some further existence — what he'd have called, writing in German, a Jenseits — in which that unused potential gets realized. It sits very strangely next to the incompleteness theorems, which is presumably part of why it isn't the first thing anyone tells you about him.

I've since joined a small philosophy discussion group that records occasional conversations with visiting speakers for a modest local audience, and I've been asked to conduct next month's interview with a historian of twentieth-century philosophy who has spent years working through Gödel's unpublished papers and correspondence. She's agreed to talk specifically about this side of him — the rationalist theology, not the mathematics everyone already knows.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for that conversation? I don't want anything that simply invites her to restate, for the audience's benefit, the inference from the world's rationality to a further existence; I'd like the questions to press on its actual joints. At minimum, I want one question on how much weight is being put on the unargued premise that the world is rational in this strong, reason-giving sense, and on whether Gödel treats that premise as established by anything or simply takes it as a starting axiom in the Leibnizian manner; one on the relationship between this confidence in reason's total reach and the incompleteness results he himself proved — whether he had a principled way of keeping "no formal system captures all arithmetical truth" and "reality as a whole is fully rational and reason-answerable" apart, or whether that took some work on his part; and one on what the Leibnizian picture of the soul as a simple, partless substance actually secures, given that a substance's being indestructible looks like a much thinner result than a person's surviving with any continuity of memory or character. For each question, please add a short follow-up pitched at the response she's most likely to give, so the conversation doesn't just stall at the first answer. If there's room, I'd also like a closing question on what, if anything, Gödel ever allowed could count as evidence against his own view.
