Robert Aumann's 1976 result that two Bayesian agents with a common prior cannot "agree to disagree" once their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge is routinely invoked well outside its original, quite austere setting — for instance, to argue that two equally well-informed forecasters who have thoroughly compared notes and still report different probabilities for the same event must be making some kind of mistake. I ended up arguing about exactly this with a friend after we watched two experienced forecasters on a panel talk past each other for the better part of an hour and then walk away still many points apart in their final numbers, and we couldn't agree on how much weight the theorem can really bear once you leave its idealized setting.

Write a dialogue between two epistemologists hashing this out backstage after the panel. Naomi holds that the forecasters' persistent, acknowledged disagreement is itself irrational: since both treat the other as an epistemic peer and both had every opportunity to make their evidence and reasoning mutually available, the residual gap must trace either to someone quietly failing to update, to information that was never actually shared despite appearances, or to differing priors that cannot both be innocent. David is unconvinced. He thinks the theorem's force depends on two idealizations that were never remotely satisfied backstage — a common prior in the Harsanyi sense, and common knowledge, not mere mutual assertion, of each other's exact posteriors — and suspects that simply assuming a common prior comes close to begging the question against the very possibility of reasonable disagreement.

Let them work through this in real detail rather than trading slogans. Naomi should be able to invoke the mechanics of common knowledge and the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result on iterated announcements of posteriors to argue that honest, repeated exchange should force convergence even without assuming the forecasters started out with common knowledge of each other's exact numbers. David should be able to press on exactly where the common-prior assumption is doing the work in that dynamic, and on what is left of the conclusion once "common knowledge" is replaced by whatever weaker, merely mutual state two people arguing backstage could actually reach. Each character should respond to the specific move the other has just made rather than restating their opening position, and the dialogue should end with the disagreement either genuinely resolved or sharpened into a clearer form, not waved away with an appeal to "reasonable pluralism."
