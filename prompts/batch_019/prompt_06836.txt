I spent thirty-five years running a condensed-matter physics laboratory, and although quantum mechanics was the working language of that entire career, I never had real occasion to ask what the formalism means: you learn the computational rules, the numbers match the experiments, and questions of interpretation get left to whoever wants to argue about them after hours. Since retiring I have finally been working through some of the foundational literature I always meant to read, starting with Bell's own essays in Speakable and Unspeakable in Quantum Mechanics and then Maudlin's Quantum Non-Locality and Relativity, and I have come away less settled than when I started. Every popular account states Bell's theorem the same way — quantum mechanics forces a choice between locality and realism, and experiment has now decided against locality — but the closer I look, the less secure that slogan seems.

What troubles me specifically is that Bell's own condition of "local causality" seems to bundle together two separable requirements: that an outcome on one side shouldn't depend on which setting is chosen on the other side, and that it shouldn't depend on the outcome obtained on the other side either, given the complete state. Ordinary quantum mechanics only needs to violate the second condition to reproduce the statistics, and that violation on its own permits no signal faster than light. Bohmian mechanics is nonlocal in a far more blatant, dynamical sense — a particle's trajectory here can depend instantaneously on the measurement arrangement over there — and yet it too forbids superluminal signalling once the equilibrium distribution over the hidden positions is taken into account. So I am no longer sure that "locality," in whatever sense actually matters to relativity, is what the loophole-free experiments of the last decade have refuted, rather than something narrower and stranger. To complicate matters further, I gather that some physicists would rather give up statistical independence between the hidden variables and the choice of measurement settings than give up locality at all (Sabine Hossenfelder defends something along these lines), that others would rather let influence run backward in time than sideways in space (this is Huw Price's line), and that committed Everettians deny there is any nonlocal influence to explain in the first place, since a definite joint outcome only exists relative to a branch. I have no settled way of weighing these against one another, or against the textbook view.

I would like your help thinking this through properly: I intend to write up my own essay on the question for a small reading group of retired colleagues who are, if anything, less patient with hand-waving than I am, and I want the structure of the argument right before I commit anything to paper. Please lay out clearly what Bell's theorem does and does not strictly force on us, weigh the main candidate responses on their merits — abandoning locality itself, abandoning outcome-definiteness ("realism"), abandoning measurement independence (superdeterminism), and the retrocausal and many-worlds attempts to avoid all three — and then commit to an actual verdict about which assumption should go and why, rather than leaving me with a tidy survey and no answer.
