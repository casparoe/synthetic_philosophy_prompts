I spent my working life as a physicist, mostly on questions in early-universe cosmology, and one of the odder side effects of retirement has been discovering how much philosophical business that career left unfinished — questions about infinity and possibility that the physics itself never forced me to think through on their own terms. Over the past year I have been reading Leibniz properly for the first time, slowly, working through the Monadology and the Discourse on Metaphysics alongside the correspondence with Arnauld, and more recently the Theodicy for its treatment of the best of all possible worlds. What keeps striking me is how much of his system is built around exactly the problem of coordination between substances that cannot causally touch one another at all: his monads are windowless, yet somehow harmonized.

A friend from my old department, who has since drifted into effective altruism circles, recently showed me some material on what she called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL, in the jargon of that literature) — the argument, which I gather traces back to Caspar Oesterheld's work on multiverse-wide cooperation via correlated decision-making, that if the universe or multiverse is large or infinite enough, it almost certainly contains enormous numbers of agents elsewhere whose values differ from ours, with whom we can have no causal contact of any kind, but whose reasoning may nonetheless be correlated with our own, and that this correlation gives us evidential, as opposed to causal, reason to act cooperatively toward the range of values those distant and unreachable agents represent. Reading this so soon after Leibniz, I could not shake the sense that he had already built a metaphysics of coordination without contact three centuries before anyone had a decision theory precise enough to pose the question properly.

I would like your help thinking through whether that impression actually holds up. Reconstructing as carefully as you can from Leibniz's own commitments — the complete concept theory, the identity of indiscernibles, his compatibilist account of freedom as spontaneous self-determination rather than contra-causal choice, and the claim that God selected the actual world as best among the infinity of possibilities surveyed in his understanding — how do you think Leibniz would have responded to the ECL argument, had it somehow been put to him? Would he recognize pre-established harmony as a genuine ancestor of the correlation on which evidential cooperation depends, or insist that the resemblance is merely superficial, since his harmony is fixed once and for all at creation rather than something individual substances achieve, or ought to achieve, through their own reasoning, in the way ECL asks living agents to do? And does his picture of God optimizing across infinitely many possible worlds give him an independent, specifically theological route to something like ECL's practical conclusion — or does it instead supply him with grounds for resisting that conclusion altogether?
