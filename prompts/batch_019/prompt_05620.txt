A little over three weeks ago I learned that the cancer my doctors treated last year has returned and is no longer operable. I have been trying to think my way through this in the only manner that has ever really steadied me, which is philosophically, and I keep running into the Epicurean argument that death cannot be bad for the one who dies, since no one remains for it to be bad for. Lucretius's addition to that argument -- that the years after I am gone are symmetrical with the years before I was born, which never troubled me at all -- strikes me as its strongest form, and I have yet to find a reply that does not quietly assume what it needs to prove. Rather than read another summary of the debate, I would like to see it actually fought out. Please write a dialogue between a committed Epicurean and an equally rigorous defender of the deprivation account of why death is bad (the sort of view Nagel defends), in which the Epicurean presses the timing/subject problem -- if death is bad, when is it bad, and for whom, given that its supposed victim does not exist to undergo it? -- and the deprivation theorist must give a genuine answer rather than simply repeating that death deprives us of a good life. Let the Epicurean respond to that specific answer in turn, so that neither side concedes more than the arguments actually force, nor is granted the last word without earning it.
