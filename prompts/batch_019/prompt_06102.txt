And here's Devon's answer:

"Non-reductive physicalism holds that mental properties are real and not identical to physical properties, but that they depend on, or supervene on, the physical -- this is usually motivated by multiple realizability (Putnam and Fodor's point that the same mental state, like pain, could be realized by totally different physical states in, say, an octopus versus a human, so pain can't just be identical to one type of physical state). Kim's argument rests on two main premises. First, Causal Closure: every physical effect that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, which basically just means that only physical events get to cause physical events. Second, the Exclusion Principle: no single event can have two distinct sufficient causes at the same time, unless it's a genuine overdetermination case like two snipers hitting someone simultaneously, and we don't think that kind of thing happens systematically between mind and body. So if a mental event causes some physical effect, but that effect already has a full physical cause, and the mental cause isn't identical to the physical one, then we've got two causes competing for the same job -- and by Exclusion, the mental cause loses and turns out not to actually be doing anything, i.e. epiphenomenalism.

This is specifically a problem for non-reductive physicalism because a reductive physicalist just says the mental property IS the physical property, so there's no second, distinct cause to exclude in the first place. Dualists have their own problem (how does an immaterial thing interact with a material one?), but since they reject Closure to begin with, this particular argument doesn't even get off the ground against them the same way it does against the non-reductive physicalist.

One way to resist the conclusion is to deny that the mental cause and physical cause are really "distinct" competitors. Since the physical property realizes the mental property, some philosophers argue the relationship is more like a determinate and a determinable (like scarlet and red) than like two separate, competing causes, so Exclusion doesn't actually apply to this pair. Kim discusses this kind of response and doesn't think it succeeds, which is basically why he concluded that physicalism can't be saved here, and that mental properties have to be treated as a separate, irreducible kind of thing after all -- pretty close to what a property dualist already says."
