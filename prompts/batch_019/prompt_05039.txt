I spent my career in observational cosmology, and in retirement I have been working slowly through the philosophy of rationality. One idea that has stuck with me is Douglas Hofstadter's notion of "superrationality": the claim that two agents who recognize each other as reasoning in exactly the same way should each choose whatever action is best on the assumption that the other reaches an identical conclusion, thereby securing cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with no communication, promises, or enforcement whatsoever. I would like you to write a short story of about 1,000 words that puts this logic under genuine strain rather than merely illustrating it. Set it between two civilizations — or two instances of a single mind — that can never stand in causal contact with one another, separated by a cosmological horizon from accelerating expansion, say, or residing in separate bubbles of an eternally inflating multiverse, so that no signal or bargain could ever pass between them; each side must nonetheless decide whether to bear a real cost for the other's unwitnessed benefit. Let the plot turn on whether the symmetry argument actually survives its awkward details — how either side could know the other reasons identically, or what follows if the symmetry is only approximate — and give your most skeptical character an objection that is genuinely difficult to answer.
