hey, for my islamic philosophy seminar we just covered al-ghazali's tahafut and the prof basically said "this is basically the medieval hume" and moved on to the next reading, which has been bugging me since. wrote up some notes for a short response paper arguing their not really doing the same thing, and i'd love comments before i turn it in - mainly on whether i've actually got the arguments right, not just whether it "sounds smart."

my argument: everyone treats the cotton-and-fire bit (i think its the 17th discussion?) as basically pre-empting hume on necessary connection, but i think they're answering different questions. hume's problem is epistemic - we never get an impression of necessary connection between cause and effect, so our idea of it comes from custom/habit, and he's pretty cagey about whether nature has real hidden powers we just can't access, not flat out denying them. al-ghazali isn't cagey at all - his point is explicitly theological: treating fire as the real agent of burning would constrain god's power by the "natures" of things, which is why he's fine with god suspending the fire-burning link for abraham. so hume denies we can know secondary causes exist (agnostic), while ghazali denies secondary causes do anything even if we could somehow perceive them (a positive metaphysical claim). one's an epistemology of causation, the other's a metaphysics of divine omnipotence wearing a causal-skepticism costume.

am i overstating the gap? and is it fair to read ghazali as making a full metaphysical denial rather than just a dialectical move to open up room for miracles (i.e., he only needs to show the philosophers cant rule out interruption, not commit to full occasionalism himself)? also if theres a cleaner way to frame the hume side of this (esp given the "new hume"/skeptical realism debate about whether he's really agnostic about powers) i'd take it.
