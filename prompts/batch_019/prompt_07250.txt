The question, worth 20 points, was: "State Arrow's Impossibility Theorem precisely, including all of its conditions on a social welfare function. Then, using the Condorcet paradox as an illustration, explain why pairwise majority rule fails to satisfy the theorem's requirements, and clarify what the paradox does and does not establish, on its own, about Arrow's result. Here is the student's answer, transcribed in full:

"A social welfare function (SWF) takes, as input, every individual's complete and transitive ranking of a fixed set of alternatives, and returns, as output, a single social ranking that is likewise complete and transitive. Arrow's theorem says that once there are at least three voters and two alternatives, no SWF can simultaneously satisfy: (i) Unrestricted Domain, meaning the SWF must be defined for every logically possible combination of individual rankings; (ii) the Pareto principle, meaning that if every individual ranks x above y, society must rank x above y; (iii) Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA), meaning the social ranking of x versus y depends only on how individuals rank x against y, never on how anyone ranks some third option z; and (iv) Non-Dictatorship, meaning there is no individual whose strict preference between any pair is always mirrored in the social ranking regardless of everyone else. Any SWF satisfying the first three conditions turns out to be dictatorial.

The Condorcet paradox is a good entry point. With three voters ranking three candidates as 1: A>B>C, 2: B>C>A, 3: C>A>B, pairwise majority voting yields A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A: a cycle, so majority rule doesn't even return a transitive ranking. But it's worth being careful about what this shows on its own: the paradox only establishes that one specific rule, pairwise majority voting, can fail. It doesn't establish that every conceivable rule must fail somewhere; that stronger claim is exactly what Arrow's general proof supplies, which is what makes the theorem far more powerful than the paradox alone. This is also the reason Arrow's theorem is often taught alongside the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem on strategic voting: Arrow's own argument already establishes that any non-dictatorial rule is manipulable, since a voter can misreport a pairwise ranking to flip the social outcome without the rule being able to detect the misrepresentation.

Is 'no voting system can be fair' a fair gloss on this? I don't think so, not without heavy qualification. Arrow's four conditions are each independently plausible, but the theorem only shows that this particular package is jointly unsatisfiable once we've committed to aggregating merely ordinal, interpersonally non-comparable rankings into a complete, transitive social ordering. Allow cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility, as classical utilitarian rules do, or restrict the domain to preferences with extra structure, such as single-peaked preferences, where Black's median voter theorem guarantees a transitive majority ranking, and the impossibility disappears. So the theorem is best understood as a precise result about one natural, but not inevitable, formalization of fairness, rather than a proof that every possible aggregation procedure is doomed to unfairness."
