I began serving as a teaching assistant for an introductory philosophy of religion course this term — my first time TAing — and this week I'm leading discussion sections on the cosmological argument. Rather than leaning on the textbook's three-sentence gloss of "the argument from contingency," I decided to work through the primary source, Samuel Clarke's 1705 Boyle lectures, published as A Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God, since it's usually treated as the classic statement of the non-kalam, contingency version of the argument, and our professor has a habit of springing pointed follow-up questions on section leaders that a secondhand summary won't survive. I've followed the overall shape of Clarke's reasoning well enough, but I've gotten stuck on one inference in the proof of his second proposition, and I'd like to actually understand it rather than just have a talking point ready in case a student pushes back.

Clarke has just set up a dilemma: either there has always existed "some one unchangeable and independent being," or else "there has been an infinite succession of changeable and dependent beings produced one from another in an endless progression without any original cause at all." Arguing against the second option, he writes: "But, if we consider such an infinite progression as one entire endless series of dependent beings, it is plain this whole series of beings can have no cause from without of its existence because in it are supposed to be included all things that are, or ever were, in the universe. And it is plain it can have no reason within itself for its existence because no one being in this infinite succession is supposed to be self-existent or necessary … but every one dependent on the foregoing. And where no part is necessary, it is manifest the whole cannot be necessary — absolute necessity of existence not being an extrinsic, relative, and accidental denomination but an inward and essential property of the nature of the thing which so exists. An infinite succession, therefore, of merely dependent beings without any original independent cause is a series of beings that has neither necessity, nor cause, nor any reason or ground at all of its existence either within itself or from without. That is, it is an express contradiction and impossibility."

What I can't quite pin down is the inference in the middle of that passage, from "no part of the series is necessary" to "the whole series cannot be necessary." I vaguely recall that in Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, one of the characters raises something like a fallacy-of-composition objection against reasoning of just this shape, though I don't have the text on hand and can't reconstruct how damaging it's actually meant to be. So there are really three things I'd like help with: first, reconstructing Clarke's inference as charitably as possible, and identifying exactly what additional premise, if any, is doing the work of moving him from a claim about every part of the series to a claim about the series as a whole; second, assessing whether his remark that necessity is an "inward and essential" property rather than a "relative" one genuinely blocks a composition-style objection, or whether it just restates the conclusion he needs in different words; and third, since this whole line of reasoning turns on modal notions like necessity and contingency rather than on the universe's having had a beginning in time, I'd like to understand how it actually relates to kalam-style versions of the cosmological argument, which do seem to depend on such a temporal beginning. Are the contingency argument and the kalam argument doing fundamentally different logical work that just happens to share a label, or is there some deeper structure connecting them that I should be pointing my students toward?
