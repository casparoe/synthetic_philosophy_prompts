Over the past year or so, I have taught myself as much as I can about an idea called evidential cooperation in large worlds, which I first came across in a comment thread on an effective altruism forum and have not been able to let go of since. It used to go by the clunkier name "multiverse-wide cooperation via correlated decision-making," which is still how I sometimes think of it. As I understand the core claim, if the universe or multiverse is large enough, an agent has reason to act partly on behalf of the values of other agents it can never causally reach, because its own decision counts as evidence about what those distant, similarly-reasoning agents will decide, so that many agents doing this in parallel can each end up better off by their own lights even though no communication ever passes between them. I have no formal background in philosophy or decision theory -- everything I know I picked up through my own reading, some of it worked through three or four times before it stuck -- but I record a small podcast about unconventional ideas in ethics, and next month I am interviewing a researcher who has spent years developing and defending exactly this argument. I do not want the episode to consist of her restating the summary I just gave, which by now I could recite from memory; I want to get her stuck on the places that still do not sit right with me even after all this reading.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for that conversation? I would like at least one question, each paired with a sharp follow-up keyed to whatever answer she is most likely to give, on the following three sticking points. First, the whole argument seems to depend on something like evidential decision theory being correct, so that my choice can count as genuine evidence about a stranger's choice I have no causal power over whatsoever -- I want to know what she would say to a committed causal decision theorist who simply denies that this sort of evidence should move a rational agent's action, and whether falling back on functional or updateless decision theory instead of naive evidential decision theory actually rescues the argument or just relocates the same problem one level down. Second, the question of who even counts as correlated with her in the first place: how similar does another agent's reasoning have to be before their decision is genuinely entangled with hers, and how could she possibly gather evidence about that, given that by hypothesis she can never observe or communicate with any of these other agents. Third, the discomfort of a framework that, as far as I can tell, has no principled way of cooperating only with kind, other-regarding agents and not also with agents whose values are actively malevolent, provided their decision-making resembles hers closely enough -- I would like to know whether she simply accepts that consequence, and if so, what stops the view from asking us to hand real practical weight to preferences that are, by any ordinary standard, sadistic. For whatever questions remain, feel free to press on whether the argument's motivating force ultimately comes down to multiplying a tiny probability of an almost unimaginably large universe by an enormous payoff, which smells uncomfortably close to Pascal's mugging to me, and close the list with one question that asks her, point-blank, what kind of discovery -- empirical or philosophical -- would actually make her abandon the argument.
