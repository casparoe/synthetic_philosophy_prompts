Last night, a friend and I spent hours arguing about Aristotle's sea battle problem without reaching any agreement. She defended supervaluationism as the cleanest solution: the sentence "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" is neither true nor false today, since it holds on some admissible future histories and fails on others, whereas "There will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be one" comes out true because it holds on every admissible history—so excluded middle survives even though bivalence does not. I could not quite articulate why this still felt like a dodge rather than a genuine resolution, and I would like a sharper version of my objection. Please argue that supervaluationism's willingness to certify a disjunction as true while withholding truth from each of its disjuncts is at least as serious a cost to our ordinary concept of truth as the bivalence failure it was introduced to avoid, so that it does not so much resolve Aristotle's puzzle as restate it in more technical language.
