I've been reading Overcoming Bias and LessWrong since roughly the era when the two blogs were barely distinguishable, and there's one load-bearing assumption from that tradition I've never seen properly settled, even though a good deal of the community's stance on disagreement and "epistemic modesty" seems to rest on it. Aumann's agreement theorem gets invoked constantly as proof that two honest Bayesians with common knowledge of each other's posteriors on some proposition cannot rationally agree to disagree, but the theorem only goes through if the two agents share a common prior to begin with -- exactly the assumption Harsanyi builds into his treatment of games of incomplete information, and which critics have always been free to dismiss as an artifact of the modeling framework rather than a fact about rational minds.

Robin Hanson tried to close that gap with his "uncommon priors require origin disputes" argument: roughly, that any two agents who agree on the causal story of how they each came to hold the priors they hold are thereby committed, via a kind of pre-rationality condition, to having started from the same prior over that larger space of possible origins, so a residual difference in priors can only be legitimate if the two parties have an actual, substantive dispute about their own origins as reasoners. A friend from my old meetup group thinks this vindicates something close to the strongly deferential posture -- update hard toward anyone you can't out-argue about your own epistemic origins -- that Yudkowsky spends a good part of Inadequate Equilibria arguing against. I've never been able to decide whether Hanson's move is a genuine rescue of the common prior assumption as a rationality requirement, or a sleight of hand that just relocates the arbitrariness one level up, from a dispute about facts to a dispute about origins.

I'd like you to write an essay that takes a real position on this rather than surveying it. Explain why Aumann's theorem needs a common prior to reach its conclusion, lay out Harsanyi's doctrine and Hanson's origin-dispute argument for treating the common prior assumption as binding on ideally rational agents, and then weigh the strongest objections available to you -- for instance, that priors might be genuinely primitive rather than the output of any origin story two parties could even in principle adjudicate, or that the whole apparatus presupposes a logical omniscience no bounded reasoner possesses, which would make the assumption true of ideal Bayesians while leaving it irrelevant to actual disagreements between humans. I want a definite verdict, not an inventory of considerations: something like "the assumption binds only among agents who satisfy condition C, and most real disputes don't meet it" would count as an answer, while "reasonable people can disagree" would not. Close by saying what your verdict actually licenses in practice -- how much a person should update on learning that someone they consider a genuine epistemic peer disagrees with them about something that matters.
