After forty years as a physical chemist, I retired a couple of years ago and have finally had time to read philosophy properly, which I am enjoying more than I expected. A family matter has now handed me a puzzle that none of my reading has quite dissolved, and I would value your perspective on it.

My sister and I inherited our mother's small vineyard jointly and agreed at our first meeting with the estate lawyer to split whatever it sold for 50/50. Settling everything took nearly two years and four further meetings, at each of which one of us proposed some adjustment to the arrangement then on the table — a smaller share for an earlier payout, say, or a piece of equipment traded for a larger cut of the proceeds. The lawyer checked the figures each time, and by her calculations every adjustment really was a fair improvement over the terms agreed just before it. Yet once the sale closed, I found that, measured against the original 50/50 split we had shaken hands on, we had each ended up noticeably worse off once the fees and tax penalties from these adjustments were tallied, even though our final shares remained equal to one another. When I raised this, my sister said the comparison is irrelevant, since I had freely agreed at every stage that the new terms were better than the old ones. I cannot quite say why, but that answer does not satisfy me — it puts me faintly in mind of a cyclic process that dissipates free energy at every turn while still returning a system to its starting state, though I am not sure the analogy holds up. Did we make some genuine error along the way, or is my sister right that a sequence of individually sound agreements simply cannot add up to a bad outcome?
