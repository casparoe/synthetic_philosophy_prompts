I'm a novelist who somewhat improbably sits on my alma mater's committee overseeing named prizes and lectureships, brought in precisely because the dean wanted at least one non-specialist in the room. Among our charges is the Frege Prize, a modest annual award, endowed in the 1970s, for the best undergraduate essay in logic or philosophy of language. A group of graduate students is petitioning to rename it, citing the diary Frege kept in 1924, the year before he died: unpublished and unknown to his colleagues, discovered among his family's papers only decades later, it records vehement antisemitism, contempt for parliamentary democracy, and a wish for authoritarian rule. Even Michael Dummett, who did more than any other twentieth-century philosopher to secure Frege's reputation in English, wrote after reading it that his feelings toward the man were permanently altered while his estimate of the work was not -- a line committee members on both sides now quote at each other. Half the committee holds that since none of this touches the soundness of the Begriffsschrift or the sense/reference distinction, the prize should stand unchanged; the other half holds that continuing to attach a student's name to Frege's is itself a form of endorsement, whatever we think of his logic. I have to bring a recommendation to the faculty senate in three weeks. Please draft the committee's decision memo -- keep the name, rename it, or some third option -- with reasoning strong enough that a member of the losing side could recognize their own best argument in it.
