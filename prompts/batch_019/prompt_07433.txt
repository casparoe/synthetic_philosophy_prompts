I'm a 64-year-old woman with no education past high school. My husband and I ran a small hardware store for almost thirty years; after he passed away six years ago I kept it going alone for a while before finally selling up and retiring last spring, which has left me with a lot more free time than I know what to do with. A few months ago I picked up a secondhand collection of Émilie du Châtelet's writings at a library sale — I only recognized her name from a documentary about Voltaire — and I got completely absorbed in the short essay she wrote near the end of her life, the "Discourse on Happiness." I've since written up my own reaction to it to bring to a small monthly reading group I have with two friends, but before I inflict it on them I'd like a more informed opinion, since none of us has any training in philosophy and I don't want to badly misrepresent a woman who, by all accounts, deserves better.

Here, more or less, is what I wrote. At one point, oddly for someone best known for translating Newton, du Châtelet describes wisdom itself in gambling terms, saying a wise person should always keep her chips in hand, since being wise and being happy basically come down to the same thing for her. She goes on to argue that happiness rests on five things: freedom from prejudice, virtue, health, active tastes and passions, and — the one that stopped me — a susceptibility to illusion, since she says most of our pleasure comes from illusions and that whoever has lost the capacity for them is unhappy. She's oddly specific about which passions are safe to indulge: ambition and the desire for glory are dangerous because they put your happiness at the mercy of other people's opinions, while study is praised as the one passion that stays entirely within your own control no matter what fortune does to you. Near the end, in what seems to have been written as a gift for a much younger lover, she describes accepting that passionate love was no longer realistic for her, and finding that a quieter affection, combined with her passion for study, was enough to get by on. That part landed for me — after my husband died I assumed the rest of my life would just get smaller, and instead I've taken up learning enough French to eventually read her in the original, which has honestly made me happier than I expected. What I can't get past is that her whole system seems to run on a kind of managed self-deception: she wants us to keep enjoying pleasant illusions we half-know aren't quite accurate, while elsewhere insisting we should never accept anything without sufficient reason behind it. My essay argues that this isn't a real contradiction, because I don't think "illusion" here means "false belief" so much as an exaggeration we're still in on, the way you can be moved by a play without forgetting it's a play — so she isn't telling us to fool ourselves, just to stop being so literal-minded about our own enjoyment.

What I'd like from you is, first, whether that's a reasonably fair reading of her actual position, or whether I'm papering over a real problem in her view that people who study her more seriously would object to; second, whether my "it's like watching a play" defense of the illusions actually resolves the tension or just moves it somewhere else; and third, since I'd rather know now than embarrass myself later, whether the essay says anything genuinely non-obvious or whether I've just rediscovered a fairly ordinary point without realizing it.
