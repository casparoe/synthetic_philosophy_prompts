So I'm drafting a novel with a subplot set in 1650s London, and one of my side characters is a stubborn old philosopher based loosley on Hobbes - the kind of guy who's convinced he's finally squared the circle and won't listen to the actual mathematicians telling him he's wrong (Wallis especially). Before I write his big confrontation scene I want to actually understand the position he'd be defending, instead of just writing him as a cartoon crank. As I understand it, Hobbes thought geometry was the one science we could be fully certain of because we literally construct the objects ourselves - a point generated by motion, a line by a moving point, a plane by a moving line, and so on - which is supposedly why it's so different from physics, where we don't have access to nature's actual construction. Can you compare that "maker's knowledge" picture of geometry with the more standard Euclidean/Platonist view, where geometers are uncovering truths about objects and relations that would hold whether or not anyone ever constructed or even thought about them? Which picture actually does a better job explaining why geometric proofs feel so certain, and isnt there something a little self-undermining about Hobbes holding this view while spending decades defending a quadrature that other mathematicians kept proving wrong?
