A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about A. J. Ayer's 1988 near-death experience: the choking incident with the smoked salmon, the four minutes without a heartbeat, and the account he gave afterward of a red light "responsible for the government of the universe" and two ministers in charge of space who had, in his telling, failed to do their job properly. What we couldn't settle was how to read his own gloss on the episode. In the first essay he wrote about it, he said the experience had "slightly weakened" his conviction that his death would be the end of him, while leaving his atheism about God completely untouched; seven weeks later, in a follow-up piece, he walked even that back, insisting that what had actually loosened was not the belief itself but his "inflexible attitude" toward holding it. My friend thinks this is a philosopher whose whole career rested on calling talk of souls and an afterlife strictly meaningless, quietly hedging in a way his own principles shouldn't permit; I think the distinction he ends up drawing is more defensible than that, and that we were both missing what's actually interesting about the case. Write a short story — any setting or genre, a page or two is plenty — about a rigorously empiricist character who undergoes an anomalous experience along these lines, where the real tension driving the plot is between what the character felt and what their own epistemic commitments allow them to say about it afterward. I'd rather you found your own way into that problem than simply restaged Ayer's anecdote with the names changed.
