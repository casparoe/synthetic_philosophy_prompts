Ok this is going to sound like a weird rabbit hole but I've been stuck on it since my rationalist book club finished David Blight's big Douglass biography a few weeks back. Everyone sort of knows the broad shape of Douglass's abolitionism, but what's been bugging me is his total reversal on the Constitution specifically. Early Douglass is Garrison's guy through and through, and Garrison's line (which by all accounts he repeated constantly, and eventually burned a copy of the Constitution over) was that it was "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell" — a pro-slavery document cover to cover, so the only clean move for an abolitionist was to refuse to vote, refuse to hold office, and basically treat the union itself as illegitimate.

Then around 1850-51, after getting close to Gerrit Smith and reading Lysander Spooner (who I already knew a bit from the libertarian internet as the "No Treason" guy — had no idea he'd also basically supplied Douglass's constitutional theory), Douglass does a total about-face and starts arguing that if you read the literal text of the document, not the framers' private intentions and not how it was actually applied, its not pro-slavery at all. His point is that the words "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in the Constitution: the three-fifths clause says "other Persons," the fugitive slave clause says "Person held to Service or Labour," the slave-trade clause says "such Persons," and he thinks that evasiveness is itself legally load-bearing, not just cowardice dressed up in legalese. By the 1850s he's out there calling the Constitution, properly read, a "glorious liberty document," which is just a wild 180 from the guy who started out agreeing with Garrison.

What I can't settle for myself is whether Douglass's mature position is actually the better piece of constitutional reasoning, or whether Garrison was being more intellectually honest and Douglass mostly needed a founding document flexible enough to organize a political coalition around instead of standing permanently outside the system as a moral witness. It doesn't feel like a purely 19th-century question either — there's a live version of the same fight over whether the founders' silence on the word "slave" reflects real anti-slavery principle getting smuggled into the text, or just a bunch of men papering over a monstrous compromise with euphemisms. Could you write me an essay that actually adjudicates this instead of both-sidesing it: lay out the strongest version of the "pro-slavery compact" reading and the strongest version of the "anti-slavery text" reading, including the interpretive principle each side is implicitly leaning on (text vs. intent vs. surrounding context), and then land on an actual verdict. A qualified verdict is fine — something like "Douglass has the better legal reading but Garrison had the better read on what the document was actually built to do" would count — but pick something and defend it rather than leaving both readings standing at the end.
