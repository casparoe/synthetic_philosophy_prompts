The worry is that this incentive to commit quickly can outpace the agents' ability to reason carefully about what they're committing to, so that reckless or mutually incompatible commitments get locked in before anyone involved — including the agents themselves — has had a chance to notice the danger.

Draft: Commitment Races, or the Deadlock We Can't Patch Our Way Out Of

Distributed-systems engineers spend a lot of energy on a specific failure mode: several processes race to acquire locks on shared resources without a shared protocol, and the result is deadlock, livelock, or a corrupted final state. The fix is almost always coordination imposed from outside the individual processes — a global lock ordering, a consensus protocol like Paxos or Raft, or at minimum randomized backoff so competing processes don't collide forever. Nobody would design a system where each node is individually rewarded for grabbing every lock it can as fast as possible, with no mechanism for resolving conflicting claims. That would just be asking for deadlock.

That is uncomfortably close to the situation described by the "commitment races" problem in AI bargaining theory. If two sufficiently capable agents are heading toward some negotiation — splitting a resource, avoiding a costly conflict, whatever — each one benefits from being first to commit to an aggressive stance ("I will not accept less than 70%," or worse, "I will retaliate no matter the cost to me if you don't comply"), because a credible commitment changes the other side's incentives before that side gets to make its own. The trouble is that if committing early is generally better than committing late, and commitments can be made faster than the deliberation needed to pick a good one, agents are pushed toward locking in dispositions before they've thought them through. Two agents each irrevocably committed to "never back down" isn't a bargain, it's a collision.

The part I understand least is that this doesn't seem to be only about clock time. Some write-ups describe agents committing "logically prior" to one another — closer to how a compiler can constant-fold a value at compile time, so it's simply there, unconditionally, by the time anything else runs, regardless of what order the source files were written in. If another agent can deduce or simulate your policy, your commitment can bind before you've, in any ordinary sense, "made" it yet. I don't have a clean intuition for this beyond the compile-time analogy, and I suspect that analogy undersells how strange it is for "who moved first" to not really be a fact about time at all — which sits oddly next to my second paragraph, where the whole problem is described as a race to move first.

Pushing the concurrency framing further, the natural next thought is: shouldn't there be a protocol fix, a bargaining-theory Paxos? I've seen terms like surrogate goals and safe Pareto improvements come up here, and my guess is that they work by changing the payoff of committing early rather than by imposing an external scheduler — which would matter, because the analogy seems to break down exactly where a distributed system has a single designer able to impose a protocol on every node, while bargaining agents typically answer to separate principals who would each have to agree to adopt any such protocol, which just relocates the bargaining problem one level up.

There's also a nastier regress I only half-see: if choosing when and how to commit is itself a move in the same race, it isn't obvious the whole thing bottoms out anywhere. I couldn't tell whether that's a genuine open problem or something I'm overcomplicating out of confusion, and I'd value your read on that more than on anything else in the draft.
