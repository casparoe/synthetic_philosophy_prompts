I've spent the past couple of weeks falling down a rabbit hole that started with an EA Forum debate about how many chicken-years of suffering should offset a human-year in cause-prioritization spreadsheets, and ended with me reading Lionel Robbins's old broadsides against interpersonal utility comparisons and the responses to them. I think I understand the surface-level story: Robbins declared in the 1930s that claims about an extra pound of income mattering more to a poor person than to a rich one are not the kind of thing economics can verify, so cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility got quietly read out of the discipline for a generation. Where I get lost is in the later argument about whether Harsanyi's aggregation theorem actually undoes this. On one reading, Harsanyi shows that if both individual and social preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, plus some Pareto-type condition linking the two, you get a social welfare function that is a weighted sum of individual utilities -- which sounds like utilitarianism falling out of formal, ethically neutral axioms almost for free. But Sen and others apparently think this is too quick, either because the theorem doesn't secure the kind of comparability it needs, or because it only delivers an arbitrarily weighted sum rather than the equal-weights utilitarianism people wanted from it.

Could you walk me through this dialectic properly, rather than just gesturing at "there's a famous disagreement here"? In particular, I'd like to understand what work the expected-utility framework is actually doing in Harsanyi's proof that supposedly lets him sidestep Robbins's objection; where the equal-weighting assumption needed for genuine utilitarianism, as opposed to some arbitrary person-weighted sum, has to come from if the theorem itself doesn't supply it; and how Sen's distinctions between comparability of levels versus comparability of units, and full versus partial comparability, show that "can we compare utility across people" is really several different questions wearing one trenchcoat. If it's not too much of a detour, I'd also be curious whether any of this bears on the more familiar observation that Arrow's impossibility theorem assumes preferences are neither cardinal nor interpersonally comparable -- that is, whether Harsanyi- or Sen-style comparability genuinely is one of the standard ways of escaping Arrow's theorem, or whether that's more myth than reality.
