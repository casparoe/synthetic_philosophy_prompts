My grandfather died two weeks ago, at eighty-seven, and I have been asked to deliver his eulogy this weekend -- "because you're the one who's good with words," as my mother put it, which is a generous way of describing someone who has spent the last four years of a philosophy PhD immersed in debates about free will and moral responsibility. He arrived in this country at nineteen with almost nothing, built a small manufacturing business from the ground up, and told the same story about it at every family occasion for half a century: that he made himself into the man he became, entirely through his own choices and effort, and that anyone willing to work as hard as he did could do the same. This is exactly the story my family wants the eulogy to affirm, and two cousins who studied philosophy alongside me in college will be sitting in the second row listening for whether I actually say it.

The trouble is that I have spent a good part of my PhD working through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I no longer think "he made himself" can be strictly true of anyone, however admirable their life. The argument's regress -- you act as you do because of the way you are; to be truly responsible for the act you would have to be truly responsible for being that way; but being responsible for your own character would require having already shaped it for reasons that reflect some still-earlier character, and so on without end -- seems to apply to my grandfather exactly as much as to anyone else, and Strawson is explicit that swapping in indeterminism at some point in the chain does not rescue the picture, since a self partly shaped by chance is not thereby any more self-made. I do not want to stand up in front of forty grieving relatives and either deliver a seminar on the regress problem or say something about him that I now believe to be false simply because it is the comforting and expected thing to say at a funeral.

Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about six or seven minutes aloud, honor the pride he took in his own story on its own terms, and leave the room with the sense of him as an admirable, self-directed man that everyone came in expecting to hear about -- without asserting anything that a committed Strawsonian would have to retract by the time the reception started.
