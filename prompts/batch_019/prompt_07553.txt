I've been down a bit of a Plato rabbit hole lately (blame a LW-adjacent thread on costly signaling and voluntary handicaps that somehow got me thinking about historical versions of "testing character via induced weakness"). Everyone remembers Plato as basically anti-drunkeness -- Book IX of the Republic has Socrates say a drunk man already has "something of this tyrannical temper," and that a soul becomes tyrannical in the full sense once it's "even as the drunken, the erotic, the maniacal" (573b-c). Eros as tyrant, appetite slipping its leash, standard undergrad-seminar stuff.

Except then I actually read Books I and II of the Laws properly and it's a totally different vibe. The Athenian Stranger argues that the Spartans and Cretans only trained half of virtue: they scare young men to test and toughen courage, but never test the opposite failure mode, caving to pleasure, so their courage is incomplete. His fix is regulated drinking parties -- get a guy mildly disinhibited under a sober symposiarch (basically the designated non-drinker in charge) and you've got a cheap, safe way to find out whether his rational side can hold up under pleasure, same as a fear-drill finds out whether it can hold up under danger. Then Book II goes further and says men over 40 should be pushed toward actual drunkenness in the choruses of Dionysus, because wine works like a "medicine" against the hardness/shame old age gives your soul, loosening it back up enough to sing and dance for the kids' education without embarrassment. That's just not the guy from Book IX.

So what I want is an essay that actually settles this instead of shrugging "different dialogue, different context." Is the Laws' whole test-and-train-self-control-via-wine program a natural extension of Plato's psychology of the soul and his habituation theory of virtue (you learn sophrosyne under pleasure the same way you learn courage under pain), or does it quietly undercut the Republic's picture of appetite as something reason has to rule rather than bargain with? Feel free to lean on stuff like the symposiarch requirement, the age-graded rules in Book II, or the question of whether the wine "test" reveals character that's already there versus actually building it -- but don't just leave it at "both readings have something going for them." Pick a side and defend it (a conditional verdict is fine, e.g. "consistent for the over-40 Dionysian version but not the younger self-control-testing version," as long as you actually argue for it rather than hedge).
