Notes: the setup is two parties, a disagreement point d (what you get if talks collapse — I used d = (0,0)), a feasible set S of jointly reachable payoffs, and an "ideal point" I, the best each party could get out of S taken separately. Nash's 1950 solution picks whichever point on the boundary of S maximizes the product of the two parties' gains over d, and it's the unique rule satisfying Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, and Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA — deleting deals nobody picked shouldn't change the outcome). Kalai and Smorodinsky's 1975 complaint was that IIA lets something weird happen: you can expand S in a way that only ever helps party B (B's own max goes up, A's options are untouched) and Nash's rule can still respond by cutting B's payoff. So they swap IIA for a monotonicity axiom instead, and that plus the other three axioms pins down a totally different point: wherever the segment from d to I crosses the boundary of S. To check I actually understood this, I built a toy version of our GPU fight: say Infra's solo-max is worth 5 "units" and ML's is worth 4, but the tradeoff curve between them kinks because of contention overhead, so the Pareto frontier is the two segments from (0,4) to (2,3) and from (2,3) to (5,0), with a disagreement point of (0,0). Grinding through the optimization, Nash's rule lands exactly on (2.5, 2.5) — dead even, despite the 5-vs-4 asymmetry — while Kalai-Smorodinsky lands around (2.78, 2.22), the point where both sides get the same fraction of their own ceiling (5/9, about 55.6% each).
