I've spent the better part of a decade in a small reading group that grew out of the old LessWrong Sequences and never quite lost the habit of working through primary texts one at a time rather than settling for summaries — we've done Jaynes on probability, most of Parfit, a whole spring on Elinor Ostrom. Two of the group's longtime members, Dana and Priya, are now doing something with all that reading: this Saturday they're launching an actual, if very small, political party at a dinner in someone's backyard. They're calling it the Common Ballot Party, and its entire platform for now is a single plank — replace partisan primaries with a single nonpartisan primary and a ranked-choice general election, on the theory that most of what's broken in American politics is downstream of the two major parties' structural lock on who is even permitted to run. I've been asked to give the toast, largely because I have spent the last month being insufferable on the subject of Simone Weil.

The piece in question is her essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," written in London during the last months of her life, and it is not the mild good-government complaint I expected going in. Weil argues that a party is, by its nature, a machine for manufacturing collective passion and then using that passion as leverage against the private judgment of its own members, and that a party's growth, having no natural stopping point, ends up functioning as its real and only goal beneath whatever platform it officially runs on. The part of the argument that has actually stayed with me is that none of this turns on a party's ideology being bad: she holds that truth and justice are things only an individual mind can perceive, so any apparatus built to bring individual judgment into conformity with a collective position is opposed to truth and justice as such, whatever that position is and however thoughtful the people operating the apparatus are. Her conclusion is accordingly not "regulate parties better" but "get rid of them entirely," and nothing in her argument suggests that being small, single-issue, or founded by unusually reflective people earns a party an exemption — if anything, Dana and Priya building an organization whose entire purpose is to fight the two-party system's grip on politics looks like a fairly on-the-nose instance of what she is describing rather than a way out of it. It hit me the way "politics is the mind-killer" did the first time I read it.

Roughly half the people in that backyard on Saturday are reading-group friends who will know exactly what is going on the moment I start muttering about Weil, and would find it faintly dishonest if the toast simply pretended this tension away; the other half are Dana's actual campaign volunteers and her parents, who have never heard of Simone Weil and are there to feel good about a hopeful new project. Could you write me that toast — around five minutes, so somewhere in the neighborhood of 600 to 800 words? I would like it to take the Weil problem seriously enough that the philosophically inclined half doesn't feel condescended to, to find whatever honest response to that problem actually exists rather than a comforting non sequitur (and if you conclude there isn't a fully honest way out, say so gracefully rather than papering over it), and still land, for everyone standing in that yard, as a toast — something that leaves people glad to be there and glad to do the work, rather than feeling like they just sat through a seminar.
