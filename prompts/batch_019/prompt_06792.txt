I'm currently taking a seminar on philosophy of perception, and this week's unit covers cognitive penetrability. Looking over the reading, I've noticed that the standard examples marshaled in favor of penetration – the wine-expert case, the Bruner–Goodman coin-size studies, Delk and Fillenbaum's color-typicality effects – all have well-worn deflationary replies available to an encapsulation theorist like Pylyshyn or Fodor, who can redescribe them as products of attention, perceptual learning, or post-perceptual judgment rather than genuine architecture-level penetration of experience by belief. For a presentation I'm giving in section next week, I need a case that resists this move better than the textbook ones do. Construct an original example (not one drawn from the literature) of an apparent instance of cognitive penetration that satisfies conditions along the lines Macpherson proposes: a non-deviant causal link between a specific belief or desire and a change in experience, one not reducible to a shift of attention or to long-run perceptual learning. Then argue the case from both sides – how a proponent of penetration in Siegel's mold would use it, and the most persuasive way an encapsulation theorist could still explain it away – and say which reading you find more convincing.
