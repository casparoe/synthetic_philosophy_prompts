A few years ago I served on a jury in a civil case, and something about how we reached our verdict has bothered me ever since. We had to answer two separate factual questions before arriving at an overall finding, and I remember the uneasy realization that the majority position on each of those two questions, taken separately, did not actually match what the majority said when we were polled on the overall verdict directly. I didn't have any vocabulary for what was bothering me at the time. It was only later, poking around online out of curiosity, that I learned this has a name in the academic literature: the "discursive dilemma," closely related to something legal scholars had already noticed about how multi-member courts reach decisions.

I have no formal training in logic, philosophy, or social choice theory; I never studied any of this at university. But over the past year I've worked through a good part of the primary literature on my own time, together with a handful of people I met through an online forum for people interested in voting theory and collective decision-making. To keep ourselves honest, we've taken to setting each other short written questions and taking turns grading the answers, purely as a comprehension exercise. This week the grading duty has landed on me, and before I send my comments back to the person who wrote the answer, I would like a more rigorous second opinion, since I suspect my own self-taught grasp of the material might let something slide that a real specialist would catch.

Here is the question we set: "Using a concrete example of your own construction (for instance, a three-member committee or panel ruling on a conjunction of two independent premises), explain what is meant by the 'discursive dilemma' in the judgment-aggregation literature and how it relates to the older 'doctrinal paradox' from legal theory. Then explain how a premise-based procedure and a conclusion-based (issue-by-issue) procedure each respond to the dilemma, and what each approach has to give up in order to do so. Finally, briefly assess whether this result should be understood as essentially a new application of Arrow's Impossibility Theorem, or as posing a genuinely distinct problem."

And here is the answer that was submitted, which I now have to grade: "Consider a three-member appeals panel deciding whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. There are two relevant premises: P, whether a valid contract existed, and Q, whether its terms were breached; the panel finds liability just in case both P and Q are true. Suppose Judge 1 believes both P and Q, Judge 2 believes P but not Q, and Judge 3 believes Q but not P. Polled directly on the conclusion, only Judge 1 votes for liability, so a straight vote on the verdict returns 'not liable' two to one. But a majority vote taken on each premise separately gives P a majority (Judges 1 and 2) and Q a majority (Judges 1 and 3), so the panel's own majority-endorsed premises actually entail liability. This is the discursive dilemma: propositionwise majority voting on logically connected questions can hand down a verdict that contradicts the verdict implied by the panel's own majority-endorsed premises, even though every individual judge's opinions are perfectly consistent taken on their own. Legal scholars had already noticed this pattern in how multi-member courts reach decisions: Kornhauser and Sager called it the discursive dilemma in the 1980s, and List and Pettit later showed that the same structure -- which they termed the doctrinal paradox -- arises for any group whatsoever that has to reach joint judgments on logically interconnected propositions, not just courts.

Two natural procedures respond to the problem differently. A premise-based procedure has the panel vote only on P and Q and then reads the verdict off logically from whichever premises win a majority, which guarantees an outcome that is consistent with the panel's own stated premises, at the cost of never letting the panel vote on the actual question it is there to decide, namely the conclusion itself. A conclusion-based, or issue-by-issue, procedure instead puts every proposition to its own direct vote, including the conclusion, which respects every member's say on every question but can no longer guarantee that the resulting collective judgments will hang together logically, exactly as the example shows.

Since the underlying pattern here -- individually rational inputs generating an irrational or contested output under simple majority rule -- is exactly the pattern Arrow identified for preference aggregation, List and Pettit's result is best understood as a direct application of Arrow's Impossibility Theorem to judgments rather than preferences, with the same dictatorship conclusion emerging once the axioms are translated across from one setting to the other."
