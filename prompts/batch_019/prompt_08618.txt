I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about the history of educational philosophy, and I've gotten stuck on a puzzle in Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education. Locke insists that young children, not yet capable of reasoning, must be governed almost entirely through the deliberate cultivation of esteem and disgrace rather than through appeals to reason, rewards that flatter the senses, or the rod, and he grounds this method in his own account of how pleasure and pain become durably, sometimes irrationally, attached to particular objects and actions in early life. Yet his stated destination for this upbringing is an adult who acts from settled, internalized virtue and reason, not someone merely well-trained to track other people's opinions of them. Working within Locke's own psychological framework rather than importing later developmental theory, could you explain the mechanism by which a child's habituated sensitivity to esteem and shame is supposed to convert into autonomous rational virtue? I'm after the underlying gears of the argument rather than a summary of his parenting advice, and I'd welcome your own assessment of whether this is a genuine seam in Locke's account or one his associationist psychology actually has the resources to close.
