So I'm auditing a seminar on early modern political philosophy this term (not really my area, I'm normally 19th century, but the timing worked out), and we just spent two sessions on Spinoza's political writings, which I'd genuinely never read before. After the second class, two people in the group ended up arguing about it over coffee for like 40 minutes, and it's been stuck in my head since because I think they're both partly right, which is annoying. Quick background in case it helps: Spinoza's political theory starts from the claim that natural right just IS power — not "might makes right" as some cynical throwaway line, but as a strict definitional point, that a thing's right extends exactly as far as its power (its conatus, its striving to persist in being) extends, first in nature and then carried over once people form a state. From there he argues democracy is uniquely the "most natural" regime, and in the unfinished Political Treatise he goes further and actually calls it the most "absolute" form of dominion there is — his word — because unlike in a monarchy or aristocracy, nobody's power ever gets handed over to somebody else; the whole body of citizens just keeps deliberating together, permanently.

Here's the argument, more or less blow by blow. Marcus's opening claim: the whole picture is inconsistent. If right just tracks power, then a tyrant who's actually held onto power for two centuries has exactly as much Spinozist "right" to rule as any democratic assembly does, and Spinoza himself says subjects generally have to keep obeying a sovereign as long as it holds power and resisting is riskier than complying — so the special preference for democracy must be coming from somewhere outside the theory, and he's just dressing up an ordinary political preference in power-talk. Sofia's reply: he's missing what "power" means here — a regime that rules purely through fear generates exactly the kind of hidden, corrosive resentment that Spinoza thinks makes power unstable and short-lived, so a state that never seperates the multitude from its own power in the first place (i.e. democracy) simply is the fullest, most durable instance of what having power amounts to; it's not an outside value smuggled in, it's the strongest case of Spinoza's own criterion. Marcus came back with: that turns a claim that was supposed to follow from the very definition of right into an empirical bet that plenty of long-lived tyrannies falsify, so at most this shows democracy is often prudent, not that it's uniquely justified the way Spinoza claims. Then Marcus brought up something the professor had mentioned: the Political Treatise literally breaks off mid-argument in its last chapter, right where Spinoza is explaining why women should be excluded from citizenship on the grounds that they don't have power or capacity comparable to men's — his point being that if "equal right" among citizens just tracks who Spinoza judges to have comparable power, the equality was never about persons as such, it's hostage to his (obviously false) empirical opinions about who counts as powerful, so the theory doesn't actually ground anything we'd recognize as democratic equality. Sofia's last move was that this conflates the argument's structure with Spinoza's false premise about women — fix the premise and the very same reasoning mandates inclusion, which is a point in the theory's favor, not against it — and that Spinoza's own typology defines democracy specifically as rule by a council of the whole citizen body, so a powerful faction ruling in its own interest would just count as a form of aristocracy by his own lights, not democracy, which means Marcus's jump from "these particular exclusions were unjust" to "so democracy just reduces to rule by whoever's powerful" isn't quite fair either.
