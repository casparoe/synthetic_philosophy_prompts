I never went on to study philosophy formally - I left school at sixteen and have spent most of my working life in a trade - but for the past several months I have been reading my way through Pascal's Pensées in the evenings, largely because his name kept coming up alongside Augustine's in something I was watching, and I wanted to understand the connection for myself. Most of the fragments yield to patience once I slow down and read them two or three times, but there is one short passage, from the section dealing with justice, that has resisted me for weeks now.

In the translation I own, it reads: "Justice, force. — It is just that what is just should be followed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wicked men; force without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and force, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; force is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give force to justice, because force has contradicted justice, and has said that it is she herself that is just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

What I cannot settle for myself is the turn in that final sentence, from the admission that we cannot make what is just strong to the conclusion that we have therefore made what is strong just - it arrives so quickly that I cannot tell whether Pascal intends it as flat cynicism, in which "justice" is simply the name the powerful give to whatever they were already inclined to do, or as something closer to a lament for a genuine justice that force has merely displaced and that remains, in principle, available to be recognised, if only we could get free of our own institutions long enough to see it. A friend of mine, who knows his Augustine far better than I do, tells me this fragment is really just Pascal restating the old idea that a kingdom, once justice is taken out of it, is nothing more than robbery carried out on a grand scale, and that the whole passage is doing specifically Christian work about fallen human nature rather than standing on its own as political reasoning. I am not equipped to judge whether that is a fair reading or whether he is importing more than the text will bear. I would be grateful if you could take the passage apart sentence by sentence, tell me what each clause is contributing to the argument as a whole, and then help me see whether it is best read as apologetics in service of Pascal's larger religious project, as a standalone observation about law and power, or as something that has to be both at once.
