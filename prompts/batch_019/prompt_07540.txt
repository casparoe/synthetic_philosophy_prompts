I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and logic this term, and we just got through the Lucas-Penrose stuff. Most of my students arrive already having heard the pop version — "Gödel proved computers can't think" — and part of my job this week is talking them out of that and into what the argument actually does. Here's the bit I never explain well live: Lucas's original 1961 version basically has the mathematician just assert that she can see her own system F is consistent, which looks question-begging the second you push on it — that's more or less Benacerraf's "God, the Devil, and Gödel" complaint, how would she even know which F she is, let alone that its sound? Penrose is supposed to have fixed this in Shadows of the Mind with a fancier version built around an algorithm whose soundness the mathematician is committed to trusting, rather than one she just baldly asserts.

What I can't get across intuitively is whether that move actually escapes Benacerraf's objection or just relocates it one level up. Chalmers and Feferman both seem to think it's the latter — that "knowably sound" is doing an enormous amount of unearned work, and that a machine could be credited with the same kind of conditional self trust if we're generous enough to credit the mathematician with hers. Could you walk me through, at a gut level rather than a formal proof, exactly what's supposed to be new in Penrose's version relative to Lucas's original arguement, and then explain where the best version of the "you've just pushed the circularity up a level" objection actually bites? I'd like something my students could walk away from genuinely understanding, not just another rehearsal of what the incompleteness theorem itself says.
