So a few weeks back I fell down one of those late night Wikipedia holes and ended up reading about two logic puzzles that are apparently pretty well known in philosophy circles: the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Quick versions in case it helps: the lottery one says that for a fair lottery with, say, a thousand tickets, it's totally reasonable to believe of any single ticket that it won't win, but it's also obviously true that some ticket will win, so if you believe all thousand "it won't win" claims plus the "one of them wins" claim, you end up with a flat out contradiction even though every individual belief in the pile was perfectly reasonable on its own. The preface one is basically: an author believes every single sentence in their book is true (presumably thats why they wrote it that way), but they also stick a line in the preface admitting that, realistically, a book that long has probably got some mistake in it they never caught. Same basic deal, a bunch of individually reasonable beliefs that can't all be true together.

Every explanation of these two I've come across just lists them back to back like they're the same trick wearing two different costumes, and that's been bugging me alot. I tried walking my sister through both and she just went "ok so that's the same thing twice," which annoyed me because my gut says there's something actually different between a known, exact one-in-a-thousand chance and a vague "come on, some mistake must've snuck in there somewhere" feeling, but I can't pin down what that difference amounts to, or if it even matters.

Then walk me through why it lands on one side and not the other. And if it turns out you genuinely think there's no real difference, that any example of one is automatically an example of the other, don't force a distinction, just tell me that instead.
