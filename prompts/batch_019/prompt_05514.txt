I'm a final-year philosophy major, and this term's advanced seminar on metaphysics has spent several weeks on questions of identity and individuation, anchored by Max Black's 1952 dialogue in which he imagines a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, some distance apart, as a challenge to Leibniz's principle that no two distinct things can share every property. We were also assigned some of the later literature on "weak discernibility" — Saunders, Muller, and others — which tries to salvage some version of the principle in light of Black's case. After the seminar, two people from my discussion section, Devon and Priya, ended up arguing about the example over coffee for a good twenty minutes, and I have been turning their exchange over in my head ever since without managing to settle it myself.

Devon opened by insisting that Black's thought experiment simply refutes the identity of indiscernibles: the two spheres share literally every property there is to share, and yet there are two of them, so the principle is false, full stop. Priya replied that "share literally every property" overstates things, since each sphere stands in the relation "is a mile from a sphere just like it" to the other one but not to itself — an irreflexive relation each bears only to its counterpart — so the pair counts as weakly discernible, and on that basis the weakest, hardest-to-deny reading of the principle, that no two things can be utterly indistinguishable from one another in every respect, relations included, comes through untouched. Devon countered that this concedes too little: the relation Priya cites is exactly as true of each sphere as of the other, so it cannot tell you which sphere is which, and such a cheap move could rescue the principle against almost any proposed counterexample whatsoever, which shows that weak discernibility is empty as a reply to Black; what actually matters, he said, are the versions of the principle restricted to qualitative properties, monadic or relational, which is exactly what Black's scenario is built to refute, and there weak discernibility has nothing to offer. Priya answered that "empty" is much too strong, since this is exactly the move philosophers of physics make when they argue that two fermions in an antisymmetric joint state — which share every monadic property — still count as two individuals rather than one, by virtue of standing in an irreflexive relation such as having opposite spin to one another; if Devon accepts that argument for electrons, she said, he cannot turn around and dismiss the identical logic as a cheap trick when it shows up for spheres, so either weak discernibility marks a real, if modest, form of individuation in both cases, or it is question-begging in both, and he owes her a principled reason to treat the two cases differently.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly couldn't tell who was getting the better of it overall, let alone at each stage.
