(b) [5 points] State the LP truth tables for negation, conjunction, and the material conditional (defined as ¬A ∨ B), and identify which truth value(s) count as designated.
(c) [5 points] Using the semantics from (b), determine whether modus ponens (from A and A→B, infer B) is valid in LP, giving a valuation that settles the matter. Briefly explain why your answer matters for Priest's use of LP to handle the Liar sentence.

(a) Dialetheism and paraconsistency are, in effect, two sides of the same coin. A dialetheia is a sentence A such that both A and ¬A are true, and dialetheism is the thesis that at least one dialetheia exists (the Liar sentence being the paradigm case). A logic is paraconsistent when it does not validate ex contradictione quodlibet, i.e. when {A, ¬A} does not entail an arbitrary B. Since accepting a dialetheia without giving up explosion would commit us to trivialism, the absurd view that every sentence is true, dialetheism just is the demand that our background logic be paraconsistent: the two notions are really just two descriptions of a single position, one semantic and the other proof-theoretic.

(b) LP has three truth values, t, b, and f (true-only, both, and false-only), ordered f < b < t. Negation swaps t and f while fixing b: ¬t = f, ¬f = t, ¬b = b. Conjunction and disjunction are the min and max under this order: v(A∧B) = min(v(A), v(B)) and v(A∨B) = max(v(A), v(B)). The material conditional is defined as usual, A→B =df ¬A ∨ B. The designated values, i.e. the ones a valid argument must preserve, are t and b, since both count as "at least true."

(c) Suppose A and A→B are both designated; the question is whether B must then be designated too. Since A→B is ¬A∨B, its value is max(v(¬A), v(B)). Because A is designated, v(A) ∈ {t, b}, so v(¬A) ∈ {f, b}. Case 1: v(A) = t. Then v(¬A) = f, so for A→B to be designated we need max(f, v(B)) ∈ {t, b}, which forces v(B) ∈ {t, b}: B is designated. Case 2: v(A) = b. Then v(¬A) = b, which is already designated on its own, so max(b, v(B)) comes out designated no matter what v(B) is; since we are given that the premise A→B is designated, this case is automatically satisfied and does not add any further constraint. Putting the two cases together, whenever the premises are designated, B comes out designated as well, so modus ponens is valid in LP. This matters for the Liar sentence because it means Priest can reason forward through instances of the relevant conditionals without the system either exploding or losing modus ponens as a usable rule of inference.
