In rationalist and decision-theory circles, I. J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information is often invoked to show that a Bayesian agent's expected utility from observing evidence before acting can never be lower than her expected utility from deciding without it, since she always remains free to act just as she would have without the observation. This seems to settle, as a matter of decision theory rather than mere practical convenience, that a fully rational agent should always want more information before acting — which would make practices built around deliberate ignorance, such as sealed-bid auctions, blind peer review, and "right not to know" provisions in clinical genetics, second-best fixes for our biases rather than things a truly rational institution would have independent reason to keep. Given that, how should we think about phasing out such provisions as our elicitation and de-biasing methods keep improving?
