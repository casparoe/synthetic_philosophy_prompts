So this happened in my open-source project's github discussions, not something I was part of myself. Two contributors, Renee and Marcus, spent like 40 comments arguing about whether we should switch our yearly "pick the next 3 roadmap features" community vote from plain plurality (everyone votes for one option) to ranked-choice/instant-runoff, and it kinda derailed the whole thread. Neither of them works in this field but they both clearly know more about voting theory than I do (I just write backend code). Paraphrasing the important bits:

1. Renee: We should switch to ranked choice. Right now whenever two similar proposals are on the ballot they split the vote, and something nobody really wanted sneaks in with like 20% support. With RCV you rank your honest favorite first and it can never backfire on you the way it does under plurality.

2. Marcus: That's not the free lunch you think it is. Theres an actual theorem (Gibbard-Satterthwaite) that says any voting rule with 3+ possible outcomes that isn't literally a dictatorship can be gamed by somebody in some situation. RCV doesn't escape that, it just moves the gaming somewhere else.

3. Renee: Sure, but that theorem is about some worst-case profile existing somewhere, not about our actual votes. The vote-splitting problem isn't hypothetical though, it happens to us basically every year. Even if RCV is "gameable" in principle, it kills a failure mode we hit constantly in practice.

4. Marcus: It's not just some exotic worst case. RCV has a specific, well-documented failure called "center squeeze" -- a candidate that almost everyone would rank second, and who'd beat every other candidate head-to-head, can get eliminated in an early round for lacking first-place votes. And it's non-monotonic: there are real cases where ranking someone higher on your ballot is literally what causes them to lose. Plurality can't even do that.

5. Renee: Those cases always look hand-built to break the method. Real preferences aren't adversarially arranged like that, so I'd bet center-squeeze and monotonicity failures are way rarer in practice than the vote-splitting we already deal with every single year. "Harder to explain" isn't the same thing as "worse."

6. Marcus: "Rare" is doing a lot of work when neither of us has actually checked. And I'd rather have a system whose one flaw is boring and visible (plurality's spoiler effect, which we can literally point to) than one that can tell a voter their own ballot is why their favorite lost. If we're ditching plurality anyway, why not just go straight to a Condorcet method?

Can you referee this comment-by-comment, like a PR review -- say who's got the better of each specific point (feel free to use something like approve / comment / request-changes per point if that's easier than prose) and call out anything you think both of them are missing. I don't need a forced overall winner, I really just want to know where each of them is actually right vs overreaching.
