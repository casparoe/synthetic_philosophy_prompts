Okay so this whole thing has been stuck in my head all week and it started from the dumbest place - I was putting together my IB Theory of Knowledge presentation on Occam's razor (basically "prefer the simplest explanation that fits the evidence") and I fell down a Wikipedia rabbit hole that went Occam's razor -> Kolmogorov complexity -> this thing called Solomonoff induction -> AIXI (apparently some guy named Hutter's idea for a theoretically "perfect" AI). From what I can tell, Solomonoff induction is basically the actual rigorous math version of Occam's razor: instead of vaguely saying "pick the simpler theory," you imagine literally every computer program that could've outputted the data you've seen so far, weight the shorter ones as more probable, and bet on what comes next based on that. Two things about it are living in my head rent free: first, that the whole procedure is actually uncomputable (not just slow - literally impossible to ever run, even with infinite time), and second, that which programs even count as "short" depends on what base machine/programming language you pick as your reference point, and even though its apparently proven that this dependence washes out if you had infinite data, for any finite amount of data (i.e. all the data we will ever actually have, about anything) it can still bias your predictions in a real, unresolvable way. That second part kind of bugs me because it feels like it sneaks subjectivity back into the one theory that's suppose to be the objective gold standard for "rational" prediction.

I think this would honestly make a great premise for a short story, kind of the way Permutation City runs with an idea about consciousness, or Three Body Problem runs with first contact, except for this specific idea. Would you write one for me? I don't have a set plot, but it'd be cool if it involved two AIs (or two civilizations, or two scientists, whatever works better) that got "seeded" with different reference machines and end up making genuinely different, equally defensible predictions off the exact same finite pile of evidence, and then have to deal with the fallout of that disagreement. Set it wherever, a space station, a research lab, deep future, doesn't matter, I just don't want it to turn into a generic "what if we're all in a simulation" story that quietly drops the actual math/CS idea to get there.

Doesn't need to be long, like something I can read in a few minutes - I mainly want to send it to the two kids in my TOK class who made fun of me for going off about "uncomputable priors" at lunch.
