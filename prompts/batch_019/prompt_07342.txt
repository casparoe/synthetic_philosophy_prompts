Anthropic reasoning about the Carter–Leslie doomsday argument comes down to a choice between two rival principles for locating yourself within a population of observers, a choice that has been rehashed on LessWrong and in the academic literature for over a decade without ever quite being settled. The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) treats you as a random draw from the observers who actually exist in your reference class, and it vindicates the doomsday shift; the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) treats you as a random draw from all possible observers, and it exactly cancels that shift — at the cost of generating Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher and similar absurdities. Write a dialogue, set at a bar after a conference panel on existential risk, between an SSA-defender who accepts the doomsday argument's conclusion and an SIA-defender who rejects it on the strength of the Presumptuous Philosopher, each trying to talk the other out of their view. They should engage each other's actual commitments rather than simply restating their own: the SIA-defender should make the SSA-defender account for why the doomsday shift isn't just an artifact of the reference class problem — an arbitrarily drawn boundary around who counts as "an observer like you" — and the SSA-defender should make the SIA-defender explain why a philosopher's armchair reasoning should be allowed to outvote a physics experiment about the size of the universe. They can reach for concrete cases, like the Incubator or Adam and Eve, if that helps sharpen the disagreement. Neither should land a clean, unanswered knockout; end with the two of them isolating some observation or consideration that would actually move one of them off their position.
