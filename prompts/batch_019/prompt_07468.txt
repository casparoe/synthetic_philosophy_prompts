Been down a rabbit hole this week on money-pump arguments -- the thing where if your preferences over some set of options cycle (you prefer A to B, B to C, but then C to A), a trader can supposedly bleed you dry by offering a sequence of trades that each look like a strict improvement but leave you right back where you started, just poorer by a fee each lap. A friend who's more into decision theory than me mentioned "resolute choice" as McClennen's answer to this kind of problem, which sent me down a side-quest into how that's actually supposed to differ from "sophisticated choice" -- the more standard-sounding response people bring up for agents whose preferences might reverse or cycle across a sequence of choices.

Could you compare the two properly for me? I don't just want the dictionary definitions -- walk me through what each strategy actually has the agent do at each choice point in something like a three-option cyclical money-pump case (or the multi-round version where the trader keeps offering you something slightly better than what you currently hold for a small fee, and because the ranking cycles, eventually walks you right back to your starting point). I want to see the difference in behavior, not just in description. And then give me an actual verdict, not a "both have their merits" cop-out: which one is the more defensible reply to the money-pump argument? My worry with resolute choice is that it kind of just defines the problem away -- saying "the agent resolves to stick to the plan" feels like it smuggles in the conclusion (that they won't get pumped) rather than explaining why sticking with the plan is itself rational once you're actually at the later choice point and, taken on its own terms, would rather switch. But sophisticated choice has the opposite weird feature, where the agent ends up foreclosing on outcomes it would genuinely prefer, purely because it doesnt trust its own future self to cooperate. So which one actually wins that trade-off, and why?
