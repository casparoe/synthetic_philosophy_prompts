During my decades as a laboratory scientist, I sat on more grant-review panels and hiring committees than I can readily count, most of which could approve a proposal or candidate only if it met two or more independent criteria. Only in retirement, reading philosophy for the first time, did I learn that such conjunctive rules generate a genuine inconsistency once individual judgments are aggregated: the verdict reached by voting on each criterion separately and then applying the rule can differ from the verdict reached by asking each member directly for their overall recommendation. Lawyers know an early version of this as Kornhauser and Sager's "doctrinal paradox"; List and Pettit later generalized it as the "discursive dilemma." Write a dialogue between two veteran committee members: one defends the premise-based procedure, on the grounds that it keeps the panel's verdict logically tethered to reasons a majority actually accepted; the other defends the conclusion-based procedure, on the grounds that it never binds a member to a bottom line that does not follow from that member's own premises. Have them argue, via a concrete case of their own devising where the two procedures diverge, over which rule their committee should adopt going forward. Both should press hard on the other's central weakness rather than talk past it; the exchange need not end in agreement, but it should leave clear exactly what deeper disagreement about the point of a collective decision procedure is doing the work on each side.
