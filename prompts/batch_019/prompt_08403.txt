A few of us on my software engineering team run an informal lunchtime book club, and we've spent the last couple of months working through a popular book on the history of set theory and infinity. Last week's chapter covered Cantor's continuum hypothesis (CH) -- the claim that there is no set whose size sits strictly between that of the integers and that of the real numbers -- and the fact that Gödel first showed CH cannot be disproved from the standard axioms of set theory (ZFC), while Cohen later used a technique called forcing to show it cannot be proved from them either, jointly establishing that CH is independent of ZFC. None of us has any formal background beyond a single discrete-math course years ago, but that didn't stop two people in the group, Leah and Owen, from getting into a surprisingly sharp argument about what this independence result actually means -- one that spilled into our team Slack channel for the rest of the afternoon and was never really resolved.

Leah's opening position was that the independence result settles the matter: since ZFC proves neither CH nor its negation, there is simply no further fact about whether CH is true, and looking for "the" answer is a category error, on a par with asking whether Euclid's parallel postulate is really true independent of which geometry you've chosen to work in. Owen objected that working set theorists plainly don't treat it that way: they argue at length about which large cardinal axioms or forcing axioms are the right ones to add to ZFC, and some well-studied candidates, like the proper forcing axiom, actually settle CH by implying the continuum has size ℵ₂ rather than ℵ₁; he added that Gödel himself thought CH was probably false and expected new axioms to eventually decide it, which makes little sense if the question were as arbitrary as a choice of geometry. Leah replied that this just describes sociology, not truth: set theorists have obvious professional incentives to keep proposing "the" next axiom, but a community's enthusiasm for a research program doesn't make its favored axioms descriptions of a pre-existing mathematical reality rather than useful conventions, any more than a novelist's fondness for a plot twist makes it true. Owen countered that the analogy fails, because our acceptance of ZFC's own axioms was never empirically forced on us either -- we accepted them because they seemed intuitively correct and turned out to be fruitful -- so if that was enough to make ZFC's theorems objectively true rather than mere convention, the same kind of justification could in principle extend to further axioms that settle CH; he cited Gödel's own hope, and Hugh Woodin's later work on inner-model theory, as evidence that plenty of serious set theorists don't think of this as picking a convention at all. Leah's last word was that Owen was still begging the question, since plenty of equally serious set theorists disagree with Gödel's and Woodin's instincts here -- she brought up Joel David Hamkins's "set-theoretic multiverse" view, on which there are many equally legitimate universes of sets, some where CH holds and some where it fails, with no principled way to single one out as the "true" universe -- and concluded that her original "no fact of the matter" position is a live, technically serious view within set theory itself, not naive amateur formalism.
