I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of economics, and this term we have worked through the revealed-preference tradition: Samuelson's original behaviorist reconstruction of demand theory, the weak and strong axioms of revealed preference, Afriat's theorem on when a finite batch of choice data can be rationalized by some utility function, and Sen's well-known complaint that an agent who is nothing more than a maximizer of whatever preference ordering happens to rationalize their choices is a "rational fool," incapable of commitment or of principled action against their own apparent self-interest. My students have become quite fluent in the formal apparatus, but I don't think most of them have really sat with the deeper worry it is meant to motivate: that "preference" and "utility," reconstructed this way, may not name anything psychologically real about an agent at all, given that indefinitely many distinct utility functions - and, on sufficiently sparse data, indefinitely many distinct preference orderings - can rationalize the very same observed choices equally well.

To make this worry register as something more than a technical curiosity, I would like you to write a short story of roughly 1000 to 1500 words that dramatizes it concretely. Consider a setting along these lines, though you should feel free to adapt it: a research station has spent years recording every trade, choice, and refusal made by a newly encountered population - biological, artificial, or otherwise, as you see fit - with no access to anything beyond that choice record itself: no introspective reports, no shared physiology of affect or reward. The station's chief economist is now asked, for the purposes of a treaty, to certify what the population "actually wants" and how a proposed resource allocation would make it "better or worse off" - a certification the choice data cannot supply on its own without smuggling in assumptions the data cannot adjudicate between. I would like the story to turn on a specific decision or incident the economist must face, rather than having a character simply narrate the theory, but it should leave a careful reader with a genuine, non-technical feel for why the gap between what an agent's choices reveal and what, if anything, that agent truly prefers is a live philosophical problem and not merely a modeling nuisance.
