After a forty-year career as a field ecologist building optimality models of foraging and mating behavior, I only started reading philosophy of biology in earnest after retiring, and I have gotten stuck on the exact target of Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels critique. Popular summaries treat "spandrel" as shorthand for "evolutionary byproduct," but as I understand it, Gould's later taxonomy with Vrba is considerably more fine-grained, distinguishing spandrels (or non-aptations) from exaptations depending on whether a byproduct has since been co-opted for a new use, and Peter Godfrey-Smith further split "adaptationism" itself into empirical, explanatory, and methodological variants that need not stand or fall together. Using these distinctions, could you help me adjudicate a charge that has bothered me since I started reading the secondary literature — that the "Panglossian paradigm" Gould and Lewontin attacked was less a position any working adaptationist actually held than a rhetorical straw man, as Dennett and others later argued? I am less interested in a full survey of the controversy than in whether, once the terminology is sharpened this way, any substantive disagreement remains, or whether the two sides were mostly talking past each other.
