Two people I collaborate with on a small side-project trading bot have been locked in a running argument about bet sizing that has spiraled well past the original question. I'd like a neutral read on it, since I can't tell who's actually right.

(1) Miguel wants every trade sized using the Kelly criterion, and he doesn't treat this as just a convenient heuristic. He says it's one instance of a broader pattern he calls "geometric rationality": whenever outcomes compound multiplicatively instead of adding up, the mathematically correct way to handle uncertainty is to work in log-space and average geometrically rather than arithmetically, and Kelly betting, the geometric-mean rule some forecasters use to pool odds, and even certain bargaining solutions are all downstream of that one fact.

(2) Dana thinks this is a relabeling, not a discovery. Maximizing expected log wealth is maximizing ordinary expected value with a logarithmic utility function plugged in, she says, so nothing has left arithmetic, expectation-based reasoning at all. Call log utility "geometric" if you like, but then "geometric rationality" is just a name for one point on the ordinary risk-aversion spectrum, not a rival to expected-value reasoning.

(3) Miguel's comeback is that you can derive the Kelly fraction purely from asking which constant betting fraction maximizes long-run growth under repeated compounding, without invoking a utility function at all -- so it isn't secretly presupposing log utility, it reaches the same formula for an independent reason. And the fact that a structurally similar geometric-mean rule shows up in forecast pooling, where nobody is compounding wealth over time, is evidence the pattern is doing real work rather than being smuggled in from one place.

(4) Dana's response is that converging on the same formula from different starting points doesn't make those starting points instances of one theory. Expected value itself can be derived from several unrelated axiom sets across different domains, and nobody concludes from that convergence that "arithmetic rationality" is incoherent or in need of unification. Until "geometric rationality" comes with its own axioms specifying in advance when you should switch from sums to products, rather than a running list of places a geometric mean happened to be convenient, it's an evocative analogy riding on top of ordinary expected-value theory, not a competing foundation for it.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just picking an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether you think their disagreement is ultimately about the mathematics of compounding, or about how liberally the word "rationality" should be stretched to cover a formula that keeps recurring across unrelated problems.
