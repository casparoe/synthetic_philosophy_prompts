I am about two-thirds through a novel set in the Dutch Republic in the late 1640s. One chapter hinges on a dinner-table argument in which a character closely modeled on Descartes defends his views on animal minds against a skeptical guest, and before revising it, I asked two friends who fact-check the philosophy in this book to look it over. They ended up disagreeing about what Descartes actually thought, not just how to dramatize it, and I would like your help adjudicating the exchange, since I cannot, without the scene leaning on either a straw man or an overly generous rehabilitation.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Nora said the scene is fine as drafted: Descartes held that animals are unfeeling automata, and that the "two very certain means" of telling humans from beasts in the Discourse -- language and general adaptability -- were meant to settle the matter for good.

(2) Julian objected that this is the popular caricature, not Descartes' considered view: in his 1649 letter to Henry More, Descartes denies thought to animals but insists he denies sensation "to no animal, in so far as it depends on a bodily organ" -- an odd thing to write if he thought animals felt nothing whatsoever.

(3) Nora replied that this concedes less than it looks: the "sensation" on offer is one Descartes has already defined as purely mechanical registration-and-response, with no experience attached, so relabeling automatism as "sensation" changes the vocabulary, not the substance.

(4) Julian answered that the same letter goes further, adding that animals' lacking thought cannot be proven either, "since the human mind does not reach into their hearts" -- a genuine hedge, not the confidence of someone sure his dinner guests' pets are clockwork.

(5) Nora's last word was that Julian is misapplying that hedge: it concerns thought, where Descartes allows real uncertainty, not the automatism-of-the-body verdict he treats as settled throughout -- so her character can stay serene about animal bodies while sounding genuinely unsure, if pressed, about anything deeper.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each step, and flag anything about Descartes' actual view both of them are missing? I want to get the character's convictions right, including exactly where they are allowed to waver.
