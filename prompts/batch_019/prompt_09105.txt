My grandma passed away back in March and left her lake cabin jointly to my mom and my uncle Rick, 50/50, with zero other instructions about what happens to it. Neither of them wants to sell to a stranger, so they've been trying to work out a buyout between themselves, and because things got tense they brought in a mediator who normally handles corporate breakups and, for whatever reason, likes to bring actual bargaining theory into family disputes. She introduced two specific concepts, the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, as candidate ways to split the cabin's value "fairly," and now my mom and Rick are having a whole side argument about game theory on top of the actual argument about the cabin. I'm a high school biology teacher, not a game theory person at all, but I get updates on both fronts every Sunday dinner and have started taking notes because I honestly don't know whos winning anymore.

Here's roughly how it's gone. (1) Rick wants to use the Nash solution, his reasoning being that it's "the" standard result here and that it lets his actual offer from a developer, his walk-away option if no deal happens, count as leverage in the split. (2) My mom said that's exactly the problem: because the Nash solution is sensitive to whatever outside options happen to be sitting on the table, Rick can just inflate that developer number and shift the split his way, so she wants to switch to Kalai-Smorodinsky instead, since it's anchored to the best-case ceiling each of them could get rather than to a contested walk-away number. (3) Rick came back that this doesn't fix anything, since the KS "ceiling" is just as made up as his developer offer, maybe more so, given it's literally each person's own best case rather than tied to a real outside number, so switching solutions just adds a second contestable figure instead of removing the first one. (4) My mom answered that even so, KS has a real advantage Nash doesn't: she'd read that because of the same "irrelevant alternatives" property Rick likes, Nash's solution can sometimes make someone worse off even when their own options only improve, which she says is definately backwards, whereas Kalai-Smorodinsky guarantees your share never drops when your own opportunities get better. (5) Rick's response was that Nash's solution isn't just some arbitrary axioms picked to sound nice, that it's supposedly the actual outcome two patient, rational people would converge to through a real back-and-forth offer negotiation, whereas Kalai-Smorodinsky is just a point picked off a graph with no negotiating procedure behind it, so Nash has the deeper justification. (6) My mom's closing point was that none of this obviously even applies here, since a lot of what she cares about isn't reducible to a dollar figure to begin with, she's the one who drove up most weekends for a decade to fix the dock and look after grandma near the end, and no set of bargaining axioms tells you how to fold that into a number in the first place. Rick's answer was that a buyout price does exactly that once you ask what she'd actually be willing to pay rather than accept, so the framework's fine, she's just unhappy with her own number under it.
