I'm working on a piece about how much scientific realism can survive radical theory change, and I keep circling back to structural realism: the view, traceable to Poincaré and developed by Russell, that successive theories preserve the abstract mathematical structure of the relations among unobservable entities even as they overturn everything we thought we knew about those entities' intrinsic nature (the textbook case being how Fresnel's equations for light survived, reinterpreted, the shift from an elastic-solid ether to Maxwell's electromagnetic field). What I can't get past is an objection the mathematician M. H. A. Newman raised against Russell's version of the view in 1928: if a theory tells us only that the world's entities stand in "some relations or other" isomorphic to its mathematical structure, then, absent further constraints on which relations are allowed to count, that claim turns out to be satisfiable by almost any sufficiently large domain of objects — which threatens to make "we only know the structure" an empty boast rather than a substantive realism. Write an essay assessing whether this "Newman problem" is fatal to epistemic structural realism, or whether some strategy — restricting the admissible relations, as some heirs of Worrall's view try to do, or retreating to an ontic rather than epistemic structuralism, as James Ladyman has urged — genuinely saves it. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but don't hedge: end on a definite verdict.
