Last night, a friend of mine who is finishing a doctorate in ancient philosophy and I got into a long argument that somehow wound its way from Newcomb's problem to Stoic determinism. I had brought up the standard one-box/two-box dilemma: a reliable predictor has already filled an opaque box with either $1,000,000 or nothing, depending on whether it foresaw you taking just that box or taking it together with a second, transparent box containing $1,000. My friend pointed out that the Stoics had confronted something structurally similar over two thousand years earlier, in what Cicero records in De Fato as the "Lazy Argument" against fatalism. As she reconstructed it, critics of the Stoa argued that if everything is fated, then deliberate effort is pointless — their stock example was that if you are fated to recover from an illness, you will recover whether or not you call a doctor, and if you are not fated to recover, calling a doctor will not help, so either way you need not bother. Chrysippus's famous rebuttal was to deny that recovery is simply fated at all; rather, recovery and calling the doctor are co-fated together, so that your action is itself woven into the very causal chain fate has fixed, and is therefore not idle.

We then spent a good hour disagreeing about what Chrysippus would say if someone explained Newcomb's problem to him. My friend is adamant that he would be a two-boxer: the Stoics were austere causal determinists who flatly denied backward causation, so once the predictor has already filled the boxes, no amount of correlational reasoning should move a good Stoic logician to leave $1,000 on the table for nothing. I argued the opposite — that Chrysippus's entire point against the Lazy Argument was to reject exactly the move the two-boxer makes. "My choice now cannot change what is already fixed, so acting on the correlation is pointless" sounds uncomfortably close to the fatalist's "my calling the doctor cannot change what is already fated, so acting on the correlation is pointless," which is precisely the inference he spent his career refuting.

So which of us has the better reading? I would like you to work through, as carefully as you can from what we actually know of Chrysippus's views on fate, causation, and assent, whether he would end up a one-boxer or a two-boxer, and, more importantly, to identify the precise point at which the analogy between "co-fated" events and Newcomb's predictor holds up and where it instead breaks down — since I suspect our whole disagreement ultimately turns on that single point rather than on any general fact about Stoicism.
