In my seminar on conditionals, we recently worked through David Lewis's 1976 triviality result, which shows that the "Equation" — the claim that P(A > C) = P(C | A), where "A > C" is the indicative conditional "if A, then C" — cannot hold in full generality without collapsing one's credence function into something trivial. I can follow the algebraic steps of the proof, but I'm still missing the underlying mechanism at an intuitive level: why is it specifically the requirement that the Equation keep holding after conditionalizing on new evidence, rather than merely holding for one's actual, current credence function, that forces this collapse? Relatedly, why do philosophers generally regard the "obvious" fix — restricting the Equation to a single, fixed probability function — as an unsatisfying dodge rather than a genuine solution to the problem the result raises?
