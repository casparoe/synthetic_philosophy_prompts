ok this is a bit of a rabbit hole but here goes. years back I read through Yudkowsky's quantum physics sequence and came away with the classic LW-brain take: many-worlds has no collapse, so there's no "spooky action at a distance," so Bell's theorem is basically defused -- the nonlocality only ever looked scary because people were assuming a collapse postulate. lately I've been reading actual philosophy-of-physics stuff (Maudlin's writing on this mainly), and it turns out the people who take Bell's theorem most seriously don't think Everettians get off that easy. their move, as I understand it, is roughly: fine, the universal wavefunction evolves locally, but you still owe an explanation for why the branch where Alice measures "up" reliably pairs off with the branch where Bob measures "down" (for entangled pairs where QM predicts perfect anti-correlation) instead of pairing off randomly -- and that pairing-up looks like its smuggling back in exactly the kind of nonseparability Bell's theorem was supposed to force on everyone, collapse or no collapse.

could you write me a dialogue between an Everettian physicist defending the claim that many-worlds is genuinely a local theory in the sense Bell's theorem cares about, and a philosopher of physics pushing the objection above? i dont want a loop of "no collapse = no spookiness" vs "but the correlations!" on repeat -- i want them actually digging into where the locality is supposed to live (parameter independence vs outcome independence, or however its best carved up) and fighting over whether "no superluminal signalling" is doing more work in the Everettian's case than it can really bear. both sides should be equally sharp -- no strawmanning either one.
