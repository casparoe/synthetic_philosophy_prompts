I'm at work on a novel about two philosophers, Elena and Marcus, who were partners for the better part of a decade before their careers—and their tempers—pulled them apart, and who now find themselves seated across from each other on a delayed overnight train to a conference neither much wants to attend. I want the chapter to turn on a long, prickly conversation that revives their oldest professional quarrel: what an emotion actually is. Elena has always held a broadly cognitivist line, in the spirit of Robert Solomon or the neo-Stoicism of Nussbaum's Upheavals of Thought, on which an emotion just is, or essentially involves, an evaluative judgment. Marcus has drifted toward something closer to Jesse Prinz's or Ronald de Sousa's perceptual account, on which emotions are perception-like states that track value somewhat independently of what one believes. Both of them dismissed a bare feeling theory in the James-Lange mold years ago as too thin to do the explanatory work, so that is not where the real disagreement lies.

What I would like from you is the dialogue itself rather than a summary of the two positions. Have Marcus lean hard on the problem of recalcitrant emotion—phobias that persist once the feared object is known to be harmless, or grief that outlasts one's own judgment that grieving no longer serves any purpose—and on the analogy with perceptual illusions such as the Müller-Lyer figure, where the lines go on looking unequal even after one has measured them and come to believe otherwise. Elena should not simply concede. I would like her to contest this in earnest, whether by distinguishing kinds of recalcitrance, by allowing for judgments that are dispositional or only partly endorsed rather than fully occurrent, or by some other route a capable cognitivist might take, and then to turn the tables by pressing Marcus on what a perceptual model can say about emotions like indignation or pride, which seem to involve propositional content—that someone wronged you, that you achieved something—in a way that sits awkwardly with a model built on the analogy with seeing colors or shapes. I would like something on the order of fifteen to twenty exchanges, with the train kept firmly in the background, two equally capable people who have plainly had this argument before, and no obligation to resolve things tidily by the end; a genuine stalemate would suit the chapter at least as well as a decisive win for either side.
