So a few weeks back I got into it with my brother-in-law about whether people who've lived in a country for years, pay taxes, have kids in the local school, but aren't citizens, should get to vote there. He said obviously not, it's not "their" country. I said well why not, they're affected by literally every law that gets passed just as much as anybody else. Anyway that sent me down a rabbit hole and it turns out theres an actual name for this problem in political philosophy - "the boundary problem" - basically, how do you decide who counts as part of "the people" a democracy is supposed to represent, without already sneaking in an answer to that question before you even start. Some guy named Whelan apparently pointed out back in the 80s that this is circular no matter how you slice it, which tracks with why every proposed answer I've since read seems to have a hole in it somewhere.

I don't have any background in this, just a lot of free time and library-website access, but here's what I've pieced together as the main "solutions" people have floated: the all-affected interests principle (you get a say if your interests are impacted, and apparently Goodin ran this to its logical extreme where basically the whole planet should get a say in every decision anywhere), the all-subjected/coercion principle (you get a say if you're subject to the state's coercive power - this is basically Abizadeh's angle when he argues that border enforcement itself coerces outsiders, so they should get a say too), the stakeholder principle (roughly, people with a real long-term stake in the community's future, I believe this ones from Bauböck), David Miller's shared-nationality answer, plain old territorial residence, and then López-Guerra's stranger idea of an "enfranchisement lottery" for people who are affected but aren't full members.
