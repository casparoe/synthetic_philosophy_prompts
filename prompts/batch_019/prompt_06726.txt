I'm a novelist working on a multigenerational family saga built around a mid-list author who has one runaway bestseller late in her career, and whose grandchildren spend much of the book's final act quietly living off the royalties and adaptation fees that single book keeps throwing off decades after her death. Because I want the political philosophy underneath that subplot to be more than set dressing, I've gone back to Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, and specifically to the Wilt Chamberlain passage: the argument that if you start from any distribution of holdings you're willing to call just, and people then freely hand over small sums to watch a gifted performer do the one thing only he can do, the resulting inequality is just as well, since every step from the first distribution to the second was a voluntary transfer nobody has standing to complain about.

The longer I sit with it, the more it looks like a description of my own trade – readers each parting with the price of a hardback because they want the one thing only a given author can supply – so I'd like to work out what the entitlement theory Nozick builds around that argument actually commits someone in my position to. Could you describe the most important implications of the entitlement theory and the Chamberlain argument for two questions I can't settle on my own: (a) whether a windfall like my character's bestseller can be called "deserved" in any sense that should make her comfortable with it, given how much of the outcome rides on luck, timing, and a marketing and distribution apparatus she had no hand in building, and given that entitlement and desert are not obviously the same notion even inside Nozick's own framework; and (b) whether her grandchildren have any claim, on entitlement grounds, to go on collecting royalties on a book none of them wrote, since the principle of justice in transfer is built around cases exactly like Chamberlain's and says remarkably little about inheritance or about property as thin and non-rival as copyright.

If you're able, please also weigh in on a rebuttal a philosopher friend once mentioned to me in passing – something to the effect that the whole argument quietly leans on a strong notion of self-ownership that an egalitarian was never obliged to grant in the first place – since I'd rather understand whether that objection actually sinks the Chamberlain case than have it left as a vague gesture. I'm less interested in a comprehensive survey of the literature than in the two or three implications that would most change how I, or the characters I've built around this premise, ought to regard money made this way.
