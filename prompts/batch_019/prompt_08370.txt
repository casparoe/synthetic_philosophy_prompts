A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing over a passage in Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education, and since neither of us managed to convince the other, I'd welcome a third perspective. Here is the passage, in Locke's own spelling (it runs across sections 40, 41, and 44 of the 1693 text):

"Would you have your Son obedient to you when past a Child; be sure then to establish the Authority of a Father as soon as he is capable of Submission, and can understand in whose Power he is. If you would have him stand in awe of you, imprint it in his Infancy; and as he approaches more to a Man, admit him nearer to your Familiarity; so shall you have him your obedient Subject (as is fit) whilst he is a Child, and your affectionate Friend when he is a Man.

I imagine every one will judge it reasonable, that their Children, when little, should look upon their Parents as their Lords, their absolute Governors, and as such stand in awe of them; and that when they come to riper Years, they should look on them as their best, as their only sure Friends, and as such love and reverence them. The Way I have mention'd, if I mistake not, is the only one to obtain this. We must look upon our Children, when grown up, to be like our selves, with the same Passions, the same Desires. We would be thought rational Creatures, and have our Freedom; we love not to be uneasy under constant Rebukes and Brow-beatings... If therefore a strict Hand be kept over Children from the Beginning, they will in that Age be tractable, and quietly submit to it, as never having known any other...

A Compliance and Suppleness of their Wills, being by a steady Hand introduc'd by Parents, before Children have Memories to retain the Beginnings of it, will seem natural to them, and work afterwards in them as if it were so, preventing all Occasions of struggling or repining."

My friend's reading was that there is no real tension here: infants cannot yet be governed by reason, so treating them for a while as subjects of an "absolute" governor is just honest developmental realism, and the whole point of getting their compliance settled before they have memories to date it from is precisely what makes the arrangement kind rather than coercive — the child is never even given a standpoint from which to feel the restraint as a restraint. I argued that this is exactly the trouble: if the "Friendship" of later years rests on habits installed before the child could remember being without them, then Locke isn't describing reason arriving late so much as foreclosing, in advance, any position from which the child could ever experience his rule as anything other than simply how things are — which sits oddly next to his own insistence elsewhere in the same book that children "love to be treated as rational Creatures, sooner than is imagin'd."

We went around on this for a long time without getting anywhere. Could you help me see more clearly what Locke is actually committing himself to here — particularly what work the "before Children have Memories" move is doing in his larger argument about awe, love, and authority — and whether one of us has the better reading, or whether the passage genuinely supports both?
