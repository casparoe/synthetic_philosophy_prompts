This term I'm taking a seminar on Warring States philosophy, and the last two meetings have been devoted to the School of Names (名家). We started with Hui Shi's paradoxes — the claim that one can set out for the state of Yue today and arrive yesterday was an instant class favorite — and this week moved on to Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue" (白馬論). I've read the primary text a couple of times now along with some of the secondary literature on the syllabus, but before Thursday's discussion section I want to actually work through the central argument rather than just be able to state its conclusion. Here is the stretch that keeps tripping me up (my own working translation of the exchange):

Objector: "Is it acceptable to say that a white horse is not a horse?"
Gongsun Long: "It is acceptable."
Objector: "How so?"
Gongsun Long: "'Horse' is that by which one names a shape; 'white' is that by which one names a color. What names a color is not what names a shape. Hence I say: a white horse is not a horse."
Objector: "If there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. And if one cannot say there is no horse, is this not just to say there is a horse? For there to be a white horse just is for there to be a horse — so how does whitening it make it not a horse?"
Gongsun Long: "If one seeks a horse, a yellow or a black horse will each satisfy the request. If one seeks a white horse, a yellow or a black horse will not satisfy it. Suppose a white horse really were the same as a horse — then what is sought in the two cases would be one and the same thing. But if what is sought is one and the same, then 'white' would be no different from 'horse.' And if what is sought is no different, why is it that a yellow or a black horse is sometimes an acceptable answer and sometimes not? Acceptable and unacceptable plainly stand opposed to one another. So the yellow horse and the black horse are each a single thing, and yet each can answer to 'there is a horse' while neither can answer to 'there is a white horse.' Hence that a white horse is not a horse is established."

What I can't pin down is what "is not" (非, fei) is doing across these lines, because the force of the whole argument seems to depend on sliding between two things it could mean. Read weakly, all Gongsun Long is entitled to conclude is that "white horse" and "horse" are not identical as terms — true, but uninteresting, since no one thought "white horse" was simply a synonym for "horse." Read strongly, the conclusion is that a white horse is not a kind of horse, i.e., that having a white horse does not count as having a horse — which looks false on its face, yet the "seeking a horse" reasoning in the middle of the passage seems specifically built to earn that stronger, more surprising reading rather than the trivial one. I've looked at Chad Hansen's treatment of Chinese general terms as closer to mass nouns than count nouns, which recasts the puzzle in mereological, part-whole terms, and at more class-logic-flavored readings, of the kind associated with A. C. Graham, that instead turn on distinguishing an "is" of identity from an "is" of class-membership — and the two approaches don't seem to locate the sleight of hand in the same place, which has left me less sure, not more, what the "official" reconstruction of this argument is even supposed to look like.

Could you help me actually work through the passage, rather than just paraphrasing its conclusion? Specifically, I would like you to: (1) lay out the "seeking a horse" argument as an explicit, numbered sequence of premises and a conclusion, flagging exactly which sense of "is not" each premise needs in order to be even remotely plausible; (2) tell me where, if anywhere, the argument equivocates between those senses, and whether that makes Gongsun Long guilty of the mere wordplay that Xunzi and other contemporaries accused the School of Names of, or whether a genuine semantic point about general versus compound terms survives the equivocation charge; and (3) say something about how this local puzzle bears on the School of Names' larger preoccupation with the relation between "names" (名) and "actualities" (實) — is the white-horse argument best treated as a freestanding logical stunt, or as a move within that larger project? I would rather walk into section with a working grasp of the mechanics than with a summary I could have lifted from an encyclopedia entry.
