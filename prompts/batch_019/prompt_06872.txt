Ok so here's a rabbit hole I've been down all week. I'm reporting a longer piece on AI safety and kept running into this "embedded agency" framework that MIRI put out a few years back (I think the main write-up was by Scott Garrabrant and Abram Demski). The basic pitch, as far as I understand it, is that classical decision theory imagines an agent sitting outside its environment like a little homunculus computing the optimal move from a god's eye view, and real agents can't actually do that because they're embedded in the world their trying to model — among other things, they can never contain a fully accurate model of themselves, since the model would have to model itself modeling itself, forever.

One of the papers leaned really hard on Löb's theorem to argue that an embedded agent can't fully "trust" its own future reasoning, or a successor agent's reasoning, without risking contradiction, which is apparently a cousin of the Gödel self-reference stuff. I mentioned this to a friend doing a philosophy PhD and she agreed it sounded like a real limit but couldn't tell me what it actually rules out in practice.

So here's my actual question: if there's a genuine mathematical result showing embedded agents can't have a complete, contradiction-free model of themselves, doesn't that basically prove a fully self-aware, recursively self-improving AI (the "foom" scenario people like Yudkowsky worry about) is a mathematical impossibility rather than just a hard engineering problem? And if Gödel and Löb already closed that door in principle, why do alignment researchers keep publishing on how to make self-improvement "safe" instead of just retiring the whole scenario?
