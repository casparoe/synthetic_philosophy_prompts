I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist working on tests of Bell's inequalities, so a great deal of my working life was devoted to thinking about when a correlation between two separated events calls for a genuinely novel explanation, and when it is instead fully accounted for by an ordinary common cause -- one that, once you condition on it, screens the two events off from each other so that no residual correlation remains unexplained. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in earnest, mostly on my own, and I recently ran across Douglas Hofstadter's notion of "superrationality" in a collection of his old Scientific American columns: the claim that two agents facing a symmetric, one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, each knowing that the other reasons exactly as she does, ought to cooperate, because whatever line of reasoning delivers one agent's decision must deliver the identical decision in the other, leaving mutual cooperation and mutual defection as the only self-consistent outcomes, and cooperation as the one that is better for both.

What troubles me is that this argument seems to treat one's own choice as evidence about one's twin's choice in exactly the way that the logic of screening off -- what Reichenbach called the principle of the common cause -- says one should not be able to, once every fact correlating the two of you has already been conditioned upon. By the time either twin actually deliberates, both already know they are superrational, both know the other is too, and both have been handed an identical payoff matrix and an identical stock of background knowledge; that shared situation is, structurally, the common cause of whatever the two of them go on to do, much as a shared preparation procedure is the common cause of the correlated outcomes of two entangled particles. On any local hidden-variable story, conditioning on that common cause in full is supposed to leave no residual correlation still needing explanation -- which is exactly the assumption Bell's theorem shows quantum mechanics cannot satisfy. Evidential decision theorists met a version of the same difficulty in their medical Newcomb cases, and their standard reply, the tickle defense, is that a fully rational deliberator already feels the pull of her antecedent disposition before she acts, so that conditioning on that introspected disposition screens off whatever further evidential weight her eventual choice might otherwise have carried. A superrational agent looks to me to be in exactly that position with respect to her twin, which would make cooperating "because it is good news about what she will do" a piece of decision-theoretic sleight of hand rather than a genuine argument.

I would like to be shown where this reasoning goes wrong, if indeed it does. Please argue for the position that the screening-off objection just described fails against superrationality even though the structurally similar objection succeeds against evidential decision theory in medical Newcomb cases -- that is, make the case for why the correlation between two superrational twins cannot be dissolved by conditioning on their shared circumstances in the way that both the entangled particles' correlation, on any local hidden-variable account, and the smoker's cancer risk, via the tickle defense, can be. I would ask that the argument be pitched at a fellow scientist rather than a professional philosopher, so please gloss any unavoidable technical vocabulary as you introduce it.
