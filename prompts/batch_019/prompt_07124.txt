I'm a backend dev, no philosophy background beyond one gen-ed class in college, but I've been going down a rabbit hole on AI alignment stuff lately just for fun, and I keep coming back to this "scalable oversight" idea, specifically the "AI safety via debate" proposal — you get two models to argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge who isn't smart or informed enough to evaluate the underlying claim directly, and the hope is that the truthful side has a structural advantage because a lie is supposedly harder to defend once the other debater starts cross-examining it. Apparently people later found a hole in this called the obfuscated arguments problem (if I'm remembering the name right) — basically a dishonest debater can smear a flaw thin across a huge pile of sub-claims so that no single piece is wrong enough for the judge to pin down, even with an honest opponent screaming that somethings off.

This weirdly reminded me of the one philosophy class I took, where we read Plato's Gorgias. Socrates spends a big chunk of it arguing rhetoric isn't a real art like medicine, just a knack for producing pleasure and persuasion in an audience that has no way to judge the real thing, kind of like cookery dressed up as nutrition. And later on there's this bit — a jury of children, or something like that — where he says a doctor would straight up lose a trial against a pastry chef if kids were the jury, because they'd side with whoever offers them something nice sounding, not whoever actually knows medicine.

So here's my actual question: if you sat Socrates down and explained the whole debate scheme to him — two superhuman debaters going at it, with one human judge playing the role of his "jury of children" relative to them — would he think this is a genuinely clever fix to the problem he was already griping about 2400 years ago, or would he say it's the same trap wearing a lab coat, broken for basically the same reason he thought persuasion contests in front of an unqualified crowd are broken?
