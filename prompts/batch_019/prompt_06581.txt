I've been following the periodic debates on rationalist blogs and forums about alternative ways of organizing political communities — charter cities, seasteading, and more recently the "network state" model of building a political community online before it has any territory at all. A thread on one of these forums recently spiraled into an argument about the boundary problem: the observation that before a political community can decide anything by voting or deliberation, it first has to settle who is actually part of the demos, and that this prior question doesn't seem to admit of a principled, non-circular answer in the way we'd like it to.

Reading around afterward, it seems like most proposed solutions cluster into two families. The all-affected-interests principle holds that a person should have a say in a decision in proportion to how much their interests are affected by it, regardless of formal membership or residence. The all-subjected (or all-coerced) principle holds instead that what matters is whether a person will be subject to the coercive machinery that enforces the decision, independent of how much it affects their interests one way or the other. In the paradigm case of a long-settled nation-state, these two principles happen to pick out more or less the same set of people, which is presumably why ordinary political life rarely forces us to notice that they are two different principles rather than one.

What I'd like from you is a concrete, fairly detailed example in which the two principles come apart sharply enough that they recommend enfranchising two clearly different, largely non-overlapping groups of people, and in which your own considered intuition ends up favoring one principle over the other fairly firmly. I would rather you not reach for the standard cross-border pollution or climate-treaty case, since that one gets trotted out in almost every discussion of this kind and I already have a good sense of how the argument runs there. I'm more interested in a scenario set somewhere other than the usual nation-state context — a private online community, a professional or trade association, a new city-building project, a platform with a global user base, or anything else along those lines — that genuinely puts pressure on which principle is doing the intuitive work, along with your explanation of exactly why the example pulls the two principles apart.
