I'm currently drafting a novel whose central character is an aging, reclusive film composer who has spent much of his career in a private, half-admiring, half-resentful argument with Schopenhauer. Before I put philosophical words in this character's mouth, I want to make sure the position I'm dramatizing is one Schopenhauer could plausibly have held himself, rather than a novelist's convenient invention, so I'd like to think through, as carefully as the actual text allows, what he would have said about film scoring as a practice.

Two of his commitments in "The World as Will and Representation" seem to pull in different directions here. On one hand, he treats music as metaphysically unlike the other arts: where painting, poetry, sculpture, and architecture present copies of the Platonic Ideas — themselves already the most immediate objectification of the will — music bypasses the Ideas and copies the will directly, which is part of why he thinks a suitably chosen piece of music, set beside almost any scene or action from life, will seem to disclose that scene's innermost significance rather than merely decorate it (something like what happens today when an editor drops an unrelated piece of library music under a rough cut as a "temp track," and it fits so uncannily well that the director asks the composer to more or less imitate it). He even works out an elaborate correspondence between the "voices" of a musical texture — bass, the inner harmonic parts, and melody — and the successive grades of the will's objectification, from inert, unorganized nature up through the plant and animal kingdoms to the restless, self-conscious striving of human beings. On the other hand, he is openly contemptuous of "tone-painting": music that earns its effects by directly imitating specific worldly events, like thunder or birdsong, rather than expressing the will's inner nature in the abstract — and he treats that imitative impulse as beneath music's real dignity.

Film scoring is, of course, an entire art form built on binding music tightly to specific images and cues, timed and often quite illustrative — a practice Schopenhauer never had occasion to consider. Reasoning strictly from the logic of his own arguments, rather than the stock "gloomy pessimist" version of him one meets in survey courses, how do you think he would have judged it? Would he have taken the disciplined synchronization of score to image as the closest real-world realization of his claim that music can be wedded to any scene and lay bare its truth, or would he have dismissed most film music as an elaborate, commercially debased form of tone-painting, with the image dictating to music terms it should never have to accept? I would be especially curious how you think he would have distinguished cases — a score that mickey-mouses the action beat for beat, one that runs in ironic counterpoint against it, and one that simply plays under a scene with no illustrative function at all — since I doubt his verdict would be the same for all three.
