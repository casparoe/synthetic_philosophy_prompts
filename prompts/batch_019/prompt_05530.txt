As I prepare a graduate seminar on the logic of conditionals, I keep returning to one specific puzzle rather than a general survey of the field. Ernest Adams's classic minimal pair runs as follows:

(1) If Oswald did not kill Kennedy, someone else did.
(2) If Oswald had not killed Kennedy, someone else would have.

Nearly every competent speaker judges (1) true and (2) false, even though the two sentences share an antecedent and a consequent and differ only in grammatical mood. Please explain why this pair is treated as a serious obstacle to Stalnaker's project of giving indicative and counterfactual conditionals a single, uniform selection-function semantics, and how a Stalnakerian tries to locate the difference in context rather than in the truth conditions themselves. Then offer your own assessment: does that maneuver actually succeed, or does the pair instead vindicate the Adams–Edgington view that indicative conditionals should be handled probabilistically rather than truth-conditionally, leaving counterfactuals as the only genuinely modal conditionals in natural language?
