For my metaphysics seminar we've been working through the conditional-analysis literature on dispositions -- Martin's electro-fink, Lewis's reply in "Finkish Dispositions," and the mask/antidote cases from Johnston and Bird. Two people in my reading group got into a real argument after class about a case one of them invented, and I couldn't settle it myself.

The case: a dormitory's sprinkler heads are wired to heat sensors and normally open within two seconds of a room crossing 68°C. Facilities also installed a relay that, the instant a sensor crosses that threshold, cuts power to the solenoid for ninety seconds -- long enough for a resident advisor to radio in and cancel what's usually a false alarm; if nobody cancels, power is restored and the valve opens as usual. Every time a sensor has fired here, an RA has canceled within the window, and -- let's stipulate -- the room stayed above 68°C for the entire ninety seconds regardless. So no sprinkler on this campus has ever discharged, even though the sensors, wiring, and solenoids are all perfectly ordinary and none has ever malfunctioned.

Karen says the sprinklers are still disposed to discharge when heated: the relay is a mask on the model of Bird's antidote, since it intervenes downstream of an unaltered causal basis to block a manifestation that would otherwise occur, and blocking an effect isn't the same as removing a disposition.

Devon says the sprinklers aren't disposed to discharge, full stop: on Bird's own account, a mask only interrupts the process after the disposition's basis has already done its causal work (as an antidote acts only after the poison has bound to its receptors), whereas cutting power removes, for ninety seconds, the very capacity that constitutes the solenoid's basis for opening -- which makes the relay a fink, no different in kind from Martin's original case, just one that has so far always activated in time.

Karen answers that this proves too much: by Devon's own standard, wrapping a fragile glass in bubble wrap also removes a capacity -- the capacity to be struck with any real force -- so every mask can be redescribed as a fink, and the distinction collapses.

Devon replies that bubble wrap changes what stimulus the glass receives, not whether the glass would break given that stimulus, whereas the relay changes whether the solenoid can open at all given the very same stimulus; that, he says, is a difference in kind, not just in description.

Go through this exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, not just who wins overall. I'm especially interested in whether Devon's closing criterion actually rescues a principled fink/mask distinction, or whether Karen's second objection survives it regardless -- and how you would classify the relay yourself.
