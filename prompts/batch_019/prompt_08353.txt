I'm finalizing the lecture notes for the unit on truth in my graduate seminar on classical American philosophy, and I'd like a check on a framing decision before I commit to it. My plan is to open with Peirce's 1878 essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" as the founding statement of the pragmatist theory of truth — summarized for students as the claim that a belief is true just in case holding it "works" or proves useful in practice — and then present James's 1907 lectures, published as Pragmatism, as essentially a more vivid, rhetorically accessible restatement of that same Peircean position for a general audience. Since James was careful to credit Peirce as the originator of the pragmatic principle, and since neither man wanted to treat truth as a static, ready-made correspondence between belief and a mind-independent reality, it seems to me that whatever differences remain between them are mostly a matter of emphasis and audience rather than substance — enough that I can use James's talk of the "cash-value" of an idea as a plain-English gloss on Peirce whenever students find the latter too austere.

A colleague down the hall who works on Peirce glanced at a draft of these notes and remarked that the framing "flattens something she'd want students to sit with," but she had a class to get to and couldn't elaborate. I'd rather work this out before I present it to a room of first-year graduate students: is James's account of truth really just a popularization of Peirce's, or does the framing above paper over a substantive disagreement between the two of them about what truth is?
