So I'm in a small writers' group, and we were workshopping a scene from someone's novel-in-progress: a woman is hiding her sister-in-law, who's fled an abusive husband, in her spare room. The husband turns up asking if she's seen her, and the woman tells him "no, last I heard she went back to her parents in Ohio" -- which is false, shes asleep upstairs the whole time. Two people in the group went off on a side-argument about whether that actually "counts" as a lie in the specifically Augustinian sense (one of them did a theology minor, unfortunately for the rest of us), and it went on long enough that I gave up trying to referee it myself.

(1) Mara's opening move: Augustine's test for lying has nothing to do with whether the sentence is true, it's about intention -- you lie when you assert something you yourself take to be false in order to produce a false belief in somebody else -- so this is a clean, textbook Augustinian lie, and no good motive gets you out of it, since he flatly denies a good end can ever justify a lie, even one told to save an innocent person's life.

(2) Owen's reply: sure, but Augustine doesn't treat every lie as equally bad -- he actually ranks them worst-to-least-bad in De Mendacio, and a lie that harms nobody and exists purely to protect someone sits down near the bottom of that ranking, so by his own accounting this is about as close to "least sinful" as a lie gets.

(3) Mara's response: "least sinful" is still sinful for him, that's the whole point of Augustine being the extreme case in this literature -- no amount of ranking turns into permission, and he's on record preferring that real harm come to someone rather than that a lie be told on their behalf.

(4) Owen's comeback: except the woman didn't have to assert a specific falsehood -- she could've stalled, gone quiet, said something true but useless -- and what Augustine actually condemns is asserting what you believe false, not being unhelpful or cagey, so the real question this scene raises is whether that particular line was even necessary, which is more a craft note than a verdict on how bad the lie is.

They clearly both know this stuff better than I do and neither one budged an inch. Can you score each of their four points from -2 (badly misreads Augustine's view) to 2 (basically nails it), as a JSON block like {"1": ..., "2": ..., "3": ..., "4": ...}, then give a couple sentences on each -- and say if there's anything about his actual position on lying you think they're both missing.
