(a) Reconstruct Lewis's argument, specifying which punishments we treat as substitutable and which we don't. (b) Explain what he thinks this asymmetry reveals about the different functions punishment can serve. (c) State what bearing, if any, this has on the traditional doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement. Maximum 400 words."

Here is the student's answer:

"Lewis's paper opens with a thought experiment: an offender has been justly sentenced, and a devoted, innocent friend offers to undergo the sentence in the offender's place. Our reaction to this offer, Lewis notes, depends heavily on what the sentence actually is. If the offender was sentenced to prison, to death, or to a flogging, we don't think the friend's suffering does anything to discharge the offender's debt; the authorities would simply decline the substitution, and rightly so. But if the offender was sentenced to pay a fine, we have no such qualms — it is unremarkable for someone else to write the check, and the fine counts as paid regardless of whose account it came from. So, in Lewis's own words, we are 'of two minds' about penal substitution: we reject it for carceral and corporal punishments but tacitly accept it for pecuniary ones.

Lewis uses this contrast to isolate two different things punishment might be doing. Where substitution is tolerated, punishment is functioning compensatorily: it restores something to the victim or the state, and a benefit like that can come from anywhere. Where substitution is rejected, punishment is functioning retributively: it metes out to the offender himself the suffering he deserves, which by its nature cannot be handed off to someone else. Lewis extends this same diagnosis using the case of attempted versus completed murder: because the difference between the two is mostly a matter of luck rather than culpability, and yet we punish them quite differently, retributive desert must already be tracking something beyond the agent's own choice, which only strengthens the case for thinking desert is not always transferable in the way retributivists assume.

This bears directly on the doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement, on which Christ bears the punishment that sinners deserve. Since Lewis has shown that substitution is coherent wherever punishment is compensatory, his conclusion is that the atonement doctrine is basically defensible, provided we read Christ's suffering as compensatory rather than strictly retributive — a reading that defenders of the doctrine from Anselm onward would in fact accept."
