I sit on the student conduct board at my university, which adjudicates academic-integrity cases referred by faculty and staff; I joined this year, and the matter below is the first contested hearing I've had to help resolve. A graduate teaching assistant has filed a complaint against an undergraduate, alleging that several paragraphs of her term paper were copied nearly verbatim from an online study guide, and the copying itself is not seriously disputed. Partway through the hearing, the student's faculty advisor disclosed, and the TA confirmed under questioning, that the TA himself was found responsible for a very similar plagiarism violation as an undergraduate at another institution eight years ago. That finding was fully adjudicated and, per the other institution's policy, expunged from his record after he completed a semester of probation; he did not volunteer this history when he took on the TA role, though nothing in our policies required him to. The board is now split roughly in half: some members think this history disqualifies him from bringing the complaint at all, or at least so compromises the proceeding that the case should be dismissed or any sanction sharply reduced, while others think the student's guilt is a separate question from the TA's past, and that giving the past any weight would effectively let students escape sanction whenever their accuser turns out to be imperfect, expunged record or not. Our bylaws state who may file a complaint but say nothing about whether a complainant's own disciplinary history bears on how a complaint should be handled.

Please draft the written decision I will circulate to the full board before we reconvene next week, and eventually to both parties. It should reach a concrete disposition of the plagiarism case itself, including any sanction or lack thereof; a concrete disposition of what, if anything, follows from the TA's history and non-disclosure, and on what timeline; and reasoning specific enough that a skeptical board member could follow it point by point and push back on any step. In particular, take seriously the strongest version of the view that the TA's history is relevant to how this case should proceed -- not the strawman that his past makes him a liar -- even if your recommendation ultimately gives that view little or no weight.
