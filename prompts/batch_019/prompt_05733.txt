Okay so in my phil of mind seminar we just spent two weeks on Chalmers's zombie argument, and I feel like everyone (me included, tbh) keeps flattening it down to "zombies are conceivable, therefore physicalism is false," when the reading made clear the actual fight is one level down, in the two-dimensional semantics machinery. Like, the whole reason "water is H2O" can be necessary and yet conceivably false is that "water" has a primary intension that can come apart from its secondary intension (Twin Earth and all that) -- and Chalmers's move is to argue that phenomenal concepts don't have that kind of gap. There's no "twin pain" scenario the way there's a twin-water scenario, so for consciousness, unlike water, the conceivability of zombies is supposed to go straight through to genuine metaphysical possibility. The type-B materialists (Loar, Papineau, that whole "phenomenal concept strategy" crowd) deny exactly that last step, and I can never hold both halves of the disagreement in my head at the same time long enough to see whos actually winning.

Could you write me a dialogue between a two-dimensionalist defending the zombie argument and a type-B materialist running the phenomenal concept strategy against it? I don't want the greatest-hits version where one person says "zombies are conceivable" and the other says "no they aren't" -- I want them actually down in it: the primary/secondary intension point, ideal vs. prima facie and positive vs. negative conceivability, and whether phenomenal concepts are really as "gap-free" as the two-dimensionalist needs them to be. Both of them should be sharp -- whenever one lands a real objection, the other should get the actual best reply that philosophers on that side give, not just concede or awkwardly change the subject. Honestly I don't know who's right here, so don't feel like one of them has to come out on top by the end.
