Longtime readers of the rationalist literature on acausal trade will know the basic picture: agents that can never causally influence one another -- separated by a cosmological horizon, or by branches of the quantum multiverse -- each reason about what a sufficiently similar agent elsewhere would do, and act as though a bargain had been struck, even though neither side can ever communicate, verify, or enforce it. The whole scheme works only because each party can, in principle, be deduced to be "the kind of agent" who would follow through, by reasoning alone. This has always struck me as a strange echo of Leibniz's monadology: substances that are causally "windowless" toward one another, yet whose internal states track each other perfectly, not through interaction but through a harmony God pre-established at the moment of creation. Put the modern case for acausal trade to Leibniz directly: would he say his own system already licenses it, since every monad represents the whole universe, however confusedly, so that a sufficiently rational one could in principle read another monad's future dispositions off its own perceptions, with no causal channel required? Or would he insist "trade" is simply the wrong word for what his metaphysics allows, on the grounds that nothing is actually being negotiated between monads once God has already fixed the one best, fully compossible arrangement at creation -- so that what looks like two parties adjusting to each other is really just two clocks independently ticking out a plan neither of them made? Work through his actual commitments rather than the surface-level resemblance, and tell me which reading he would find truer to his own system, or whether he'd reject the comparison altogether, and why.
