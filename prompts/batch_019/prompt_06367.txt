Quick one from deep in a research hole for my current novel. I've got a subplot set in Warring States China with a character loosely based on Gongsun Long, the "a white horse is not a horse" guy from the old School of Names. Supposedly (this might be apocryphal, I've seen it repeated a few places but can't actually verify it) he once tried to use the argument on a border guard to get his horse past a checkpoint where horses were banned from crossing, and I really want to write that scene — the guard getting more and more irritated while this guy calmly logics at him. Only problem is I've realized I don't understand the argument well enough to make my guy sound like he's making a real case, rather than just reciting nonsense with a straight face and hoping the guard blinks first.

Here's the bit I'm stuck on. I stitched this together out of a couple of rough translations I found, so its clunky, and since my classical Chinese is exactly zero I can't check it against the original:

"Is it acceptable to say a white horse is not a horse? — It is acceptable. — Why? — Because 'horse' is what names a shape, and 'white' is what names a colour, and naming a colour is not naming a shape; therefore a white horse is not a horse. Furthermore: if a man asks for a horse, a yellow horse or a black horse will do. If he asks for a white horse, a yellow horse or a black horse will not do. Now if 'white horse' were really the same thing as 'horse,' what is asked for in each case would be the same, and whatever answers to the one should answer to the other. Since a yellow horse answers to 'horse' but not to 'white horse,' the two cannot be the same — so a white horse is not a horse."

Apparently it keeps going from there into whether "white" and "horse" are "seperate" from one another, which connects to his other, even stranger essay about a hard white stone and whether you can ever actually perceive its hardness and its whiteness "together" — I'm leaving that one alone for now, one paradox at a time. What I can't work out about the passage above is whether the second argument (the "asking for a horse" bit) is doing genuine logical work, or whether he's quietly sliding between two different questions — what a word means versus which actual animals happen to satisfy somebody's request — and just hoping nobody clocks the switch. Is that gesturing at something like an intension/extension distinction, or a class-versus-member point, or is it simply equivocating on "is" (identity vs. "is an instance of")? I also vaguely remember that later Mohist logicians went after this exact style of argument with something like "riding a white horse is riding a horse" — if I'm remembering right, does that actually finish him off, or does he have a move available to survive it given what's in the passage? I'd like my guard to be able to push back with something sharper than "that's stupid," so I need to know where the real pressure points are.
