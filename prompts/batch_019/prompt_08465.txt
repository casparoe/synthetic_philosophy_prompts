**Question (25 marks).** In "The Enforcement of Morals" (1959), Patrick Devlin argued that a shared morality is as necessary to a society's existence as a shared government, and that the criminal law is therefore entitled to protect that shared morality against erosion. H. L. A. Hart challenged this argument, first in a 1959 lecture and then at greater length in *Law, Liberty, and Morality* (1963).

(a) Reconstruct Devlin's argument for legal moralism, including the "disintegration thesis" and his proposed test for when a departure from conventional morality becomes the law's business. [10]
(b) Explain Hart's response to this argument. Does Hart's reply leave him with something close to Mill's original, unqualified harm principle, or does Hart part ways with Mill on any material point? [15]

**Student answer.**

Devlin's starting point is that no society can persist without a shared morality any more than it can persist without a shared government, so the criminal law's function is not exhausted by preventing harm between individuals; it also legitimately protects the shared moral fabric that holds the community together. If the state stands by while individuals flout that consensus, the cohesion of the community is put at risk over time — this is the disintegration thesis. Devlin does not think every departure from conventional morality warrants punishment, however. He limits the reach of the law with a test asking whether the conduct provokes, in the mind of the ordinary reasonable member of society, a reaction of "intolerance, indignation, and disgust"; and he counsels further restraint on separate grounds, holding that privacy should be respected wherever possible and that the law should move cautiously rather than race ahead of, or lag too far behind, actual public sentiment.

Hart's central objection is that this argument proves too little. He points out that Devlin offers no real evidence that toleration of private immorality actually erodes social cohesion, so the disintegration thesis is asserted rather than established. More fundamentally, Hart accuses Devlin of trading on a conflation between positive morality — the moral code a rational, disinterested observer would endorse on reflection — and critical morality — whatever code of conduct a given society happens to hold at a given time. Devlin's disgust test can only tell us that a practice is currently disapproved of, not that it deserves to be; on Hart's view, the bare fact of shared disapproval cannot by itself justify using the criminal law.

Having dismantled Devlin's case for legal moralism, Hart is left more or less exactly where Mill was: the only legitimate ground for criminal prohibition is the prevention of harm to persons other than the actor, and Hart accordingly rejects paternalistic legislation — laws restricting what a competent adult may do to or with herself — as beyond the proper business of the law. On this reading, Hart's liberalism wins by default: once the disintegration thesis is shown to rest on a conceptual confusion, there is no independent argument left for treating a society's morality, as such, as any business of the criminal law.
