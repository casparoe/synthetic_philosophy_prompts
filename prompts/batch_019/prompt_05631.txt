A common observation in the ethics of blame is that hypocrites seem to lose some authority to condemn others, even when the people they are blaming are genuinely guilty. At least two different explanations for this phenomenon have been proposed. On a reciprocity-based account, hypocritical blame is defective because it enforces a double standard: the hypocrite demands of others a level of conduct from which they exempt themselves. On an equal-standing account, the defect lies elsewhere: blame presupposes that the blamer addresses the blamed as a moral equal, and the hypocrite's own conduct falsifies the parity their blame implicitly claims.

Compare these two accounts, being precise about where exactly each locates the wrong in hypocritical blame. Then test them against a harder case: a "reformed" hypocrite, who committed the very offense they now condemn but has since stopped and sincerely regrets it. Does each account predict that this person retains full standing to blame, diminished standing, or none at all, and why? Which account gives the more defensible verdict overall?
