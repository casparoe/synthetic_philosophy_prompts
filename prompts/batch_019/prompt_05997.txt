As I research a piece on the deep history of debates over mechanism and mind, I keep returning to a short passage from Leibniz's Monadology (1714), where he imagines a machine built to think, enlarged to the size of a mill:

"Moreover, it must be confessed that perception, and that which depends upon it, is inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is to say, by figures and motions. And, supposing that there were a machine so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, we might conceive it increased in size while retaining the same proportions, so that we might be able to enter it as we would enter a mill. On going inside, we should see only parts which work upon one another, and we should never find anything to explain a perception."

This is often cited as a forerunner of the "hard problem" of consciousness, and by extension of the modern conceivability argument for philosophical zombies. I'd like help getting clearer on its actual logical structure, though. Is Leibniz here running a conceivability argument in something like David Chalmers's sense — claiming that we can coherently conceive of all that mechanical shuffling occurring with no perception present at all — or is he making a different kind of claim, closer to a bare explanatory-gap objection about what a mechanical description could ever entail, without any commitment about what is conceivable or metaphysically possible? And if it's the latter, does the mill argument still lend real support to the zombie argument, or could someone fully accept Leibniz's point while denying that zombies, in Chalmers's sense, are genuinely conceivable?
