Three weeks ago I started a position as a junior research analyst for a legislative committee currently holding hearings on tariff policy, and my first real assignment was to draft a background memo tracing the intellectual history behind the arguments both sides keep invoking. That sent me back to David Hume's economic essays — "Of Commerce," "Of Refinement in the Arts," "Of Money," "Of Interest," "Of the Balance of Trade," "Of the Jealousy of Trade," "Of Public Credit," and "Of Taxes" — which I had previously only encountered as a single footnote in an undergraduate survey course ("Hume anticipated the specie-flow mechanism," full stop, next slide). Reading them properly, I was struck by how many distinct, separable claims Hume is actually making, and by how unevenly they seem to have aged: some read like early statements of positions mainstream economists still hold, while others look like artifacts of an economy without floating exchange rates, central banks, or national accounts. This week, both the free-trade witnesses and the more protectionist-leaning committee staff have quoted "Hume" approvingly at me, and I don't think either side has noticed that his actual position is a set of separable and unevenly defensible claims rather than a single doctrine that neatly supports them.

Before I finalize the memo, I would like an outside assessment of exactly which of these claims still hold up. Below are eight specific propositions drawn from those essays. Please sort them into tiers by how well each has been vindicated by subsequent economic theory and evidence, ranging from claims mainstream economics would still largely endorse to ones now generally regarded as mistaken or as artifacts of Hume's own historical moment. A. Money is not itself wealth but only a conventional token that represents and facilitates the exchange of labor and commodities, which are the real measure of a nation's riches.
B. A sudden increase in the quantity of money stimulates real output and employment only while it is still gradually diffusing through the economy and before wages and prices have caught up; once they do, the same increase leaves output unchanged and simply raises prices proportionally.
C. No nation can maintain a permanently "favorable" balance of trade, because an inflow of specie raises domestic prices and wages, eroding competitiveness until exports fall and imports rise enough to reverse the flow.
D. Interest rates are governed chiefly by the profits available from trade and by a society's frugality and extent of commerce, not by the sheer quantity of money in circulation.
E. The enrichment of one's neighbors and trading partners tends to enrich oneself as well, so that trying to keep other nations poor through restrictive trade policy is ultimately self-defeating.
F. A large national debt financed on public credit is a serious long-run danger to the state, tending eventually to force either the repudiation of the debt or the ruin of the finances that depend on it.
G. Commerce and the refinement of manners that comes with luxury go together with industriousness, the spread of knowledge, and even the preservation of civil liberty, rather than corrupting a people.
H. Moderate taxation, by giving people an incentive to work harder to maintain their accustomed standard of living, need not depress a nation's output and can under some conditions even increase it.
