The question was worth 25 points (5 / 5 / 10 / 5 across the parts):

"Explain (a) why Kant's shopkeeper, who doesn't overcharge his customers, is meant to illustrate action that is 'in accordance with duty' but not 'from duty'; (b) how Barbara Herman's 'limiting condition' reading of Kant is supposed to answer the objection that Kantian ethics can't make room for acting from love or sympathy; and (c) why Nomy Arpaly thinks her discussion of Huckleberry Finn is a problem case even for Herman's revised picture. Then say briefly whether Julia Markovits's Coincident Reasons Thesis does any better than Kant/Herman at capturing what's admirable about Huck."

Here's the student's answer:

"(a) Kant's point with the shopkeeper is that merely acting the way duty requires isn't enough for moral worth. The shopkeeper doesn't overcharge his customers, including the ones who wouldn't know any better, which is the right thing to do, but Kant is explicit that he does this because a reputation for fair dealing is good for business, not because he has any special warmth toward his customers or has reasoned his way to a principle of honesty. Because his honest dealing flows from self-interest rather than duty, Kant denies it moral worth, however convenient the resulting policy is for everyone who walks into the shop.

(b) Herman is responding to the standard complaint that this makes Kant prefer a cold, dutiful merchant to a warm, generous one, which seems backwards. Her fix is to say the motive of duty doesn't have to be doing the motivating work on any given occasion. Instead it operates as a 'limiting condition' -- a standing disposition that would correct the agent's conduct if her inclinations ever recommended something impermissible. So a Kantian agent can act from love most of the time; what makes her a good Kantian is that her sense of duty is always there in the background, ready to override the inclination if it ever pointed the wrong way.

(c) Arpaly argues that Huckleberry Finn is too hard a case even for Herman's picture. Huck helps Jim escape purely because he's grown attached to him, and Arpaly's diagnosis is that a boy raised the way Huck was simply hasn't developed anything worth calling a conception of duty yet -- his conscience is still forming, so there's no standing disposition of any kind sitting in the background of his mind, dutiful or otherwise. That absence is the problem for Herman: with no limiting condition present at all, she can't credit some Kantian second-order disposition for the good outcome, since on this reading Huck simply doesn't have one, and yet we still want to say what he did was admirable.

Markovits's Coincident Reasons Thesis is supposed to do better here, since it drops the requirement that the agent act from duty and asks only that her motivating reasons line up with the reasons that actually make the act right. On this view Huck gets full marks because the very facts that make helping Jim right -- his personhood, his suffering, the friendship between them -- are also the facts moving Huck to act. Where Markovits still sides with Kant, though, is in thinking the agent has to grasp, at some level, that these facts add up to a moral requirement; she just doesn't insist the agent dress that requirement up in the vocabulary of duty. So her account softens Kant's language without really giving up his thought that some kind of belief that one is doing what morality demands has to be present for an act to count as worthy."
