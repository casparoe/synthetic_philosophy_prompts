As a software engineer who spends a fair amount of time on rationalist and effective altruism forums, I have long found Hofstadter's notion of "superrationality" compelling: in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two agents who are exact copies of one another, cooperation is supposed to be rational because whatever one of them decides, the other—being identical—decides too, so "I cooperate, they defect" was never really an option on the table. I have since come across Evidential Cooperation in Large Worlds (ECL), which is usually presented as the same insight scaled up: if the universe or multiverse is large or infinite, it contains vast numbers of causally disconnected agents whose decision procedures are correlated with mine to some uncertain degree, and evidential decision theory says that my cooperating with their (different, largely unknown) values is good news about their cooperating with mine, even though nothing I do can causally reach them. I cannot tell whether ECL is a genuine extension of the twin case or a much shakier analogy dressed in the same vocabulary. Please argue that the twin-style justification for superrational cooperation does not straightforwardly generalize into a justification for ECL's recommendation of cooperation across the large, heterogeneous, only partially correlated population that a large or infinite world would actually contain.
