I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I got pulled into this material through a side project building calibration tools for prediction-market traders, which is how I first ran into diachronic Dutch book arguments and, from there, van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- the idea that your present credence in a proposition, conditional on learning what your credence in that same proposition will be at some later time, should already equal that future value, on pain of being exploitable by a bookie who bets against you at both times. It's often glossed as a matter of treating your future self the way you'd treat a recognized expert, or the way Lewis's Principal Principle has you treat objective chance. A friend of mine hosts a small podcast on formal epistemology and decision theory for a rationalist and quant-minded audience, and he's asked me to help prepare questions for an upcoming episode. The guest is a philosopher who has built a good part of her career defending a fairly robust, undiluted version of Reflection against the standard objections, and I don't want the episode to just let her restate the principle in its friendliest form.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions for this conversation? I'd like at least one question on whether the diachronic Dutch book argument really shows that violating Reflection is an epistemic defect, rather than just a way of being exploitable by someone who happens to know more than you do at a single time (her go-to response, I'm told, is some version of "a rational agent shouldn't be guaranteed to lose money no matter what happens, full stop," so I'd like the questions to make that line progressively harder to fall back on); at least one on the proviso restricting Reflection to cases where your future credence is expected to result from rational updating on new evidence rather than memory loss, manipulation, or drugs, and whether that proviso ends up doing so much work that the principle risks collapsing into something close to "defer to your future self, except when you shouldn't"; and at least one that uses the Sleeping Beauty problem as a stress test (the setup has Beauty anticipate, before it even starts, that on one branch her memory of an earlier awakening will be wiped before she's ever told how the coin landed, which sounds like exactly the sort of case the proviso exists to carve out), asking whether Reflection has anything coherent to say about Sleeping-Beauty-style cases at all. For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so we aren't stuck if she just restates her position more emphatically. I'd like to close with one or two questions on what kind of case, if any, she would accept as genuine evidence against Reflection, rather than just another instance for the proviso to absorb.
