My father is picking up his twenty-year chip at his home group next month, in the same church basement where they've met for over thirty years, and it's become a custom there for a family member to say a few words before the person is called up to take it, so that job has landed on me. The sobriety date itself comes from one specific night: he blacked out on the drive home from a bar, clipped a parked car a few streets from the house, and came to a stop against another one a block later. That was the end of his drinking. What I keep circling back to, now that I actually have to write something, is a man my father has mentioned a handful of times over the years -- someone he was in and out of meetings with during the last bad stretch before he finally got sober, whose drinking was by every account exactly as far gone as his. On one of that man's own blackout drives home, a pedestrian crossing at the wrong moment didn't survive it, and he has been in prison ever since. A couple of the old-timers who'll be sitting in that room know exactly who I mean without my having to say a name. When I went looking online for whether there was even a word for the thing I couldn't stop turning over, it turned out there's a whole minor literature on it called moral luck, and when I skimmed a philosophy encyclopedia entry on the subject, my father's situation looked uncomfortably close to the standard example -- except that nothing I read there was written to be spoken out loud to a room that's mostly there for cake.

Could you write the speech I actually have to give? I don't want to stand up and tell a clean story about hitting bottom and turning it all around through sheer will, because I'm not sure I believe that story, and I don't think my father fully believes it either -- he's said more than once, unprompted, that he has no idea why he's the one still walking around instead of the one still locked up. But I also can't get up on what's supposed to be a happy night and tell a room half full of people in early recovery that whether you end up with a chip or a cell number is basically a coin flip, or turn his twentieth year into a lecture on moral philosophy, or say anything that could sound like it's making excuses for what that drive home could have done to somebody. Whatever you write needs to hold all of that honestly, run no more than about four minutes, and still sound like a daughter who's proud of her father, not like someone who's spent too many nights reading philosophy websites.
