I recently sat in on a seminar on comparative epistemology in which the discussion turned to the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, the principle of "non-one-sidedness" according to which reality has innumerably many aspects and no single assertion can exhaust the truth about it. The instructor sketched the supporting apparatus: nayavāda, the theory that any given standpoint (naya) captures one legitimate but partial aspect of a thing without thereby denying the others, and syādvāda, the practice of qualifying every predication with "syād" ("in some respect") to keep a standpoint from overstepping what it can actually establish -- formalized in the sevenfold schema (saptabhaṅgī) of conditional affirmation, denial, and their combinations. Most of the class came away treating this as essentially a South Asian anticipation of relativism about truth, while a couple of students, pointing to the Jain claim that a fully liberated, omniscient being (a kevalin) grasps all aspects of reality simultaneously and without contradiction, argued that this reading gets the doctrine backwards.

I would like your help adjudicating that disagreement. Please compare anekāntavāda, as elaborated through nayavāda and syādvāda, with epistemic or alethic relativism in the sense familiar from Western philosophy: roughly, the view that truth (or warranted assertability) is constituted by, rather than merely accessed through, a framework or standpoint, so that there is no framework-independent fact about which of two conflicting standpoints is correct. Do the two positions actually agree on this point, or does anekāntavāda's commitment to a single, complete, and consistent truth that is in principle available to an omniscient knower -- together with its treatment of one-sidedness (ekāntavāda) as a specific cognitive error rather than just another, equally valid perspective -- make it a substantively different, and perhaps even anti-relativist, position that merely shares relativism's suspicion of any single finite viewpoint? Please defend a definite answer rather than simply listing points of similarity and difference.
