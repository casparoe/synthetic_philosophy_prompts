A friend of mine and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about Humeanism versus anti-Humeanism about laws of nature. I was defending something in the spirit of David Lewis's best-system account, on which a law is simply whichever regularity earns a place in the deductive system that best combines simplicity and strength, with no further metaphysical ingredient -- no relation of necessitation between universals, for instance -- needed to make something a law. My friend objected that this can't be the whole story, since it seems to make lawhood depend on facts about which systematizations we (or some ideal epistemic community) would find simple or informative, rather than on some feature of the world that would hold whether or not anyone was around to do the systematizing; he wanted something closer to Armstrong's view, on which laws are contingent relations of necessitation between universals, robust enough to explain the regularities rather than merely summarize them.

Trying afterward to settle whose intuitions had the better historical pedigree, I went back to Hume's first Enquiry and found two passages harder to reconcile than I remembered. In Section VII ('Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion'), Part I, arguing that no single operation of body or mind ever yields an impression of power, Hume writes: "In reality, there is no part of matter, that does ever, by its sensible qualities, discover any power or energy, or give us ground to imagine, that it could produce any thing, or be followed by any other object, which we could denominate its effect. Solidity, extension, motion; these qualities are all complete in themselves, and never point out any other event which may result from them. The scenes of the universe are continually shifting, and one object follows another in an uninterrupted succession; but the power or force, which actuates the whole machine, is entirely concealed from us, and never discovers itself in any of the sensible qualities of body." A few pages later, summing up the whole discussion in Part II, he instead writes: "All events seem entirely loose and separate. One event follows another; but we never can observe any tye between them. They seem conjoined, but never connected. And as we can have no idea of any thing, which never appeared to our outward sense or inward sentiment, the necessary conclusion seems to be, that we have no idea of connexion or power at all, and that these words are absolutely without any meaning, when employed either in philosophical reasonings, or common life."

The first passage reads to me as though Hume thought there really is some power or force "actuating the whole machine," merely permanently hidden from us -- which sounds a lot closer to my friend's anti-Humean sympathies (or at least to some sort of realism about hidden causal powers) than to the purely eliminativist regularity view I'd always attributed to him, and that I'd assumed stood behind Lewis. The second passage, by contrast, seems to retract the very idea that "power" or "connexion" picks out anything at all, hidden or otherwise, reducing cause and effect to nothing but constant conjunction plus our own habit of expectation. Could you help me work out whether these two passages are really in tension, and if so how a careful reader ought to resolve it -- is there a standard scholarly line on this, or is it a genuinely live interpretive dispute? And, stepping back: would it even matter for the contemporary Humean/anti-Humean debate about laws which reading is correct -- that is, would that debate be affected if Hume himself turned out to have intended only a modest epistemic thesis (that regularity is all we could ever know of any causal connection) rather than the full metaphysical claim that regularity is all there is to it? I'd like to know whether the "Humean" side of that modern debate can really claim Hume as an ancestor in good conscience, or whether it rests on a reading of him that he might well have rejected.
