For my IB Theory of Knowledge class, I've been putting together an Extended Essay on the nature of justified belief, and while doing background reading for it I fell down a rabbit hole involving two puzzles that keep turning up together in the literature: the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. As I understand the lottery case, which I gather goes back to Kyburg, if I hold one ticket in a large, fair lottery it seems perfectly rational for me to believe that my ticket will lose, and exactly the same reasoning applies to every other ticket, so it looks rational to believe, of each ticket considered on its own, that it will not win; yet it also seems rational to believe that some ticket will win, since we stipulated that exactly one of them must. If justified beliefs can always be combined the way ordinary logic would suggest — so that being justified in believing P and justified in believing Q lets you justifiedly believe P-and-Q — this generates an outright contradiction. The preface paradox, usually credited to Makinson, is supposed to be the very same structural problem wearing different clothes: an author can be individually justified, sentence by sentence, in everything printed in her book, while also being justified, on general grounds about how error-prone long books tend to be, in believing that the book contains at least one falsehood somewhere.

While reading around the topic, including some of the older responses and some newer ones that try to treat the two cases very differently (for instance, by pointing out that believing a given ticket will lose isn't grounded in anything that would actually explain its losing, unlike ordinary inductive beliefs, a feature I've seen called something like "normic support"), I put together the six "standard" proposed solutions below. I would like help sorting them by how well they actually succeed philosophically, on the assumption that a genuinely good answer should ideally handle both paradoxes together rather than just one of them:

1. "Deny conjunction closure" – reject the idea that being justified in believing P and justified in believing Q always licenses being justified in believing P-and-Q.
2. "Raise the bar for belief" – deny the "Lockean thesis" that sufficiently high credence is enough for outright belief, so that none of the individual lottery or preface propositions were rationally believed outright to begin with.
3. "Credence instead of belief" – give up outright, all-or-nothing belief as a target of rational evaluation altogether, and evaluate only graded credences, on which no contradiction arises.
4. "Tolerate inconsistency" – accept that a finite believer's justified commitments can be jointly inconsistent, and treat this as an unavoidable feature of finite reasoners rather than a symptom of a genuine mistake.
5. "Treat the cases asymmetrically" – argue that the statistical evidence behind lottery beliefs and the ordinary inductive evidence behind preface-style beliefs are different in kind, so that only the latter can support outright belief, meaning the two paradoxes actually need separate treatments rather than one unified solution.
6. "Contextualize justification" – adopt a contextualist view on which the evidential threshold for justified belief shifts with context, so that a given ticket or sentence counts as justifiedly believed in some contexts and not in others.
