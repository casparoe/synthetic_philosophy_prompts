In my IB Theory of Knowledge class, we spent a couple of lessons on testimony as a source of knowledge: essentially, the question of when we are entitled to believe something just because someone told us it is true. The class discussion stayed fairly surface-level, mostly concerning whether eyewitnesses or social media posts count as reliable testimony, but I found myself genuinely interested and started reading further on my own, even though a couple of friends in the class thought I was overthinking a TOK presentation. That is how I came across a debate between so-called "reductionists" and "anti-reductionists" about testimonial justification that never came up in class. As I understand it, reductionists (starting with Hume) hold that our right to trust testimony must ultimately be grounded in ordinary perception, memory, and induction: we are justified in believing people only because we, or humanity collectively, have built up a track record of testimony proving reliable. Anti-reductionists (following Reid, and later defended by philosophers like Coady) deny that this is possible, partly because the inductive evidence we would need for testimony's general reliability is itself mostly gathered through more testimony, and so they treat trusting others as a basic, non-inferential entitlement, comparable to our entitlement to trust perception or memory.

I think I follow the basic positions, but I am stuck on how an actual exchange between the two sides would unfold once you get past the opening statements: how would a reductionist answer the circularity worry, and how would an anti-reductionist answer the charge that their view makes justified belief too easy to come by, since it seems to license trusting almost anyone unless we already have specific evidence against them? Could you write a dialogue between a committed reductionist and a committed anti-reductionist about testimony, in which they genuinely engage with each other's strongest objections rather than simply restating their opening positions? I would like both characters to come across as equally sharp, with neither being an easy target, and it would be great if more refined intermediate positions, such as Fricker's "local reductionism" or Lackey's hybrid account, could come up naturally if the conversation heads that way. It is fine if nothing gets fully resolved by the end, since from what I have read, this still seems to be a genuinely live disagreement among epistemologists.
