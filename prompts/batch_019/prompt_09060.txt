Over the last few months, a few of us in an informal reading group have been working through the "moral uncertainty" literature that has grown out of MacAskill, Bykvist, and Ord's work, and I've been trying to apply it to my own case rather than to the toy examples the literature typically uses. My credences are currently split roughly as follows: about 45% on a broadly consequentialist view where welfare aggregates in a fairly standard cardinal way; 30% on a Kantian-ish view that treats a handful of constraints (against deception, coercion, and using people merely as means) as close to inviolable rather than as one more input to be weighed against everything else; and the remaining 25% spread across virtue ethics and a more particularist skepticism that any single systematic theory captures the moral facts at all. The standard recommendation in this literature is to act so as to maximize "expected choiceworthiness" across the theories one takes seriously, weighted by credence in each. But the more I try to apply this to actual decisions — whether a small, otherwise harmless deception is licensed by a sufficiently large downstream benefit, say, or whether a promise to a friend can be outweighed by a few more impactful hours spent volunteering elsewhere — the more I run into the fact that my consequentialist credence outputs a real-valued "amount" of choiceworthiness while my deontological credence mostly just outputs "permitted" or "forbidden," with no obvious common currency between the two.

I understand this is more or less the "intertheoretic comparisons" problem that the literature spends a great deal of effort on, and that several fixes and alternatives have been proposed: normalizing each theory's choiceworthiness scale by something like its variance or range before combining them; giving certain constraints lexical priority over any amount of expected consequentialist gain; simply acting on whichever single theory one finds most credible ("my favourite theory" approaches) rather than aggregating at all; or setting up something like a bargaining or voting procedure among sub-agents representing each theory (a "moral parliament"). I don't have a good sense of how these actually compare to one another, or of how seriously to take the worry that a hedging agent constructed this way might end up "exploitable" in some sense that a committed single-theory agent would not be (or vice versa). Could you lay out the most important implications of this literature for how someone in roughly my position should actually make decisions — including which, if any, of these approaches you think best survives scrutiny, and whether there is a principled reason to hedge across theories at all rather than simply committing to whichever single theory I currently find most credible?
