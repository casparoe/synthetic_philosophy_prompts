A friend of mine who works adjacent to AI safety research got me into a long, somewhat heated argument last weekend about something he called the "commitment races problem." As he explained it, the worry isn't simply the familiar game-theoretic point that a credible, irreversible commitment can hand an agent a bargaining advantage — the sort of thing Schelling illustrated with a general burning the bridges behind his own army, or that shows up in the doomsday-device logic of Dr. Strangelove. His claim was that once several agents all understand this logic, each one has an incentive to commit earlier, faster, and more irreversibly than the others, precisely so as not to be the party left capitulating — and that this dynamic becomes structurally worse, not just higher-stakes, once you allow for agents (such as advanced AI systems) that can make extremely fast, verifiable, or even self-modifying commitments, say by publishing source code or provably binding themselves in advance.

I pushed back that this sounded like a more dramatic redescription of ordinary brinkmanship in games like Chicken, where the logic has always been "whoever commits first wins" and where it's obvious that things can end badly if both sides commit simultaneously — and I didn't see why the committing agents being pieces of software rather than nuclear-armed states should change the underlying structure of the problem rather than just its speed and its cast of characters. He conceded the continuity with the older theory but insisted that something changes in kind, not merely in degree, once commitments can be made this fast and this legible, though he couldn't quite spell out why, and neither of us could settle it on the spot. Could you help settle it now: what, precisely, does the "commitment races" framing add on top of the standard Schelling-style theory of strategic commitment; why is the speed and verifiability of an agent's commitments supposed to make the resulting race qualitatively more dangerous rather than merely faster and higher-stakes; and what kinds of proposed mitigations, if any, actually address the racing dynamic itself rather than just amounting to "don't build agents capable of bargaining with each other"?
