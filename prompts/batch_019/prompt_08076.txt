Okay so this has been bugging me (pun intended) ever since a debate with my roomate last week. We were watching a horror movie and I jumped about a mile when the monster finally showed up on screen, and afterward we got into an argument about whether that reaction even makes sense. I went down a bit of a rabbit hole afterward and found this old philosophy thing called the "paradox of fiction" that apparently formalizes exactly this problem: the argument is that emotions are only a rational response to things you actually believe are real or actually happening, we obviously don't believe the monster (or Anna Karenina, or whoever) is real, so any fear/sadness/whatever we feel toward fictional stuff is, strictly speaking, irrational. Kind of like a false positive, where our brain's threat-detection system doesn't sandbox the input properly and just treats the movie as live data instead of a simulation.

Given that this has apparently been a known, formalized paradox for decades now, I'm trying to figure out what the "correct" rationalist response is supposed to be. Should I actually be trying to train myself out of jumping at horror movies or tearing up at Pixar films, on the grounds that its a cognitive bug rather than a real signal? Or is there some way to keep enjoying fiction while being honest that the emotional reaction doesn't hold up logically? As a backend dev, "broken system produces a predictable, reproducible error" is a very intuitive framing for me, so I want to know: now that philosophers have apparently proven these reactions are irrational, why hasn't anyone come up with an agreed-on way to correct for it, the same way we've got de-biasing techniques for stuff like anchoring or confirmation bias?
