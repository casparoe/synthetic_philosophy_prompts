This term I am taking a seminar on philosophy of language, and we have just spent two weeks on Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*. I think I follow the basic shape of the sceptical paradox: since I have never actually computed "57 + 68" before, nothing in my past use of "+" settles whether I have always meant addition or some deviant function "quus" that agrees with addition on every sum I have actually performed but returns 5 whenever either addend is 57 or greater. Dispositional accounts do not rescue the individual case either, since Kripke's finiteness objection is that my actual dispositions only ever cover a finite range of cases (there are sums large enough that I would never live to compute them, and I would sometimes err even within that range), whereas addition is an infinite function, so a finite disposition can at best correlate with addition, not constitute meaning it.

What I cannot make sense of is the move to the "sceptical solution," which Kripke explicitly models on Hume's treatment of induction and causation: instead of some further fact about me, what is supposed to license the practice of calling my answers correct or incorrect is the wider community's agreement in judgments. But this looks like it reintroduces the very problem it was meant to dissolve. The community's collective usage is just as finite as any individual's — there have only ever been finitely many speakers who have performed finitely many computations — so Kripke's own finiteness argument should apply to communal dispositions exactly as it applied to mine. We have to write a short paper on this chapter in a few weeks, and I want to make sure I am not building an argument on a mistake, so I raised the issue in office hours; the answer I got was that the "sceptical solution" is not "answering the same question" as the paradox, but I could not get much further than that before the hour ran out.

Given that communal usage is exactly as finite as individual usage, what is the appeal to the community actually contributing that individual dispositions could not, and why isn't Kripke's positive proposal just the original paradox re-run on a larger sample rather than a genuine response to it?
