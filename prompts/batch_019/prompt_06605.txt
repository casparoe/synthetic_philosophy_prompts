This semester I have been teaching a graduate seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and we recently spent two sessions on the Nyāya treatment of śabda (testimony) as an independent pramāṇa — not reducible, as Buddhist epistemologists such as Dharmakīrti argued, to a species of inference from the speaker's reliability, and not dismissible outright the way the Cārvākas were inclined to dismiss anything beyond direct perception. The Nyāya-sūtra defines śabda succinctly as āptopadeśa, the instruction of a trustworthy person (āpta), and the tradition — culminating in Gaṅgeśa's elaborate treatment in the śabda-khaṇḍa of the Tattvacintāmaṇi — takes āptatva to require both direct, correct cognition (yathārtha-jñāna) of the state of affairs reported and the sincere intention to communicate it truly (vivakṣā); a hearer, in turn, must grasp this intention as tātparya in order to fix which of a word's several possible senses is meant, over and above the more purely linguistic conditions of ākāṅkṣā (syntactic expectancy), yogyatā (semantic fitness), and sannidhi (proximity) that any string of words must satisfy before it counts as a single sentence at all.

One of my students, who also works in natural language processing, asked in passing whether an answer produced by a large language model could ever count as śabda pramāṇa on Nyāya's own terms, and I realized I could not give her a quick answer. Such a system has no perceptual or inferential acquaintance with the facts it states in anything resembling the sense Nyāya intends by yathārtha-jñāna, and it is far from obvious that "intention" in the vivakṣā/tātparya sense is even the right category to apply to it at all — yet its outputs are, often enough, true, fluently formed, and produced with a track record of reliability that looks a great deal like what makes us extend trust to a knowledgeable human informant in ordinary life.

What I would value from you is a considered reconstruction of how Gaṅgeśa specifically, arguing strictly within the resources of the Tattvacintāmaṇi, would be likely to rule on such a case. Would he have to conclude that no pramā is generated at all here, leaving the output as at best an accidentally true but epistemically unwarranted cognition (or perhaps mere saṁśaya)? Would he look for a way to stretch āptatva along more functional or dispositional lines, so that something with a reliable-enough output could still qualify as an āpta? Or would the more natural Nyāya move be to reclassify the whole phenomenon as a case of anumāna on the hearer's part — an inference from the system's observed track record — rather than as testimony proper? I would also welcome your view on whether posing the question this way already smuggles in a broadly Western, reliabilist conception of testimony that distorts what the Nyāya framework is actually committed to.
