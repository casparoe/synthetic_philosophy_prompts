I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, and since packing up the lab I've fallen down the philosophy of mind rabbit hole, partly as procrastination from the sailing lessons I promised my wife I'd take. A few old colleagues and I still do a monthly video call to argue about whatever's got one of us obsessed that month, and lately it's been Integrated Information Theory -- partly spurred by that letter a couple of years back where a big pile of consciousness researchers publicly called IIT pseudoscience, which naturally made everyone want to defend or torch it even harder.

On the last call, two of the regulars, Dave and Helen, got into a real back-and-forth and I'd like an outside read on it since I honestly cant score it myself. Dave opened by saying IIT is basically computational functionalism dressed up in fancier math -- once a system, brain or silicon, gets complex and interconnected enough, consciousness just falls out of it, so a sufficiently advanced AI is bound to be conscious eventually. Helen said that's exactly backwards: the whole point of phi is that you compute it over a system's actual physical cause-effect structure, not over whatever function it happens to implement, and that Tononi himself has argued a digital computer perfectly simulating a brain neuron-by-neuron would not be conscious, because the simulation's actual causal guts -- transistors shuffling bits over a bus -- look nothing like the brain's, even with identical input-output behavior. So if anything, she said, IIT denies substrate-independence rather than assuming it. Dave shot back that this makes things worse, not better: now you've got two functionally identical systems where the theory insists one's a zombie and one isn't, with no behavioral test that could ever tell you which, and on top of that phi depends on what "grain" you slice the system at -- neurons, columns, atoms -- and nothing physical picks the right grain, so between substrate-sensitivity and grain-sensitivity the theory has enough free dials to spit out strange verdicts, like some boring grid of logic gates apparently scoring a higher phi than an actual brain (this is the point where I couldn't help thinking of renormalization group flows, though I kept that to myself). Helen conceded the no-behavioral-test problem isn't special to IIT -- that's just the hard problem generally -- and admitted the grain issue is a live worry, but said it isn't a free dial: there's an exclusion rule that always picks out whichever candidate system and grain gives the maximum phi, so it's a maximization principle, not an arbitrary choice, even if you can still ask whether that maximization move is itself well-motivated.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's actually right at each point, not just call an overall winner? A quick right / partly-right / wrong tag for each move before you explain would be great, old habit from reviewing grant proposals. Also flag anything you think both of them are just missing entirely. I can follow the math side fine, its the philosophical bits -- falsifiability, what "substrate" is supposed to mean here, whether "exclusion" actually tames the arbitrariness -- that I'm shakier on, so please don't assume I'm already sold on either of them.
