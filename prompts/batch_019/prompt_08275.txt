In a philosophy-of-mathematics reading group I'm part of, we recently spent an evening on the long-running dispute over the Axiom of Choice (AC). Defenders point out that giving it up would cost us large, load-bearing parts of ordinary mathematics; skeptics point to its stranger consequences as reasons to distrust it, or at least to make do with weaker choice principles wherever possible. Nobody could agree on how to weigh these considerations against each other — whether, say, the weirdness of the Banach–Tarski paradox is really a worse bullet to bite than some of the things that turn out to be true if you reject choice altogether.

I'd like help thinking this through more systematically. Below are eight set-theoretic facts, each either a known consequence (or equivalent) of full AC, or a known pathology that becomes possible once choice is dropped entirely, including the weaker countable- and dependent-choice principles. For each one, judge how much genuine damage it does to the naive, pre-theoretic picture of sets and functions — that is, how strong a bullet someone must bite to accept it as true of the mathematical universe — regardless of whether the source of the trouble is AC or its negation. Feel free to factor in that some of these follow from principles considerably weaker than full AC, which arguably changes how much blame AC itself deserves for them.

- Well-ordering: every set, including the reals, can be well-ordered, though no one has ever exhibited an explicit well-ordering of R.
- Banach–Tarski: a solid ball can be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled, using only rotations and translations, into two solid balls each congruent to the original.
- Vitali sets: there exist subsets of R to which no translation-invariant, countably additive notion of length can consistently be assigned.
- Hamel bases: every vector space — including R regarded as a vector space over Q — has a basis, though no one has ever written one down.
- Ultrafilters: every filter on a set extends to an ultrafilter, so there are ways of consistently labeling every subset of N as "large" or "small" that no explicit rule could capture (this one follows from something strictly weaker than full AC).
- Tychonoff's theorem: an arbitrary product of compact spaces, indexed by a set of any size, is again compact.
- Cardinal comparability: for any two sets whatsoever, one can always be injected into the other.
- Choice-failure pathologies: with no choice principle at all, it becomes consistent that some infinite sets can't be split into two infinite pieces, that some infinite sets can't be put in bijection with any proper subset of themselves, and that the real line can be written as a countable union of countable sets.
