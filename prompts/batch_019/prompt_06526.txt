I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, and this term I've been TAing a graduate seminar cross-listed with the economics department's game theory sequence; we recently spent a week on Aumann's "Agreeing to Disagree" and the common prior assumption it rests on. After one session, two of the economics students in the seminar -- not me -- kept arguing about it over coffee, and I've been turning their exchange over ever since without being able to settle it myself.

(1) Elif opened by arguing that the theorem shows persistent disagreement between honest, rational people who share a common prior is strictly impossible once their posteriors are common knowledge, so when two well-informed forecasters still disagree after fully laying out their reasoning to one another, the natural diagnosis is that common knowledge of the posteriors must have failed, since people rarely report their true credences with complete candor. (2) Marcus objected that this move makes the common prior assumption unfalsifiable: any real disagreement can always be blamed on some breakdown in common knowledge rather than on the assumption itself, so the theorem never has to answer to the data. He suggested the far more natural diagnosis is that the forecasters simply never shared a common prior to begin with -- their starting points were shaped by different training and different formative cases, and there is nothing irrational about that. (3) Elif replied that this is exactly what the Harsanyi doctrine rules out: two agents with access to the same information should assign the same prior, because a prior is supposed to encode only the logical structure of the decision problem, not personal biography, and if training and formative experience are doing real work in fixing someone's prior, that just shows those experiences are themselves evidence, which a fully rational agent should already have folded into a common starting point rather than treated as a permanent private baseline. (4) Marcus answered that this proves far too much: it would convict every reasonable disagreement that turns on how to frame ambiguous evidence, or which reference class applies, of irrationality, and not only forecasting disputes -- and that Elif's reply quietly converts "common prior" from a modeling idealization into the substantive claim that any two careful reasoners must have started out identical, which is exactly the premise on the table and not something she is entitled to assume while defending it.

Could you work through moves (1) through (4) and say, for each one, who has the better of the argument and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also value your view on whether the common prior assumption is best understood as a factual claim about how ideally rational agents relate to shared evidence, a pure modeling convenience, or something else again, and on how that reading bears on who comes out ahead in this exchange.
