I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and AI, and this week we went through Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" pretty closely -- not just the imitation game setup that everyone already knows, but the whole run of objections in section 6: the theological one, "heads in the sand," the mathematical objection, the argument from consciousness, the various-disabilities one, Lady Lovelace, continuity of the nervous system, informality of behaviour, and then, last on the list almost as an afterthought, the argument from extra-sensory perception. One of my students basically laughed out loud at that last one -- Turing seems to just grant that the statistical evidence for telepathy (from card-guessing experiments) is overwhelming, and then patches the imitation game by putting everyone in what he calls a "telepathy-proof room." I didnt have a great answer for why a paper this careful spends real estate on that, and skimming a couple of standard commentaries afterward, most of them just note the section exists and move on rather than actually engaging with it.

So here's what I'd like help thinking through properly, rather than the usual "Turing was a product of his time" hand-wave: first, why he treats ESP as a threat serious enough to need a structural fix to the game, rather than something to wave away the way he does with the mathematical or Lovelace objections; second, whether the telepathy-proof room actually solves the problem given how he's set the game up, or whether it just relocates the worry -- walls presumably don't stop telepathy if telepathy is real, and he doesn't say much about precognition or psychokinesis, which seem like they'd survive physical shielding in a way ordinary signals wouldn't; and third, the bigger issue I think this exposes -- that once "intelligence" has been operationalized purely as indistinguishability under interrogation, any reliably exploitable side-channel, paranormal or not, becomes exactly as threatening to the test as ESP is, which seems to say something uncomfortable about the whole operationalizing move. Is that last worry a genuine problem with Turing's proposal, or am I over-reading a couple of paragraphs he clearly tossed off in passing?
