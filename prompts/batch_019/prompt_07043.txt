So a friend of mine (call her Priya, not her real name) has been building this online-first intentional-community project for about two years now — basically a network-state-lite thing, pretty openly inspired by Balaji's book and by stuff like Prospera and Zuzalu, except way smaller and less crypto-brained than most of that scene. About 35 of us have been the "core" community online for a while, and this coming weekend we're all flying out to a rented lake house to actually ratify the founding charter in person: bylaws, membership rules, a governance structure, the whole deal, with the eventual (very long-shot) goal of one day leasing land and running an actual settlement.

I'm one of the 35, and because I'm the resident LessWrong-poisoned blogger type, I got asked to give the toast at Friday night's dinner, before Saturday's actual signing session. Here's what's been bugging me: the charter has a clause about how future members earn voting rights (something like a year of probation plus a 2/3 vote of existing members), and that clause, like every other clause, is being decided entirely by the 35 of us who happen to already be in the room — for people who by definition don't get a say in the rule that will govern their own admission. It's exactly that old "boundary problem" thing from democratic theory — I think Whelan coined the term and Goodin has the paper everyone cites on the regress you hit if you try to use a democratic vote to decide who's even in the demos allowed to vote. A couple of the founders will absolutely clock this if I paper over it (one guy's masters thesis was literally on the Abizadeh/Miller fight about border coercion), but most of the room just wants a warm, excited toast about the cool thing we're building, not a fifteen-minute seminar on regress problems.

Can you write me that toast? Four or five minutes, dinner-toast register, not a lecture. The one hard constraint is honesty: it can't pretend we've found some clever procedural fix for the regress, because we haven't, and the theory crowd will clock a cheap out immediately — but it also can't just sit there being bleak about it two nights before we all actually sign the thing. If you can find one honest joke or image that lands for both the philosophy nerds and the people who just want to eat dinner and feel good about Saturday, even better. There's also a further wrinkle about the eventual workers and neighbors who'd be subject to the place's rules without ever getting a vote, if this ever gets big enough to matter — gesture at it if it fits naturally, but don't let it take over the toast.
