My younger sister was hospitalized for a manic episode two months ago, and as part of her discharge planning she and her psychiatrist are now drafting a psychiatric advance directive. Almost in passing, the psychiatrist mentioned that the strongest version of such a document, one in which she would pre-authorize hospitalization and medication even if she objects at the time and is judged to retain some decision-making capacity, is sometimes called a "Ulysses contract," after the episode in the Odyssey in which Odysseus has his crew bind him to the mast and orders them in advance to disregard whatever he commands once he hears the Sirens' song. That offhand remark sent me down a research rabbit hole, where I discovered that political philosophers invoke the very same myth, and much of the same vocabulary of precommitment, to describe how constitutions are supposed to work: a founding generation binds its own future majorities through entrenched clauses and supermajority amendment rules, in something like the way Odysseus's arrangement binds his future, Siren-hearing self. I even came across the old Jefferson-Madison exchange about whether one generation has any standing to bind the next at all.

What I cannot get past is that the same underlying problem seems to appear in both cases, and nothing I have read actually resolves it. The whole justification for tying Odysseus to the mast is that his future, Siren-hearing self is not to be trusted, so that self's judgment in the moment counts as corrupted rather than as a legitimate change of mind. But nothing about the future moment itself seems to announce which of those two descriptions applies to it. My sister, reasonably enough, is uneasy about granting away in advance the authority of whatever self she will be during her next crisis, on the grounds that her psychiatrist and I are more likely to know her own good in that moment than she will be herself. This looks to me like exactly the question constitutional theorists face when they ask why a founding generation's judgment should bind ours, given that we, too, might simply be revising our considered views rather than succumbing to some momentary passion. Is there a principled way that philosophers working on precommitment (Elster's name keeps coming up, though I have not worked through much of his writing) actually distinguish "this future self is being overridden by passion or illness and may be justifiably bound" from "this future self has legitimately changed its mind and should not be bound at all"? Or is that distinction ultimately question-begging, in a way that should make me warier of Ulysses contracts, personal or constitutional alike, than I currently am?
