A friend from my rationalist meetup has been on a Molinism kick lately, which sent me down enough of a rabbit hole that I've drafted a short piece connecting it to something I picked up years ago from decision-theory blogging; I'd appreciate your comments on it before I inflict it on anyone else. Quick primer on the theology, for context: Luis de Molina divided God's knowledge into three logical moments — natural knowledge of all necessary truths and bare possibilities; middle knowledge (scientia media) of every "counterfactual of creaturely freedom," i.e., what any possible person would freely do in any possible circumstance; and free knowledge of the actual world, available only after God freely chooses which possibility to actualize. Middle knowledge is the load-bearing piece: it's supposed to let God providentially arrange history by choosing which circumstances to create, while the creatures placed in them remain genuinely libertarian-free. The classic objection, from Robert Adams onward, is the grounding objection: since the creature doesn't yet exist and the choice is free in the strong sense of not being fixed by antecedent circumstances or character, nothing seems available to make a claim like "Ann would take the apple rather than leave it" true rather than false or neither.

What struck me is how closely this echoes a complaint leveled at functional decision theory and its relatives like updateless decision theory: the claim that there's a determinate fact about what an agent's decision algorithm would output on inputs it never actually receives — the "logical counterfactuals" that let FDT one-box on Newcomb's problem, pay up in counterfactual mugging, and handle trickier variants like transparent Newcomb. Both cases want a fact of the matter about what an agent would do off the actual path — a free choice in one case, a fixed-but-uncomputed algorithmic output in the other — and both get accused of lacking any truthmaker for it. My draft's thesis is that the analogy is instructive mainly because it breaks down: an algorithm's counterfactual output can be grounded in mathematical structure even when no one can compute it — a Turing machine's behavior on a given input is fixed whether or not the halting problem lets us find out — whereas libertarian freedom is supposed to rule out exactly that sort of prior determination. That would make the Molinist's problem strictly worse than the decision theorist's rather than merely its cousin, except that Molina himself already tried to head this off with the idea of "supercomprehension": God knows each possible creature's nature so thoroughly that the counterfactual is settled without being entailed by it. I can't decide if that move is a real answer or just relocates the mystery one level down.

I'd appreciate three things from you: first, whether this comparison is doing real philosophical work or mostly trades on an ambiguity in the word "counterfactual"; second, whether my central claim — that FDT-style counterfactuals are better-grounded because they're at least in-principle mathematical facts — actually survives the supercomprehension-style rejoinder, or whether I'm helping myself to a disanalogy I haven't earned; and third, whether this connection is already well-trodden ground in the philosophy-of-religion or decision-theory literature that I ought to read before posting this anywhere.
