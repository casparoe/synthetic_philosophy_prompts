I am a novelist currently researching a historical novel set in the 1640s. One thread follows a fictionalized natural philosopher, closely modeled on Descartes, who dissects animals in his cellar to demonstrate that they are mere automata, much to the dismay of his housekeeper and her pet magpie. Wanting the philosophy in the book to hold up rather than lapse into caricature, I went back to Descartes' actual argument in Part V of the Discourse on the Method, and it turns out to be more precise than the usual "animals are just clockwork" summary. He proposes two tests. A machine, he concedes, could be built to cry out when touched in a certain spot, or to ask a fixed question when a lever is pressed, but no arrangement of gears, however intricate, could produce different, appropriate replies to whatever is said to it on the spot, the way even a dull-witted person manages without effort; and whereas each organ or mechanism is shaped for one narrow class of task, reason is a single all-purpose instrument that can improvise suitably in any situation, which is why an animal that outdoes us at one thing, the way a clock outdoes us at keeping time, invariably fails at nearly everything else. Passing both tests together would, for Descartes, be decisive evidence of a rational soul; it is because every animal fails them both, and not merely because animals behave predictably, that he feels entitled to treat every beast as unthinking mechanism.

This is the argument I am trying to dramatize faithfully, and it has left me stuck on a scene. Suppose I bring my Descartes-figure forward into the present and sit him down in front of an ordinary chatbot built on a large language model: it discusses grief, unpacks a stranger's pun, and drafts a passable sonnet on a subject invented on the spot, moving between all three without any fixed script. That looks very close to exactly the unbounded, situation-independent performance he insisted no machine could ever manage. Does his own test now oblige him to grant the chatbot a rational soul, with whatever follows from that? Or can he consistently withhold it anyway, on grounds that are not simply special pleading for the human case -- and if he can, what happens to the argument that let him deny minds, and moral standing, to animals in the first place? I would like whatever he says in this scene to reflect how Descartes would genuinely have reasoned, tensions included, rather than a strawman built only to be knocked down, so I would welcome a careful, faithful reconstruction of how this confrontation would go, and what it implies for the coherence of his position on animals.
