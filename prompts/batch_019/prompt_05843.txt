Over the past year my reporting has taken me from a conference on central bank digital currencies, to a state legislature debating how race should be recorded on driver's licenses, to a panel on the legal definition of sex and gender in sport. In every one of these very different rooms, I heard some version of the same sentence: "well, of course, it's a social construct." The phrase always seemed to function as a conversation-ender rather than a conversation-starter, and it has started to bother me that it gets used to cover money, race, and gender as though these were obviously the same kind of phenomenon. When I pushed back on this in a recent interview, the philosopher I was talking to rattled off several names and distinctions I only half-remembered from an undergraduate course, and I did not want to admit on the record how much of it I had forgotten.

Could you write me an essay that actually engages with that material, rather than just restating the slogan? I am specifically interested in how three well-known frameworks bear on the question of whether race, gender, and money are "socially constructed" in the same sense: Ian Hacking's distinction between interactive and indifferent kinds, and his notion of looping effects; John Searle's account of institutional facts and status functions, built around the formula "X counts as Y in context C," together with his claim that such facts are ontologically subjective yet epistemically objective; and Sally Haslanger's ameliorative analyses of race and gender, which define them in terms of social position tied to perceived bodily features. Please use these tools to work out what, if anything, genuinely unifies the three cases, and where the analogy might break down -- for instance, whether each is "interactive" in Hacking's sense, how explicit and deliberately designed the relevant constitutive rule is, and what is normatively at stake in "unmasking" each construction.

Please do not let the essay end on a diplomatic shrug. I would like an actual verdict: either that "social construction" names one genuine, unified phenomenon of which race, gender, and money are all instances, or that the label is being stretched to cover at least two importantly different things -- in which case, tell me exactly where the fault line runs and why it matters, both philosophically and for how carefully we should reason about each case.
