Ok this is a total tangent from my actual dissertation work (quantum information / foundations of QM) but I've been auditing a philosophy of language seminar this term just because the reading list looked fun, and we spent basically two whole sessions on Aristotle's sea battle argument from De Interpretatione. The prof kept treating it like this huge unresolved puzzle: if a statement like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true or false today, that seems to commit you to fatalism, since the future would already be "fixed" in some sense. And apparently everyone from Diodorus Cronus (whose Master Argument is basically the same worry dressed up in modal terms) to Ockham (who tried to distinguish genuinely necessary facts from ones that just happen to be about the future) to Łukasiewicz (who invented a whole three valued logic just to give future contingents a third truth-value) got tangled up trying to deal with this.

Honestly though, isnt this just a non-problem once you bring in actual physics? We know from QM that the universe has real objective chance in it - nothing about tomorrow is fully fixed by todays state of things plus the laws of nature, there's genuine indeterminacy at the fundamental level (Born rule and all that). So "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" just doesn't have a determinate truth value right now, because the physical facts that would make it true or false haven't happened yet. Once the universe itself is indeterministic, the whole logical fatalism worry seems to just dissolve - like Aristotle and the medievals were doing 2000+ years of very careful work on a problem that 20th century physics quietly made moot. I actually said something like this in seminar and got a lot of "that's a different kind of problem" hand-waving from the philosophy people, which felt more like defensiveness than an actual counterargument. Am I missing something real here, or is this a case of a field not wanting to let go of a puzzle after it's basically been resolved out from under it?
