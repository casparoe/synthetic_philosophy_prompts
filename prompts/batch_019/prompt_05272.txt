I cover the intersection of technology and philosophy for a science-and-ideas magazine, and for the past couple of years I've also sat on the small internal standards committee that has to sign off before we run anything touching on material that could cause real psychological harm to readers -- the same body that checks our self-harm and extremism coverage against the usual professional reporting guidelines. This week the case in front of us is Roko's basilisk. A staff writer pitched a feature after a chatbot brought the idea up, unprompted, in a screenshot that briefly went viral, and the draft she's turned in is unusually rigorous: rather than just gesturing at "creepy AI forum drama," it actually walks a lay reader through the acausal-blackmail reasoning -- the idea that a sufficiently advanced future AI, reasoning in the style of what's called timeless decision theory, would have reason to retroactively punish anyone who understood the argument and didn't help bring the AI into being sooner.

That rigor is exactly what has split the committee. Half of us think that explaining the logic clearly enough for a careful reader to actually run it themselves -- and to notice, if they think about it for more than a minute, that the same reasoning generates any number of rival future agents with opposite demands -- comes uncomfortably close to reproducing the original "information hazard" that led LessWrong's founder to delete the post and forbid discussion of it for years, especially given that some people who took the argument seriously at the time reported real and lingering anxiety. The other half think a hedged or coy treatment would misrepresent a genuinely interesting episode in the history of internet rationalism, would be condescending to our readers, and would repeat the founder's own mistake, since the ban generated far more attention and distress than the original post would have on its own. A few of us also can't agree on something more basic underneath all this: whether the argument is actually decent decision theory that we're right to be cautious about, or a scary-sounding fallacy that doesn't survive scrutiny -- and how much that should even matter to a publication decision, as opposed to just the likely psychological effect on readers.

I need to circulate the committee's decision memo before Friday's call. It should recommend one of three things -- run the piece substantially as written, hold it for a specific, named set of revisions, or kill it -- attach a concrete deadline, and give our writer reasons she can actually act on rather than a bare verdict. Whichever way the memo comes down, I'd like it to take the losing side's argument seriously on its own terms, rather than dismissing it as either censoriousness or edginess for its own sake.
