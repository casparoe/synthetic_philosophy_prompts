So I help run a tiny community fund with four other volunteers - we give out small grants (like $1500 to $8000) to independent writers and researchers doing AI-safety-adjacent work. None of us have any real academic background in this, we just got sucked into the whole area from reading blogs and forums for a few years and ended up being the people willing to do the unpaid admin work. Anyway, we got an application from a guy asking for $6000 to spend a few months writing a long piece arguing that the "coherence arguments" a lot of our own outreach materials lean on are way shakier than we present them. The argument he's going after is the one where: if an agent's preferences aren't complete and transitive (basically, if they can't all be boiled down to maximizing one consistent utility function), then a clever enough trader can "money-pump" it - exploit gaps or cycles in what it prefers to drain resources from it forever - and so any agent smart and self-improving enough will eventually get forced by this pressure into acting like an expected-utility maximizer with stable goals. Thats basically the reasoning a lot of the instrumental convergence stuff rests on - why people expect advanced AI to resist shutdown, hoard resources, keep its goals from being changed, etc. His counter-argument is that an agent can have genuinely incomplete preferences (not just indifference - actual gaps) and still dodge the money pump, as long as it follows a simple policy of not trading away from where it currently stands when there's no preference either way, and that this matters because it would mean you could maybe deliberately build an AI with a gap in its preferences around "being shut down vs. not," without reality (or training) eventually ironing that gap back out of it for being "irrational."

The committee's split down the middle on this. Two of us think this is exactly the kind of "check whether our own foundational argument actually works" piece a fund like ours should exist to pay for - if he's right it's a big deal, and if he's wrong, having a careful rebuttal out there is still useful. The other two think the coherence argument is basically sound as usually stated, that he's leaning on a technicality that wouldn't survive contact with a real training process, and that funding someone to publicly poke holes in the exact argument we use to explain AI risk to newcomers is just going to muddy our own messaging and get quote-mined by people who think the whole field is overblown. We already told the guy we'd get back to him by the end of the month, and I got voted into writing up the actual decision since I read his draft outline most closely.

Could you draft the short memo I'd send to the other four - approve, reject, or approve with conditions - that actually argues the case on the merits of whether incomplete preferences really do let an agent dodge the money-pump argument for coherence, rather than just settling it on fund politics? Whatever you decide, I need it to take the strongest form of the losing side seriously, since I'll have to defend it to two people who won't be happy either way.
