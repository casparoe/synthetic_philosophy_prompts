I want him to have this habit of quoting bits of Aristotle to himself while he's lowballing people on guitars and wedding rings, half as a coping mechanism and half because he genuinely can't turn the old training off, and I landed on the "justice in exchange" chapter of the Nicomachean Ethics (Book V, ch. 5) because its literally about how you figure out what one thing is worth in terms of another — felt too on the nose not to use. Problem is I've now read the actual passage about five times trying to understand the argument well enough that Marek's internal monologue doesn't just sound like a guy who skimmed a summary, and I keep losing the thread of what Aristotle's actually claiming, as opposed to what I vaguely remember being told he claims.

Here's the chunk, in Ross's translation, picking up right after he's said a doctor and a farmer, being unequal, have to be "equated" for exchange to happen:

"For it is not two doctors that associate for exchange, but a doctor and a farmer, or in general people who are different and unequal; but these must be equated. This is why all things that are exchanged must be somehow comparable. It is for this end that money has been introduced, and it becomes in a sense an intermediate; for it measures all things, and therefore the excess and the defect—how many shoes are equal to a house or to a given amount of food. The number of shoes exchanged for a house (or for a given amount of food) must therefore correspond to the ratio of builder to shoemaker. For if this be not so, there will be no exchange and no intercourse. And this proportion will not be effected unless the goods are somehow equal. All goods must therefore be measured by some one thing, as we said before. Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together (for if men did not need one another's goods at all, or did not need them equally, there would be either no exchange or not the same exchange); but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma)—because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless. There will, then, be reciprocity when the terms have been equated so that as farmer is to shoemaker, the amount of the shoemaker's work is to that of the farmer's work for which it exchanges... That demand holds things together as a single unit is shown by the fact that when men do not need one another... they do not exchange, as we do when some one wants what one has oneself, e.g. when people permit the exportation of corn in exchange for wine. This equation therefore must be established. And for the future exchange—that if we do not need a thing now we shall have it if ever we do need it—money is as it were our surety; for it must be possible for us to get what we want by bringing the money. Now the same thing happens to money itself as to goods—it is not always worth the same; yet it tends to be steadier... Money, then, acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them; for neither would there have been association if there were not exchange, nor exchange if there were not equality, nor equality if there were not commensurability. Now in truth it is impossible that things differing so much should become commensurate, but with reference to demand they may become so sufficiently. There must, then, be a unit, and that fixed by agreement (for which reason it is called money); for it is this that makes all things commensurate, since all things are measured by money. Let A be a house, B ten minae, C a bed. A is half of B, if the house is worth five minae or equal to them; the bed, C, is a tenth of B; it is plain, then, how many beds are equal to a house, viz. five."

The thing I can't shake is that Aristotle seems to want it both ways about what's actually doing the commensurating. First "demand" (chreia) is "in truth" the real unit holding everyone together, and money is only its conventional stand-in, useful mainly because its steadier than raw need. But then the worked example at the very end just runs everything straight through minae, like need was never in the room to begin with — a house is worth five beds because that's what the numbers say, full stop, no reference back to anybody's need for anything. So is there an actual coherent claim buried in there about how a social/psychological fact like need is supposed to generate a determinate ratio like "1 house = 5 beds," or does Aristotle basically paper over a gap between an ethical story (money as placeholder for mutual need, all in service of the proportionate reciprocity that holds the polis together) and a bare pricing story, and just hope nobody clocks the seam? Relatedly, what work is the "surety" language even doing — is money standing in for my present need, or for some future need I don't have yet, and does that distinction matter for the argument? I don't need the textbook-jacket version ("Aristotle thought money represents need"), I've already got that much. I'd like someone to actually walk the logic of that paragraph with me line by line and tell me where it does or doesn't hold together, because right now I genuinely can't tell whether I'm missing a move or the passage is just this patched-together.
