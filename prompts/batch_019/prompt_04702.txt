For the past year or so I have been doing calibration training and amateur forecasting — assigning explicit probabilities to everyday claims and then scoring myself against the outcomes — and this hobby has pulled me into a corner of the philosophy of probability I never expected to end up in. I keep running into the claim that, quite apart from Dutch book arguments about being exploited by a clever bookmaker, simply having credences that violate the probability axioms (say, my credence that it will rain tomorrow and my credence that it will not do not sum to 1) already guarantees that some rival, coherent assignment of credences would have scored better than mine in every possible outcome, provided accuracy is graded with a "strictly proper" scoring rule, such as the Brier score used in forecasting competitions. What I cannot get past is how mere accuracy — with no bookmaker and no one to exploit — could force this conclusion on its own. Every explanation I have found either simply states the result or dives into a full convexity proof I cannot follow. Could you walk me through the gut-level version: why does caring only about closeness to the truth already commit you to obeying the probability calculus, and why does the scoring rule specifically have to be strictly proper, rather than merely proper, for the argument to go through?
