I sit on the ethics subcommittee that sets our research biobank's policy on returning genetic findings to participants. We currently disclose a finding only if it is well-validated and medically actionable; one member wants to extend disclosure to well-validated findings for serious, untreatable conditions -- Huntington's disease is the clearest case, since the variant is highly predictive and no treatment yet delays onset or progression -- arguing that since a participant can always decline to read what we send, offering the information can never leave a rational participant worse off in expectation than withholding it. Others worry this reasoning proves too much and would commit us to disclosing virtually anything we ever learn about anyone. Draft the memo I will circulate before our next meeting: it should recommend a specific disclosure policy for well-validated, non-actionable findings -- including what advance choice, if any, participants get, and what happens by default if they haven't chosen -- and it should directly assess whether the "they can just ignore it" argument actually supports the conclusion its proponents draw from it, rather than simply accepting or dismissing it.
