Last night a close friend of mine—who spends her summers doing fieldwork on cichlid species flocks in the East African rift lakes—and I ended up in a long, only half-resolved argument about the species problem. Her position, roughly, was that the whole debate is overblown: working biologists reach for whichever species concept is locally tractable (morphology in a museum drawer, reproductive compatibility in a common-garden experiment, concordant gene trees once sequencing is cheap enough), and demanding a single "correct" concept is a bit like demanding a single correct map projection. I pushed back, arguing that if the major species concepts don't even converge in principle on the same boundaries for the same organisms, that is itself evidence that some of them are simply worse attempts at carving nature at its joints than others—even granting that none is perfect and that some pluralism is probably unavoidable. We ended up trying to rank the concepts we could half-remember from an old philosophy of biology seminar, but neither of us could produce anything we were willing to defend cold sober.
