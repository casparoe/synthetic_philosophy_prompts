Having followed Overcoming Bias and the LessWrong Sequences for many years, I keep noticing that rationalist writing treats two norms about anticipating one's own future beliefs as more or less interchangeable, when I suspect they are importantly different: Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle and Yudkowsky's Conservation of Expected Evidence.

Van Fraassen's principle, from "Belief and the Will," holds that one's present credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that one's credence in A at some later time will be r, must itself equal r: one is obligated to defer to one's future opinion as though it were expert testimony, regardless of the process that will produce it. Conservation of Expected Evidence holds that one's expected posterior on a hypothesis must equal one's current prior, and Yudkowsky deploys it chiefly against motivated reasoning: if one already knows which way a forthcoming observation will move one's belief, one should move there immediately rather than waiting.

Please compare these two principles in some depth. Is Conservation of Expected Evidence best understood as simply a special case of Reflection, restricted to future credences that arise from Bayesian conditionalization on a known evidence partition, so that the most troublesome feature of Reflection -- that it is meant to hold for every individual value of r, and for belief changes that need not be evidential at all -- never has occasion to arise? And is that missing generality the genuinely interesting part of Van Fraassen's principle, or precisely the part that exposes it to the classic brainwashing- and memory-loss-style counterexamples? I would value your honest judgment as to which principle is doing more substantive epistemic work, and whether Yudkowsky's version quietly smuggles in any of Reflection's difficulties by another route.
