So this is a live situation at my actual job, not a hypothetical, and I can't stop reading it through an Ian-Hacking-looping-effects lens ever since an old philosophy-major friend pointed that out over drinks (fair warning, I only minored in phil and haven't touched Hacking since undergrad, so my gloss on him might be off — feel free to correct me rather than just running with it). I do data/outcomes work for a mid-size hospice and palliative care nonprofit, and I've somehow ended up on the small clinical policy committee that decides what goes into our standard intake and follow-up screeners for bereaved family members. Since Prolonged Grief Disorder got its own official DSM-5-TR diagnosis back in 2022 (roughly: intense, impairing grief that's still going strong after 12 months for adults, well beyond what you'd expect given the person's culture and circumstances), our bereavement team wants to add a short PGD screener to every single intake for family members of patients who die in our care, so cases get flagged and coded early instead of surfacing ad-hoc, six or nine months later, when a counselor happens to notice something's off. The clinicians pushing this call it straightforward good triage: earlier flag, earlier access to the grief-specific treatment that actually has decent trial evidence behind it. But a few of us keep snagging on the worry that once grief has a checkbox, a diagnostic code, and a 12-month deadline attached to it, we're not just detecting a pre-existing condition anymore, we're partly manufacturing the thing we're measuring — people start anchoring on the timeline, narrating their own mourning through "am I past the point where this counts as pathological," and the category ends up producing some of the very cases that later get cited as proof the category was needed. Part of me wants to call this a Goodhart's law problem, but my friend insists looping effects are a genuinely different mechanism than a metric getting gamed, and honestly im not sure I can articulate the difference well enough to defend it in a room full of clinicians who dont read the same blogs I do.

I need to bring the committee something more substantive than a bad feeling before we vote next Thursday, so could you draft the actual memo? I want a real bottom line — adopt the universal screener as proposed, scrap it, or adopt some modified version (narrower population, opt-in instead of universal, different framing, paired with specific safeguards, whatever you think is defensible) — with a concrete timeframe attached rather than a "more research is needed" punt. It should engage the looping-effects worry specifically enough that the clinicians who don't buy it would recognize their actual position in how you've represented it, not a strawman about labels being bad, but it also needs to take the strongest version of the pro-screening case seriously: earlier treatment access, and the argument that naming a real, common experience might reduce stigma rather than manufacture pathology.
