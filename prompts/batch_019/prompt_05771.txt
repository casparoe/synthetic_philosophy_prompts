I teach a graduate seminar on epistemology, and after last week's session on the norms that govern assertion, two of my students stayed behind arguing about it well after the seminar had formally ended. One of them recounted the whole exchange to me the next day, and I confess I still haven't been able to settle it to my own satisfaction. The dispute was triggered by a case involving their friend Theo, who at dinner one night had announced, with total confidence, that a novelist the group had been discussing had died shortly before her latest novel came out. Theo had this from a single tweet he never bothered to check further, and, as it happened, the tweet was true.

(1) Nora maintained that Theo had done nothing wrong whatsoever: what he said was true, and truth is the only thing that could reasonably be demanded of an assertion -- that is simply what it is to assert something honestly. (2) Paul objected that this can't be right: had the tweet instead been false, and had Theo repeated it with exactly the same confidence, Nora would surely say something had gone wrong -- yet Theo's epistemic position, and everything he actually did, would have been identical in both cases, so whatever is being assessed can't turn on a fact, the tweet's truth, that simply wasn't available to Theo at the moment he spoke. (3) Nora granted the case but didn't fold, only retreated: very well, add that the speaker must also believe what he asserts, as Theo did -- once you have true, sincerely held content, there is nothing further that could reasonably be asked of an assertion. (4) Paul replied that this still isn't enough, offering a variant: suppose Theo had gotten the very same tweet from an account he already half-suspected of fabricating celebrity-death hoaxes for engagement, believed it anyway mainly because he relished being the one to break the news, and it still happened to be true -- most of us, Paul said, would still think Theo had no business asserting it, which shows the relevant standard has to be considerably more demanding than true, sincere belief, something in the neighborhood of knowledge. (5) Nora answered that "knowledge" is a strange thing to write into an everyday norm, since almost nothing anyone confidently asserts at dinner would survive a sufficiently determined skeptical challenge, and that all we should really ask of a speaker is that the belief be reasonable and well-justified, whether or not it amounts to knowledge strictly speaking.

Neither of them would budge, and I would like a considered outside verdict.
