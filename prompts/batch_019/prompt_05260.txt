So this is a random one that came out of a coffee chat with a colleague in the math department — I'm redoing the foundations segment of my philosophy of mathematics seminar this term, and I want to stop letting students get away with treating Zorn's Lemma as some neutral bit of mathematical plumbing that only occasionally spits out something embarrassing like Banach-Tarski. Every year it's the same pattern: they remember "oh, Zorn's Lemma gives you Banach-Tarski, spooky," and then completely forget that a huge chunk of the math they rely on every week — existence of bases, maximal ideals, algebraic closures, you name it — is quietly proved by the exact same non-constructive move, and their on the hook for all of it at once if they accept the lemma.

So this year I want them to build an actual tier list of these consequences, ranked by how philosophically troubling / hard-to-stomach each one is if you have genuinely constructivist or finitist sympathies — not "how useful is this result" but "how much should biting this particular bullet bother you." Here's the shortlist I was planning to hand out (there's some deliberate redundancy in there, since I want them arguing over whether two of these are really separate "costs" or the same cost wearing a different hat):

- Every vector space has a basis (a Hamel basis)
- Every field has an algebraic closure
- The Banach-Tarski paradox
- There exists a Lebesgue non-measurable set of reals
- Every commutative ring with unity has a maximal ideal
- Every filter on a set extends to an ultrafilter
- Tychonoff's theorem for arbitrary (not just finite) products of compact spaces
- The real numbers can be well-ordered
- There's a discontinuous, non-linear solution to f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y)
