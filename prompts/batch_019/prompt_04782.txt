I teach a senior seminar on personal identity, and I think I've come up with a clean way to frame Parfit's teletransporter case for tomorrow's lecture, but I want a sanity check before I try it out on 40 undergrads. Parfit's central claim is that what matters in survival is Relation R -- psychological continuity and connectedness, memories, intentions, personality and so on -- rather than strict numerical identity, which is why he thinks we should regard the person stepping out of the machine on Mars as just as good as ordinary survival, even though nothing physical actually made the trip.

Here's the extension I want to make: if Relation R is really the thing that matters, then ordinary death shouldn't bother us all that much either, since a persons memories, values, and characteristic quirks don't just disappear when they die -- they persist, just spread thinner, in the people who knew them, the students they taught, whatever they happened to write down. So dying just looks like a more diffuse version of what happens in the teleporter case: the same Relation R, distributed across a bunch of weaker successors instead of concentrated in one strong one, which by Parfit's own standard should still count as the very thing he says matters, just less concentrated. That feels like a genuinely satisfying, non-gimmicky payoff for the whole reductionist-view-of-persons unit -- basically turning Reasons and Persons into a rigorous argument that ordinary death is nothing to fear, not just a weird result about sci-fi machines. Does that framing actually hold up, or is there a wrinkle in it I'm not seeing?
