The question, worth 12 points, was: "(a) [5 pts] State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem precisely, listing every condition needed for the impossibility conclusion to hold. (b) [3 pts] In two to three sentences, explain how the theorem relates to Arrow's impossibility theorem. (c) [4 pts] Give one voting rule (real or hypothetical) that is strategy-proof, and identify exactly which condition from part (a) it violates in order to escape the impossibility result."

Here is the submission, copied verbatim:

(a) "The theorem states that any social choice function which (i) is defined for every logically possible combination of voters' strict preference rankings and always outputs a single winner, and (ii) is such that at least three voters' ballots can actually affect which alternative wins, must be either a dictatorship — some one voter's top choice is always the winner, regardless of what anyone else reports — or manipulable, meaning that in some situation some voter could secure a better outcome for themselves by misreporting their true ranking rather than reporting it honestly."

(b) "This result is closely tied to Arrow's impossibility theorem, and can really be viewed as a relabeling of it: Arrow showed that no rule aggregating individual rankings into a social ranking can simultaneously satisfy the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. Gibbard and Satterthwaite's contribution was to notice that independence of irrelevant alternatives and strategy-proofness are, at bottom, the same requirement expressed in different vocabulary, so their result essentially falls out of Arrow's theorem for free once the vocabulary is translated."

(c) "Simple majority rule between exactly two candidates is strategy-proof: since there are only two options, no voter can ever gain by voting for the candidate they actually like less. This rule escapes the impossibility result because condition (ii) fails here — although the electorate can be arbitrarily large, there are never three or more candidates on the ballot, so the 'at least three' requirement from part (a) is not met."
