Rawls and Harsanyi both derive their favored principle from a choice made behind a veil of ignorance, yet they reach opposite conclusions: Rawls's maximin reasoning yields the difference principle, while Harsanyi's equiprobability model yields average utilitarianism. Compare these two derivations, focusing on Rawls's argument that the parties should reason via maximin rather than expected-utility maximization -- an argument that rests on the claim that no determinate probabilities can be assigned to occupying any given social position, on the limited value of further gains once a secure minimum is guaranteed, and on the "strains of commitment" involved in binding oneself to a principle one might be unable to live with if the worst outcome materializes. Does this argument succeed in blocking Harsanyi's route to average utilitarianism, or does it merely smuggle in a substantive assumption of risk-aversion that Harsanyi's impartial observer has no particular reason to share? Defend a verdict on which derivation from the veil of ignorance is more philosophically defensible, and indicate what, if anything, the disagreement reveals about the limits of the veil-of-ignorance device itself.
