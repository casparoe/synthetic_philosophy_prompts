So my grandpa passed away this spring and I ended up with all his old camera gear plus a shoebox of negatives he never even got around to printing. I've been teaching myself darkroom printing over the last couple months just so I could see what's actually on them, and it turns out most of it's boring vacation stuff but like a dozen shots are genuinely great, he had a really good eye. I posted a few in a photography discord and got alot more into this than I expected, which led to this whole two hour argument with my friend Mara, who's way more plugged into the fine-art-photography-collecting scene than me.

Her argument was basically: since I'm the one deciding exposure time and dodging/burning and picking the paper, the prints coming out of my darkroom are more mine than his — like, the image might be his, composition and all, but the physical print is a different thing, and that's not a minor point. She backed this up with the fact that apparently in actual photo auctions there's a whole hierarchy for this exact issue: a "vintage print" made by the photographer near the time they took the shot sells for way more than a "later print" made by that same photographer's own hands decades after, which in turn sells for more than a "posthumous" or estate print made by someone else after they've died — same negative every time in all three cases. So according to her, the market itself treats the printing (and even the timing of the printing) as doing a ton of the work in what makes a print "real," not just whoever pointed the camera.

I don't think these stop being grandpa's photos just because my hands were the ones in the developer tray, but I couldn't actually explain why the auction thing doesn't prove her point, especially since even prints made by the original photographer lose value once they're not "vintage" anymore, which seems to show its not simply about preserving the photographer's own artistic choices either. So who's actually right here — is there a real, principled line for whose photo this is, or is Mara basically right that this kind of "originality" comes down to the physical object and the moment it was made, more than to who's responsible for the creative decisions?
