So I've been reading through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed on my own time (picked up a beat up copy at a used bookstore, no class or anything backing me up here) and I got stuck on the section where he explains why God commanded animal sacrifices in the first place. As I understand it -- and I think this is around part 3, chapter 32 -- his answer is basically that the Israelites coming out of Egypt were so steeped in sacrificial worship as just "how you talk to god" that yanking it away overnight would've never worked, so instead the law kept the sacrifices but redirected them only to God and slowly hemmed them in (one temple, one altar, etc) with the real long-term goal being to wean everyone off sacrifice entirely and get them to prayer and pure knowledge of God instead. He even throws in a prophet quote (Jeremiah, I believe) to back up the idea that god never actually cared about the sacrifices themselves.

Then I went poking around in his other major work, the Mishneh Torah, specifically the part about kings and the messianic age, and he says pretty plainly that when the messiah shows up he's gonna rebuild the Temple and the sacrifices are coming back as part of restoring everything properly. And that just seems totally backwards to me? If the sacrifices were only ever a temporary crutch and the whole arc of religious history is supposed to bend toward MORE prayer and MORE pure intellectual worship and less of the primitive stuff, shouldn't the most perfect version of Judaism -- the messianic one -- be the version where sacrifice finally disappears for good, not the version where it comes roaring back? I mentioned this to a friend who knows way more about this stuff then me and he said apparently some medieval rabbi wrote a whole rebuttal of Maimonides over more or less this exact issue, but he couldn't really explain to me how (or if) Maimonides gets out of it.

I want you to actually make the case, as strong as you can, that this is a genuine and basically unresolved contradiction in Maimonides' thinking -- not just "well these are two different books written for different audiences," but a real argument for why the reasoning he gives in the Guide, followed to its logical end, commits him to thinking the messianic restoration of sacrifice would be a regression rather than the completion of the religious ideal he's describing. Feel free to use his own logic against him if that's what it takes, I just don't want a cop out answer that just says smart people have gone back and forth on this for centuries.
