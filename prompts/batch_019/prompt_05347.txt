Since my retirement from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading philosophy fairly systematically, if unsystematically chosen, and lately I have been working through the history of early analytic philosophy, particularly Frege's logicism. In the course of this I came across a fact that was new to me: that Frege kept a diary in 1924, the year before he died, which went unpublished for decades and turned out to be full of intensely nationalist, anti-parliamentary, and antisemitic sentiment, including remarks suggesting that Jews should be stripped of full political rights. From what I gather, this genuinely unsettled a number of Frege scholars, including, I believe, Michael Dummett, when it finally came to light, precisely because Frege is normally presented as the founder of a discipline, modern formal logic, whose whole appeal rests on ideals of objectivity and universality that are supposed to be indifferent to the particular psychology of whoever happens to be reasoning.

I have sketched an argument below about how to think about this, and would like your comments before I take it any further.

My claim is not the crude one that the diary discredits Frege's logic; that would plainly be a genetic fallacy, since the Begriffsschrift and the sense/reference distinction stand or fall on their own merits regardless of who devised them. My conjecture is subtler: that Frege's antisemitism and his lifelong anti-psychologism about logic are two expressions of one underlying temperament, rather than unrelated facts that happen to coexist in the same man. Throughout his career, Frege fought against anything that threatened to relativize a single objective order he took to bind everyone regardless of individual or communal perspective. In logic this meant an unrelenting polemic against psychologism and against any suggestion that the laws of thought could vary between individuals or peoples. In politics, I want to suggest, it took the form of hostility to Weimar pluralism and parliamentary compromise, and correspondingly to Jews, whom he seems to have regarded as exponents of a kind of rootless relativism at odds with a unified national order. On this reading, the same horror of a merely perspectival world that made Frege a great logician also helped make him a bad citizen.

I am aware that this sort of move is risky and might just be a tidy "just-so" story, built by projecting a resemblance between two quite different senses of "objectivity" — logical necessity and political authority — onto a single psychological source. So I would especially value your view on the following: whether the thesis has genuine philosophical content or trades only on that verbal resemblance; whether anything in Frege's actual logical writings supports or tells against it; and whether a more deflationary explanation — that he was simply an unremarkable product of his milieu — is more plausible. I am relying on secondary sources for the historical details, so please also flag anything I seem to have gotten wrong.
