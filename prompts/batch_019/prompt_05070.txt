Two people in my decision theory reading group started arguing about a puzzle from the problem set and wouldn't let it drop. The setup: flip a fair coin til it lands heads. If the first head lands on toss n, you win (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n/n dollars -- so +$2 if it's heads on flip 1, -$2 if it takes two flips, +$8/3 for three, -$4 for four, and so on, alternating sign and growing every time. Our professor called it the Pasadena paradox -- I think it's named that as an echo of the St. Petersburg paradox.

Mia's take: the expected value is just ln(2), about 69 cents. For toss n the probability is (1/2)^n, so probability times payout comes out to (-1)^(n+1)/n; add those up in order (n=1, 2, 3, ...) and that's what "expected value" means, end of story.

Jae's comeback: too fast. That sum only converges conditionally, not absolutely, and the Riemann rearrangement theorem says a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to add up to literally any number you want, or to diverge. Nothing in probability theory says you're obligated to sum "in toss order" instead of some other order, so ln(2) isn't privileged -- it's one answer among infinitely many, which really just means the game has no expected value at all.

Mia: summing in toss order isn't an arbitrary pick from some menu of equally good options, it's the order the flips actually happen in. Calling that "arbitrary" is ignoring the one ordering that's physically real.

Jae: doesn't matter, because you don't even need to reorder anything to see the problem. Split the outcomes into "first head on an odd toss" and "first head on an even toss." Each half, summed in plain ordinary toss order with zero funny business, already comes out infinite on its own -- one side +infinity, the other -infinity. So this isn't about reshuffling terms, it's that the gamble is built out of two infinite pieces that happen to almost cancel. That's a real defect in the gamble itself, not a dispute about bookkeeping.

They both walked away sure they'd won. Can you go through this move by move, say who's right at each step and about what exactly, and flag anything important you think they're both still missing?
