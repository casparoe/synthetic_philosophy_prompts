I've been reading a few old economics essays outside my usual software-engineering wheelhouse, and I recently spent an evening with John Maynard Keynes's 1930 piece "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." I went in expecting only the famous headline claim, that within a hundred years growth and compounding capital would have solved the "economic problem" and left everyone working something like fifteen hours a week, but what actually stuck with me was the back half of the essay, where Keynes gets almost anxious about whether people would know what to do with themselves once scarcity stopped forcing their hand. He explicitly worries about a kind of collective psychological strain as centuries of being bred to strive and accumulate collide with sudden abundance, argues that thrift and the profit motive are really just useful fictions we adopted to claw our way out of scarcity rather than virtues worth keeping once we're out of it, and even reaches for the Biblical image of the lilies of the field, that neither toil nor spin, as a picture of the kind of person who might already know how to live well without needing a purpose.

Since his hundred-year clock is now almost up, I'd be curious how you think he would read the "good life" side of his argument against how things actually turned out, as distinct from the purely economic side (I already know the standard story economists tell about why the fifteen-hour week never arrived: positional competition, inequality, advertising manufacturing new wants, and so on, so you don't need to rehearse that for me). What I'm more interested in is how he'd interpret two very different modern phenomena in light of his own psychological predictions: on one hand, something like hustle culture and voluntary overwork among people, including plenty of software engineers, who are already comfortable enough that they don't need the extra hours; on the other, compulsive social-media use and doomscrolling among people who do have the free time but arguably squander it. Would Keynes see both as essentially the same failure of nerve he predicted, just wearing different costumes, or would he think one of these poses a much deeper problem for his vision of the good life than the other? I'd like an answer that actually engages with the specific moves he makes in the essay rather than one that just recycles the "the fifteen-hour week didn't happen because of consumerism" talking point.
