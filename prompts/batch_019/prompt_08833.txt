For a term paper in the philosophy elective at my high school, I have been working through Spinoza's Ethics, and one tension in it keeps nagging at me. In Part I, Spinoza argues that nothing in nature, including any human decision, could ever have occurred otherwise than it did: necessity is total and universal, extending even to God's own will. Yet in Part V he still maintains that some people are freer than others — for Spinoza, a free person is simply one who acts from the necessity of their own rational nature rather than being driven by external causes they do not understand, not someone who could have chosen differently. This reminded me of Harry Frankfurt's 1969 argument against what's sometimes called the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, where an agent is held morally responsible for an action even though a counterfactual intervener would have forced that very action had the agent shown any sign of choosing otherwise. Here is what I cannot work out: Frankfurt treats the absence of alternate possibilities as a special, engineered exception, worth an elaborate thought experiment to bring out, whereas on Spinoza's picture no one ever has alternate possibilities in any situation whatsoever — his metaphysics seems to already hand him Frankfurt's conclusion without needing any rigged neuroscientist at all. Would Spinoza have welcomed Frankfurt-style cases as a useful weapon against people who still believe alternate possibilities are the norm, or would he have thought they conceded too much by treating a supposedly universal truth about nature as though it were a special case?
