I'm taking a course on classical Indian philosophy this semester, and we just wrapped up the Nyaya unit on the pramanas -- the different ways you can actually come to know something, not just believe it. Perception and inference make total sense to me: either you're directly experiencing the object, or you're reasoning your way to a conclusion from evidence. What I can't get past is why sabda, verbal testimony, gets to count as a fully independent fourth pramana on the exact same footing as those two.

As far as I can tell, that commits Nyaya to saying that just being told something by another person is, all on its own, enough to generate genuine knowledge and not mere belief. But that seems way too cheap compared to actually perceiving or inferring something -- it would mean literally any sentence anyone says to you counts as a source of knowledge, no matter who's saying it or whether they have any clue what they're talking about, since the pramana is just "sabda," full stop. My professor said this isnt actually a real problem for the Naiyayikas but kind of breezed past why in class, and I felt too lost to raise my hand about it. So am I misunderstanding something about how testimony is supposed to work here, and if it turns out there IS some built-in restriction that keeps it from being totally unrestricted like that, doesn't that just mean sabda secretly collapses into a form of inference (since you'd basically be inferring that the speaker is reliable), which would make treating it as its own separate pramana kind of redundant anyway?
