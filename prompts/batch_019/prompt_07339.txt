I'm a second-year counseling psychology graduate student completing a practicum placement at my university's community training clinic, and this term I'm also taking a graduate seminar on moral psychology that keeps bleeding into my clinical work in ways I hadn't expected. As the trainee representative on the clinic's care-quality committee, I've been asked to produce a first draft of guidance on client requests to be matched, or re-matched, with a clinician of a particular race. The clinic's current intake policy says only that "reasonable requests for clinician reassignment will be accommodated when feasible," without specifying what makes a request reasonable, and two cases from this past month have exposed how much work that word is quietly doing. In the first, a Black client asked after two sessions to be transferred from her assigned white therapist to a Black one, explaining that she did not feel able to be fully honest with him about her family history; intake approved the switch within a day, and no one on staff thought twice about it. In the second, a white client ended his third session early and asked the front desk for "someone who isn't Black," offering only that he would feel "more comfortable that way." The clinic approved that switch too, without further inquiry, and it is only in retrospect, discussing the two cases side by side, that the committee has split. Some supervisors want a single uniform rule -- honor any client's stated preference about a clinician's race, as a matter of policy, since it is not the clinic's place to rank whose racial comfort counts as legitimate -- while others, including the therapist reassigned in the second case, argue that treating the two requests as interchangeable ignores everything the clinic otherwise knows about the asymmetry between a marginalized client's guarded trust and a member of the majority's racial aversion, and that calling the second request a mere "preference" simply launders something the clinic would refuse to tolerate in any other context.

Could you draft the short memo I have been asked to bring to next week's meeting? It should recommend a rule specific enough for intake coordinators to actually apply the next time such a request arises, rather than falling back on "weigh the factors case by case," and it should set out the reasoning behind that rule and not just the bottom line -- including a fair-minded statement of the strongest version of whichever side it does not adopt, since I will need to defend the memo against whichever supervisor ends up feeling overruled.
