I'm a cognitive science PhD student working on confirmation bias in clinical diagnostic reasoning, and this term I've been auditing a graduate epistemology seminar to get a firmer grip on the philosophical literature my empirical work keeps gesturing toward. Last week the seminar spent a session on the dogmatism paradox, usually traced to an argument Kripke gave in an unpublished lecture and later written up by Gilbert Harman. As I understand it, the argument runs as follows: if I know that p, then I already know that any evidence which might later surface against p would have to be misleading, since evidence against something true is, by definition, evidence pointing away from the truth. And if I already know that a piece of evidence is misleading, I'm entitled to discount it rather than let it move my credence once it actually shows up. Put the two premises together, and the conclusion is that knowing p now licenses me to write off, in advance, any future evidence against p, however compelling it looks when I encounter it.

This struck me as a surprisingly direct vindication of something I had always treated as a performance error in the clinicians I study: once a physician is genuinely confident that she has the right diagnosis, discounting a contradictory test result isn't a lapse in rationality at all, but precisely what the logic of knowledge recommends. When I raised this point in seminar, the pushback I got was uniformly of the form "that conclusion is obviously unacceptable," but nobody could point me to a premise they actually thought was false; it felt much more like the room's intuitions were overriding a logically respectable argument than a genuine rebuttal of it. I would like to know whether I am missing a real flaw in one of the two premises, and if there isn't one to be found, whether the honest upshot is that confirmation bias among sufficiently confident experts is, at least in this narrow sense, more defensible than the decision-making literature I work in generally assumes.
