Okay this is a pretty niche question but its been living in my head for like two weeks now. In my epistemic logic seminar we covered the problem of logical omniscience: if you model "knowing" with the standard possible-worlds setup (an agent knows P at a world iff P holds in every world it can't distinguish from that one), the closure conditions force agents to know every logical consequence of what they know and every tautology, whether they've ever derived it or not. Taken seriously, this means I'd already "know" the truth value of any open number-theory conjecture that happens to follow from axioms I already know, which is obviously absurd. My professor mentioned offhand that Leibniz basically ran into a version of this centuries before Hintikka wrote it up in the 60s -- something about how necessary truths get proved by a finite analysis that bottoms out in identities, while contingent truths need an "infinite analysis" that not even God performs step by step, he just sees the whole thing in one intuition -- but we had to move on before she could actually connect the dots for us.

I happen to be taking a rationalism seminar the same semester where we spent ages on Leibniz's theory of truth, the complete concept doctrine, and the monadology (confused perception, monads as little mirrors of the universe, the whole characteristica universalis dream of a calculus for settling arguements), so I've got the raw material, I just can't fit it together myself. One of my study group friends thinks Leibniz would basically just bite the bullet: a genuinely perfect finite reasoner -- an angel, say, or God, at least as far as necessary truths go -- really should know every logical consequence instantly with zero computation involved, and our failure to do this is just proof that we're confused, low-grade monads, full stop, not evidence of a real problem with the logic itself. I'm not so sure, partly because of that infinite-analysis stuff about even God not reaching contingent truths by demonstration. Could you reconstruct, using Leibniz's actual apparatus rather than a generic "he was a rationalist so obviously he's pro-omniscience" take, what his considered position on the logical omniscience problem would probably be, and where (if anywhere) he'd think the standard epistemic logic idealization goes wrong?
