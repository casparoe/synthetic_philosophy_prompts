My doctoral advisor is retiring at the end of this term, and after her farewell colloquium the department is holding a small dinner at which I've been asked, somewhat to my alarm, to give the toast. She has spent essentially her whole career on Frege: her monograph on the philosophical stakes of the Grundgesetze is still the first thing anyone starting out on Frege gets handed, and she is also, more distinctively, the scholar in our subfield most associated with the claim that Frege's insistence on a "third realm" of objective thoughts, graspable only by minds disciplined enough for pure logic, cannot be kept as cleanly apart as most Fregeans would like from the antisemitic and anti-democratic diary he kept in 1924, the last full year of his life. She has spent a decade defending some version of that claim against colleagues who think the diary is a biographical footnote with no bearing on the Begriffsschrift or the theory of sense and reference, and several of those colleagues -- along with a couple of her own students who find the whole thesis overreaching -- will be at the dinner, seated not far from her sister, who has never read a page of Frege and mainly wants to hear that her sibling was a good teacher.

Could you write me a toast of about five minutes that is honest about what she has actually devoted her career to -- not a scrubbed "she just loves logic" version of her -- without relitigating the argument that already happened once today at the colloquium, and without requiring anyone who has never heard of a Sinn or a Gedanke to feel excluded. I would like it built around Frege's own distinction between what a sentence strictly says and its mere tone or coloring (Färbung) -- the sort of thing he held to be logically inert but that no speaker can actually do without -- since that distinction sits uncomfortably close to the very question her career has been about, and using it as the toast's hinge would be a better tribute than any direct compliment could manage. I am not looking for a verdict on whether she is right about Frege; I want a toast that only someone who had sat with her work for years could have written, and that still lands as a toast rather than as a paper.
