So I'm doing a PhD in CS focusing on AI safety / decision theory, and our reading group has been going chronologically through the corrigibility and "shutdown problem" literature. I feel like at this point I can recite the whole story but I don't actually get the mechanism at a couple of the key steps, and I want to understand it properly rather than just have the talking points memorized.

Quick recap of where we're at: first the old Soares/Fallenstein/Armstrong/Yudkowsky "Corrigibility" paper from 2015, with the utility indifference trick — you bolt on a correction term so the agent's expected utility comes out the same whether or not the shutdown button gets pressed, so in theory it has no incentive to fight shutdown or cause it. Then we did Hadfield-Menell et al's off-switch game, which reframes the whole thing as a game under uncertainty about the true reward function, where the robot lets itself be switched off if it's sufficiently unsure about its own reward and treats the human's action as evidence about it. And now we're on the newer stuff (Thornley, I think?) which argues there's definately an impossibility-style result lurking here — a standard expected-utility maximizer with complete preferences basically can't satisfy the shutdown desiderata, full stop — and that the proposed way out is to drop completeness, so the agent can only rank trajectories that end at the same time and otherwise follows some kind of dominance rule rather than maximizing expected utility across trajectories of different lengths.

What I'm missing is the actual "why" at each step, at a gut level rather than a full formal proof. Why does utility indifference actually break down in practice — why wasn't that just... the solution? Why is the off-switch game's result so fragile, i.e. what specifically goes wrong once the robot gets confident in its reward estimate, or the human's shutdown decision is noisy/irrational rather than a clean signal? And for the incompleteness move, why is completeness specifically the axiom that has to go — why not transitivity, or independence, or something else — what's structurally special about giving up completeness that lets you dodge both the "agent manipulates the button" problem and the "agent is money-pumpable" problem at once? My advisor's take is that this whole research line is going to keep needing a new patch every few years because corrigibility and coherent agency are just fundamentally in tension, and before I decide if I buy that, I want to actually understand why the incompleteness fix is supposed to work mechanically, not just that people say it does.
