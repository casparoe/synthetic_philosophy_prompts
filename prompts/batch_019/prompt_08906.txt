ok so in my econ elective we're doing a cost-benefit analysis unit and my teacher gave us this thought experiment: you can either help someone right now with $1000 of real benefit, or guarantee that exact same amount of benefit, with inflation and growth already stripped out and no risk involved, to someone 200 years from now. she called it "pure time preference" when you discount future wellbeing just because it's later, separate from normal stuff like interest rates or risk. two kids in my class, Owen and Maya, got into it about this in the hallway after and neither would drop it, so i wrote down roughly how it went:

(1) Owen said you should treat them the same, because discounting someone purely for WHEN they live is just as arbitrary as discounting someone for living far away.

(2) Maya said thats not fair, since waiting normally costs you something (you could invest the $1000 and have more later), so preferring it now is rational, not biased.

(3) Owen said thats exactly the stuff the thought experiment already removed -- something like discount rate = impatience + growth, and the growth part is fine -- so once you take that out, the real question is just whether the leftover "impatience" is justified, and he doesn't think it is.

(4) Maya said ok, but if you never discount at all, then any policy with even a tiny chance of helping a big enough number of future people beats almost anything you could do for people alive today, and being willing to sacrifice everything now for a maybe-payoff that huge seems just as crazy, so zero cant be right either.

(5) Owen said thats a problem with being uncertain the future people even exist or that your action reaches them, not a reason to ALSO knock down how much they matter once you've already priced in that uncertainty -- you shouldnt stack both discounts.

(6) Maya said Owen's not even consistent about this -- if his 80 year old self is basically a different person by then (kind of like how a country stays "the same country" even after literally everyone in it has been replaced), then maybe present-Owen caring less about 80-year-old-Owen's problems is fine, and if that logic works on his own future it should work on future strangers too.

neither of them budged before i had to leave for practice. can you go through it move by move and say who's actually right at each point -- not just who "won" overall -- and point out anything you think they're both missing?
