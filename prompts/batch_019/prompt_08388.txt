So I spent my career as a physicist doing Bayesian data analysis, and that's actually how I first stumbled on Frank Ramsey — turns out the idea that probability is just your personal degree of belief, measured by the odds you'd actually bet at, traces back to one essay of his from the 1920s, written before he died at only 26. Digging around more I found he'd also written this short, half-joking piece on the meaning of life where his basic point is that the universe being enormous doesn't make us small or unimportant — he wasn't remotely humbled by the size of the stars, since sheer scale just isn't the kind of thing that confers importance. Now here's what's bugging me: the "longtermism" crowd today (Bostrom, MacAskill, that whole scene) lean on pretty much the expected-value machinery Ramsey invented to argue that because the future could hold astronomically many people, even a tiny probability of shaping it should swamp everything else we care about right now — its basically Pascal's mugging dressed up in decimal points. Given his own view that vastness of scale isn't what makes something matter, do you think Ramsey would've bought that argument, or would he have called it out as a fanatical misuse of his own math? Genuinely curious what you think he'd actually say, and why.
