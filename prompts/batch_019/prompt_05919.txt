The Frege–Geach problem is usually summarized as "expressivists have trouble with embedded sentences," but the actual difficulty is sharper than that slogan suggests. Consider the inference: (1) If tormenting the cat is wrong, then getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong; (2) Tormenting the cat is wrong; therefore (3) Getting your little brother to torment the cat is wrong. This is an ordinary, valid modus ponens, and "wrong" seems to mean exactly the same thing in premise (1) as in premise (2). But if asserting "Tormenting the cat is wrong" is a matter of expressing disapproval rather than describing anything, it is unclear what disapproval is being expressed by "wrong" inside the unasserted antecedent of (1) — nobody who merely puts forward a conditional is thereby expressing disapproval of anything. Expressivists have spent decades trying to earn back the right to this inference: Blackburn's "logic of attitudes" locates the needed inconsistency at the level of combinations of attitudes that cannot jointly be held; Gibbard's norm-expressivism represents normative claims by sets of "factual-normative worlds" so that logical relations among them can be modeled formally; Schroeder's "being for" account tries to give a single, unified story of how any normative predicate embeds. Critics have replied, in various ways, that these strategies either fail to secure genuine sameness of meaning across the asserted and unasserted occurrences of "wrong," or succeed only by borrowing so much realist-looking apparatus that it becomes unclear what distinctively expressivist claim is left standing — a worry James Dreier later named "creeping minimalism."

Write a dialogue, set during the question period after a metaethics talk, between two philosophers of equal sophistication: an expressivist who thinks some version of this technical program (Blackburn's, Gibbard's, or a related successor) secures both validity and sameness of meaning without positing moral facts or truth-apt moral beliefs, and a cognitivist who thinks that any account sophisticated enough to secure those things will, on inspection, have smuggled in something that functions just like a truth-apt belief — so that the "solution" is really a covert concession to cognitivism, a version of creeping minimalism — and who argues it would be more honest to grant from the outset that moral sentences express genuine beliefs with truth conditions. The expressivist needs a real answer to this charge beyond simply re-describing his own machinery, and the cognitivist should recognize that helping herself to truth-apt moral belief brings its own costs (about what moral motivation consists in, or about what could make such beliefs true) that explain why one might have wanted to avoid cognitivism in the first place. Let their exchange contain genuine back-and-forth, each pressing the other on their strongest points rather than their weakest, and let it end wherever the disagreement actually leads — even if that is a stalemate over whether anything substantive still divides them.
