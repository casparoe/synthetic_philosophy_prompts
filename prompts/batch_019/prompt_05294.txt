The question, worth 20 points, reads: "In Word and Object, Quine imagines a linguist doing radical translation of a hitherto unknown language, and introduces the native's utterance 'gavagai,' produced on the occasion of a rabbit's scurrying by. (a) Explain the predicament the translator faces in rendering 'gavagai,' and show how the notions of stimulus meaning and analytical hypotheses each contribute to Quine's case that translation is indeterminate. (b) Quine repeatedly insists that the indeterminacy of translation is a stronger and more surprising thesis than the ordinary underdetermination of physical theory by observation that afflicts all of empirical science. Reconstruct his grounds for holding the two apart, and say what would be lost, philosophically, if we simply treated indeterminacy of translation as underdetermination applied to meaning."

Here, verbatim, is my classmate's submitted answer: "Quine's gavagai example is meant to show that even in the most favorable case for translation — actually watching a native's behavior in the very situation that prompts the utterance — there is no way to pin down what the word means. When the native says 'gavagai' as a rabbit hops by, the linguist can translate this as 'rabbit,' but 'undetached rabbit part,' 'rabbit-stage,' and 'rabbithood is present' would all fit the evidence equally well, since each of these is true on exactly the occasions on which a whole rabbit is present. What constrains the linguist initially is stimulus meaning: the set of sensory stimulations that would prompt a native speaker's assent to or dissent from a sentence. But stimulus meaning only pins down occasion sentences like 'Gavagai' one at a time; it says nothing about how to translate the working parts of a sentence, or about sentences not tied to any current stimulus, like 'the rabbit that bit me yesterday.' To go further, the linguist has to adopt analytical hypotheses: background assumptions, not read off of any native's behavior, about how the language handles individuation, plurality, identity, and the like. Different linguists could adopt different and mutually incompatible sets of analytical hypotheses that nonetheless fit all of the natives' speech dispositions equally well, with no further evidence available to adjudicate between them. This is ultimately why Quine treats the indeterminacy of translation as, at bottom, the same kind of phenomenon as the underdetermination of physical theory by evidence that he had already argued for in 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism': in each case, rival hypotheses — competing physical theories in the one case, competing translation manuals in the other — are compatible with the entire body of possible evidence and yet remain genuinely incompatible with one another, since the evidence available radically underdetermines any theory's claim to uniqueness (the same point Quine makes about physical theories via the Duhem-Quine thesis). Indeterminacy of translation is thus the special, close-to-home case in which the underdetermined 'theory' happens to be a semantic one: a system mapping the natives' sentences onto ours. In each case, some determinate fact — about the world, or about what the natives mean — is what our theorizing or translating is trying, and permanently failing, to track down, which is why Quine concludes that, strictly speaking, no translation of 'gavagai' can be singled out as the correct one, and that radical translation between sufficiently different conceptual schemes is, in the end, impossible."
