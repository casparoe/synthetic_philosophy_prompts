For the last couple of years I've been part of a small home-game poker group, and like a lot of people in that world I eventually ran into the Kelly criterion as a way of deciding how much of my bankroll to risk on a given edge. It worked well enough in practice that I got curious about where the formula actually comes from, which sent me down a rabbit hole of blog posts and a couple of popular books — Poundstone's Fortune's Formula was the main one — about the long-running dispute between people like Kelly, Thorp, and Latané, who argued you should bet to maximize the expected logarithm of your wealth (equivalently, the long-run geometric growth rate), and economists like Samuelson, who insisted this was an arbitrary rule with no more claim to being "rational" than any other utility function, and who was reportedly annoyed enough about it to publish a whole rebuttal written entirely in one-syllable words.

The more I read, the less this looked like a narrow dispute about bet-sizing and the more it looked like two genuinely different pictures of what it even means to choose rationally under uncertainty. The Samuelson side seems to say that rationality just means maximizing the expectation of whatever utility function represents your preferences, full stop, and that the Kelly rule is at best a special case of that (log utility) with no privileged status. But some of what I've since read under the label "geometric rationality" — mostly people connected to what's called ergodicity economics — argues the opposite: that classical expected-utility theory quietly averages over parallel possible worlds you will never actually live through (an "ensemble average"), whereas any real bettor experiences one sequence of bets played out over time, and for a genuinely multiplicative process like wealth, the time-average and the ensemble-average come apart. On that view, maximizing long-run growth isn't just one utility function among many; it's closer to what "doing well for yourself, over time, in the one world you actually get" means, and risk-aversion turns out to be a fact about the dynamics of the bet rather than a free parameter of anyone's psychology.

What I'd like from you is a real comparison of these two pictures, not a summary of the math I could get from a textbook. Please compare geometric rationality, in the Kelly/time-average sense, with classical expected-utility rationality as accounts of what rational choice under uncertainty actually is, rather than just as competing rules for sizing bets. In particular, does the ergodicity argument succeed in making geometric rationality a genuinely distinct and better-grounded notion of rationality, or is it, as the Samuelson side would say, ultimately just expected-utility maximization with logarithmic utility dressed up in different language? Please commit to a definite answer and justify it, and give the side you reject its strongest possible case before you set it aside.
