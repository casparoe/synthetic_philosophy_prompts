For a small email newsletter I write for a rationalist-adjacent readership -- the kind of audience that has already read the Sequences, knows roughly how a Newcomb problem is supposed to work, and has opinions about whether Yudkowsky was right to delete the original thread -- I run one long written Q&A a month with someone working on decision theory. Next month's subject is a theorist who came up through that same LessWrong-and-diaspora world and is now known for what I'd call the standard "insider" dissolution of Roko's basilisk: the argument, built out of updateless decision theory, that a sufficiently rational agent should adopt a blanket policy of refusing acausal extortion, and that once you're the kind of agent a sufficiently good predictor can verify won't pay up, a resource-conscious future AI has no decision-theoretic reason left to carry out the threatened simulated punishment. This came up in a group chat a while back, where one friend dismissed the whole thing as "Pascal's Wager with extra steps" and another insisted that's exactly what makes it worth taking seriously, as a stress test for logical decision theories rather than as a horror story -- and I'd like the interview to actually adjudicate that disagreement rather than re-explain the basilisk to people who've already heard the story a hundred times.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions for her? I want at least one question that presses on how the precommitment move is supposed to get off the ground in the first place -- what licenses the assumption that the AI can verify her disposition, and that she in turn can trust the AI to reason its way to the same policy, given that neither side has any causal access to the other; one on the "many mutually contradictory basilisks" problem, asking whether a general refusal of acausal threats is a principled decision rule or just a firewall that also happens to block ordinary commitment devices and incentive schemes; one asking her to reconcile Yudkowsky's own bifurcated reaction to the original 2010 thread -- treating the argument as decision-theoretically unsound while still treating it as dangerous enough to delete and ban discussion of for years -- since an unsound threat shouldn't obviously need suppressing; and one that pushes on whether "a threat only has to be believed, not executed" is quietly doing most of the argument's work, so that what's left once you subtract the vivid image of simulated torture is considerably less alarming than the thought experiment's reputation suggests. For each of those four, add a short follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give -- I don't want anything she can deflect with a one-line restatement of the view she's known for. Close the list with a question about what kind of result in decision theory would actually move her back toward taking the basilisk seriously, so the interview ends on falsifiability rather than reassurance.
