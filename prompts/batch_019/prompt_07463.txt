Ok so I've been falling down a rabbit hole of old rationalist/AI-safety blog posts for a decision theory reading group a few of us run on the side, and I keep bumping into this idea of a "commitment race." My rough gloss on it (correct me if I'm garbling it) is: in a bargaining situation, credibly committing early — Schelling-style, tying your own hands so you literally can't back down — gives you leverage over an opponent who can still adjust. But if both sides can anticipate that whoever commits first (and hardest) wins, then everyone's incentivized to commit as fast and as irreversibly as possible, before they've actually thought it through, which can leave both sides worse off than if they'd just bargained like normal people. I originally read about this in the context of advanced AI systems negotiating with each other, but it's been stuck in my head for a totally different reason lately.

My grad student union is heading into our first contract fight with the university this spring, and half the bargaining committee wants us to publicly announce, before we've even sat down with admin, that we won't ratify anything under a 15% raise and that we're prepared to strike. Part of me thinks that's just smart Schelling-point strategy — tie our own hands so admin knows we're not bluffing. But another part of me thinks this is literally the failure mode the commitment-races people are worried about: we'd be locking in a position before we know what admin can realistically offer, and if their negotiators feel the same pressure to look tough in front of the regents and dig in just as hard right back, we could end up on strike over a gap that a slower, less committal process would've closed.

I'm not asking you to referee our actual negotiation (that's what the endless commitee meetings are for), but I'd find it genuinely useful to have the theory itself spelled out properly by something that isn't emotionally invested in our specific fight. Could you lay out what you think are the most important implications of the commitment races problem for a small bargaining unit like a grad union heading into a first contract, especially given that, unlike the idealized agents this idea was originally about, none of us can rewrite our own source code or credibly prove to the other side exactly what we've committed to?
