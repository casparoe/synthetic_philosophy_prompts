I've just finished a novel narrated entirely in an invented, corroded English — something in the vein of Russell Hoban's *Riddley Walker* or the Nadsat slang of *A Clockwork Orange*, though mine drifts according to its own logic rather than borrowing an existing argot. While drafting an afterword, I found myself reaching for Donald Davidson's account of radical interpretation to describe what I'm actually asking of my reader, and I'd like your honest comments on the following sketch before I commit to it in print.

My claim, roughly: opening the book, a reader is doing something close to what Davidson's radical interpreter does — working out a speaker's utterances from scratch, with no dictionary and no independent access to what the speaker believes, by assigning meanings and beliefs together in a single pass rather than one after the other, and getting a foothold at all only by assuming the narrator is, for the most part, truthful and making sense (Davidson's charity).

I want to push this further, though, and say the more interesting parallel isn't with that 1973 picture, where the interpreter is in effect building something like a Tarskian truth theory adequate to a whole, stable language, but with the later Davidson of "A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs," who denies that speaker and hearer share a fixed language in that sense at all, and has each side improvising a "passing theory" on the fly, revised for the other's malapropisms and inventions as they go. That sounds much closer to what a reader actually does, sentence by sentence, with a narrator's private mutations of English.

Could you tell me, first, whether I have the Davidson right on both counts — the 1973 picture and the later one — and second, whether the shift I'm proposing, from "reader as radical interpreter constructing a truth theory" to "reader as party to a passing theory," is doing real philosophical work, or is just a more flattering way of describing ordinary reading comprehension?
