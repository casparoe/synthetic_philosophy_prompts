I'm part of an informal reading group that works through papers in decision theory and axiology outside of any course. Our most recent session was on Nick Bostrom's work on infinite ethics, and it ended in a disagreement between two members, Nadia and Theo, that the rest of us couldn't settle. The paper's central worry is this: cosmological models don't rule out a spatially infinite universe, and if the universe already contains infinitely many good and bad lives, standard total-utilitarian aggregation runs into serious trouble, since no finite intervention can budge an infinite total one way or the other. Nadia and Theo kept arguing about it after the meeting broke up, and I've reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can below.

(1) Nadia argued that this should worry anyone who wants to rank actions by the total value they produce: if the universe already contains an infinite amount of both happiness and suffering, then curing a disease or preventing a catastrophe leaves the total exactly as infinite as before, so on a naive summing view no finite action could ever be shown to make things better or worse overall. (2) Theo answered that this has to be a reductio of the naive view rather than a real practical conclusion, since it would equally imply there's no total-value difference between curing the disease and deliberately causing an equivalent harm -- both leave the infinite total "unchanged" -- which can't be right. (3) Nadia agreed the reductio works, but said the fix isn't to throw out the infinite-universe hypothesis; it's to stop comparing worlds by summing them to a single number, and instead say one infinite world is better than another only when it is at least as good in every region and strictly better in some -- a dominance condition rather than a total. (4) Theo pointed out that this repairs the paralysis only for the tidy cases where one option cleanly dominates region by region, and that almost no real choice looks like that, since our interventions are scattered across a universe otherwise full of independent processes; so the fix buys formal consistency at the price of leaving nearly every actual comparison undefined. (5) Nadia granted this, but raised a complication she remembered only loosely from the reading: even a region-based comparison depends on how you carve the universe into regions or order it into a sequence, and just as reordering the terms of certain infinite sums can change what they add up to, reordering the very same infinite world can flip which of two worlds comes out ahead, so there may be no way to say which infinite world is better except relative to an arbitrary partition. (6) Theo took this as a reason to bracket infinite ethics entirely when making actual funding decisions, on the grounds that real options are never going to be ranked by a canonical, partition-independent comparison anyway, so we might as well reason in ordinary finite cost-effectiveness terms and leave the rest to theorists. (7) Nadia replied that bracketing isn't free, since some longtermist arguments for prioritizing speculative, high-upside interventions already reason in terms of tiny probabilities of astronomically large payoffs; if we're not willing to just set those probabilities to zero, something close to the paralysis problem reappears in practice, and "bracketing" it is itself a substantive choice rather than a neutral default.

Neither of them budged, and the rest of the group ended up arguing about the argument rather than the paper itself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point -- including any cases where someone is right but for a shakier reason than they realize, or wrong despite a reasonable-sounding premise -- and then close with your own sense of whether the disagreement can actually be resolved, and how?
