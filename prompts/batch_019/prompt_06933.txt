I've been a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, where a recurring idea is that you learn what a person (or a system) is really made of by exposing them to a controlled, low-stakes perturbation rather than waiting for a costly high-stakes trial — the logic behind stress-testing, red-teaming, and the like. A tangent in one such discussion mentioned, in passing, that Plato had already tried a version of this argument, which sent me into Book I of the Laws — a dialogue I'd somehow never actually read, unlike the Republic or the Symposium. I was struck by how much space the Athenian Stranger spends defending, against Spartan and Cretan objections, the practice of supervised communal drinking as a genuine instrument of civic education, via an elaborate analogy with a hypothetical "fear-potion." Two stretches of the argument (quoting Jowett's translation) are giving me trouble, and I'd appreciate help reconciling them.

Earlier in the discussion, around Stephanus 645, the Stranger asks Cleinias about the effect of wine on the soul:

"Ath. And are perception and memory, and opinion and prudence, heightened and increased? Do not these qualities entirely desert a man if he becomes saturated with drink? Cle. Yes, they entirely desert him. Ath. Does he not return to the state of soul in which he was when a young child? ... Then at that time he will have the least control over himself? ... And will he not be in a most wretched plight? ... Then not only an old man but also a drunkard becomes a second time a child?"

But a few pages later, around 649, having built up an analogy with the fear-potion (which would test courage by flooding the drinker with imaginary terror), the Stranger concludes that wine tests a very different, opposite disposition:

"Ath. Now, let us remember, as we were saying, that there are two things which should be cultivated in the soul: first, the greatest courage; secondly, the greatest fear— Cle. Which you said to be characteristic of reverence, if I am not mistaken. Ath. ... as the habit of courage and fearlessness is to be trained amid fears, let us consider whether the opposite quality is not also to be trained among opposites. ... Are not the moments in which we are apt to be bold and shameless such as these?—when we are under the influence of anger, love, pride, ignorance, avarice, cowardice? or when wealth, beauty, strength, and all the intoxicating workings of pleasure madden us? What is better adapted than the festive use of wine, in the first place to test, and in the second place to train the character of a man, if care be taken in the use of it?"

What I can't work out is how these two stretches are meant to fit together. If wine indiscriminately knocks out perception, memory, and prudence, reducing the drinker to something like a child, why should whatever behavior emerges afterward count as evidence about that person's settled, sober character, rather than mere noise thrown off by a temporarily disabled mind? The "in vino veritas" premise the Stranger clearly needs doesn't sit comfortably with his own description, a few pages earlier, of what wine does to the reasoning faculties. There is a hint of an escape route just before the second passage, where the well-trained drinker is praised for leaving "off before he arrived at the last cup, fearing that he... might be overcome by the potion" — which suggests the real test might be the capacity to self-regulate one's exposure, rather than one's conduct once actually incapacitated. But that seems to rescue only a fairly modest claim (people can be tested on whether they know their limits) rather than the much grander one the Stranger wants, namely that whole supervised symposia reveal and train courage-like and temperance-like virtues generally. Is there a way of reading the passage that dissolves this tension without deflating what the Athenian is actually claiming, or is it a genuine fault line in the argument? I'd also value a view on whether mapping "the greatest fear" onto reverence or shame specifically, rather than onto something closer to sophrosyne or rational self-command in general, is doing real philosophical work in the analogy, or whether it's more of a loose verbal echo of the courage case that doesn't quite bear the weight placed on it.
