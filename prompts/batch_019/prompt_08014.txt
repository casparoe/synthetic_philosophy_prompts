Ian Hacking's account of "looping effects" holds that classifying a person under a human-kind category — especially a psychiatric one — can change how that person behaves, experiences themselves, and describes their own condition, and that this change can in turn feed back into the classification itself, producing a loop that has no counterpart in the classification of indifferent kinds such as quarks or copper. Hacking develops the idea mainly through cases like multiple personality disorder and the nineteenth-century fugue diagnosis, and later extends it to autism.

Construct a different example — not multiple personality disorder, dissociative fugue, or autism — of a contemporary diagnostic or quasi-diagnostic category that plausibly displays looping effects. Specify concretely: (a) what change in self-understanding, behavior, or self-presentation the classification tends to induce in those to whom it is applied; (b) how that change plausibly feeds back into the diagnostic criteria, the population who seek or receive the label, or clinical practice; and (c) why this should count as an instance of Hacking's "interactive kind" rather than simply a self-fulfilling prophecy, a diagnostic bias, or run-of-the-mill social influence on behavior.
