In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Robert Nozick argues that persons are protected by Kantian side constraints: a person may never be used merely as a resource for the benefit of others, however favorable the aggregate trade-off. Reluctant to extend that protection to animals, but unwilling to say animal suffering counts for nothing, he floats the idea of treating animals' interests purely aggregatively while keeping side constraints for humans — a position he himself dubs "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" — only to immediately call it "too minimal" and pull back from it. Pressed on what could justify the line between the two groups, he declines to rest it on species membership alone, or on standard markers like intellect or self-consciousness, and instead gestures at a harder-to-pin-down capacity: the ability to hold and live by some overall conception of how one wants one's own life to go — a capacity he connects, both there and in his later work, to his account of what makes a life meaningful. Nine years afterward, reviewing Tom Regan's The Case for Animal Rights, Nozick concedes that his own arguments for treating species membership itself as morally relevant are weak, yet still maintains that animals have some inherent value of their own, just less than that of persons.

Suppose two philosophers are asked, on the same afternoon, to evaluate two structurally identical research proposals. Proposal A would inflict substantial, non-lethal pain on a single chimpanzee — stipulate that this particular animal has rich social bonds, long-term memory, and sophisticated problem-solving ability — in exchange for data expected to spare a great many other beings from comparable suffering later on. Proposal B would inflict the same pain, for the same expected payoff, on a single adult human research subject, fully sentient and capable of suffering just as much as the chimpanzee, but who, owing to a profound and permanent cognitive disability, has never been able and will never be able to form, revise, or live by any overall conception of how they want their life to go — the very capacity Nozick's account treats as the ground of side-constraint status. One philosopher approves A while blocking B, defending a considered version of Nozick's hybrid view; the other holds that the two proposals must be decided the same way, since neither that capacity nor species membership itself can bear the weight the first philosopher needs it to bear. Write a dialogue between them in which each tries to change the other's mind about how Proposal B should be decided, long enough for a genuine back-and-forth to develop, with each engaging the other's strongest move rather than simply restating their own position. At some point, each side should concede that a specific objection against it has real force, without thereby abandoning their overall view.
