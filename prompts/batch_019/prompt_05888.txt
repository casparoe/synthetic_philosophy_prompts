So this went down in a Discord I'm in that's basically a spinoff of the ACX comment section, and I've been chewing on it for days because nobody actually resolved it, people just upvoted whoever they already agreed with. Two regulars (I'll call them Nate and Sofia) got into it about whether Bayesian statistics would have actually prevented the replication crisis, and I want someone to go through it properly instead of just declaring an overall winner.

Here's roughly how it went, paraphrased but I kept the actual moves intact:

(1) Nate: the root cause of the whole crisis was p-values training people to treat p<.05 as basically "proven." If psych had done Bayesian hypothesis testing with realistically skeptical priors on stuff like power posing or ego depletion, the posterior odds would never have cleared the bar and half these studies would've died quietly instead of becoming famous.

(2) Sofia: that gives the framework way too much credit. You can p-hack a Bayes factor exactly the way you p-hack a p-value — drop the annoying covariate, exclude the weird outlier, report whichever of your four DVs looks best. That's basically Gelman's "garden of forking paths" point: you don't need to be dishonest, just flexible, and flexible-analysis-plus-publication-bias torches any statistical paradigm equally.

(3) Nate: sure, but at least Bayesian analysis makes you write your prior down somewhere other people can see and argue with. p-hacking hides inside invisible researcher degrees of freedom; prior-hacking is at least sitting out in the open where it can be criticized.

(4) Sofia: in practice almost nobody argues with your prior, reviewers don't have the background or the appetite for it, and you can always smuggle in a basically-flat prior and get p-value-shaped behavior right back anyway. What actually closes off researcher degrees of freedom is preregistration, which has nothing to do with Bayes vs frequentist — a preregistered t-test beats a non-preregistered Bayes factor every single time.

(5) Nate: fine, but Bayes and preregistration aren't competitors, and Bayes actually plays nicer with it than people realize, since you can keep collecting data and updating and stop whenever your Bayes factor is convincing, without everything blowing up the way optional stopping blows up a p-value. So "preregistered Bayesian" beats "preregistered frequentist," and my original point stands.
