So this happened at the pub after a job talk last month, and I've been turning it over since. The candidate presented a paper on using cost-benefit analysis to prioritize pandemic relief spending, and afterward two of my colleagues -- Nadia, who does formal social choice theory, and Owen, a moral philosopher, very Rawls-adjacent -- ended up arguing about it for like a hour while the rest of us just sat there with our drinks.

(1) Nadia opened with the classic Robbins move: the candidate's whole ranking assumes you can weigh a rich person's minor inconvenience against a poor person's real hardship on some common scale, and there's no thermometer for that -- interpersonal comparisons of utility just aren't the kind of thing you can observe, so "a dollar matters more to the poor person" is a value judgment wearing an economist's lab coat. (2) Owen shot back that refusing to compare isn't neutral either: the Pareto criterion alone is silent on almost any real policy, since real policies make some people better off and others worse off, which is exactly why real cost-benefit work leans on Kaldor-Hicks compensation tests instead -- and those, by asking whether winners could in principle compensate losers in dollars, quietly treat a dollar in any pocket as equally weighty, which is itself a massive interpersonal comparison, just one nobody voted for. (3) Nadia conceded you need some aggregation rule but brought up Harsanyi: yes, his theorem gets you a social welfare function that's a weighted sum of individual utilities, but the theorem itself is silent on what the weights are or how you'd line up the zero-points and units across different people's utility functions, so you haven't recovered an interpersonal comparison at all, you've just relocated the arbitrariness into the weights. (4) Owen's comeback was that we fix rough weights the same way we know a friend's migraine outweighs our own hangover -- some non-measured "extended sympathy" judgment where you imagine actually living the other person's life -- and that requiring thermometer-grade precision before permitting any comparison would also outlaw QALYs, minimum wage laws, progressive brackets, basically anything redistributive. (5) Nadias closing point was that "rough and sympathetic" is precisely the problem, since those intuitive comparisons are known to track who's vivid and nearby rather than who's actually worse off, so grounding the weights in sympathy just launders bias through the vocabulary of welfare, whereas an openly arbitrary weight is at least honest about being a choice rather than a discovery.

Then somebody's Uber showed up and it just... stopped, unresolved, and it's been bugging me since. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each one, whether Nadia or Owen actually has the better of that specific exchange (or if it's a wash), rather than jumping straight to an overall winner? And once you've done that, tell me if there's a move sitting there that neither of them made but should have.
