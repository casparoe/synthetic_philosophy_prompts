Here's the question from the exam:

"In the Lectures on Religious Belief, Wittgenstein argues that religious belief is not 'reasonable' or 'unreasonable' in the way an ordinary empirical belief is. Using his Last Judgment example, explain what he means by this. Then explain (a) how this connects to his notion of a 'picture' (Bild) that a person holds in front of themselves throughout their life, and (b) how it relates to the debate over 'Wittgensteinian Fideism.' Finally, does Wittgenstein's account commit him to saying that religious statements have no truth value? Defend your answer."

And here's one student's answer (anonymized):

"Wittgenstein's point is that when a believer says 'I believe in a Last Judgment,' they aren't making a claim of the same kind as an ordinary prediction. He makes this vivid by contrasting it with someone hedging about a mundane matter: if a person said 'I'm not so sure, possibly' about there being a German aeroplane overhead, we'd say they were pretty close to the person who was sure; but if they said the same thing about the Last Judgment, Wittgenstein says there is an 'enormous gulf' between the two speakers, because the unsureness itself is out of place - it shows the person isn't even playing the same game as the believer. So asking a believer for their 'reasons' or 'evidence,' the way you'd ask for reasons behind a scientific claim, misdescribes what's going on entirely.

This is where the picture comes in: for the believer, the Last Judgment isn't a hypothesis they're tracking evidence for, it's a picture that regulates their whole life - e.g., governing how they feel about their own guilt and choices. Wittgenstein makes essentially this same argument a decade later in On Certainty, where he says all reasoning rests on 'hinge' propositions - things like the proposition that the earth existed long before I was born - which aren't themselves justified by evidence but form the groundless framework within which evidence-giving happens at all. Religious pictures are just one instance of the general phenomenon of groundlessness he's describing there too.

That's basically the position Kai Nielsen approvingly named 'Wittgensteinian Fideism': each language-game, religious included, carries its own internal criteria of rationality, so you can't fault religious belief for failing to meet the standards of science, because those standards were never the relevant ones in the first place.

As for the truth-value question, I don't think Wittgenstein is committed to a flat denial of truth-aptness. His target throughout is the idea that we should assimilate religious 'belief' to scientific hypothesis-holding, not that we should reclassify religious sentences as expressive noises with no cognitive content, the way an emotivist treats 'murder is wrong.' He's much more interested in the different use these sentences have than in handing down a semantic verdict about whether they're truth-apt. So the honest answer is that Wittgenstein doesn't really settle the question, and reading him as a straightforward non-cognitivist overstates what's actually in the text."
