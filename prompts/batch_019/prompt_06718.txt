I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term that pairs primary texts from Kant's moral philosophy with contemporary readings in the ethics of technology, and this week's session put the account of imputation (Zurechnung) from the introduction to the *Metaphysics of Morals* side by side with the "responsibility gap" literature that begins with Andreas Matthias's well-known 2004 paper on learning automata. Matthias's worry, as I understand it, is that once a machine's behavior is the product of a learning process that its designers and operators can neither fully predict nor fully control, nobody left in the causal chain still satisfies the ordinary knowledge and control conditions we normally require before blaming someone for an outcome, so a gap opens up between the harm done and any agent who could justly be held responsible for it.

What I haven't been able to work out on my own is what Kant would actually say about this, and I'd like help thinking it through rather than a general summary of his ethics. Kant's notion of autonomy is quite specific and technical -- the capacity of a rational will to legislate the moral law to itself -- which sits oddly next to the way engineers use "autonomous" to describe a drone or a trading algorithm that simply runs without a human continuously in the loop; would Kant have any patience for calling such a system "autonomous" at all, or would he think the word is being used so equivocally that it already distorts the debate? More substantively, does his theory of imputation actually leave room for a "gap" in the first place, or would he insist that responsibility remains fully and straightforwardly located in whoever designed, commanded, or chose to deploy a system while knowing they could not predict its particular outputs, given that on his view only a person -- never a mere thing -- can be the author of a deed? I'd also be curious whether his distinction between treating rational beings as ends and treating mere things as means gives him independent grounds to condemn delegating lethal decisions to such systems, quite apart from the separate question of who is liable after the fact.
