Over a thirty-six-year career as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I do not believe I ever once paused to ask what a law of nature actually is. I simply used them: Ohm's law, the London equations, the various empirical scaling laws we fitted to data near phase transitions, the ideal gas law whenever a rough estimate would do, and I trusted that they captured something real about how matter behaves, whether or not I could have said precisely what that "something real" amounted to. Since retiring, I have joined a small philosophy reading group at my old university, and we have just worked through David Lewis's best-system analysis of lawhood alongside David Armstrong's rival account of laws as relations of necessitation between universals, together with a look at the dispositional essentialists who locate lawhood in the essential powers of properties themselves. I had assumed, going in, that this was a fairly bloodless terminological dispute among people who agreed about all the physics and merely described it differently. I no longer think that, and I would like help seeing exactly why.

What I would like is for you to work out the most important implications of taking a Humean view of laws, in something like Lewis's sense, versus an anti-Humean view, whether of the necessitation-relation sort or the dispositional-essentialist sort, for three matters that came up constantly in my working life but that I now realize I never examined. First, whether extrapolating a well-confirmed law into a regime far outside the data used to establish it rests on genuinely different, and differently secure, epistemic footing under each view, or comes to the same thing described in two vocabularies. Second, whether it even makes sense, on each view, to ask why the fundamental constants take the values they do, in the way cosmologists debating fine-tuning and the multiverse are fond of doing. And third, whether an idealization we relied on constantly and knew to be strictly false of every real gas, such as the ideal gas law, should count as a law of nature at all on either picture, or as something else entirely, such as a derived or approximate regularity standing in for laws stated at some more fundamental level. I am less interested in a survey of the two camps, which I think I now grasp well enough, than in what actually turns on the choice between them for someone who spent a career relying on laws without ever asking what made them laws.
