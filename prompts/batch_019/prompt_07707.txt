The question on the exam read: "Bell's theorem is often glossed, loosely, as showing that 'no local hidden variable theory can reproduce the predictions of quantum mechanics.' Using the EPR setup (a pair of spin-1/2 particles prepared in the singlet state and measured by Alice and Bob along freely chosen axes), reconstruct the argument that gets from the assumption of locality to the Bell/CHSH inequality, and explain precisely what the experimental violation of that inequality does and does not entitle us to conclude about the world. Be explicit about what work, if any, the assumption of determinism is doing in the derivation, and say how the result bears on Einstein's own hope that quantum mechanics could be completed by local hidden variables."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen pointed out that if two particles are prepared in the singlet state, measuring Alice's particle along some axis a lets us predict with certainty what Bob will get if he measures along the same axis, since the outcomes are perfectly anti-correlated whenever a = b. EPR argued that, given locality — the assumption that nothing done at Alice's wing can instantaneously affect the real state of Bob's particle — this predictability means Bob's particle must already have possessed a determinate value for that measurement before any measurement was made; otherwise Alice's measurement would have to be influencing Bob's particle from a distance. Generalizing across all the axes Alice could have chosen, each particle must carry a complete set of predetermined values, λ, fixing the outcome for every measurement that could be performed on it. This is the route from locality to a hidden-variable theory of the form A = A(a, λ), B = B(b, λ), with neither outcome depending on the other side's setting.

From here, Bell showed that any theory of this form must satisfy a constraint on the correlations between the four combinations of two settings on each side: the Bell/CHSH inequality. Quantum mechanics predicts, and experiments (Aspect 1982, and the loophole-free tests some thirty years later) confirm, that the singlet-state correlations violate this inequality, reaching 2√2 against the classical bound of 2. Since the inequality was derived from locality plus the EPR argument for determinism, its violation refutes that conjunction and rules out any local, deterministic hidden-variable theory of the EPR type.

It's worth being careful about scope, though. Because the route to the hidden-variable structure goes via EPR's argument for determinism, the inequality as Bell originally derived it strictly constrains theories in which λ fixes the outcomes themselves; a theory on which λ only fixes probabilities for each outcome is a further step removed from that derivation, so, on the letter of the argument, indeterministic local hidden variables are not directly addressed by the 1964 proof. This also tells us something about Einstein's program: since what gets refuted is specifically the deterministic completion that EPR's reality criterion pointed to, the experiments are best read as forcing a retreat from determinism rather than from locality as such — which, if anything, is the part of the picture a defender of Einstein's approach could more easily have lived without."
