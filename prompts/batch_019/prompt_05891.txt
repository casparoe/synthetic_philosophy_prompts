So I've been stuck in a bit of an internet rabbit hole lately, reading through some AI-alignment / rationalist blog stuff (a lot of it traces back to LessWrong) about why people think a sufficiently smart agent will basically end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer even if nobody designed it that way on purpose — the whole "coherence theorem" argument. The move that keeps showing up is the money-pump: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and then (weirdly) C to A, a bookie can trade you around that loop, taking a cut each time, and hand you back the exact same thing you started with, just poorer. Okay, fine, I get why that's supposed to show cyclic preferences are irrational.

But then some of these writeups also want to say your preferences need to be complete — meaning for any two options you either prefer one, prefer the other, or are indifferent, no "these just aren't comparable" option allowed — and they claim incompleteness can be money-pumped too, same as cyclicity. When I actually tried to work out how that pump is supposed to go, it felt like it secretly needs an extra assumption that the cyclic version doesn't: basically that the agent is willing to pay a small fee to swap one incomparable thing for another whenever its offered the chance. But why would an agent with incomplete preferences be stuck with that disposition in the first place? Seems like it could just adopt a policy of only trading when its a strict improvement on what it already has, never trade for something merely incomparable, and then it can't be pumped, no completeness required.

I brought this up with a friend who's read more decision theory than me, and he mentioned something about "resolute choice" — like, maybe an agent can dodge the pump just by committing in advance to a plan, and if that same trick also saves cyclic preferences from being pumped, then maybe money pumps don't actually prove what people think they prove in either case. He wasn't totally sure though. Could you walk me through what the real extra ingredient is in the incompleteness version of the argument (versus the plain cyclic one), and whether "just refuse trades that aren't strict improvements" (or resolute choice, or whatever) actually gets an agent off the hook? I want to understand whether incomplete preferences are genuinely exploitable the way cyclic ones supposedly are, or whether that extension of the argument is quietly begging the question somewhere.
