I produce a philosophy podcast for a general audience, and the idea for next month's episode actually grew out of an argument with a friend: she wanted to know whether her sister had any right to criticize her for lying to their parents, given that the sister has told plenty of self-serving lies of her own. It turns out this is a live question in ethics rather than just a debating trick, and our guest is a moral philosopher who has staked out a strong position on it. She defends a "Non-Hypocrisy Condition" on the standing to blame: a blamer who is guilty of the same, or a relevantly similar, wrong forfeits her standing to blame someone else for it. Crucially, on her view this doesn't make the original wrongdoing any less wrong or excuse the wrongdoer; the hypocrite's blame is inapt because, in the act of blaming, she implicitly claims an exemption from a standard she isn't willing to hold herself to. She is also careful to keep this apart from two neighboring standing failures that tend to get lumped in with it -- losing standing through complicity in the very wrong at issue, and blame that fails simply because it is none of the blamer's business.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the position. I'd like at least one question apiece, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, on: (1) the worry that her view hands wrongdoers a rhetorical trump card, letting them dodge a legitimate charge by impugning the accuser instead of answering to it; (2) a constructed case that seems to trigger hypocrisy, complicity, and mere meddling all at once -- for instance, a manager who criticizes a subordinate for padding an expense report on a shared project, having padded her own reports on unrelated projects in the past -- and how she sorts out which standing failure, if any, is actually doing the work; (3) whether, and how much, moral reform can restore forfeited standing -- how long, say, a recovered addict must stay clean before she can blame other addicts without being a hypocrite herself; and (4) how an account pitched at the level of individual blamers is supposed to scale up to the collective cases that first made this topic prominent, such as one country condemning another's human-rights record while tolerating comparable abuses at home. Rather than inviting her simply to elaborate, each follow-up should press a specific cost of her likely answer: a counterexample, an unwelcome commitment, or a tension with something she said earlier in the interview.
