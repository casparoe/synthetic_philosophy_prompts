So I'm drafting a scene in my novel where my protagonist, a sleep researcher, spends 48 hours in a flotation tank — total darkness, body-temp salt water, dead silence — and I keep thinking about Avicenna's flying man: a guy created fully grown, suspended in a void, limbs spread apart so nothing touches anything else, blindfolded and unable to hear a thing, who Avicenna insists would still be certain of his own existance even though he'd affirm nothing about having a body or senses at all. Thing is, actual sensory-deprivation research (Lilly's isolation tank work, the old McGill deprivation studies) usually turns up hallucinations, panic, or a weird dissolving sense of where you end and the room begins — not the calm bedrock self-awareness the thought experiment assumes. If someone explained that research to Avicenna, would he treat it as a genuine problem for the flying man argument, or does his account of the soul and its faculties give him an easy way to wave it off as beside the point? Curious about your best reconstruction of his actual reasoning, not just a yes or no.
