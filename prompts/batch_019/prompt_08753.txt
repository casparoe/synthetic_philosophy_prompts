Contemporary panpsychism is troubled by the combination problem: the difficulty of explaining how a multitude of simple micro-level subjects of experience, posited to exist at the level of fundamental particles or fields, could combine to constitute the single, unified experience of a macro-level subject such as a human being. The worry is often traced back to William James, who argued that no number of discrete feelings could simply sum into one larger, unified feeling, and it remains central to the work of philosophers such as Chalmers and Goff.

Leibniz predates this debate, but his monadology already commits him to definite views about combination and unity: monads are simple, "windowless" substances whose changes are coordinated only by a pre-established harmony, and any composite body is merely an aggregate, a "well-founded phenomenon," rather than a genuine unity in its own right.

Given this framework, how would you expect Leibniz to respond to the combination problem? Would his system amount to a genuine dissolution of the problem, or would it simply relocate the same difficulty to another part of his metaphysics?
