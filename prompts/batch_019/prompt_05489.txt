Using Bostrom's 'presumptuous philosopher' thought experiment, explain why this case is usually treated as a problem for SIA specifically, rather than for SSA. And here's Theo's answer, typed up exactly as he sent it to me:

"Both principles try to say how you should update your beliefs once you factor in not just your evidence but the fact that you are the one having it. The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) says that, other things equal, you should reason as if you're a random sample drawn from the reference class of all observers who actually exist — past, present, and future. The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) says instead that you should reason as if you were a random sample from the class of all possible observers, which has the consequence that hypotheses predicting more observers automatically become more probable, since there were more 'slots' for you to have turned out to occupy.

Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher case is supposed to show how odd this consequence is. Physicists have narrowed things down to two theories, T1 and T2, which fit all the evidence equally well, except T2 implies a universe with vastly more observers in it than T1 does. They're preparing an expensive experiment to settle the matter when a philosopher shows up and announces that no experiment is needed: SIA already tells him T2 is enormously more probable than T1, purely because T2 has more observers in it. That's absurd, because it lets someone become extremely confident about a question of physics without looking at any physics. This is standardly read as a problem specifically for SIA, not SSA, because plain SSA reasoning doesn't give a theory a boost just for containing more potential observers — it only kicks in once you compare how likely your specific evidence is under each theory, and 'more observers in the universe generally' by itself isn't that kind of evidence.

That said, SSA has its own well-known skeleton in the closet: its the engine behind Bostrom's Doomsday Argument, on which treating myself as a random sample among all humans who will ever be born makes my early birth rank surprising if humanity is destined to spread across the galaxy for eons (and so have trillions of future members), and unsurprising if we're likely to peter out relatively soon — which pushes credence toward sooner rather than later extinction. Interestingly, SIA doesn't come to the rescue here: because it also rewards hypotheses with larger total populations, the same 'more observers' consideration that made T2 look good to the presumptuous philosopher cuts in favor of a large future population too — but since a large future population is exactly what makes my own early birth rank surprising in the first place, SIA's extra boost for big populations ends up reinforcing the doom-soon conclusion rather than undercutting it. So although the presumptuous philosopher exposes a real cost that's distinctive to SIA, it would be too quick to conclude SSA is thereby home free — it has its own, arguably worse, skeleton, and reaching for SIA doesn't get you out of it."
