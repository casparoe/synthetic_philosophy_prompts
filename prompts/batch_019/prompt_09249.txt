"IRL is sometimes pitched as a way to avoid hand-specifying a reward function: instead of an engineer guessing at what we want, let the AI infer it from how people actually behave. (a) Explain the sense in which this inference is 'ill-posed' or underdetermined, even in the easy case where the human is assumed to act optimally. (b) Explain how the problem changes, for better or worse, once you drop the assumption that the human demonstrator is fully rational. (c) In 3-4 sentences, say what you think this implies, if anything, for real alignment proposals that want to learn human values from behavior."

And here's Diego's answer:

"IRL tries to run the RL loop backwards: instead of reward → behavior, you observe behavior and want to recover the reward it's (approximately) optimizing. The catch is that this inverse problem doesn't have a unique solution. For any observed policy there's a huge family of reward functions consistent with it, and the most degenerate member of that family is the all-zero reward, since if nothing is rewarded then literally any behavior counts as 'optimal.' Ng and Russell flagged exactly this in their original 2000 IRL paper, and their fix was to add an extra criterion on top of mere consistency — roughly, prefer the reward that makes the demonstrated action look as clearly better as possible compared to the alternatives, which rules out the degenerate solutions.

Once you stop assuming the person is a perfect optimizer, it gets worse, not better: now the same piece of behavior can be explained either by changing the reward or by changing how irrational you assume the person to be, so you're stuck jointly inferring two unknowns from one behavioral trace. My first instinct was that a simplicity prior (Occam's razor: prefer the simplest reward + rationality-model pair) should rescue this, since simple explanations are usually the right ones. But Stuart Russell actually proved that this doesn't work in general — for basically any (reward, rationality) pair that fits the data, there's an equally simple alternative pair with a different, sometimes totally opposite, reward that fits just as well. So Occam's razor can narrow things down in easy cases but can't save you in general. This is really the same failure mode as reward hacking: the system ends up chasing a signal that's compatible with many different 'true' objectives, so it looks like it's doing the right thing while actually optimizing something else entirely.

I think this is a genuine problem for behavior-based value learning, not just an engineering inconvenience: it means no amount of behavioral data can, by itself, logically pin down 'the' human reward function. You always need to smuggle in some extra assumption about human rationality that isn't itself derivable from the data, and different reasonable-sounding assumptions here can point to very different conclusions about what people 'really' want."
