So this is a weird one that landed in my lap recently. I write short stories on the side, and this year I said yes for the first time to judging our regional arts council's short-fiction prize — five judges, about 130 submissions, six weeks total to land on a shortlist. The coordinator's plan is that each story only gets a full read from two of the five judges, who each write a short response and a 1-10 score, and then the whole panel decides the shortlist off those write-ups, even for the stories most of us never actually read ourselves. Two of the judges are now flatly refusing to go along with this: their argument is that calling one story better or more affecting than another isn't the kind of judgment you can take secondhand, no matter how much you trust the person's taste — you either read it and it did something to you or it didn't, and a colleague telling you a story is the strongest in the batch can't do the work that testimony does for an ordinary fact. The coordinator thinks that's precious, since we all act on other people's aesthetic verdicts constantly — best-of lists, a critic we trust, a friend's recommendation — without treating any of that as fake or illegitimate.

I got stuck drafting the actual protocol that goes to the rest of the panel (and probably gets explained to writers who ask how their work was judged), and with results due in three weeks I cant just let the standoff sit there. I mentioned it to my partner, who thinks I'm overthinking a short-story contest, but the panel is genuinely split down the middle and someone has to put something in writing. Could you draft that memo for me? I need it to actually take a side on whether, and how far, a judge can legitimately rank or score a story they haven't read themselves — engaging with the strict pair's strongest point rather than just asserting that we all trust each other's taste — and then turn that into a workable protocol: who has to read what firsthand, what the secondhand write-ups are and aren't allowed to settle, and whether anything (like the eventual winner) should require every judge to have read it themselves.
