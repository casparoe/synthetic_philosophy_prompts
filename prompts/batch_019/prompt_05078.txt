I started my PhD last year in a lab run by someone kind of legendary in the field — honestly the whole reason I applied there. A couple weeks ago, a postdoc in the lab, Meera, was reanalyzing data from one of my advisor's most-cited old papers and noticed the summary numbers in one table don't quite square with what the methods section says was done. She wants to email the journal and request the raw files to check it properly.

I told her I didn't think that was necessary. I've sat through a year of his group meetings, watched how borderline obsessive he is about controls, and I just know this man doesn't fudge results. And if I really do know that, then whatever's "off" about that table has to be a rounding error or an old-software quirk rather then real evidence of a problem — because if it were real evidence, I wouldn't actually know what I think I know. So skipping the deep-dive isn't closed-minded, it's just what follows from actually being sure. Meera wasn't having it. She said "I already know he's honest" isn't an argument, it's just a fancy way of refusing to look at anything, and that this exact move is what lets stuff slide for years in other labs before it blows up. I said that's not fair — there's a real difference between refusing to look at evidence and correctly recognizing that something doesn't count as evidence to begin with.

Typing this all out, I'm honestly not sure I'm right anymore, but I can't find the hole in my own logic either. Is there something actually wrong with going from "I know he's honest" to "so I can safely ignore whatever looks like contrary evidence," or was I onto something and Meera's the one being unreasonable here? (Sorry for the wall of text — this has been eating at me for two weeks and my partner is sick of hearing about it.)
