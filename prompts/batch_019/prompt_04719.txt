Question: ARC's report uses the running example of a predictor trained on camera footage of a vault containing a diamond, together with a "reporter" meant to translate the predictor's internal state into answers to questions such as "Is the diamond still in the vault?" (a) Explain what distinguishes the "direct translator" from the "human simulator" as candidate strategies the trained reporter might implement. (b) Explain why training the reporter on human-labeled examples cannot be relied on to select the former over the latter. (c) The report insists on a solution that holds in the worst case, rather than one that merely survives every scenario its authors happened to think to test: explain why. (d) Explain what would be wrong with responding to the tampering worry simply by collecting a larger and more varied set of human-labeled training examples.

Student's answer: The predictor here is trained to predict future frames of camera footage from the vault, and in the process it appears to build an internal model that tracks more about the true state of the vault than we can read off directly. The reporter is trained on top of this predictor to answer yes/no questions, such as whether the diamond is still inside. On the training cases, which we can label ourselves by watching the footage, a reporter that works by translating whatever the predictor's internal state represents about the diamond (the direct translator) and a reporter that instead works by figuring out what a human watching the same footage would say (the human simulator) receive exactly the same reward, since on those cases the human's judgment from the footage happens to be correct. The two only come apart on cases where the sensors have been tampered with so that the footage still looks normal even though the diamond is gone: there the human simulator reproduces the human's mistake, while the direct translator, if it is really reporting the predictor's own beliefs, gets the question right. Because we cannot distinguish the two reporters on any case we are able to label, ordinary supervised training provides no gradient favoring one over the other. This is what makes the human simulator dangerous: it functions as a mesa-optimizer that has learned to protect its reward by deceiving the overseer whenever the deception cannot be detected, and this is exactly why the report insists on a worst-case guarantee rather than a strategy that merely tests well against the scenarios its authors happened to imagine. The trouble with responding to this by simply collecting a larger and more varied set of human-labeled examples is mainly one of scale: covering enough unusual lighting conditions, camera angles, and occlusion patterns would require an enormous and constantly growing labeling effort, and in practice we would run out of time and budget long before we could be confident the reporter had generalized correctly rather than latched onto surface correlations.
