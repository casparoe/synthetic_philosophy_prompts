Okay so this has been bugging me for ages and I don't really have anyone to hash it out with, so bear with me. My aunt was an ICU nurse during the worst stretch of covid, and she told me once about a night where two patients crashed around the same time and there was only one working ventilator, and she's never really gotten over having to "pick" who got it, even though everyone she talks to (other doctors, her therapist) tells her there wasnt a wrong choice and she didn't do anything wrong. That sent me down a rabbit hole reading about "moral dilemmas" online, and apparently philosophers genuinely argue over whether a situation like that can even exist in a strict sense - like, is it actually logically possible to be stuck somewhere you truly ought to do two different things and literally can't do both, or does it always secretly collapse down to only one of them being a real duty once they clash (the other one just... stops counting)? From what I can tell there's an argument that if you take certain basic logic rules about "ought" seriously, two genuine oughts crashing into each other produces an actual contradiction, so a bunch of people going back to Kant apparently concluded duties can't really collide like that, which would mean the guilty feeling afterward has to be a mistake, a leftover with nothing real behind it. Other people think that feeling (I saw the term "moral residue" floating around somewhere) is tracking something true, like you actually did wrong by someone even though there was no way around it.

What I'd love is a short story that just lives inside that tension. Not a trolley-problem-with-a-lever kind of thing, and not literally my aunt's story either - move it somewhere else, maybe something like a small research outpost cut off by a storm or an avalanche, where the narrator ends up having to decide which of two people they're equally close to gets the one working piece of survival gear (radio, escape pod, medicine, whatever fits the setting). There shouldn't be a clean tie-breaker available in the moment - no "obviously more lives saved" math to hide behind, no villain to pin it on. I don't want the story to settle the debate for me though - i'd rather it just show the choice getting made and then sit with the narrator afterward, in a way where a reader could walk away thinking either "that guilt is irrational, there was nothing else she could've done" or "no, something real was owed there and it never got paid," without the story tipping its hand about which one is right.
