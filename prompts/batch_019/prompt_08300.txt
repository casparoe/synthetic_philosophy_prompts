I trained as a philosopher before I became a novelist, and the person most responsible for that training, Professor Alden, is retiring this June after thirty-nine years in the department. Her former students have put together a small Festschrift dinner in her honor, and, in a decision I still don't entirely trust, they've asked me — the one person in the room without a current faculty appointment — to give the toast. I suspect the reasoning is that everyone else who might do it is a colleague she has spent decades arguing with, and a toast from any of them would turn into a rebuttal.

She has spent that career defending, with a stubbornness her critics call admirable and she calls merely consistent, Quine's marginal and under-read line on moral value — the one from a slim, late essay of his called "On the Nature of Moral Values," which people who know him only for "Two Dogmas" have generally never opened. Her version of it, rehearsed at more dinner tables than I can count, is that there is no freestanding court of moral fact for our judgments to answer to: values enter the same web of belief as our beliefs about quarks and continents, revisable only from inside it, plank by plank, Neurath's boat with no dry dock ever available for inspecting the hull from outside. She also has a running bit that every philosopher at that dinner will recognize instantly, and that quietly drove her late colleague across the hall — a lifelong realist — up the wall for the better part of twenty years: she will not say "I did the right thing." She says a thing "cohered better with everything else I believed than the alternative would have."

I need a toast for that dinner, roughly five minutes long, that can move the half of the room with no idea who Neurath was — her sister, her book club, two former students who left the field entirely, like me — while still being a toast she herself could sit through without wincing on philosophical grounds. That rules out the ordinary toast-language that treats her decency or her generosity as some pre-existing virtue she simply perceived correctly and answered to, since dismantling exactly that picture has been most of her life's work. Could you draft it for me? I'd like it built around one image — the boat, ideally, though I'm open to a better one drawn from her actual views — that pays out differently for the philosophers in the room than for everyone else, and I'd like it to end up sounding like love, not like a defense of naturalized epistemology.
