I spent thirty-some years as an epidemiologist running cancer screening studies before I retired, and lately I've fallen into reading philosophy just for fun, which is how I ended up down the rabbit hole of the Doomsday Argument. I wrote up my own take on it below and I'd like your honest comments before I bring it to a reading group I just joined - tell me if its any good, if I've botched the logic somewhere, or if I've just reinvented a wheel that already has a name I don't know.

Here's the argument as I understand it: treat your own birth rank among all humans who will ever live as though it were drawn at random, then use Bayes' rule to shift your credence away from "humanity has a huge future" and toward "humanity ends relatively soon," on the grounds that a huge future population would make your early rank a wildly atypical draw.

What's been bugging me is this. In screening work we're obsessed with length-biased sampling: if you enroll people by grabbing whoever's sick on a given day rather than following a cohort from the moment of onset, you systematically overcatch the slow-growing, long-duration cases, just because they're around longer and so more likely to be caught by a random glance. It's the same reason a random arrival at a bus stop is more likely to land inside a long gap between buses than a short one - bigger intervals just eat up more of the timeline, so naturally you land in one more often.

It seems to me the Doomsday Argument treats your birth rank as a random draw from a population of some fixed size N without ever asking whether the different hypotheses about N should themselves get weighted, going in, by how many "slots" they contain - a bigger total population is a bigger interval, so by the bus-stop logic it should be more likely to be the one your existence lands inside. Build that weighting into the prior and my back-of-envelope sense is it exactly cancels out the shift the argument is famous for.

So: have I basically reinvented a move that's already standard in this literature, and if so what's it called? Does the bus-stop analogy actually survive scrutiny or is it broken somewhere I'm not seeing? And I have this nagging memory of some clever thought experiement designed specifically to make this kind of "weight by population size" move look absurd once you push it far enough - if I'm remembering right, what is it, and does it actually sink my version of the argument or just a cruder one?
