Here's the student's answer, copied over exactly as they wrote it:

"Kripke's puzzle starts from the observation that in my life I've only ever computed sums using small numbers, so when I write '68 + 57' and answer '125,' nothing in my past use of '+' logically rules out that I actually meant 'quus' (⊕), a function that agrees with addition for every pair of numbers I've previously computed but which returns 5 for any pair involving a number as large as 57. The skeptic's challenge is: what fact about me, behavioral or mental, made it the case that I meant plus and not quus? Kripke considers and rejects the obvious dispositionalist answer, that meaning plus just consists in my disposition to answer '125' to that very question. This fails for two reasons: first, my dispositions are finite and would run out for sufficiently large numbers, whereas addition is an infinite function, so my dispositions can't add up to (pun intended) the whole meaning of '+'; second, and more importantly, a disposition is merely descriptive of what I would do, not what I should do, so it can't underwrite the normativity built into meaning-talk — the fact that some answers are correct and others are mistakes. Appealing to a qualitative mental state or a rule formulated in the head doesn't help either, since any candidate rule is itself open to the same quus-style reinterpretation, so we get a regress.

Wittgenstein's answer to this is not to find the missing fact but to dissolve the demand for one: the skeptical solution grants that there is no fact that makes 'Jones means plus by +' true, but insists such attributions are nonetheless perfectly in order because they're grounded in whether Jones's answers agree with what the rest of the community would say. On this view, what makes it correct that I mean plus by '+' just is that my usage matches the community's usage; if I started answering '5' to large sums, the community would step in and correct me, and it's this pattern of agreement and correction that constitutes the meaning-fact, only now located at the level of the group rather than the individual. That's also why the argument feeds directly into the private language argument: a solitary speaker like Crusoe, with no community able to check him, has no meaning-fact available to him at all, so a private language turns out to be impossible even as a conceptual possibility. So the upshot is that Kripke doesn't abandon the idea that there are facts about what we mean, he just relocates them from individual dispositions to communal ones."
