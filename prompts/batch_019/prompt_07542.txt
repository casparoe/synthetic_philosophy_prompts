I'm a cognitive science PhD student (dissertation's on belief updating and motivated reasoning), and this term I've been auditing a philosophy epistemology seminar my advisor thought would be good for the normative side of my thesis. Last week we covered the dogmatism paradox -- I think it's usually credited to Gilbert Harman, though apparently Kripke had a version of it floating around in old lecture notes too -- the argument that if you know some proposition P, you're thereby already entitled to know that any future evidence against P would have to be misleading (since P's true, nothing that actually points away from it can be tracking the truth), and since you're entitled to disregard evidence you already know is misleading, you can basically write off in advance whatever counter-evidence to P shows up later. Sitting with that for a while, it strikes me as a genuinely rigorous result showing that the "closed-minded" move of discounting contrary evidence isn't a bias at all in a big range of cases -- its just what rationality recommends once your credence in something clears the bar for counting as knowledge.

I want to lean on this for a paper connecting the paradox to the heuristics-and-biases literature on motivated reasoning: my thought is that a decent chunk of what gets pathologized in that literature as "confirmation bias" is actually, at least whenever the underlying belief happens to be true and well-supported, epistemically licensed by this result, and that psychologists have been too quick to code "discounting counter-evidence to things you already know" as a reasoning failure rather than the rational response.

Does that hold up as a legitimate extension, or is there some standard reason this doesn't travel from the seminar room into an actual claim about when discounting evidence is rational? I vaguely remember the seminar leader mentioning "defeasibility" theories of knowledge as somehow relevant to blocking arguments like this one, but I couldn't quite follow how that's supposed to interact with the paradox, and I'd rather actually understand it than cite something I half-remember.
