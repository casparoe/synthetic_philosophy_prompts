I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy of biology, and two weeks ago we spent a session on etiological versus causal-role accounts of biological function: Wright's and Neander's view that a trait's function is whichever effect explains, via natural selection, why the trait exists and persists, set against Cummins's view that a trait's function is just whatever it contributes to some capacity of a containing system that we are trying to explain, without any essential appeal to history. Two of my students, Devon and Nora, kept arguing about it long after the room had cleared, and one of them later forwarded me the email thread that resulted. I've reproduced the substance of it below, and I find I can't fully settle it to my own satisfaction.

(1) Devon opened by insisting the etiological view has to be basically right, because only it makes sense of malfunction: when a heart pumps blood badly, we say it's failing to do what a heart is for, and that's only intelligible because pumping is what hearts were selected and maintained for. A causal-role theory, he said, only ever tells you what a part currently contributes to whatever capacity the theorist has chosen to explain, so a badly pumping heart could just as easily be assigned some other "function" matching what it now actually does; there is no room in the theory for a part to fail at its function, only to differ from it. (2) Nora granted that malfunction is etiology's strongest card, but offered a case against the theory's reliance on actual selection history: suppose a single mutation appears in one generation and happens to help its bearer evade a newly arrived predator, before there has been any generation-on-generation differential reproduction because of that very effect. On the strict etiological story, this brand-new heritable trait has no function yet, since nothing has been selected for anything. But that seems to get the explanatory order backwards, she said: the trait does exactly what it will later be selected for from the moment it appears, so its being selected looks like a consequence of what it already does, not the thing that constitutes its doing it. (3) Devon answered that this is a feature of the view, not an embarrassment: a first-generation beneficial mutation genuinely has no function yet in the sense at issue, any more than a sailor's survival because he happened to grab the one floating plank makes plank-grabbing "the function" of anything he did; usefulness on a single occasion is not yet the kind of thing that can be for anything. (4) Nora accepted that the plank case is different, since it isn't a heritable trait at all, but pressed a different worry: field biologists routinely and confidently attribute functions to structures in organisms whose selective history is entirely unrecoverable, and what they are actually tracking is current causal contribution to some specified capacity, not a reconstruction of selection pressures nobody can access. Maybe, she suggested, she and Devon were simply answering two different questions -- his about what makes a trait's having an effect a matter of biological design at all, hers about the explanatory notion that actually does the work in most day-to-day biological practice -- in which case there was no real disagreement to have. (5) Devon didn't accept the truce: he pointed out that when biologists discover a trait they had assumed was an adaptation is in fact a byproduct that was never selected for the effect in question, they treat this as a substantive discovery that overturns what they are willing to call the trait's function, rather than as a piece of historical trivia that leaves current practice untouched. That, he said, shows people don't merely track current causal contribution when convenient and switch to history when it's available; they treat etiological facts as authoritative over naive descriptions of what a trait presently does, which means the dispute is real after all.
