I'm currently taking a course on mathematical logic that recently moved from Gödel's incompleteness theorems into provability logic, and this week's lecture introduced Löb's theorem alongside its use in the AI safety literature under the name "the Löbian obstacle" — the problem, discussed in a MIRI paper on "tiling agents" by Yudkowsky and Herreshoff, that a proof-based agent cannot adopt, across the board, a policy of trusting whatever it (or a successor it builds via self-modification) proves: a blanket schema of the form "if I can prove P, then P is true" is exactly what Löb's theorem rules out for a consistent system, on pain of trivializing it. My professor treated this as basically old news, remarking in passing that the obstacle is "just Gödel's second incompleteness theorem wearing different clothes," since substituting falsehood for the arbitrary sentence in Löb's schema recovers the familiar fact that no consistent, sufficiently strong theory can prove its own consistency. A couple of us in the class weren't convinced that's the whole story, but nobody managed to pin down exactly what, if anything, is missing from that deflationary picture.

Could you compare the Löbian obstacle with Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem in some depth for me? I'm especially interested in whether the obstacle is genuinely worse for an agent that has to go on trusting an indefinite chain of successors than it is for a single, static system merely reflecting on itself. You needn't survey the various proposed workarounds (hierarchies of successively weaker proof systems, probabilistic substitutes for proof, and so on) in any detail — I'm mainly after a careful comparison with Gödel's theorem itself.
