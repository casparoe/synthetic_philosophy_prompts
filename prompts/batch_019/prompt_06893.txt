I've spent enough time on LessWrong and the wider AI-safety blogosphere that the "commitment races problem" feels almost old hat to me, though I've never been sure what to make of it. The worry, roughly, is that advanced AI systems bargaining with one another have an incentive to lock in early, extreme, irrevocable demands — perhaps by provably committing via exposed source code — since an agent that destroys its own room to maneuver first thereby gains leverage over one that hasn't; if several agents race to do this at once, the result is collision and mutual ruin rather than the stability a single credible commitment produces in isolation.

This is precisely the terrain Thomas Schelling mapped decades earlier: that burning bridges, delegating to an unreasonable subordinate, or making a threat "leave something to chance" can be sources of strength rather than weakness, and that this logic turns dangerous exactly when both sides can play it, as in his work on brinkmanship and the game of Chicken.

Schelling died in 2016, before anyone had packaged this particular dynamic as a problem for AI systems negotiating at machine speed with mutually legible source code. Confronted with it now, how do you think he'd diagnose it? Would he treat it as essentially the brinkmanship problem he had already partly tamed with ideas like verification, hotlines, and staged reciprocity, and expect those remedies to transfer — or would he think something about bargaining between AI systems breaks his own framework, and if so, exactly what? Please reconstruct his likely position from his actual arguments, rather than simply asserting that he'd "probably worry about this."
