I spent my working life as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and only since retiring have I had the leisure to read philosophy properly, rather than in the stolen hours between experiments. Over the past few months I have been working through the literature on Bayesian confirmation theory, and I keep getting stuck on what is called the problem of old evidence, which I gather Clark Glymour first pressed against Bayesianism in his 1980 book *Theory and Evidence*. The textbook illustration, and the one that resonates with me as a physicist, is the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion: astronomers had known since Le Verrier's nineteenth-century calculations that Mercury's orbit precesses about 43 arcseconds per century more than Newtonian mechanics can account for, decades before Einstein wrote down the field equations of General Relativity in 1915. Everyone agrees that the perihelion anomaly counted as powerful confirmation of General Relativity, yet the standard Bayesian formula for confirmation — E confirms H, relative to background K, just when P(H | E&K) > P(H | K), equivalently when P(E | H&K) > P(E | K) — seems to rule this out, since the astronomers' background already contained E with probability 1 long before H was ever formulated, leaving no room for E to raise anything's probability.

The resolution I keep encountering in the secondary literature is the move made by Garber, Jeffrey, and Niiniluoto in the early 1980s: drop the idealization that Bayesian agents are logically omniscient. On this view, what really confirmed General Relativity was not the perihelion data itself, which was old news, but the logical discovery — new to everyone in 1915 — that the field equations entail exactly that anomaly; since no one had previously assigned probability 1 to the claim "H entails E," learning it could legitimately raise one's credence in H. A friend from my old physics department, who has since moved into philosophy of science, assures me this fully dissolves the puzzle, but I remain unconvinced. It seems to me that the very same difficulty recurs, undiminished, for a hypothetical Bayesian agent who really is logically omniscient and so already assigns probability 1 to every entailment among the propositions in its language: for such an agent there is no logical discovery left to make, so the maneuver would leave it permanently unable to be confirmed by old evidence, by either route. If I am right, relaxing logical omniscience would only mask the human, historical version of the problem, while leaving untouched the deeper, in-principle question of what old evidence could possibly mean for an ideal reasoner.

I would like you to make the strongest case you can for exactly that conclusion: that the Garber-Jeffrey-Niiniluoto maneuver fails to dissolve the problem of old evidence even in principle, because a structurally identical difficulty re-emerges for an idealized, logically omniscient Bayesian reasoner, and to do so with enough precision that a probability theorist could not simply dismiss it. Please also engage directly with the most natural rejoinder to my argument: that positing a "logically omniscient agent who nonetheless learns something at the later time" is simply incoherent, so that the scenario I am describing is not one the Bayesian owes any account of in the first place. Tell me whether that rejoinder succeeds and, if it does not, what a Bayesian who wants to retain both logical omniscience and old evidence's confirmatory power should say instead.
