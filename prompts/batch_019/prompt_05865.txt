Over the summer I decided to actually read Descartes instead of just repeating the "I think therefore I am" line everyone knows, and I got stuck on a passage in Part Five of the Discourse on Method that I haven't been able to stop thinking about since. He imagines a machine built to look and move exactly like a human being, and asks how we could ever tell it apart from a real person. His answer comes down to two tests. The first is about language: such a machine, he says, could be built to produce words in response to specific triggers (poke it in one spot and it cries out, ask it a fixed question and it gives a fixed answer), but it could never rearrange its words freely enough to give a sensible, relevant reply to absolutely anything said in its presence, the way even a rather dim human being manages every day. The second test is about the flexibility of behavior in general: he thought a machine would need a separate mechanism arranged in advance for every single thing it could do, so it would inevitably fail at some ordinary task even while excelling at others, whereas human reason is one general-purpose tool that can improvise its way through any situation at all.

Here's what's been bothering me. Modern chatbots seem to pass Descartes' first test pretty easily, or at least come far closer than anything he could have imagined, since they produce relevant, freely varied replies to almost anything typed at them. I mentioned this to a friend who works in software, and she brought up something from philosophy of mind called the "intentional stance" - the idea that we're entitled to treat a system as if it has beliefs and desires whenever doing so makes its behavior easier to predict, without ever settling whether it "really" has an inner mental life at all. That struck me as a completely different way of dodging the question Descartes was actually trying to answer.

So I'd like your help working through this properly. If Descartes were shown a modern large language model - something that holds open-ended conversations, answers questions on almost any topic, and will even describe its own "beliefs" if you ask it to - would you say it passes his language test, his flexibility test, both, or neither? Please engage with his actual two-part argument rather than just his general reputation for treating animals and bodies as machinery, and tell me where you think his criteria would strain hardest against something like an LLM. I'm also curious how you think he'd have reacted to the "intentional stance" move itself: would he have accepted it as a legitimate, modest way of talking about machines that fall short of true thought, or would he have insisted that it quietly changes the subject away from the question he actually cared about - whether something in there truly understands?
