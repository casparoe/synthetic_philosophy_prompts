So this came up in a reading group I'm in on theories of truth (we just finished Kripke's "Outline of a Theory of Truth" and are now slogging through chunks of Gupta & Belnap's revision theory book), and I want an outside opinion because my cohort is kind of split on it. Both are trying to give a rigorous, non-ad-hoc semantics for a truth predicate that can meaningfully apply to sentences of its own language, and both are direct responses to the liar, but the machinery is pretty different. Kripke builds up the extension of "true" via a monotone jump operator using the strong Kleene scheme, starting from the empty extension and climbing to a least fixed point; grounded sentences come out true or false, and the liar just sits in the gap, neither true nor false, at every fixed point. Gupta-Belnap instead treat "true" as a genuinely circular predicate fixed by the Tarski biconditionals used as a *revision rule*: you start from an arbitrary hypothesis about the extension of "true," revise it over and over by classically evaluating the biconditionals against that hypothesis, and track what happens over a revision sequence. Ordinary sentences settle into a stable value no matter where you start; the liar just flips forever, never stabalizing.

What I can't get a clean answer on (including from my advisor, who literally just shrugged) is which of these is the better *philosophical* diagnosis of the paradox, not just the more elegant formalism. Kripke's gaps seem to invite the classic revenge/strengthened-liar problem: "this sentence is not true" looks like it should just be true, precisely because it IS gappy/ungrounded, which smells like the paradox sneaking back in through the informal metalanguage we use to state the theory. Does revision theory actually dodge that, or does "instability" just relocate the same problem somewhere else (e.g. what do we even say about a sentence that asserts its own instability)? And is giving up bivalence in the object language (Kripke's cost) really worse than giving up the idea that every well-formed, meaningful sentence gets a single determinate truth value at all, which seems to be revision theory's cost? I'd love an actual comparative verdict on which one comes closer to a real theory of truth rather than just a fancier redescription of the paradox -- happy to be told they're both dead ends, if that's the honest answer.
