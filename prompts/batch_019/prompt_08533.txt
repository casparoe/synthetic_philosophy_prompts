And here's the student's answer, transcribed exactly as written (typos and all):

"Quine's indeterminacy of translation thesis grows out of his thought experiment of radical translation, where a linguist is dropped into a totally unfamiliar tribe and has to build a translation manual from scratch, with no bilingual dictionary or informant to lean on. Quine borrows the basic setup from Davidson's idea of radical interpretation and gives it his own name. The famous illustration is gavagai: a rabbit scurries by, the native speaker says 'gavagai,' and the linguist's first guess is that it means 'rabbit.' But Quine's point is that the exact same pattern of assent and dissent to 'gavagai' in the presence of rabbits fits equally well with translating it as 'undetached rabbit part,' 'rabbit-stage,' or 'instance of rabbithood.' Since no possible observation of the natives' verbal behavior could ever tell us which of these rival manuals is correct, Quine concludes there is simply no fact of the matter about what gavagai refers to. This is the inscrutability of reference, and its a predicament that belongs specifically to the field linguist facing a language with no shared history with ours -- once a manual is already up and running, as it is for the words of our own language, this kind of indeterminacy doesn't come up anymore, since we're not starting from scratch the way the radical translator is.

This is supposed to be a much deeper problem than the underdetermination of theory by evidence that philosophers of science already accept, where two rival physical theories about unobservable entities can both fit all possible evidence and yet we have no way of deciding between them. What makes translation worse, on Quine's view, is that in the underdetermination case the two theories are still both trying to describe one and the same objective world, so at least in principle one of them could be the true one -- evidence just doesn't reach that far. Indeterminacy of translation isn't like that: it's not that the evidence runs out on a question that still has a determinate answer somewhere out there. Once all the facts about speakers' dispositions to respond to stimulation are fixed, there is no further question left to settle. That's why Quine thinks translation indeterminacy, unlike ordinary underdetermination, shows that meaning itself -- not just our knowledge of the world -- fails to be fixed by all the facts there are."
