I'm a philosophy PhD student, and I've been asked to give the toast at the wedding of a close friend and fellow student, Priya, next month. I'm currently taking a seminar on moral psychology that has spent the last few weeks on the situationist critique of virtue ethics — the argument, associated above all with Gilbert Harman and John Doris (whose book on the subject is bluntly titled Lack of Character), that behavior is far more sensitive to minor, morally irrelevant situational cues, and far less predictable from broad character traits like honesty, courage, or compassion, than folk psychology and traditional virtue ethics tend to assume. We've been reading some of the less overexposed evidence for this, such as Hartshorne and May's old studies showing how weakly children's honesty generalized across superficially similar tests, and Isen and Levin's experiment in which finding a dime in a phone booth made passersby dramatically more likely to help a stranger pick up a dropped stack of papers. Priya's dissertation defends virtue ethics against exactly this critique, in the spirit of writers like Kamtekar and Sreenivasan, who argue that the evidence refutes only a crude, global picture of character traits and leaves plenty of room for traits that are local and cue-sensitive, bound up with the kind of situational perceptiveness Aristotle called practical wisdom. She has told me, only half-joking, that if anyone at her wedding stands up and praises her as "the same generous person in every situation, no matter what," she will privately count it as grounds for a second dissertation chapter.

Roughly half the guests will be philosophers — her committee, our cohort — who will know exactly what's at stake if I get this wrong; the rest are family and friends who have never heard of the fundamental attribution error and shouldn't have to learn about it at a wedding. Could you write me a five-minute toast for Priya that praises her character in terms a situationist-informed virtue ethicist could actually endorse, rather than one that quietly commits the naive cross-situational generalization her own work is devoted to criticizing? I'd like the philosophical point to genuinely constrain what the toast can and can't say, rather than just decorating a joke, while still landing, for the non-philosophers in the room, as a toast and not a lecture.
