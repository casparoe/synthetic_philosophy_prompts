In epistemic logic, ever since Jaakko Hintikka introduced possible-worlds semantics for knowledge in his 1962 book Knowledge and Belief, the framework has been dogged by what is usually called the problem of logical omniscience. On the standard semantics, an agent knows that p just in case p is true throughout every possible world compatible with everything the agent knows; and it follows automatically that the agent knows every logical consequence of what they know, including every tautology of the underlying logic, whether or not they have ever entertained it, would recognize it as true, or could derive it in a lifetime of effort. Logicians usually treat this as an unwelcome idealization to be patched away, through impossible-worlds models, "awareness" functions that restrict which propositions an agent is actually attending to, or resource-bounded and algorithmic notions of knowledge, precisely because it is so obviously false of any real mathematician, chess player, or computer.

It struck me recently that this "defect" might make far richer material for fiction than for a technical footnote. I'd like you to write a short story, in the spirit of writers like Borges or Ted Chiang, who enjoy taking a dry formal premise and following it out to its full human (or post-human) consequences, about a character who is, or suddenly becomes, logically omniscient in this precise sense: at every moment they already know every logical and mathematical consequence of everything they believe, instantly, effortlessly, and without any possibility of deriving it incorrectly. I am much less interested in a generic superintelligence story than in the specific epistemic and experiential strangeness of this exact premise: what happens to notions like proof, discovery, effort, and surprise for a mind that was never logically ignorant of anything to begin with? Is there even still a difference, for such a character, between knowing that a theorem holds and understanding why it does?

You're free to invent whatever setting, plot, and voice you like, human, machine, or otherwise, so long as the story stays anchored in that specific tension rather than drifting into a more generic tale about an all-knowing being.
