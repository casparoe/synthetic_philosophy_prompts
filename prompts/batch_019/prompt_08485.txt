So I went down a rabbit hole a while back reading about Ayer's near-death experience -- I think I first ran into the story in a Scott Alexander comments thread, actually. He choked on some smoked salmon while in hospital in 1988, his heart stopped for a few minutes, and he came out of it talking about a red light and some kind of "ministers" in charge of space who weren't doing their jobs right. He wrote the whole thing up in an essay (something like "What I Saw When I Was Dead," if I'm remembering the title right) and then had to write a follow-up after his own doctor basically said he probably wasnt oxygen-starved for long enough for it to count as a real NDE. What gets me about the story is that this is the same guy who built his whole career on the verification principle, and yet all he ever concedes is that the experience "slightly weakened" his conviction that death is the end -- never budges further than that. Its honestly a nice little case study in not overupdating on weird personal evidence, and I want to use it for a recurring bit on the philosophy podcast I co-run, where instead of the usual highlight-reel interview we imagine a historical philosopher stuck in the hot seat at one specific moment in their career.

Could you draft me 8-10 interview questions for a hypothetical sit-down with Ayer right after he wrote that follow-up piece -- still a little prickly about the pushback, but too much of an empiricist to just drop the subject? Nothing that lets him get away with just re-describing what he saw; I want questions that actually push on the philosophical tension. At minimum, give me one question on whether the experience counts as exactly the kind of first-person self-verification he himself once allowed for -- I recall him conceding, way back in Language, Truth and Logic, that "I will survive my death" could be meaningful in principle because a surviving-me could verify it later even if the rest of us can't, so does the vision of the red light actually satisfy his own old criterion or does it fall short somehow; one on the "you probably weren't starved of oxygen long enough for this to be interesting" objection from his own doctor, and whether his response to it is principled empiricism or just motivated reasoning; and one on whether he'd extend that same "fine, that nudges my credence a little" generosity to a religious patient who had the identical experience and concluded it was evidence of God, or whether that's a double standard he can't actually defend. For each of those, plus whatever other questions you add to round it out to 8-10, bake in a quick follow-up for the answer he's most likely to give -- I've got a pretty good guess what dodge he reaches for on at least two of these, and I don't want him getting away with it.
