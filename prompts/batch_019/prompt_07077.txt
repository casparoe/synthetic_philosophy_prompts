I cover tech and money for a business magazine, and I've been on the AI-funding-boom beat for a few months now, which is probably why I couldn't stop replaying an argument two friends of mine had at dinner last week. I'd love an outside read on it since I'm too close to the story to referee it myself.

My friend Jonah, a venture partner, was defending the eye-watering valuations going into AI labs right now. (1) His claim: this isn't irrational at all, it's textbook rational herding — a Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch information cascade. Once a couple of funds with genuinely superior technical diligence commit at a huge number, every later investor is doing the Bayesian-optimal thing by treating that commitment as a strong signal and discounting their own weaker private doubts, same as picking the crowded restaurant over the empty one next door. (2) My other friend Renata, who does behavioral econ for a living, said that's too generous: a real BHW cascade is fragile, its supposed to shatter the second a contrary public signal shows up, and this market has kept climbing right through plenty of bad news (blown compute budgets, underwhelming launches, the works). What's actually happening, she said, is reputational herding in the Scharfstein-Stein sense: fund managers aren't updating on information about the technology at all, they're managing their own career risk with their LPs, since sitting out a winner alone looks far worse to your investors than losing money on a loser everyone else also bet on. (3) Jonah shot back that Renata's distinction is fake: Scharfstein-Stein managers are rational Bayesian agents too, just optimizing against LPs who are themselves inferring manager skill from outcomes — so it's cascades all the way up, and there's no principled reason to call inferring-from-other-investors rational while inferring-from-what-will-please-your-LPs isn't. (4) Renata's comeback was that both mechanisms can be "rational" in the modeling sense and still make different predictions: genuine information cascades should be reversible almost as soon as new public information lands, while reputational cascades can survive a lot of bad news since the career risk doesn't resolve until the whole fund cycle gets judged years later — and she thinks that's the better fit for a market that keeps shrugging off bad headlines.

They never settled it, I just topped up everyone's wine. Could you adjudicate this move by move?
