I've been reporting a piece for our science section timed to the anniversary of Galileo's run-in with the Inquisition, which sent me down a rabbit hole reading his 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina and Bellarmine's letter to Foscarini from that same year. I brought this up at dinner with two friends -- one did a masters in early modern history, the other just reads way too much for fun -- and it turned into a real argument that I couldn't referee myself, so I'm hoping you can.

Nate (the historian) opened by saying Galileo's scriptural argument in the Letter was serious theology, not spin: he's drawing directly on Augustine's own principle that scripture's purpose is salvation, not astronomy, so when nature is conclusively demonstrated to be some way, any passage that seems to contradict it is one we've misread -- it's the interpretation that has to move, not the physical fact. Sofia said fine, that's a defensible principle in the abstract, but Galileo was cashing a check he hadn't earned, since heliocentrism wasn't conclusively demonstrated in 1615 -- there was no detectable stellar parallax, the telescope evidence (phases of Venus, Jupiter's moons) fit the Tychonic system just as well as Copernicus's, and Galileo's own pet physical argument, the one from the tides, turned out to be flat wrong. So really he was asking the Church to reinterpret scripture on credit. Nate came back that Bellarmine's insistence on a "true demonstration" rather than a hypothesis that merely "saves the appearances" was itself a double standard, since nobody was demanding that kind of proof from the Ptolemaic or Tychonic pictures either, which were every bit as underdetermined by the data -- so the bar was being raised specifically for Copernicus. Sofia's comeback was that even a real double standard on Bellarmine's part doesn't let Galileo off the hook: showing your opponent applied a standard unevenly isn't the same as showing you met it, and Galileo's slide from "in principle scripture should yield to conclusive demonstration" to "therefore reinterpret it now, for my particular theory" is exactly the move that got him in trouble, whatever you think of Bellarmine.

They both want to know who actually won this, so could you go through it move by move -- I'll call them 1 (Nate), 2 (Sofia), 3 (Nate), 4 (Sofia) in the order above -- and say who's got the better of each individual point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if there's some bigger thing about what Galileo was actually doing that both of them are talking past, I'd love to hear that too.
