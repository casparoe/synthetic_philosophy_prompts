I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in formal epistemology and decision theory, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on dynamic choice — which is probably why the following has been bothering me more than it should. A few of us in the department run a standing reading group with people from the university's AI safety lab, since so much of the interesting recent work on Newcomb-style problems comes out of that world, and after last week's session two regulars, Felix (finishing a CS PhD on AI safety) and Amara (a philosophy postdoc working on rational choice), got into an argument over "counterfactual mugging" that nobody managed to settle before people had to leave.

For anyone who hasn't run into it: Omega, stipulated to be a perfect predictor, tells you it has just flipped a fair coin. Had the coin landed heads, Omega would have handed you $10,000 — but only on condition that you would hand over $100 were the coin to land tails instead. The coin landed tails, and Omega is now asking for the $100. Felix, defending Wei Dai's updateless decision theory, argued that paying is simply correct: the only coherent way to evaluate the choice is by asking which policy — fixed before the coin flip, as a mapping from "heads" or "tails" to an action — has the higher expected value, and "pay on tails" beats "refuse" by $4,950 in expectation, so that settles it. Amara didn't dispute the arithmetic, but said it doesn't show what Felix needs it to show: by the time you're actually holding the $100, you already know the coin came up tails, so the $10,000 isn't a missed-but-live possibility, it's simply off the table, and paying just hands money away for nothing; an earlier hypothetical self's preference over policies isn't automatically a reason for the self who's actually being asked to pay, any more than a contract that self never signed. Felix answered that this is the old dispute between "sophisticated" and "resolute" choosers by another name: an agent who re-evaluates from scratch at every decision point, ignoring earlier commitments, is exactly the agent Omega wouldn't have rewarded to begin with, and the same structure is what strands people in Parfit's Hitchhiker — refusing to pay once you're safely in town is exactly what a perfect predictor would have foreseen, leaving you in the desert to begin with. Amara granted that having the disposition to pay is good for that very reason, but said this shows only which disposition is good to have, not that the act of paying, once nothing further hangs on it, is itself rational; she added that the whole ex-ante calculation quietly assumes we're the kind of agents likely to encounter predictors who reward this disposition, when nothing rules out a mirror-image predictor who rewards the opposite, so treating "expected value across the branches" as the neutral, forced way to score the decision already smuggles in an assumption Felix hasn't earned.

Neither of them budged, and I'd like a more careful read than either of us managed on the spot. I'm especially interested in whether Felix's insistence that Omega is a genuinely perfect predictor defuses Amara's disposition-versus-act distinction, since it rules out merely appearing resolute without being so, or whether her distinction survives that stipulation anyway; and separately, whether her closing point about mirror-image predictors is a real problem for updatelessness or just a restatement of her first objection in fancier dress.
