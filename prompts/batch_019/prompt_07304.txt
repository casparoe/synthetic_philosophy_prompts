I've been stuck on this point in the philosophical zombie debate for a couple weeks now and could use some help untangling it. The basic argument I get fine: Chalmers says you can coherently conceive of a being that's physically and functionally identical to a normal human, down to the last atom and behavior, but which has no inner experience at all, "all dark inside." From that conceivability he wants to move to the claim that zombies are metaphysically possible, and if they're possible then physicalism about consciousness must be false, since physicalism says the physical facts fix all the facts, phenomenal ones included.

My roommate, who's more into phil of language, keeps bringing up what seems like an obvious objection: we can also "conceive" of water turning out not to be H2O, or of Hesperus not being Phosphorus, yet those are supposedly metaphysically impossible given that they're necessary a posteriori truths a la Kripke, we just didn't know the relevant empirical facts yet. So why doesn't the zombie case work the same way? Maybe consciousness just turns out, a posteriori, to be identical to some physical or functional process, and our ability to "conceive" otherwise is merely a symptom of not knowing that yet, rather than evidence of genuine metaphysical possibility. I know Chalmers has this whole apparatus of two-dimensional semantics (something about primary vs secondary intensions) thats supposed to explain why the zombie case is different from the water case, but every summary I've found either waves at it in one sentence or dives straight into notation that loses me completely. Could you walk me through, fairly intuitively, how that response is actually supposed to work, and give me your honest take on whether it actually succeeds in blocking the Kripkean objection, or whether its just relocating the problem one level up?
