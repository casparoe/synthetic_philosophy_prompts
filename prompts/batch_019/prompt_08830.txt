The question, worth 20 points, read as follows: "The situationist critique, drawing on results from social psychology, is often presented as posing a serious challenge to Aristotelian virtue ethics. In no more than two paragraphs: (a) reconstruct the argument from the empirical findings to the conclusion that few if any people possess the kind of character traits virtue ethics requires, citing at least one classic study; and (b) explain how a defender of virtue ethics might resist the argument by rejecting one of its premises, rather than by disputing the empirical findings themselves."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"Situationists like John Doris and Gilbert Harman argue that when psychologists actually go looking for the robust, cross-situationally stable character traits that virtue ethics needs -- traits like honesty or courage that should produce consistent behavior across many different circumstances -- they don't find them. The clearest case is Hartshorne and May's studies of schoolchildren's honesty from the 1920s: a child who didn't cheat on an exam was not reliably the same child who wouldn't steal a coin left in a desk or lie about a game, and the correlations between a child's behavior in one setting and the next were consistently low. Milgram's obedience experiments make a similar point at the level of adults: ordinary people's willingness to keep shocking a stranger tracked features of the immediate situation -- whether the experimenter was physically present, how audible the victim's protests were -- far better than it tracked anything about the individual's own settled character. Harman goes furthest, arguing in a paper literally titled 'The Nonexistence of Character Traits' that people do not, in general, possess character traits in anything like the sense virtue ethics requires: not merely that traits are hard to discern under experimental conditions, but that behavior is essentially a function of the immediate situation, so that a person's own stable contribution to what they do is negligible.

A defender of virtue ethics can resist this by denying that Aristotelian virtue was ever supposed to yield the context-blind consistency the studies are looking for. On Aristotle's view, the virtuous person acts from phronesis, practical wisdom, which is exactly the capacity to discern what a given situation calls for and calibrate one's response to its particular features rather than apply one fixed rule regardless of circumstance. If that is right, a low correlation between someone's behavior in one situation and their behavior in another is not evidence against virtue; it is exactly what virtue should look like, since virtue is supposed to be responsive to situational detail rather than blind to it. On this reading, the fact that Milgram's participants behaved so differently depending on small changes to the setup -- whether the order came by phone or from someone standing in the room, whether the victim's protests were audible or not -- should be read not as evidence against character but as confirmation of the same fine-grained situational responsiveness that practical wisdom involves."
