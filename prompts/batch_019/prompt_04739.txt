I'm a cog sci PhD student (i actually work on metacognition and confidence judgments, which makes this extra annoying), and I signed up as a paid subject in a labmate's study on a mild over-the-counter nootropic stack, nothing exotic, just the kind of caffeine/l-theanine combo you can buy on amazon. Didn't think much of it until the debrief email went out after the study wrapped: turns out the treatment group didn't score any higher on the actual reasoning tasks than the placebo group did, they just rated their own answers as noticeably more likely to be correct. And I was in the treatment group. So this isn't some abstract "people in general get overconfident" thing, it's specifically "you, right now, were running on a brain that does this."

Here's the part that's actually bugging me. During that exact week of the study, I finally cracked a gnarly proof for my dissertation that I'd been stuck on for like two months. I've gone back over it since, cold, on separate days, and it still looks completely solid -- my advisor looked at it too and didn't spot an issue. But I can't shake the thought that if the finding is "this stuff makes people feel unusually sure of themselves without being any more accurate," I now have a specific, personal reason to distrust exactly this feeling of "yeah, this is definately right." The annoying recursive part is that re-checking doesn't actually settle it, since a correct-feeling recheck is exactly what the effect predicts too. I need to decide pretty soon whether this becomes an actual chapter, and "I checked it carefully and it still holds up" doesn't feel like enough anymore -- but I don't know what would be enough, or whether that's even the right question to ask. Is there a principled way to figure out how much to discount a belief you can't currently find anything wrong with, just because of where the confidence came from? My advisor's take was basically "if the proof is right, it's right," which feels like it's dodging the actual issue.
