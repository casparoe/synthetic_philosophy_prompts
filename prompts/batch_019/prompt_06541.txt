The question I gave was:

"State Aumann's agreement theorem precisely, including the assumptions it needs. Explain what work 'common knowledge' is doing in the result, as opposed to the two agents merely both being aware of each other's posterior. Then describe how the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result is supposed to get you from a situation with no common knowledge to one where it holds. Finally, give one reason why real human disagreements don't obviously run afoul of any of this."

Here's the answer:

"Aumann's theorem says: if two Bayesian agents share the same prior over the state space, and it's common knowledge between them what each one's posterior probability for some event E currently is, then those two posteriors have to be equal — even though the two agents can have totally different private information going into it. So 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree,' at least about a single event, given these assumptions.

Common knowledge is doing more work here than just each of us knowing the other's number. If I know your posterior is 0.7, that's evidence I should fold into my own estimate — so for a persistent gap to survive, it has to be common knowledge that we disagree: you know my number, I know yours, you know that I know, I know that you know, and so on up the chain. That's exactly the mechanism Aumann's proof relies on: he has the two agents alternately state their current probability for E out loud, and because the underlying state space is finite, they run out of room to keep revising their estimates and are forced into agreement after finitely many rounds. Because they keep updating on each other's stated number until neither has anything new to add, by the end each has effectively absorbed the other's private information too, not just the bottom-line probability — so the shared number they land on is common knowledge and equal, matching the theorem.

As for why this doesn't make two smart people who disagree in real life irrational: mostly because the common prior assumption almost never literally holds, and even leaving that aside, ordinary conversation basically never produces genuine common knowledge of each other's exact posteriors. People convey impressions, not calibrated numbers, and don't fully trust each other's honesty or rationality the way the model assumes."
