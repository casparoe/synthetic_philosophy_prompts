I'm a backend engineer with no formal background in philosophy or decision theory, but a few of us from my old university house have kept up a monthly video-call "puzzle night" for years, cycling through logic puzzles, probability brain-teasers, and the odd bit of game theory. Last session someone brought up the absent-minded driver problem, and it derailed the rest of the call because two of my friends got stuck on how to interpret it and never really made up. I went home and wrote a quick script to brute-force the optimal number, mostly to confirm the arithmetic wasn't the issue, and I'd like a second, more philosophically literate opinion on who had the better of the argument before I bring it back to the group.

For context in case you want to double-check anything: a driver is on a highway with two consecutive, visually identical exits, and needs the second one to get home. Taking the first exit by mistake pays off 0; missing both and driving straight through pays off 1; finding the second exit pays off 4. The driver is "absent-minded" in the strong sense used in this literature: standing at an exit, he cannot tell whether it's the first or the second, and he has no memory of whether he's already passed through one. Since he can't tell the two occasions apart, he's stuck using one and the same probability, call it α, of continuing at either exit. Maximizing the expected payoff over α gives α = 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3 (versus 1 for always continuing and 0 for always exiting). Nobody in the group doubts that part; my script agrees to several decimal places. The disagreement was entirely about what that 2/3 is supposed to tell the driver to actually do once he's standing at an exit. Roughly reconstructed, with Marcus (who reads a lot of decision-theory blogs) and Sofia (who did some formal game theory in a stats master's) going back and forth:

(1) Marcus: the rational thing is to commit to the α = 2/3 policy and execute it at every exit, no further thought required — that's the policy that maximizes expected payoff, full stop.
(2) Sofia: "committing in the abstract" isn't a decision; at some point you're actually standing at an exit, not knowing if it's the first or the second, and you have to act. Conditional on being at some exit, simple counting says you're more likely to be at the first than the second — weights of 1 versus α, i.e., 3-to-2 odds when α = 2/3 — and once you plug those odds into an expected-value comparison, continuing beats exiting no matter what α you started with, including 2/3 itself.
(3) Marcus: that can't be the right way to reason, because if it were sound at every exit, you'd continue at both of them, and driving past both is worth only 1 — strictly worse than the 4/3 the actual policy gets you. If in-the-moment reasoning talks you out of the provably best plan, the reasoning is what's broken, not the plan.
(4) Sofia: that's not a rebuttal, it's just a restatement of the puzzle. Nobody's disputing that always-continuing is worse in expectation; the question is whether "expectation, computed before you ever started driving" is even the right thing for the person standing at the exit, right now, to be maximizing. This is exactly the move philosophers debate in the Sleeping Beauty problem, where plenty of serious people think you should update your credence upon waking in just this destabilizing way.
(5) Marcus: he thinks the fix is that "continue now, then definitely exit at the next one" was never a real option, because taking it would require telling the two exits apart, or at least remembering you already chose to continue once — exactly what the driver can't do. Any rule adopted right now automatically governs the other, indistinguishable occasion too, so the honest comparison is continue-and-keep-using-the-same-rule versus exit-now, not continue-then-definitely-exit versus exit-now. Insist on that, and 2/3 stops looking unstable and turns back into the consistent answer.
(6) Sofia: granting that this patches the specific calculation, she doesn't think it dissolves the puzzle — it just shows that ordinary Bayesian updating had to be specially restricted to avoid a bad outcome here, and that's itself the interesting result. Standard decision theory assumes there's always some principled, agent-independent fact about "what are the odds I'm at the first exit versus the second"; this case suggests that for a mind that can't individuate its own past moments, there might not be one.
