I'm reporting on how scientists invoke the idea of "emergence" to explain phenomena that resist easy reduction to their parts, and I want to get the philosophical grounding right before I write this up. Two quite different technical accounts of emergence recur throughout the philosophy of science literature. On C. D. Broad's classical account, a genuinely emergent property is one governed by a fundamentally novel "trans-ordinal" law: a regularity linking a lower level to a higher one that could not be predicted, even in principle, from complete knowledge of the lower level alone. On Mark Bedau's "weak emergence," by contrast, a macro-property counts as emergent provided it is fully fixed by, and derivable from, the micro-level — just not by any analytic shortcut, only by brute-force simulation. Please compare these two accounts of emergence, and then use the comparison to adjudicate a live case: when neuroscientists describe consciousness as an "emergent" property of the brain, is weak emergence really all they are entitled to claim, or does consciousness call for something closer to Broad's stronger notion? Defend a definite answer rather than presenting the two options as equally balanced.
