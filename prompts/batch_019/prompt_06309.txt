I co-run a reading group that mixes my philosophy of mind grad students with a few people from the CS department, and a few weeks back we spent two sessions working through recent interpretability papers -- the sparse-autoencoder "features" work, the Golden Gate Claude stunt, the Othello-GPT board-state probing stuff. Afterwards two of the regulars, Nathan (he's on the ML side) and Ilana (philosophy side), ended up arguing about it at the pub for like an hour after everyone else had left, and I've been chewing on it since. I stayed out of it at the time and I honestly still can't decide who's got the better of it, so I'd like your read on the actual exchange, not just the topic in general.

(1) Nathan's opening move: when Anthropic clamped the "Golden Gate Bridge" feature they'd pulled out via dictionary learning, the model brought up the bridge constantly no matter what you asked it, and when you intervene on the linear direction that tracks board state in Othello-GPT, the models predicted legal moves change exactly the way they should if that direction really encoded "my piece is on this square." That's a causal, systematic, predictive relationship between an inner state and worldly content, he said -- the same kind of evidence a neuroscientist accepts for saying a hippocampal place cell represents location, so the nets really have representations and this isn't just a convenient way of talking. (2) Ilana's reply was that causal sufficiency for a behavior change can't be enough, or every lever counts as a representation -- stimulate a motor neuron and you reliably get a twitch, turn a thermostat's dial and you reliably change when the furnace kicks on, and nobody wants to call the neuron a representation of the twitch. What's actually missing, she said, is what Ramsey called the job description challenge: a state only counts as a representation if the system itself uses it as a stand-in, consulting and combining it with other states, not just because an experimenter outside the system found a knob that moves behavior. She also brought up feature splitting -- crank up the sparse autoencoder's dictionary size and one feature fractures into a dozen narrower, sometimes barely related ones -- which she took as a sign we're imposing a grid on the network rather then discovering its actual joints. (3) Nathan came back that feature splitting is predicted by superposition theory, not arbitrary: a network packs in more features than it has dimensions by treating them as a roughly hierarchical structure, so a feature fracturing under higher capacity is more like zooming into a real map than drawing a new one. And against the "used by the system" requirement he pointed to steering vectors -- pull a direction from a handful of examples and it generalizes to shift behavior on totally unseen prompts in systematic, composable ways, which is exactly the free compositionality Fodor and Pylyshyn once argued networks couldn't deliver in principle, so getting it is not nothing. (4) Ilana's last move, before I called it a night, was that behavioral generalization still isn't the system consulting a representation the way it consults a belief -- smooth interpolation over a well organized statistical space can mimic compositional behavior without anything playing that further role. She reminded him that word2vec did "king - man + woman = queen" over a decade ago and nobody thought that settled realism about its representations, so maybe predictive success at this level is just silent on the metaphysical question rather than evidence for either side of it.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who has the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd also like to know what you think both of them are still missing -- I have a nagging feeling they're talking past each other on what "representation" even has to mean here. And close with your own bottom line in a sentence or two: given everything on the table, should we be realists, instrumentalists, or something in between about these posited neural-network "representations"?
