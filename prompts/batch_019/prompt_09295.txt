A close friend of mine works in the student success office at her university, and last week we spent most of a dinner arguing about a predictive analytics tool her office is piloting. The tool assigns every incoming first-year student a numerical dropout-risk score so that a limited pool of advising hours can be targeted at whoever needs it most, and the vendor markets it as "fair" on the grounds that it is well calibrated across first-generation and continuing-generation students alike: among students given, say, a 70 percent risk score, roughly 70 percent of each group actually withdraw within the year. What troubled my friend is that the tool's false-negative rate is nonetheless nearly twice as high among first-generation students, so at-risk first-gen students are disproportionately the ones who never get flagged for outreach. I suggested the office simply ask the vendor to retune the model so as to equalize the false-negative rate across the two groups instead; she said a data scientist on her team had told her this was mathematically impossible to do without sacrificing calibration, given that the two groups' actual dropout rates are not equal to begin with. Neither of us could explain why calibration ought to take precedence over equalized error rates, or the reverse, and we went back and forth for the better part of an hour without getting anywhere.

I gather this is more or less the situation that the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness were developed to formalize -- I have in mind the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan result on the incompatibility of calibration with balance for the positive and negative classes, and Chouldechova's closely related result for binary classifiers -- but I don't know the literature well enough to see what actually follows from it in practice. Could you lay out the most important implications of these results for a real institutional decision like the one my friend's office is facing, where the two groups' base rates genuinely differ and the tool is far from a perfect predictor? I am especially curious whether the literature offers any principled, rather than merely political, grounds for preferring one criterion over the other in a case like this; what is actually gained and lost by the alternative some data scientists favor of applying different score thresholds to different groups so as to equalize error rates directly; and whether there is any way to avoid simply picking a side and being transparent about the trade-off, or whether that is in fact the honest conclusion the literature points to.
