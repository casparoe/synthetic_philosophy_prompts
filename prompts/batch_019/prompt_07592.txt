Karl Popper's charge that Darwinian natural selection fails his own test for a genuine scientific theory is one of the more contested episodes in his career, not least because he later said he'd changed his mind about it. I came across an old exam question on this, along with one student's answer, while researching a piece on how that episode gets taught, and I'd like an independent, rigorous read on the answer before I make any use of it.

The question was:

"Karl Popper claimed that Darwin's theory of natural selection does not qualify as a testable scientific theory. Reconstruct his argument, note any examples that were used to illustrate the theory's (un)falsifiability, and explain whether Popper maintained this position for the rest of his career."

The student wrote:

"Popper's charge, developed in his later writings such as Unended Quest, was that Darwinism does not meet his own criterion for a genuinely scientific theory: it cannot be falsified. The worry centers on the concept of fitness. If 'fitness' just means whatever traits happen to lead to survival and reproduction, then 'the fittest survive' reduces to 'those who survive, survive' — a statement no observation could ever contradict. Popper concluded that natural selection, so understood, is not a strictly testable theory but what he called a 'metaphysical research programme': not meaningless, but not yet science either, since it forbids nothing.

Even so, Popper did not think the theory was empty of content. He pointed out, for instance, that if today's mammals really descended with modification from earlier forms, we should never expect to find a fossil rabbit in Precambrian rock, and that such a find would refute the theory outright — evidence, in his view, that natural selection does rule something out. But this concession was limited: his considered position through most of the 1970s remained that natural selection, understood as the claim that existing species arose from earlier ones through a long history of adaptive modification, is still too accommodating to count as properly falsifiable, since virtually any surviving trait can be redescribed after the fact as the fitter option.

Popper did not maintain this verdict indefinitely. In a lecture given late in his life, he said plainly that he had changed his mind about the testability and logical status of the theory of natural selection, and described this as a recantation of his earlier position. After that, he stopped calling Darwinism 'merely' a metaphysical research programme, though he continued to think it had a somewhat different logical character from theories in physics — closer, in some respects, to a framework for historical explanation than to a set of universal laws."

Could you grade this out of 20 points?
