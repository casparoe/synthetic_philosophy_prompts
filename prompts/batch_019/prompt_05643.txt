Since retiring from a career in experimental physics I have been spending my afternoons on decision theory, and one argument I keep meeting there refuses to settle in my mind. The money-pump construction is meant to show that any agent whose preferences are incomplete, or otherwise fail to satisfy the axioms needed for an expected-utility representation, can be exploited through a sequence of trades that leaves it worse off for certain — and hence that a rational agent, or, as is now argued in discussions of artificial intelligence, a sufficiently capable artificial one, will be driven toward maximizing some fixed utility function. What I cannot see is why this forces the conclusion rather than merely recommending it: an agent that commits to a plan in advance, and declines whichever trade would complete a foreseen losing sequence, seems able to keep its preferences incomplete indefinitely without ever paying the pump. And even where full coherence is granted, nothing in the argument guarantees that the resulting utility function is simple or stable enough to underwrite the further, more alarming claim that such an agent will pursue one overriding, long-term goal. Please write a dialogue between two decision theorists of equal sophistication, one who thinks the money-pump argument survives these worries and one who thinks it does not, in which each engages the other's specific moves rather than simply restating a position.
