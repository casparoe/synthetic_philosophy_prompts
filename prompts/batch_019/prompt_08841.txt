After spending the better part of three decades running an experimental condensed-matter physics lab, I retired two years ago and have since thrown myself into philosophy with, as my wife puts it, the enthusiasm of a convert. I now help a small university-affiliated humanities forum produce a recorded interview series, and next month's guest is a political philosopher who has built her recent reputation on defending and extending Hannah Arendt's writing on lying in politics: the 1967 essay contrasting the fragility of "factual truth" with the relative security of "rational" or mathematical truth, and, a few years later, her reflections on the Pentagon Papers, where she distinguishes the traditional statesman's lie from a specifically modern, image-management style of political deception whose practitioners end up deceiving themselves as much as anyone else. The monograph that earned her the invitation reapplies this framework to contemporary "post-truth" politics, and I have spent the last two weeks rereading both essays in preparation.

I would like your help drafting eight to ten interview questions that move well past a simple restatement of these distinctions. Coming to this from the sciences, I would especially like one question that presses Arendt's claim about the peculiar fragility of factual truth against the obvious rejoinder that all observation is theory-laden, and that her own fallback -- corroboration by a plurality of witnesses -- simply relocates the contingency she worries about into intersubjective agreement rather than escaping it. I would also like a question on the puzzle that, on her account, the modern liar ends up deceiving himself as much as his audience: why exactly should self-deception make organized political lying more dangerous than the traditional kind, given that the classic worry about lying was always that the liar keeps a clear, cynical grip on the very truth he is hiding? And I want at least one question that tests the account against something Arendt never had to reckon with -- generative media and deepfakes -- asking whether her claim that power can destroy factual truth but can never manufacture one in its place still holds once wholesale fabrication becomes nearly costless.

For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is likely to give. If, for instance, she defends the self-deception point by pointing, as Arendt does, to the Pentagon's own "problem-solving" systems analysts, I would like the follow-up to ask whether that diagnosis does not uncomfortably implicate quantitative, model-driven reasoning quite generally, including in the sciences, or whether Arendt's target is really something narrower. Please close the sequence with a question asking her to specify what would count, by her own lights, as spin, honest error, or after-the-fact reassessment rather than the specific phenomenon she calls lying, so that the conversation ends on the boundaries of the concept rather than on one more illustration of it.
