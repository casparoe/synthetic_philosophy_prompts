I sit on my university's research-prioritization subcommittee, a small faculty body that recommends each year how a modest discretionary fund for "high-risk, high-reward" scholarship should be allocated across departments. This cycle's most contentious case is a renewal request from a three-person group in the mathematics department working on the fine-grained statistics of prime gaps, a problem in analytic number theory that, as the applicants themselves acknowledge, has no anticipated use outside number theory itself. Rather than promising future applications, their proposal argues from history: it invokes Wigner's remark on the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics in the natural sciences, and notes that Hardy devoted part of A Mathematician's Apology to celebrating number theory precisely because it seemed too pure to serve any practical, let alone military, purpose -- only for the arithmetic of prime factorization to become, a generation later, the basis of RSA encryption. On this basis, the applicants argue that the committee has good reason to keep funding work with no current use.

Two other subcommittee members are unpersuaded, for different reasons, and I'd like the memo to take both seriously. One argues that the appeal proves too much: if a historical record of eventual, unpredictable usefulness is reason enough to fund this proposal, it is reason enough to fund almost any pure mathematics whatsoever, so it cannot be doing the discriminating work a funding decision actually needs. This member points out that the very department under discussion has already had one once-purely-internal line of work -- elliptic-curve arithmetic -- become the basis of elliptic-curve cryptography decades after the fact, while other lines, this proposal's problem included, remain without any known use after a comparably long history. The other thinks the appeal to Wigner rests on a conflation. They read his essay as posing a puzzle about why mathematics invented for internal, aesthetic reasons, in advance of any empirical motivation, so often turns out to fit nature with great precision once applied -- not as an empirical generalization about how often "useless" mathematics eventually finds a use. They add that critics of that weaker reading, such as the engineer Derek Abbott, have argued it survives only by selectively remembering the number theory that became RSA while forgetting the much larger body of pure mathematics that never found any application at all.

I need to circulate a short written recommendation before next month's meeting on whether to renew this group's funding. Please draft it. It should reach a specific recommendation -- renew, decline, or renew with conditions -- attach a time frame for revisiting the question, and engage honestly enough with whichever of the two objections ends up on the losing side that the member who raised it would recognize their own argument in your response.
