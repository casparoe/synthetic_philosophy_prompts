"In 'Internal and External Reasons,' Williams argues that every genuine reason-for-action statement is, at bottom, an internal one. (a) Explain the internal/external distinction as he draws it, including the roles of the 'subjective motivational set' S and of sound deliberation. (b) Reconstruct his central argument against the intelligibility of external reasons. (c) Use the distinction to say what Williams is, and isn't, entitled to conclude about an amoralist who has no interest whatsoever in being moral."

Here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"Williams splits reason-statements into two kinds. An internal reason statement says A has a reason to phi just in case phi-ing would serve some element already in A's S -- his desires, projects, evaluations, and loyalties -- where this connection is one A could reach by sound deliberation from where he currently stands. Because S is just whatever the agent happens to want, internal reasons are at bottom self-interested: they bind you only insofar as satisfying them is good for you. External reason statements, by contrast, claim A has a reason to phi regardless of his S. Williams's case against these turns on the idea that practical deliberation is basically inference from an existing end to whatever serves it, so if nothing in S already answers to the alleged external reason, no amount of correct reasoning could produce a new motivation -- reasoning can reorganize your ends, but it can't manufacture one out of nothing. That's why appealing to 'sound deliberation' can't save external reasons: deliberation is calculation from a given end, and calculation needs a starting end to work from.

Applied to the amoralist: since morality answers to nothing in his S, it is simply false, on Williams's view, that he has a reason to be moral -- we can wish he were different, or try to frighten or condition him into compliance, but we can't rationally argue him into it. And since this sinks the whole external-reasons project, Williams basically ends up in the same place as Mackie's argument from queerness: talk of requirements that bind us 'no matter what we want' was always a mistake, so moral judgments are never strictly speaking true or false."
