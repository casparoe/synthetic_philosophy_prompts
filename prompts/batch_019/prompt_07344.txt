I'm reporting a longer piece on undercover journalism right now — the recent run of reporters posing as renters, patients, job applicants etc to get into places that would never let a notebook through the front door. While digging into the history of 'lying for a good cause' I fell down a genuinely weird rabbit hole: apparently around 420 AD a guy named Consentius wrote to Augustine proposing that someone fake-convert to the Priscillianist heresy so they could infiltrate the sect and expose it to the authorities — basically a 5th-century undercover investigation. Augustine's answer in Contra Mendacium was a flat no, and it wasn't just 'lying is bad' — in De Mendacio he'd already worked out this whole scheme where even a lie told purely to help someone, with zero intent to harm anyone (he calls these 'officious' lies), still counts as a sin, just a smaller one than a malicious lie. No amount of good intention or good outcome moves the needle for him, which is a way more hardcore stance than literally anyone on my newsrooms ethics committee holds, including the guy who votes no on everything.

Which is the actual problem, because I somehow got roped onto that committee this year and we've got a real case in front of us. A colleague wants to get hired under a fake name at a regional assisted-living chain we've had whistleblower complaints about for eighteen months, after FOIA requests and on-the-record interviews went nowhere — she'd lie on the application and pretend to be a aide so she can get access to the floors we can't otherwise document. Our policy just says undercover reporting is a 'last resort for stories of overriding public importance,' full stop, with no guidance on how to actually weigh the wrongness of the deception against the story's value, so naturally the committee's split down the middle and everyone's just repeating their priors, louder. Could you draft the memo I'll bring to Tuesday's meeting — something with an actual bottom line (approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions) rather than a both-sides shrug, and reasons that whichever side loses would recognize as a fair hit on their position. I'd really like it to engage with something like the Augustine-style objection above — that the wrongness of lying doesn't just wash out once the cause is good enough — instead of pretending that objection isn't there.
