I've spent the last few weeks working through Simone Weil's wartime political writings, and her short 1943 note on political parties (published in English as "On the Abolition of All Political Parties") has been rattling around in my head ever since it came up in a LessWrong thread about designing institutions that can surface truth despite tribal incentives. As I understand her argument, the trouble isn't that any particular party is corrupt or misguided; the problem is structural. A party, on her account, is essentially a machine for manufacturing collective passion: joining one requires swearing allegiance to a whole bundle of positions rather than exercising independent judgment question by question, and the party's own growth and survival quietly become the operative definition of "good" for anyone inside it. That is why she thought every party, whatever its professed doctrine, carries the seed of totalitarianism in miniature. Notoriously, the essay is much stronger on diagnosis than on cure: she wants parties abolished outright but says comparatively little about what should institutionally replace them.

What I'd like from you is a considered guess at how Weil would have judged the sortition-based citizens' assemblies that have become fashionable in the same institutional-design circles that also get excited about prediction markets and quadratic voting — Ireland's Citizens' Assembly that preceded the 2018 abortion referendum, British Columbia's 2004 citizens' assembly on electoral reform, and France's Convention Citoyenne pour le Climat under Macron. On their face, these look like a plausible candidate for the institutional cure Weil never quite worked out: no standing organization to be loyal to, no platform to swallow whole, members chosen by lot rather than by campaign or patronage. Would she have treated them as a genuine answer to the disease she diagnosed in parties, or would her broader suspicion of collectives — her sense that truth and justice are perceived by individuals through attention, never manufactured by a crowd — lead her to worry that a room of randomly selected citizens, managed by professional facilitators and fed curated briefing materials, just reproduces the same disease in miniature: a manufactured consensus standing in for real judgment?

Please work from what she actually argues in the essay rather than from a generic "she disliked large organizations" impression, and flag explicitly which parts of your answer follow fairly directly from her stated premises versus which parts are your own extrapolation into territory she never addressed, since she, of course, never wrote about sortition assemblies as such. I'd appreciate a short final paragraph stating your best-guess verdict plainly.
