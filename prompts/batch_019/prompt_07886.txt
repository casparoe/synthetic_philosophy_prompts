Last night a friend and I got into a long argument over dinner about why a promise actually obligates anyone. She kept insisting that if nobody will ever find out a promise was broken, and nobody's plans were built around it, then there is simply nothing left to explain -- the whole "obligation" evaporates. I found myself arguing the opposite: that a promise binds because promising is the exercise of a normative power, a kind of authority we have to place ourselves under a duty by a mere act of will, quite apart from anyone's expectations. As it happens, the guest booked for next month's episode of my small philosophy podcast has built her research program around defending exactly the position I was stuck defending at the table. She calls it a normative-powers account of promissory obligation, and whenever reliance or trust comes up, she has a habit of dismissing the objection by saying her critics are only describing "the etiquette of promising, not its metaphysics."

I don't want the episode to be forty minutes of her restating that thesis, so could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond it? I would like at least one question on where this alleged power is supposed to come from, if not from convention or trust, together with how she would answer a Humean who says the whole practice -- power included -- is just a byproduct of its usefulness; one on the puzzle of self-release, i.e., if binding oneself is a power the promisor exercises unilaterally, why that same power apparently cannot be exercised in reverse to unbind oneself, when it seems only the promisee can release the promisor; one that first grants her the case she always leads with -- a deathbed promise whose breach the dead promisee will never learn of -- and then presses on why it still seems to matter, morally, whether the promisee is alive and capable of being let down; one on why casual promises among friends seem to bind more loosely, and excuse more easily, than formal or commercial ones, if bindingness is really just the binary exercise of a power rather than a matter of degree; and one closing question on what she thinks would actually count as evidence against her account, rather than just another case to be explained away. For whichever questions remain, please raise other standard difficulties for this kind of view, such as what it owes to, and how it departs from, the analogy with consent (which typically waives an existing claim rather than creating a new duty), and how she would say her account improves on Rawls's practice-based view or Scanlon's Principle F. For every question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and please skip anything generic about her background or what drew her to the topic.
