For the past several years, I've used the Bayesian doomsday argument as a running example in the probability courses I teach: Brandon Carter's original anthropic reasoning about our rank among all the humans who will ever be born, John Leslie's book-length defense of it, and Richard Gott's independently derived version, which he traces back to standing at the Berlin Wall in 1969 and reasoning, purely from how long it had already stood, roughly how much longer it was likely to last — an estimate the Wall's fall in 1989 comfortably vindicated. I've always taught it as a delightful, low-stakes classroom curiosity, complete with an exercise applying Gott's method to how much longer our department's decrepit photocopier has left. This year, though, my department has asked me to give the commencement address to the graduating statistics and applied mathematics majors, and for once I don't want to do the fun classroom version: I want to say what I actually think, which is that I find the argument considerably more compelling than most of my colleagues do, even granting the self-indication-assumption objection that's supposed to cancel it out and the genuine, unresolved mess of the reference class problem.

The trouble is that the people best placed to notice if I fudge any of that — misstate the self-sampling assumption, say, or quietly skip past its strongest objections — are exactly the graduates the day is meant to be for, while the rest of the room — parents, siblings, a university president — has never heard of anthropic reasoning and is there for an occasion that is supposed to end in hope, not in a statistics professor musing that humanity's expected future may be shorter than its past. I'd like a full commencement speech, roughly six or seven minutes read aloud, that treats the argument with enough actual rigor that the specialists in the room recognize it as the genuine, contested thing rather than a simplified crowd-pleaser, but that still ends up somewhere a graduate could walk out into the world feeling good about — a real send-off, not a lecture with the joy carefully removed.
