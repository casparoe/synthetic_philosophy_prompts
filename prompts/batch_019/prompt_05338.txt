and heres my classmate's answer, copied exactly:

"Radical interpretation is Davidson's name for the situation where an interpreter has to figure out what a speaker means with no dictionary, translator, or prior grasp of their language to rely on -- all they have to go on is the speaker's behavior, especially which sentences they hold true in which observable circumstances. The hard part is that you can't read off someone's beliefs just from watching what they assent to, because what a sentence shows about the world depends on what the speaker already believes, and you can't know what they believe without already knowing what their sentences mean. Belief and meaning have to be solved for together. Davidson's fix is the Principle of Charity: the interpreter assigns truth conditions to the speaker's sentences in whatever way makes their beliefs come out mostly true and rational, since assuming widespread error or irrationality would make interpretation impossible to even get off the ground. This lets the interpreter build a Tarski-style truth theory for the language, where the T-sentences double as a theory of meaning.

Basically this is the same project as Quine's radical translation, just under a new name. Davidson even leans on Quine's gavagai case, where a native's word for a rabbit scurrying by could equally be translated as "undetached rabbit part," since no amount of behavioral evidence could settle which translation is right. Both thinkers end up concluding that meaning is radically indeterminate -- there's no fact of the matter about what anyone really means, just translation schemes that are more or less useful to us. Davidson's own addition to this is what he calls the Principle of Humanity, which pushes further than assigning true beliefs and says we should attribute whatever beliefs we ourselves would have in the speaker's situation, since a false but understandable belief is sometimes a better fit than a bizarre true one."
