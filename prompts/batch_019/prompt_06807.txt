Me and my friend Jonas got into a whole thing last night about this wierd David Lewis paper he dug up for his philosophy of religion seminar, "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" The setup is deceptively simple: everyone agrees a debt can be discharged by a third party — if Alice owes Bob $500 and Carol just pays it off, the debt is gone, full stop, doesn't matter whose money it was. But punishment seems totally different. If Alice commits a crime and Carol volunteers to serve the sentence instead, most people's gut reaction is that Alice is still guilty and still deserves to be punished, Carol's time served notwithstanding. Lewis uses that asymmetry to go after the Christian doctrine of penal substitution, i.e. the idea that Christ was punished "in place of" sinners so that God could justly let them off the hook. Jonas kept insisting that once you actually take the asymmetry seriously, the doctrine basically collapses into incoherence (or gets watered down into something that isn't really about justice anymore), and I kept trying to argue there's some notion of transferable liability, short of transferable desert, that could still do the job. Neither of us budged, and it got surprisingly heated for a conversation that started over pizza.

Anyway, I want an actual verdict out of you, not a "well it depends how you define punishment" shrug — if that IS the real answer, then pick the theory of punishment you think is correct and follow it all the way through to a conclusion. Write me an essay that lays out the strongest version of the debt-vs-punishment disanalogy argument against penal substitution, gives the best moves available to someone who wants to defend a robust, non-watered-down doctrine of penal substitution against it, and then actually decides: does the doctrine survive Lewis's challenge more or less intact, survive only in some deflated form, or basically fail? I don't care which side you land on, I just want the reasoning laid out and a real conclusion at the end, not a summary of "both sides have a point."
