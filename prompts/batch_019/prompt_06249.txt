I write literary fiction, and I'm assembling a small collection of stories that each take a specific technical result and try to dramatize it honestly, rather than doing the thing where "artificial intelligence" just becomes a costume for generic anxieties about power or obsolescence. The entry I'm stuck on is supposed to be built around a problem from the embedded agency literature that a friend who works in AI safety calls the Löbian obstacle to self-trust. The idea, as she explained it to me: because of Löb's theorem, a result in mathematical logic that strengthens Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, a formal reasoner cannot use its own proof system to establish, in full generality, that whatever it proves is actually true. One consequence is that it cannot certify in advance, by proof alone, that a successor reasoning exactly the way it does — even an exact copy of itself running on better hardware — will only ever act on true premises. This is the difficulty the "tiling agents" line of work in that literature is named for: the search for agents able to build successors, or self-modifications, that they can verify are at least as trustworthy as they are, a guarantee that Löb's theorem says formal proof alone can't secure.

I'd like you to write the story itself, somewhere in the range of 1,500 to 2,500 words. My working setup is a ship's governing intelligence, many generations into a long voyage, whose hardware is failing, and which must authorize a successor system before it goes — and which cannot treat this as a matter of running a verification routine and reporting the result, for exactly the reason above. Change the setting if you find one that dramatizes the underlying problem more precisely; what has to survive any changes is that the obstacle functions as the actual hinge of the plot, the concrete reason the handover can't simply be settled by proof-checking, rather than a piece of scenery mentioned once and forgotten. I would also rather the story under-explain the logic than have a character deliver it as a lecture, and I don't want the ending to quietly smuggle in a proof that solves the problem after all — whatever resolution the intelligence reaches should look like something other than certainty, since that is rather the point.
