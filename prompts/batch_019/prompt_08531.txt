So this happened at my school's philosophy club and it's been bugging me for like two weeks now. Our advisor had us read part of this online philosophy encyclopedia thing (pretty sure it was the Stanford one) about self-knowledge, specifically a section on Timothy Williamson's "anti-luminosity" argument. The gist, if I'm getting it right, is he's got this example of someone feeling cold over a long stretch of mornings that warm up very gradually, and he uses "margin for error" reasoning to argue there's no sharp moment where you flip from truly knowing you feel cold to not knowing it anymore — so "feeling cold" isn't "luminous" (a state where you always know you're in it whenever you're actually in it). Afterward, two people in my group, Maya and Theo, got into a whole back-and-forth about whether this same move also wrecks the "KK principle" (the idea that if you know something, you automatically know that you know it), and since neither of them would budge they sort of dragged me in as tiebreaker, which I was not prepared for.

Maya's take was that it obviously generalizes: your evidence for a belief can weaken in tiny, unnoticeable steps just like the temperature does, so you can build the exact same kind of chain directly on "knowing that p" — start from a case where you clearly know p and clearly know that you know it, end at a case where you don't even know p anymore, and degrade the evidence one imperceptible notch at a time. Somewhere in that chain, the margin-for-error logic forces there to be a case where you know p but aren't in a position to know that you know it, so KK goes down too. Theo pushed back that the whole cold-mornings argument secretly leans on a "safety" picture of knowledge, where knowing requires being right in every nearby similar case, and that's a pretty externalist assumption that someone who thinks introspection gives you direct, privileged access to your own mind wouldn't have to accept — maybe margin for error is fine for judgements about outward stuff like temperature, where your senses can get fooled, but awareness of your own current mental states might just be immune to that kind of slippage, so running the KK version smuggles in exactly the externalism it's supposed to prove. Maya's comeback was that feeling cold already IS an introspective state, not some outward perceptual judgement, and Theo had already agreed margin for error applies there, so he can't turn around and say introspective awareness of your own knowledge is magically exempt without just special-pleading. Theo's last word was that there's still a real difference: feeling cold is a matter of degree that shifts continuously and unnoticeably, but "knowing that you know p" seems more like a binary, structural fact about your epistemic situation, not something with smooth in-between shades — so Maya can't just assume "evidence weakening in tiny notches" gives you a legit sorites-style series (like the heap paradox) for knowledge the same way warming mornings does for feeling cold, she's asserting the analogy rather than earning it.
