After forty years as a field ecologist, I have spent much of my retirement reading through the social-choice-theory literature, which has become something of a second career. I sit on the governance committee of a scientific society I have belonged to since graduate school, and we are reconsidering how the society elects its president. Plurality voting among three or more nominees has twice in the past decade put a candidate into office whom most members ranked last or second-to-last, because several like-minded candidates split the sympathetic vote. One committee member, who chairs our statistics section, argues that reform is beside the point: by the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, every non-dictatorial rule with three or more candidates is manipulable, so moving to ranked-choice or approval voting would add complexity without buying any real protection against tactical voting. Others want ranked-choice adopted immediately. I must bring the committee's recommendation to full council next month. Please draft that memo: recommend one specific method with a timeline for phasing it in, and engage the manipulability objection on its merits rather than dismiss it with a slogan about no system being perfect.
