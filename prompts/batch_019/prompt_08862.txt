I've been fascinated by probability puzzles ever since a friend introduced me to the St. Petersburg paradox: the coin-flipping game whose expected payout works out to infinity, even though almost every time you actually played it you'd walk away with almost nothing. That result felt strange but at least graspable, since infinity is an odd verdict, but it is still a definite one. More recently, while poking around some old philosophy forum threads on the subject, I came across what people described as a nastier cousin of it, called the Pasadena game, which I believe comes from a paper by Alan Hájek and Harris Nover. The setup is almost identical: you flip a fair coin until it first lands heads, and if that happens on flip number n, you win 2^n/n units when n is odd but must pay 2^n/n units when n is even. Weighting each payoff by its probability of 1/2^n, as you're supposed to when computing an expected value, the terms collapse to plus-or-minus 1/n, so the expected value looks like it should just be the series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + 1/5 - ..., which I recognize as the alternating harmonic series, something I vaguely remember settles down to the natural log of 2 once you add it up in that order.

This is where I run into trouble. From further reading, I gather that the whole point of the Pasadena game is that this series is only conditionally convergent rather than absolutely convergent (the series of the absolute values of the terms, 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ..., diverges), and that because of this, you can apparently rearrange those same infinitely many terms so that they add up to any number you like, shoot off to positive or negative infinity, or never settle down at all. I have no formal training in mathematics or philosophy, and what vocabulary I do have here comes from reading around rather than coursework, so although I can accept in the abstract that reordering an infinite sum can change its value, I don't have a gut-level feel for why that fact should make this particular expected value genuinely undefined, rather than it simply being the natural log of 2, on the grounds that summing the terms in the order the flips actually happen (n = 1, then 2, then 3, and so on) is obviously the "natural," privileged way to do it. Could you help me build some real intuition here, ideally without leaning on a full statement of the rearrangement theorem, for why decision theorists don't think there is a privileged ordering in a case like this; why that makes it a fundamentally different, and apparently more troubling, situation than the plain infinite expectation in the St. Petersburg game; and what, if anything, you think it should lead us to conclude about whether "maximize expected value" can really be treated as a perfectly general principle of rational choice?
