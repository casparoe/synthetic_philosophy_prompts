I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and for the past few weeks I have been working through Lara Buchak's book "Risk and Rationality" for a longer piece on philosophical challenges to orthodox rational-choice theory. As I have come to understand it, the significance of her "risk-weighted expected utility" theory is that it finally gives decision theorists a principled way to call ordinary risk-averse behavior — preferring a certain $50 to a coin flip between $0 and $110, say — rational, correcting a standard expected-utility framework that had treated such preferences as a bias to be modeled away rather than as a legitimate response to risk. A philosopher I interviewed for background more or less confirmed this reading, describing Buchak's risk function as the missing ingredient that finally lets risk aversion count as rational rather than as one more item on the list of cognitive biases that Kahneman and Tversky spent their careers cataloguing.

That leaves me with something I would like to get right before I file the piece: if expected-utility theory really offered no principled way to accommodate risk-averse preferences until Buchak's work, how did decades of economists manage to build insurance pricing, portfolio theory, and much of applied microeconomics on that same framework without anyone flagging that it branded one of the most basic and common patterns of human behavior as irrational? I would like to understand what, precisely, was broken before her theory came along, so that I do not overstate the case in print.
