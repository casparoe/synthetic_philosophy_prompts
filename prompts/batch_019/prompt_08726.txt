Here's the question I wrote: "Compare Peirce's and James's accounts of truth. Make sure you address (a) how each of them ties truth to practical consequences, (b) whether either of them thinks a true belief still has to correspond to reality in some sense, and (c) why Peirce eventually stopped using the word 'pragmatism' for his own view and switched to 'pragmaticism' instead."

And here's Marco's answer: "Peirce and James are usually credited as the two founders of pragmatism, the idea that the meaning of a concept comes down to its practical, experiential effects. Peirce worked the core idea out first, in the 1870s, kicking it around with his old Metaphysical Club friends, but it was actually James who put the word 'pragmatism' into public circulation, in a famous 1898 lecture at Berkeley, crediting Peirce as the source. (a) For Peirce, truth basically just is whatever belief ends up working and satisfying us in practice. He thought that if you let the scientific method run long enough on any given question, inquiry will eventually settle into one fixed, agreed-upon opinion, and that settled opinion just is what 'true' means for that question. Since this settling-down happens separately inside whatever community is doing the investigating, different communities can end up landing on genuinely different truths on Peirce's view, which is part of why he can come across as more relativist than you'd expect from someone so obsessed with logic. James, on the other hand, is the one associated with the 'cash value' line: a belief is true if it pays off, if acting on it carries you through experience without your expectations getting cashed out as false along the way, which is a more individual, moment-to-moment test than Peirce's community-wide convergence. James took heat for this, Russell and others accused him of saying truth is just whatever's comforting to believe, and James spent a fair amount of ink insisting that was a caricature of what he meant by 'working.' (b) Neither of them thinks truth requires matching some reality that sits totally outside our practices of inquiry, that's kind of the whole pragmatist move, replacing 'does this idea mirror reality' with 'does this idea function well for us.' So both are pushing away from the traditional correspondence theory, just at different speeds, James pretty much all the way, Peirce a bit less completely since he still wants inquiry to be answerable to something, but for Peirce that 'something' just turns out to be the future settled opinion itself, not an independent world standing behind it. (c) Peirce got annoyed that James and people like Schiller were stretching 'pragmatism' to cover looser, more popular ideas he hadn't fully signed onto, so in 1905 he coined 'pragmaticism' for his own stricter, more technical version, joking that the new word was ugly enough that no one would want to steal it."
