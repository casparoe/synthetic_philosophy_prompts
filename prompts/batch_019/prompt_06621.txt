Spent my career doing bench chemistry and only got into philosophy properly after retiring -- lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole on Bertrand's chord paradox, the old chestnut where the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle comes out to 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on how you set up the randomization. What got me hooked was Jaynes' fix: he argued the answer shouldn't depend on the exact size, position, or orientation of the circle, and that these invariance requirements force a unique distribution, matching the "random radius" method (1/2) -- he even proposed settling it for real by tossing straws onto a circle and measuring, very physicist of him: turn a hopeless-looking question into a symmetry argument you can go test on a bench. Except then I found a paper claiming his own trick doesn't work, because you can run the identical kind of invariance argument for a different randomizing procedure (throwing darts at the circle instead) and it forces the answer 1/4 just as "uniquely" -- so the arbitrariness never really left, it just moved up a level, into which procedure gets to count as the symmetric one.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people hashing this out? It's basically the objective vs. subjective probability fight playing out on one small example. Picture them literally crouched over a circle chalked on the floor with a handful of straws, about to run the experiment -- one thinks the invariance argument (plus the experiment) genuinely rescues an objective answer for a well-posed physical setup like this one, the other thinks its a shell game and no experiment could ever settle the deeper question. I want real back-and-forth, each of them actually engaging with the other's strongest point instead of just restating their own case -- and see if they can at least agree on exactly where their disagreement lives, even if they don't agree on whose right.
