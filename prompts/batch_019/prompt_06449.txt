After a forty-year career as a glaciologist -- most of it spent extracting and dating ice cores from the Greenland and Antarctic sheets -- I have entered an unexpected second act since retiring: I've been reading philosophy seriously for the first time, auditing courses and working through primary texts with a patience I never had as a working scientist. This has left me in a slightly awkward position. I have been asked to give the commencement address next month to the graduating class of the environmental science and policy master's program at the university where I did my own doctorate, and my instinct was to build the speech around the obvious, well-worn appeal: that depleting resources and delaying emissions cuts now amounts to harming specific people who will one day exist and live with the consequences, and that the graduates owe it to those future people, their own eventual grandchildren included, to do better.

A philosophy reading group I joined this year worked through the relevant part of Parfit's Reasons and Persons, and I now understand that this appeal is not nearly as clean as I had assumed. His non-identity problem points out that large-scale policy choices of exactly this kind -- his own example contrasts a depletion policy with a conservation one -- change who is later conceived, since who marries whom and when children are conceived depends on background economic conditions that the two policies would themselves alter. Run either policy for a couple of generations and the resulting populations no longer overlap at all. So the people who do exist under the depletion policy are not, in the relevant comparative sense, worse off than they would otherwise have been: had conservation been chosen instead, they specifically would never have existed to be worse off, and if their lives are still worth living, it becomes surprisingly hard to say whom, exactly, we have wronged. The reading group also looked at some of the standard patches for this -- impersonal versions of the harm principle, so-called "wide" person-affecting views -- and none of them struck me as something I could honestly compress into a soundbite without a room full of policy students catching the sleight of hand.

I would like your help writing that address. There will be roughly sixty graduates who have spent two years studying exactly this kind of intergenerational question, sitting next to parents and partners who have not, and I don't want a lecture on population ethics from either of us. What I would like is a speech, about eight minutes read aloud, that quietly retires the "you are protecting specific future people from harm" framing rather than leaning on it as though I had never heard the objection, and that finds something honest to put in its place -- so that the graduates leave the hall still convinced that what they are about to spend their careers on matters enormously, for reasons that would hold up even under their own training in this material.
