So this has been rattling around in my head since a rationalist meetup a few weeks back where we somehow spent an hour arguing about backward induction. Everyone already knows the cute pop version of the centipede game "paradox": backward induction says the first player should grab the pot immediately, real players (even trained game theorists playing each other for actual money) keep cooperating for a while, ha ha isn't game theory quaint, its basically a rationalist blog meme at this point. What's actually been bugging me is the more technical fight underneath that. Aumann has this famous-ish result where he tries to show that common knowledge of rationality among the players doesn't just make backward induction a reasonable convention, it literally forces the backward-induction outcome as a theorem. And then Binmore (and I wanna say Stalnaker too, maybe Reny?) pushed back that this is sort of question-begging, because reaching move 4 of a 6-move game already means somebody deviated from what "rational" play was supposed to look like -- so what exactly are the remaining players even supposed to believe about each other's rationality, in a common-knowledge sense, from that point on? Apparently depending on exactly how you formalize the belief-revision story here -- does a single deviation permanently trash your opponent's reputation for rationality, or is it just a "tremble" you shrug off and keep assuming they're rational going forward -- you get genuinely different verdicts about whether backward induction is still the uniquely rational thing to do from that node onward.

I've only ever seen this dispute compressed into a paragraph in survey papers, and it always comes out as "some people think X, some people think Y" with nobody actually slugging it out. So could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp game theorists who, being the sort of people who take this stuff seriously, actually sat down to play a real-money 6-move centipede game against each other -- feel free to just make up plausible numbers, e.g. the pot starts at $2 and doubles each round, and whoever "takes" keeps two-thirds of it -- and are now paused at move 4, pot sitting on the table, hashing this out before continuing. One of them is an Aumann-style true believer who thinks the common-knowledge argument still holds even here, so that taking the pot right now is provably the only rational move; the other is a Binmore/Stalnaker-style skeptic who thinks the whole argument quietly smuggles in a specific and contestable theory of what that earlier deviation is supposed to imply about future play. I want them to genuinely engage with each other's actual point instead of just re-asserting conclusions past each other -- like, I want the skeptic to really pin the believer down on what exactly is supposed to happen to common knowledge of rationality after an off-path move, and I want the believer to have a real answer instead of just retreating to "well it's off the equilibrium path so the model doesn't have to say anything about it."
