So I've been slowly making my way through Benjamin Franklin's autobiography (got into it after a friend told me his little virtue chart is basically where habit-tracker apps stole the idea from), and I ended up writing up my own take on the part where he lists his 13 virtues and tries to become "morally perfect." I don't have any background in philosophy or history, I just read that section a few times and took notes, so I want someone to poke holes in my reasoning before I get too attached to it.

Here's basically what I've got: most of the 13 virtues (Order, Frugality, Industry, stuff like that) read to me like a checklist for being a productive, trustworthy tradesman, not "good" in any deep moral sense — and he literally kept a little book with a page per virtue and a column for each day of the week to mark a dot every time he slipped up, which feels more like a performance log than a soul-searching exercise. Then Humility gets tacked onto the very end of the list, and only because a friend of his (a Quaker, I think?) sat him down and told him people found him arrogant and basically insufferable to argue with — so it's not like he caught himself being prideful through introspection, someone had to warn him it was hurting his reputation. And theres that story he tells about the guy getting his axe ground at the smith's, wanting it polished bright all over, who gets tired of turning the wheel and decides he likes it "speckled" instead and just stops — Franklin brings that up right around where he's admitting he never fully nailed most of these virtues (Order especially), and to me it reads less like humility about human limits and more like he's giving himself permission to stop short, because looking virtuous enough is what actually pays off socially, not the real thing underneath. So my conclusion is that this whole "moral perfection" project is basically reputation management and self-discipline dressed up in moral language, not a sincere attempt at goodness for its own sake.

Is that a fair reading, or am I just projecting a cynical modern take onto an 18th century guy who genuinely meant something different by "virtue" than I'm assuming? I'd love some comments on whether this actually holds up against what's in the book, whether I'm leaning way too hard on the humility and axe bits specifically to make my whole case, and whether there's a more generous way of reading the project that I should sit with before I show this to anyone else.
