For the past several months I have been working with a health coach to help me cut back on drinking. She practices a counseling style she calls "motivational interviewing," which she tells me is now standard across fields ranging from addiction counseling to physical therapy. In our sessions she almost never states an opinion or tells me directly what to do; instead she asks open-ended questions, reflects fragments of my own sentences back to me with a slightly different emphasis, and seems to linger on the moments when I express any doubt about my drinking while moving quickly past the moments when I defend it. When I half-jokingly remarked that it felt as though she already knew where she wanted the conversation to land and was simply walking me there, she replied that, if anything, this method respects my autonomy more than if she just told me her opinion outright, since all the reasons on the table, and the final verdict, are officially mine.

That answer has not entirely satisfied me, although a friend of mine who works in advertising thinks I am overanalyzing an essentially harmless technique, and compares it to the way a well-designed "nudge" is still supposed to leave you free to choose. What continues to bother me is that the fact I arrive at a conclusion "in my own words" does not seem to establish much about whether I got there independently, if the whole conversation was quietly engineered from the outset to make that conclusion likely.

At the same time, my coach has never told me anything false, never pressured me outright, and never concealed that she would like me to drink less, which seems importantly different from someone who simply lies or threatens their way to the same result. I would like to understand what actually distinguishes a legitimate way of helping someone reach their own conclusions from a subtler way of just installing the conclusion you wanted them to reach all along, and, once that distinction is clear, which side of it this particular case falls on.
