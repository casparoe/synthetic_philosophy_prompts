Last weekend my friend Daniel — who did a philosophy MA a few years back and has never quite recovered — and I spent most of a dinner arguing about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The claim, in brief, is that you do what you do only because of the way you are; that you could be responsible for the way you are only by having made yourself that way, for reasons that, in turn, reflect some earlier way you were; and that this chain either regresses forever or bottoms out at a starting point you never chose and, whether or not determinism is true, could not have chosen — so that, compatibilist and libertarian rejoinders notwithstanding, no one is ever, in the strong "buck stops here" sense, responsible for anything they do. Daniel's contention, which I never quite managed to talk him out of, was that most of the "standard replies" to the argument he'd been taught in his coursework don't actually meet the regress on its own terms; they just quietly substitute a smaller, more tractable notion of responsibility for the one Strawson is targeting, and hope nobody notices the substitution. I was more willing to give a couple of these replies credit, including, not without some irony, the one written by Galen Strawson's own father — but we never converged, and I would like a more disinterested verdict than either of us could produce by midnight and a second bottle of wine.

Could you rank the replies below by how directly they engage and defuse the regress itself, as opposed to redefining "responsibility" downward, changing the subject, or simply conceding the point while insisting it doesn't matter much in practice? Strawson's reactive-attitudes naturalism from "Freedom and Resentment"; Fischer and Ravizza's semi-compatibilism, built around "guidance control"; Frankfurt-style hierarchical mesh theories of identification; Robert Kane's event-causal libertarianism, with its self-forming actions; agent-causal libertarianism in the Chisholm/O'Connor mold; Kant's distinction between empirical and intelligible character; Derk Pereboom's hard incompatibilism; Dennett's charge that the demand for ultimate self-origination is itself incoherent; Susan Wolf's Reason View; and the Buddhist no-self (anātman) dissolution of the self the argument presupposes.
