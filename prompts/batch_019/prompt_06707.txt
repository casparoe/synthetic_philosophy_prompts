I have been slowly working my way through some popular history of the German resistance to Hitler, and that reading pulled me into the much older philosophical and theological debate about whether it is ever permissible to kill a tyrant. I have no formal background in philosophy, so I have mostly been piecing the picture together from secondary discussions and whatever fragments of primary texts I have managed to find translated online, but one name that keeps coming up as a kind of foundational figure for this whole tradition is Thomas Aquinas, mainly on the strength of scattered passages in what I believe is usually translated as something like "On Kingship," plus a few remarks elsewhere in his work.

What surprised me is that his actual position seems considerably more hedged than the simplified version I started out with, which was basically that the medieval Church taught that killing tyrants is acceptable. As best I can reconstruct it, Aquinas distinguishes between a ruler who is a tyrant because he seized power illegitimately in the first place and a ruler who came to power legitimately but then governs tyrannically, and my impression is that, especially in the second case, he is quite wary of leaving the question of when and how to remove a tyrant to the private judgment of individual subjects, preferring instead that any such action be undertaken by some kind of public or communal authority. I am honestly not sure how much that distinction between a defect in title and a defect in the exercise of power is meant to change the moral picture, or whether it mainly affects who has standing to act rather than whether forceful removal is justified at all.

Assuming that picture is roughly right, I would like to understand what the most important implications of Aquinas's actual view are for how we ought to think about modern, real-world cases in which private individuals or small groups take it upon themselves to assassinate, or otherwise violently remove, an authoritarian ruler — cases like the various plots against Hitler, or present-day debates about the targeted killing of dictators. Does his framework end up counselling against essentially all such private action, however terrible the ruler in question, or does it leave more room for ordinary people to act on their own than the emphasis on "public authority" initially suggests? I would be grateful if you could walk me through the reasoning behind whatever conclusion you reach, rather than just stating it, since what I am really struggling with is the internal logic of his position and where exactly it does or does not stretch to cover cases that he could never have anticipated.
