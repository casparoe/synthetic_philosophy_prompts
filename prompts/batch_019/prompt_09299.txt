I'm a philosophy PhD student working mostly on ethics and decision theory, and for the past three years I've also volunteered with my county's backcountry search-and-rescue unit, partly for the fresh air and partly because I like the radios, if I'm honest. Our unit only has one helicopter and pilot certified for after-dark extractions, and on alot of nights that's exactly one usable mission before weather or fuel forces us back to base. Back in March we got two calls almost at once: a solo climber with a probable spinal injury on one ridge, and a group of four hikers stranded by a flash flood on a totally different ridge, with no way to serve both. Dispatch sent the helicopter to the four, on what's really just an unwritten habit rather than an actual policy: when in doubt, go where the most people are. The climber ended up self-rescuing and is fine, so nobody got hurt by the call this time, but its split our operations committee pretty badly. About half the committee wants to write "prioritize the larger group" into the manual as an explicit rule. A few of the old-timers, including one guy who's been doing this since before I was born, are adamant that the lone climber's claim on that helicopter doesn't just evaporate because there happened to be four people on the other ridge that night, and they want some kind of randomizing tiebreaker built into the protocol even when the numbers aren't equal.

Everyone on the committee knows I'm "the philosophy one" (mostly because I once rambled at a barbecue after a training day about a seminar cold-call I fumbled, on whether numbers should count morally in rescue-type cases), so I've somehow ended up drafting the actual recommendation memo for next week's vote, and I'm kind of freezing up trying to write something that isn't just four hedgy paragraphs that don't decide anything. Could you draft it for me? I need it to land on one specific protocol we could actually put in the manual, not "weigh the following factors," show how it would've played out with the March call, and give a real, non-strawmanned hearing to whichever side it doesn't land on, instead of just waving at "obviously you save more lives" or, on the other side, "obviously a person isn't a statistic." Since 1-vs-4 won't always be the split we're dealing with, say something about whether your rule would still hold up at, say, 1-vs-20, or whether it needs to bend past some point. It'd also help if it named a date to revisit the policy, since nobody wants this carved in stone after a single incident.
