I'm a software engineer on a small, fully remote team of about eight people. Over the past couple of years we've settled into unwritten conventions for things like commit-message formatting, branch naming, and pull-request etiquette: no one proposed or voted on any of it, newcomers pick it up within days just from watching how things are done, and anyone who drifts from it gets a quiet correction. The question stopped being academic when we merged codebases with another team that had its own, equally workable habits, and for the first time we had to decide, as a combined group, which set of habits to keep. Please describe the most important implications of David Lewis's account of convention -- a regularity sustained by common knowledge, self-fulfilling mutual expectation, and the fact that some equally good alternative regularity was always available -- for (a) why unilaterally switching to a different approach feels far costlier than the size of the underlying disagreement would warrant, and (b) what it would actually take to engineer a deliberate switch rather than let the merged team's habits settle by drift. I'm also curious where Lewis's picture, built to explain pure coordination, stops being adequate once one side's convention has picked up a mild sense of obligation that the other team never felt bound by at all.
