I spent the better part of four decades working on fluctuation phenomena in condensed-matter statistical mechanics, and only since retiring have I taken up philosophy in any serious, systematic way. This has led me back, by a route I did not anticipate, to some unfinished business from my own field. Boltzmann, replying to Loschmidt's and Zermelo's objections to the H-theorem, once suggested that the low-entropy world we observe might simply be a rare statistical fluctuation out of an eternal background equilibrium. Critics of that proposal were quick to point out that the smallest fluctuation capable of producing an observer with impressions just like ours is vastly more probable than the large, extended, truly ordered universe we seem to inhabit, so that the hypothesis, taken as a serious explanation, predicts we should find ourselves in a far more truncated and chaotic setting than we do.

I have since learned, from some cosmology reading, that this old idea has been revived in a much sharper form. Because a universe dominated by a positive cosmological constant behaves, in the relevant sense, as a thermal system with a tiny but nonzero temperature, the eternally expanding de Sitter phase that appears to lie in our future will occasionally throw up isolated, momentarily conscious 'Boltzmann brains,' complete with false memories of a whole cosmic history; and because that future is infinite while any universe's hot, structured early period is not, such phantasm observers should eventually swamp the ordinary ones in any reasonable accounting.

What I have not managed to work out to my own satisfaction is exactly what kind of argument this is supposed to be, and why physicists treat it as a genuine constraint on theory-building rather than as an entertaining but idle skeptical fantasy. An old colleague from my lab, now equally retired, insists it is nothing more than Descartes' evil demon dressed up in a lab coat, and that no amount of cosmological machinery changes its status as unfalsifiable metaphysics. I suspect he is missing something, since the argument seems to do substantive comparative work, counting against some cosmological models and measure choices rather than others, in a way that garden-variety radical skepticism was never asked to do. Could you set out the precise logical anatomy of the Boltzmann brain argument for me: how the bare fact that small entropy-lowering fluctuations are exponentially more probable than large ones gets converted into evidence against a theory; how this bears on the notorious difficulty of comparing infinities of observers across an eternal spacetime (the 'measure problem'); and whether the argument can escape the following bind, namely that a theory predicting you are probably a deluded, momentary brain seems, in the very act of predicting it, to undercut your standing to trust the reasoning that produced the prediction. What I would most like to understand is whether that self-undermining structure differs in kind from my friend's Cartesian demon, or only in costume.
