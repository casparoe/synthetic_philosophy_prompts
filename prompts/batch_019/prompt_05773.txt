I've spent the better part of the last fifteen years reading LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and the blogs that grew out of that scene, and the question of what norm governs assertion has bothered me for a long time — it sits right at the intersection of two things this scene cares about, calibration and the ethics of just telling someone something flatly, and I've never found the standard three-way split between truth, belief, and knowledge norms fully satisfying. I've finally written up my own attempt at a synthesis, and before I cross-post it to my blog and to LessWrong I'd like your comments on it: not encouragement, but the strongest objections you can find, and an honest check on whether I've represented the positions I'm arguing against fairly.

Here's the draft.

**Assertion as an Unhedged Bet**

Epistemologists have spent two decades arguing about which norm governs flat assertion. Timothy Williamson's answer, defended at length in *Knowledge and Its Limits*, is the knowledge norm: assert that P only if you know that P. It's a tidy rule, and it explains something weaker rules struggle with — our discomfort with lottery propositions. Even holding a ticket in a fair, million-ticket lottery, and knowing the odds exactly, it sounds wrong to flatly assert "my ticket lost" before the draw, however close to certain you are. A norm requiring only truth, or high credence, or justified belief has to explain that discomfort away; the knowledge norm predicts it directly, since on most accounts mere statistical evidence, however overwhelming, doesn't add up to knowledge.

The trouble is that the knowledge norm has to explain away its own counterexamples, and the sharpest one I know is Jennifer Lackey's case of the creationist science teacher who is assigned to teach evolution. She does so honestly and well: presents the evidence, states the claims, answers questions, all without a single hedge — and privately believes none of it, because her prior commitments rule it out. On the knowledge norm, and on any norm requiring belief (knowledge entails belief on the standard analysis), she does something improper every time she opens her mouth in that classroom. That verdict seems backwards. She's doing exactly what a good teacher should do.

I want to suggest both cases are symptoms of one thing, and the thing isn't knowledge. To assert P, on my proposal, is to place an unhedged bet: you stake your standing as an informant on P, in a way that invites your hearer to rely on it exactly as they'd rely on something they'd verified themselves. The norm governing a bet like that isn't "you must know the outcome" — nobody thinks that, or bets would be impossible — it's closer to: your credence in P has to be high enough, and stable enough under the kind of scrutiny this conversation invites, that taking the bet is the right call given what's riding on it.

That dissolves the lottery case without needing knowledge to do the work. Your credence that the ticket lost is high, but unstable under the scrutiny a flat assertion invites: the moment someone asks "are you sure?", the honest answer is that you're going on the base rate alone, and the base rate is exactly the kind of consideration a single further fact — who actually won — can override completely. An ordinary perceptual claim can carry a credence no higher, numerically, yet be much harder to dislodge by any single further consideration. It's the shape of the credence, not just its height, that a flat assertion has to answer for. This owes an obvious debt to the pragmatic encroachment picture defended by Jason Stanley and by Fantl and McGrath, on which how good your epistemic position needs to be depends on the stakes; I just want to run the stakes through the speech act rather than through the word "knows," so I never have to argue about whether the ticket-holder "really" knows.

It explains the teacher in the other direction. What she stakes her standing on isn't her personal credence, but the evidential record she's presenting and vouching for as a competent transmitter of it; her private disbelief is beside the point in the same way an auditor's personal hunch that the books are cooked is beside the point once she's checked them and found them clean. The bet is about the evidence, not about her.

If this is right, most of the knowledge norm's genuine successes survive, restated, and most of its failures get explained away, at the cost of trading the question "does she know it?" for the harder-to-dodge but more tractable question "is this the kind of credence a flat assertion is entitled to spend?"

**What I'd like from you specifically:**

- Have I represented Williamson and Lackey fairly, or have I flattened one or both views to make my synthesis look better than it is?
- Is there a clean counterexample — perhaps from the testimony or Gettier literature — that breaks the "shape, not just height, of credence" move?
