I'm a PhD student in communication studies writing a dissertation on why fact-checking interventions so often fail to shift people's beliefs about contested claims, and lately the puzzle has stopped being academic and become personal. My father-in-law is convinced that a dam failure in our region a few years back was deliberately engineered by state regulators to clear the way for a land deal, and nothing dents this: not the engineering reports, not the satellite imagery, not interviews with the outside reviewers, not even documents he requested and read himself. What doesn't fit the models I usually work with is that he isn't defensive about it, and it doesn't seem tied to his identity or politics the way these things usually are: he runs a small engineering firm, reads inspection reports critically for a living, and changes his mind about plenty of other technical questions when new data comes in. On this one topic alone, the claim functions less like a conclusion he argued his way to and more like something obvious sitting in the background — the kind of thing nobody thinks to defend, the way no one bothers defending the claim that solid objects don't pass through each other.

At Thanksgiving I tried a different approach: instead of bringing more evidence, I asked him what, in principle, would change his mind. He wasn't evasive, but he genuinely seemed not to understand the question, the way you'd be stumped if a stranger asked what would convince you that the face you saw in the mirror this morning wasn't really yours. That response has bothered me far more than the original belief did, and it isn't something the backfire-effect or motivated-reasoning literature I usually cite really explains, since both assume he's weighing evidence badly rather than not weighing it at all. When I described this to my advisor, she said, half-joking, that I'd wandered into a philosophy problem and should take it up with a philosopher rather than fold it into my lit review, so I'm asking you instead. Is there a genuine difference between disagreeing with someone about a fact and disagreeing with them about what's even eligible to count as evidence in the first place, and does ordinary argument have any grip at all on the second kind of disagreement? And is there a principled way to tell what's going on with him apart from someone simply being unreasonable — or, if rational argument really cannot reach a conviction held this way, is there any legitimate route by which people do change their minds about things like this, or should I just accept that this topic is permanently off the table at family dinners?
