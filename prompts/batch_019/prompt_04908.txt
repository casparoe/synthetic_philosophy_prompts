I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of philosophy and computational cognitive science, and for the theoretical chapter of my dissertation I've been trying to work out how much weight the so-called "dark room problem" should really carry against the free-energy principle (FEP) and its cognitive-science instantiation in predictive processing and active inference. My advisor is broadly sympathetic to the framework and keeps waving the objection away as a solved problem, but I'm not convinced the standard rejoinder is as clean as it's usually presented, and I'd like a genuinely adversarial second opinion before I commit to a position in writing.

The objection, in its simplest form, runs as follows: if biological agents are systems that act so as to minimize their own long-run variational free energy — a tractable proxy for surprisal, i.e., how improbable their sensory states are given their internal generative model — then the theory seems to recommend retreating to whichever environment is most predictable and least informative, such as a dark, silent, unchanging room, rather than the noisy, exploratory behavior real organisms display. The standard reply within the active-inference literature is that this conflates instantaneous sensory surprise with the *expected* free energy of a policy, which decomposes into a pragmatic term (satisfying preferences/priors over outcomes) and an epistemic or novelty-seeking term (reducing uncertainty about hidden states of the world), so that exploration is often exactly what expected-free-energy minimization recommends, since sitting in the dark room forecloses the information needed to minimize surprise over the longer run. My worry — and the thing I want pushed on — is that this rejoinder looks worryingly elastic: because almost any observed pattern of behavior can be rationalized after the fact as expected-free-energy minimization under some suitably chosen generative model and prior preferences, the reply may purchase its escape from the dark-room problem at the cost of the theory's falsifiability, so that the "solution" is really just a confession that the framework was never substantially predictive to begin with.

Please write an essay that lays out the dark-room objection and the expected-free-energy reply in their strongest respective forms, engages seriously with the further charge that the reply is an ad hoc, unfalsifiability-purchasing maneuver rather than a principled one (for instance, by considering whether the epistemic/pragmatic decomposition is independently constrained and testable, or whether it is simply read off from whatever behavior needs explaining after the fact), and then reaches a definite verdict on whether the free-energy principle survives the dark-room problem as a substantive explanatory framework for cognition and behavior, or whether the problem exposes it as unfalsifiable once its defenders are granted this kind of move. Please don't retreat into an "it depends how one interprets the FEP" non-answer; I need an actual position I can defend or attack in front of my committee.
