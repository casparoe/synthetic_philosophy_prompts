The question, worth 30 points, read as follows:

"A sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it, at t1, into a statue. Call the lump 'Lumpl' and the statue 'Goliath.' At t2, both are squashed into a ball and cease to exist together. (a) [10 pts] Reconstruct, using Leibniz's Law, the argument for the conclusion that Lumpl and Goliath are not identical. (b) [10 pts] Explain how Gibbard's response to this argument is supposed to work, and say what it costs him to give it. (c) [10 pts] Explain how a constitution theorist such as Baker instead resolves the argument, and state the 'grounding problem' that this sort of view faces."

Here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"(a) By Leibniz's Law — the principle that if x is identical to y, then x and y share all the same properties — we can argue that Lumpl and Goliath are distinct. Lumpl could survive being squashed into a ball; it would just be a squashed lump of clay. Goliath could not survive being squashed, since squashing it destroys the statue. So Lumpl has a modal property, namely possibly surviving squashing, that Goliath lacks. Since they differ in their properties, by Leibniz's Law Lumpl is not identical to Goliath, even though the two share all the same matter and occupy exactly the same place at every moment they coexist.

(b) Gibbard's response is to grant that Lumpl and Goliath are in fact two numerically distinct objects that merely coincide spatially for the whole of their careers. Because they are two different things sharing all their matter but not their identity, there is no violation of Leibniz's Law: Lumpl can have a modal property that Goliath lacks precisely because the two were never the same object to begin with. What this costs Gibbard is an explanation of how two numerically distinct objects can be made of exactly the same matter, arranged in exactly the same way, at exactly the same place and time, without one simply reducing to the other.

(c) Baker's constitution view agrees that Lumpl and Goliath are not identical, but holds instead that Lumpl constitutes Goliath: constitution is a real, unifying relation, weaker than identity, in virtue of which Goliath has properties — such as being valuable as an artwork, or being on display in a museum — that the clay does not have on its own. This lets Baker deny that the co-location here violates any deep metaphysical principle, since a constituted object and what constitutes it are not wholly independent existences. The cost of this view is the grounding problem: because Lumpl and Goliath share every intrinsic, non-modal property at every moment they coexist, and are built out of exactly the same particles arranged in exactly the same way, it is unclear what could possibly explain how the two objects manage to occupy the same place at the same time in the first place, given that ordinary objects of the same kind generally cannot."
