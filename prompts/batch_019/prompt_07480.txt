A few weekends ago I was at a dinner celebrating a friend's engagement, and once the ring had made its rounds of the table, two other guests fell into a genuinely interesting argument about whether it meant anything at all. Nadia did her graduate work in animal behavior before moving into something more lucrative, and Owen has a standing habit of interrogating whatever everyone at the table already agrees on, so between the two of them it got more technical than engagement-ring banter usually does. I ended up arguing with both of them in the kitchen for another twenty minutes afterward without moving anyone an inch, which is the only reason I remember their original exchange in this much detail, and I would like a more careful read of it than I could manage myself.

Here is roughly how it went between the two of them, before I got dragged in. (1) Nadia said the ring was a clean real-world case of the handicap principle: Zahavi's point about the peacock's tail is that a costly display is trustworthy precisely because it is costly, since an unfit or heavily parasitized bird could not grow one and still survive, and a ring works the same way because someone who is not serious cannot justify the expense. (2) Owen said this gets the logic backward: a handicap only screens for quality if paying it is disproportionately harder for the low-quality type. But the ring is not scaled to commitment, it is scaled to income, following the old convention of pricing it in months of salary, so a wealthy person with no intention of sticking around pays exactly the same fraction of their earnings as an equally wealthy person who is devoted, and the expense ends up sorting for wealth rather than sincerity. (3) Nadia answered that the trait being signaled need not be love as such; it can be revealed priority. You had countless other things to spend that money on, and choosing this one reveals how you rank the relationship against all of them, which is cheap to claim out loud and expensive to fake in cash. (4) Owen replied that this is still a single, publicly scripted purchase rather than an ongoing cost. A peacock cannot take out a loan for one big display and then coast; it has to keep dragging the tail around every day. So a ring looks less like a genuine handicap than what is sometimes called a conventional signal, kept honest by social convention and the cost of backing out rather than any built-in cost gradient, especially since the months-of-salary convention was itself the invention of a De Beers ad campaign rather than a discovery about commitment. (5) Nadia said fine, call it conventional rather than biological, but conventional signals are still costly signals in the broader sense game theorists use the term, held honest by the cost of reneging and the humiliation of a broken engagement, so Owen was relabeling the mechanism rather than refuting it. (6) Owen said the label was not just semantics, because it changes what should reassure you: a conventional signal is only as honest as the sanction standing behind it, and broken engagements are not especially scandalous anymore, so whatever used to keep the signal honest may have quietly eroded even though everyone still buys the ring.

I cannot tell who actually comes out ahead here, or whether each of them is right about something different and they are partly talking past each other. Could you go through the six moves in order and say, for each one, who has the better of it and why? I am especially interested in whether Nadia's move in (3) actually rescues her original claim in (1), whether Owen's distinction in (4) between a one-off purchase and an ongoing cost is doing the work he thinks it is, and whether there is any real disagreement left by (6) or whether it has dissolved into a dispute over labels. I would also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing about what makes a signal like this honest in the first place. Please close with a couple of sentences giving your overall verdict on whose position holds up better, on balance.
