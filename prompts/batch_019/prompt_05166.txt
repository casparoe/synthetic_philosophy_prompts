In discussions of decision theory for advanced AI systems, one scenario that comes up is the "acausal threat": two agents — say, two AI systems built by unrelated projects, perhaps never in causal contact, or situated in different, causally disconnected regions of a very large universe — may each be able to predict or simulate the other well enough that one can issue a credible threat against the other with no ordinary means of enforcement: "comply, or I will bring about outcome Y, which I know you disvalue enormously," backed by nothing but that predictive link. Since a blanket policy of never giving in to threats is hard to make credible on its own, and can make an agent worse at ordinary, non-adversarial bargaining, some writers on this topic have proposed a partial fix called a "surrogate goal": the agent arranges, in advance, to care intensely about some otherwise inconsequential invented state of affairs (the standard toy example is the total number of paperclips in existence), and commits to resisting any threat against this surrogate exactly as fiercely as it would resist a threat against its real values. The hope is that threateners, wanting to spend their leverage efficiently, end up aiming at the surrogate, so that even a threat that actually gets carried out leaves the agent's genuine interests largely untouched.

Something about this has been nagging at me, and I haven't seen it addressed head-on. Whatever mechanism lets the agent make its commitment to the surrogate credible to a threatener — transparency about its own source code, some formal or reputational guarantee, or anything else — should, by the same token, let it make an equally credible direct commitment to resist threats against its real values. If so, the surrogate seems to add nothing, since the agent could just point that same commitment device at its actual values and skip the decoy. But if no such general-purpose commitment device is available, it's unclear how the agent manages to make even the surrogate commitment credible, since "declare a policy and have it believed" looks like exactly the kind of free credibility that shouldn't be available in any setting where threats are a live problem to begin with. Call this the parity puzzle: either the surrogate is doing no real work, or it's achieving something that should have been equally out of reach.

Could you construct a concrete, novel scenario — with two specific artificial agents, and please not just a restatement of the standard paperclip case — that gets some real traction on this puzzle? I would be equally interested in an example that sharpens it, by depicting a situation where it is genuinely mysterious how the surrogate commitment could be credible while a direct commitment on the real values isn't, or one that dissolves it, by pointing to a concrete asymmetry between the two kinds of commitment that I seem to be missing. Please close with a sentence or two saying which of these your example ends up supporting, and why.
