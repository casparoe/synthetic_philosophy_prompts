In my philosophy of religion seminar we just spent two weeks on the fine-tuning argument for design, and after our last class two of my classmates, Claire and Marcus, kept arguing about it while everyone else packed up, then carried on over email (Claire forwarded me the thread, which is how I have the exchange in front of me nearly verbatim). I'd like your help adjudicating it, since I couldn't quite do it myself.

(1) Claire opened by saying the argument is basically sound: the life-permitting ranges for constants like the cosmological constant and the strong nuclear force are so narrow that their landing where they did by chance would be an enormous coincidence, and enormous coincidences are exactly the sort of thing that calls for explanation. (2) Marcus answered that this gets the logic backwards: it's just the weak anthropic principle that any observers whatsoever will find themselves in a universe whose constants permit observers, so once you factor in that selection effect there's nothing left over to explain. (3) Claire came back with a case from our reading: if you're stood in front of fifty expert marksmen, they all fire, and you're still alive, you don't shrug it off on the grounds that you wouldn't be around to wonder about it otherwise -- you rightly suspect something else is going on. So the fact that fine-tuning was a precondition for our being here to notice it doesn't actually kill the demand for an explanation of it. (4) Marcus then shifted ground to the multiverse: if a vast ensemble of universes exists with differing constants, it's unsurprising that at least one of them, ours, is life-permitting, so cosmology does the explaining, not theology. (5) Claire pulled out Hacking's "inverse gambler's fallacy" objection, also from the reading: inferring that many other universes exist from the fact that ours happens to be fine-tuned is supposed to be exactly as fallacious as inferring that other dice were rolled elsewhere because you yourself rolled double sixes -- your roll is statistically independent of anyone else's, so it can't be evidence for how many other rolls occurred. (6) Marcus wasn't ready to give up the multiverse move; he said the dice case and the universe case might not really be parallel, since we could only ever have found ourselves observing a life-permitting universe regardless of how many others exist, whereas the gambler isn't in any comparable position. But he admitted, a bit sheepishly, that he couldn't say why that difference should matter to the inference, and Claire took the hesitation as a concession.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step? I'm especially interested in whether Claire is entitled to treat (6) as a concession, or whether Marcus's inchoate worry there can actually be turned into a real reply to Hacking's charge -- in which case her citation wouldn't be the last word on the multiverse response after all. Please also flag anything both of them are simply taking for granted that a more careful treatment of the argument wouldn't let them get away with.
