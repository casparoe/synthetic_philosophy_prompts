In my high school's philosophy club, we recently covered a thought experiment devised by Derek Parfit known as Parfit's hitchhiker: you are dying of thirst in the desert, and a driver will only take you into town if she predicts, before you get in the car, that you will pay her $1,000 once you arrive. She is a near-perfect predictor of what people will actually do, and once you reach town she has no way to force payment. Suppose she predicts correctly, drives you in, and you arrive safely. Should you pay?

Two members of the club, Jake and Nora, kept arguing about this after the meeting ended, and neither one convinced the other.

(1) Jake said that once you are safely in town, paying is irrational: nothing you do at that point can reach back and change whether you already got the ride, so a rational person, faced with that choice on its own, keeps the money.

(2) Nora said this reveals something wrong with Jake's picture of rationality, not with paying: if agents who reason as Jake does refuse to pay, a near-perfect predictor would never pick them up in the first place, so his "rational" agents die of thirst while the "irrational" ones who pay end up alive and $1,000 poorer. A theory of rationality that predictably gets you killed isn't obviously the one you should want.

(3) Jake replied that this doesn't refute his claim, only shows that rationality can be unlucky: a rational person can still lose a fair bet, and paying is still not what maximizes expected value at the moment of deciding, whatever it may have cost you earlier to reason that way.

(4) Nora answered that Jake is only evaluating the choice in isolation. If you instead ask what disposition you would want to carry into the desert, the disposition to pay wins outright, and it isn't incoherent to call the rational choice whichever one a rational disposition would produce, even when that same choice looks costly on its own.

They eventually turned to me to settle the argument, and I honestly had no idea what to say. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Please also point out anything important that both of them might be missing.
