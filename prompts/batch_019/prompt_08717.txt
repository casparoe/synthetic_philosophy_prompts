My uncle Reinhard has told the family for years that his estate will go to whichever relative genuinely admires him the most, and he is quite serious about it, raising the subject at almost every gathering. At Thanksgiving, two of my cousins, Marek and Sofia, got into a long argument about it that I have not been able to stop turning over since.

(1) Marek said there is nothing irrational about trying to actually cultivate admiration for Reinhard, since the inheritance gives him an excellent reason to admire the man, and a reason is a reason.

(2) Sofia said that is a mix-up: the inheritance gives Marek a reason to want to admire Reinhard, or to act as if he does, but only Reinhard's actually having admirable qualities could give him a reason to admire him.

(3) Marek answered that if he looks honestly at Reinhard's life and settles on something real, such as the discipline it took to keep the family business afloat through two recessions, and admires him for that, then the admiration is a response to a genuine quality, so it counts, whatever originally sent him looking for it.

(4) Sofia replied that what troubles her is not that Marek might land on something real, but that he will keep "finding" more of it for as long as the inheritance is undecided, in a way he would not if nothing depended on the verdict; a process guaranteed to keep producing favorable findings cannot be trusted even on the occasions when it happens to be right.

(5) Marek pointed out that paid biographers sometimes come to genuinely admire their subjects, and we do not usually treat that admiration as worthless just because they were paid to look closely in the first place.

(6) Sofia said the cases are not parallel: a biographer is paid to produce a book, and whether they privately admire the subject is beside the point of the payment, whereas Reinhard is effectively paying for the admiration itself, which is a strange thing to be able to reliably deliver on demand, and that should make Marek trust his own resulting feeling less, not more.

(7) Marek said that proves too much, since everyone has some incentive to think well of people who have power over them, such as bosses or wealthy relatives, and we do not treat all admiration for the powerful as automatically fake, so Sofia owes him a principled account of how much incentive is too much, rather than just an appeal to how extreme Reinhard's case is.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of each exchange, not just who wins overall?
