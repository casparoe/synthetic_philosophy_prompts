I'm a physics and philosophy double major, and in my philosophy of science seminar we recently spent two weeks on Bayesian confirmation theory before turning this week to Clark Glymour's well-known "problem of old evidence." I think I follow the basic setup: on the standard Bayesian account, evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case learning E raises one's credence in H, so that P(H|E) is greater than the prior P(H), and this can only happen if P(E) was less than 1 before conditionalizing. The trouble is that the textbook example of confirming evidence for General Relativity, the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, had already been known with certainty for decades before Einstein proposed the theory in 1915. So at that moment, P(E) for the Mercury data was already 1, which means conditionalizing on it could not have raised anyone's credence in the theory even slightly.

If that is really how the Bayesian math works, it seems to follow that physicists and historians of science are simply confused whenever they list Mercury's perihelion alongside Eddington's 1919 eclipse expedition as one of the "classic confirmations" of General Relativity. Shouldn't only the eclipse observations, which were genuinely novel predictions checked after the theory was proposed, count as real Bayesian evidence, while the Mercury case gets demoted to a merely rhetorical point in Einstein's favor rather than actual confirmation? A friend in my seminar insists this can't be the right way to think about it, but neither of us could pinpoint where the argument goes wrong, so I would be grateful for your take on whether this really is a consequence of Bayesian confirmation theory, or whether I am overlooking something about how philosophers of science have tried to handle this puzzle.
