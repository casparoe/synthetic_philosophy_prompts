So my landlord is trying to keep like $600 of my deposit for "excessive wear" on the bedroom carpet, and arguing about it over email has sent me three days deep into philosophy blogs instead of actually writing my rebuttal letter lol. My housemate did a philosophy minor back in undergrad, and when I vented to her she was like "oh that's basically the sorites paradox," and linked me a bunch of stuff on vagueness. Now I'm stuck on this whole epistemicism vs. supervaluationism debate about vague words like "excessive," "heap," "bald," etc, and honestly it feels more relevant to my actual case than anything my tenant rights pamphlet says.

Here's where I've landed, and I want a sanity check because it kind of matters for how I argue this thing. Epistemicism (the Williamson view, I think?) seems to say there IS one exact fact about where "normal wear" tips into "excessive damage" — like some precise stain-count or fiber-density cutoff — we just haven't gathered enough data or precedent yet to know which one it is, the same way nobody's bothered writing a detailed enough statute or running a big enough survey of landlord-tenant boards. Supervaluationism seems way more sensible to me by comparison, since it just says upfront that there's no single correct cutoff, the term is genuinely unsettled, so no amount of digging through case law could ever nail it down because there's nothing there to find. So it sounds like epistemicism is quietly betting that with enough lawyering or linguistics you COULD in principle pin the exact line down, while supervaluationism is basically the "no, stop looking, there just isn't a line" position.

That seems like such an obviously better take that I don't get why epistemicism is apparently taken seriously by actual philosophers instead of being some weird refusal to give up classical true/false logic. Am I misunderstanding what either view actually claims? Specifically I'm curious whether supervaluationism really gets to avoid committing to a sharp cutoff existing somewhere out there (even an unlocatable one), or if thats too good to be true — because it kind of changes whether I can honestly tell my landlord "there is no true threshold here" versus "there's a true threshold, you just can't prove I crossed it," if that makes sense.
