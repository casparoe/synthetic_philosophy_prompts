My school doesn't offer a philosophy course, so a few of us started an informal club that meets once a week to work through classic arguments together, and this month we've been stuck on the Epicurean case against fearing death. We covered the basic argument — that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, since there is no one left around to be harmed by it — along with Lucretius's extension of it: his observation that the eternity of time before we were born is just as much a stretch of our non-existence as the eternity after we die, so if we don't waste any grief on the former, it's not clear why we should waste any on the latter. We also went over Thomas Nagel's reply that death can still be bad as a deprivation of future goods even without a "subject" around to suffer that deprivation, but our club's faculty advisor pointed out that this doesn't actually block Lucretius's argument by itself, since the same logic seems to imply we were equally deprived of all the goods we'd have enjoyed had we been born decades earlier, which none of us feel any need to regret.

What I found more promising, after some further reading on my own, is Derek Parfit's discussion in Reasons and Persons of how people seem to be generally biased toward the future rather than the past: his example involves a hospital patient who would rather have already endured a long, painful operation yesterday than face a much shorter one tomorrow, even though this preference makes no sense if you're just adding up total pain. It seemed to me that this general "future-bias" might explain, and perhaps even justify, why we treat posthumous and prenatal non-existence so differently, without needing Nagel's deprivation account at all. But then I came across the claim (I believe the philosopher was Meghan Sullivan, though I'm not fully confident I'm remembering the name correctly) that a fair amount of recent work actually argues our bias toward the future is itself irrational, similar to other well-documented cognitive biases — which would mean I can't simply rely on it to rescue our asymmetric attitude toward death, since I'd be resting on a preference that is independently suspect.

So here is what I would like your help with: please make the strongest case you can that our asymmetric attitudes toward posthumous and prenatal non-existence are nonetheless rational, building on Parfit's future-bias point, and make sure to directly confront the debunking worry above rather than sidestepping it — that is, explain why, even granting that future-bias looks irrational in a stripped-down case like Parfit's hospital patient, this would not automatically show that a future-biased attitude toward one's own existence and death is equally unjustified. Please don't just re-run the standard Epicurus-versus-Nagel exchange for me; I already have that part down. What I haven't been able to resolve is whether the Parfitian move can be defended against the debunking challenge, or whether, in the end, Lucretius wins.
