At a field station on the edge of the Serengeti, two researchers spend an evening reviewing camera-trap footage of Thomson's gazelles stotting — leaping stiffly on all four legs in full view of an approaching cheetah rather than simply bolting — and fall into a long-running disagreement about what the display actually proves. One of them, a committed defender of something close to Zahavi's original, strong version of the handicap principle, holds that a signal can be trusted only to the extent that it constitutes a genuine handicap: an absolute drain on the signaler's time, energy, or safety, since nothing less could rule out cheap imitation by an animal in poor condition. The other, trained in the game-theoretic signaling tradition that grew up partly in response to Zahavi, argues that the formal models which vindicated his idea never actually required absolute cost — only that the marginal cost of producing or faking the signal differ across signalers of different quality — and points to "index" signals, such as body size or vocal formants, that are automatically honest by being mechanically tied to the signaler's real attributes, as proof that waste itself does none of the explanatory work Zahavi's slogan suggests.

Write the dialogue that unfolds between them as they argue this out over the footage, in which each is a fully serious interlocutor for the other rather than a foil. The skeptic of the strong handicap view should have to confront why real displays so often overshoot whatever minimal cost the equilibrium models require — tipping into performances, like stotting a few meters from a predator's jaws, that look far more dangerous than strictly necessary. The defender of the strong view should have to confront why index signals involving no handicap at all are so widespread, and why a case as central to Zahavi's own theory as the peacock's train might be better explained by condition-dependent development than by sheer extravagance. Neither should concede the point outright, but each should be forced, by the end, to qualify their view in light of an objection they cannot easily wave away.
