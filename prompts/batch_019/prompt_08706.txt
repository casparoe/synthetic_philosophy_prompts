Okay so I've been re-reading a bunch of old LessWrong posts on functional/updateless decision theory (the Yudkowsky and Soares stuff) and Parfit's hitchhiker keeps getting trotted out as THE example for why causal decision theory breaks down. You know the setup: dying of thirst in the desert, a driver who's basically a perfect lie detector will only give you a lift into town if she's sure you'll pay her once you're safe, and the whole paradox is that a purely self-interested agent has no reason to actually pay once they're already in town — so if you reason that way, you never get picked up in the first place and you just die out there. Parfit's whole point (this is from Reasons and Persons, i think the section is literally called something like "rational irrationality") is that it can be rational to want to become the kind of person who'll do something irrational later, because having that disposition is what saves you. I get the logic intellectually but at this point i've read the exact same desert-and-truck version of the story probably a dozen times across different blog posts and it's started to feel like a password i can repeat rather than something i actually feel the weight of.

So here's my ask: could you write me a short story, a page or two, that has the same decision-theoretic skeleton as Parfit's hitchhiker but is NOT set in a desert and does NOT involve a truck driver? Move it somewhere else entirely — could be mythological (Charon and the Styx would be a fun one), could be sci-fi (some kind of predictor AI or alien first-contact scenario), could be totally mundane like a negotiation or a card game, whatever you think works best. The one thing I really want preserved is the actual bite of the puzzle: there's a character who only gets rescued/helped/paid/whatever if some other party can accurately tell in advance that they'll follow through on something that, once they're safe, they'll have zero remaining incentive to actually do — and I want the story to sit inside that character's head for a second at the exact moment they'd be tempted to renege, instead of skipping over it. Bonus points if the "predictor" figure can't be fooled by a hasty last-second resolution, so it's clear what's being detected is a genuine standing disposition and not just a promise made under pressure.

No need for a tidy moral-of-the-story wrap up at the end either, honestly I'd prefer if it just ends right on the moment of choice (or its immediate aftermath) and lets the reader sit with it — I kind of want to send it to a couple friends from my book club who've never heard of Parfit and see if the dilemma lands for them on its own, without me having to explain decision theory jargon first.
