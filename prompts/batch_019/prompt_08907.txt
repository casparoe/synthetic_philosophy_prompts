I've been a longtime lurker on LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and Astral Codex Ten, and observation selection effects are one of the few technical ideas from that corner of the internet that I feel I've actually internalized well enough to deploy on my own rather than just nodding along. A discussion in my in-person rationalist reading group last week turned to the fine-tuning argument for theism: the claim that the physical constants (the cosmological constant, the relative strengths of the fundamental forces, the various particle mass ratios) sit inside an extraordinarily narrow range compatible with the existence of any observers at all, which proponents take to require explanation by design or by some kind of multiverse.

My contribution was that the argument dissolves once you apply Bostrom-style observation selection effects properly: we could not possibly find ourselves reasoning from within a universe whose constants were not life-permitting, so discovering that our universe's constants are in fact life-permitting is not evidence of anything — design included — once you have properly conditioned on our own existence. It struck me as exactly the mistake people warn against when they say a lottery winner shouldn't be shocked at having won, once you remember that somebody had to. Before I write this up as a comment on the original thread, I would like an outside check: is there an actual flaw in this reasoning, or has the fine-tuning argument essentially already been dissolved by observation selection effects, such that the philosophers of religion — including the atheists among them — who keep publishing serious papers on fine-tuning are really just exhibiting a kind of disciplinary inertia rather than tracking any real unresolved question?
