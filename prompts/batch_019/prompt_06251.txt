Over the last year or so, I've followed the recurring epistocracy debates that surface on LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and similar corners of the rationalist internet — the argument, associated these days with writers like Bryan Caplan and Jason Brennan, that ordinary voters are too ignorant and too systematically biased for direct democracy to reliably produce good policy, and that we should therefore shift power toward experts or otherwise restrict the franchise. Tracing that argument back to its early-twentieth-century source, I ended up reading Walter Lippmann's *Public Opinion* and *The Phantom Public*, and from there John Dewey's *The Public and Its Problems*, which is usually read as Dewey's attempt to answer Lippmann directly. Dewey doesn't really dispute Lippmann's diagnosis that the "omnicompetent citizen" presupposed by classical democratic theory is a fiction; instead he tries to relocate the whole debate, arguing that democracy should be understood as an extension of his general theory of inquiry — a social, experimental method for transforming a disorganized public into one capable of genuine collective self-governance — rather than as a scheme that stands or falls on how well-informed individual voters already happen to be.

What I haven't been able to work out is whether this actually answers the epistocratic challenge or just restates it one level up. If democratic "inquiry" is supposed to do real epistemic work — treating policies as hypotheses, correcting course based on their consequences, and building the habits of communication that let a public recognize its shared interests — it seems to presuppose exactly the widespread capacity for disciplined, self-correcting judgment that Lippmann denied most citizens have, and that Caplan's and Brennan's empirical case for systematic voter irrationality suggests can't simply be trained into existence through better civic habits. Then again, maybe that objection proves too much, since Dewey could reply that comparably disciplined judgment is just as scarce among whatever body of experts an epistocracy would empower.

I'd like you to write an essay that settles this for me. Please reconstruct Dewey's experimentalist argument for democracy in enough detail that its specific commitments are visible, rather than just restating generic pro-democracy sentiment; lay out the strongest version of the epistocratic challenge it has to survive; and weigh the two against each other. End with an actual verdict rather than a diplomatic "both sides have a point" — a hedged or conditional conclusion is fine (for instance, "Dewey's argument succeeds only if such-and-such empirical claim about trainable public judgment turns out to be true"), but only if you spell out and defend the condition rather than just gesturing at it.
