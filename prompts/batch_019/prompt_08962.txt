I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology, and this term I've been pulled into the small but growing literature on acausal decision theory that circulates in the effective altruism and longtermism research communities. Specifically, I've been working through Caspar Oesterheld's writing on multiverse-wide cooperation via correlated decision-making and the more recent formulations that travel under the name "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL), associated with researchers at the Center on Long-Term Risk. As I understand the core move: in a sufficiently large or infinite universe there will be vast numbers of agents whose decision procedures are correlated with mine to some degree, even though I can never causally interact with them; evidential decision theory then tells me that if I act as though I'm compromising my own goals to promote theirs, this is itself evidence that suitably correlated agents elsewhere are doing the same for me, generating something like mutually beneficial trade with no causal channel whatsoever.

In a decision-theory reading group my advisor runs, I brought this up and someone pointed out how much it resembles something I'd first met as an undergraduate: Douglas Hofstadter's notion of "superrationality," developed in his Scientific American columns and later collected in Metamagical Themas, applied to the one-shot prisoner's dilemma. Hofstadter's superrational agent cooperates because she reasons that any other sufficiently rational agent facing the very same symmetric problem must reach the same verdict, so choosing to cooperate is really choosing the output for every relevantly similar reasoner at once, herself included. That looks, on the surface, like exactly the trick ECL is playing on a much larger canvas: let your own decision settle, in part, what causally disconnected agents will do, and let that purely evidential link do the normative work that causal influence can't.

I'd like to go beyond noting the family resemblance for a term paper I'm drafting. Is ECL best understood as superrationality generalized from a single, essentially identical opponent to a vast, heterogeneous population of only partially correlated agents, or does that generalization quietly discard the very thing that made superrationality persuasive in the first place, namely the tight logical symmetry between two identically-reasoning parties? And, more importantly, which of the two do you think offers the more defensible rationale for cooperating with agents one cannot causally affect: Hofstadter's appeal to symmetry among like reasoners, or ECL's appeal to partial, estimated correlation across a large or infinite world? Please justify your answer rather than simply observing that both positions are controversial.
