In the comments under a LessWrong repost of MIRI's Embedded Agency sequence, two regulars have spent a week arguing over whether Functional Decision Theory's "logical counterfactuals" actually make sense, and I'd like an outside verdict on who has the better of each exchange.

FDT's motivation, as I understand it: a Cartesian agent, cleanly separated from its environment, never has to worry about another process sharing its exact decision-making structure, while an embedded agent can be predicted, copied, or simulated by other parts of the same universe -- which is supposedly what gives Newcomb-like problems their force.

(1) Elena opened by calling logical counterfactuals incoherent: a decision procedure is a fixed computation, so asking what would happen "if" it output something else is like asking what would follow if 7+5 equaled 13 -- there's no possible world for the counterfactual to pick out, so the whole apparatus rests on a category error.

(2) Marcus replied that this conflates two things: logical counterfactuals model an agent's (and a predictor's) uncertainty before a computation is carried out, not a world where mathematics differs -- which is the position any embedded agent is actually in, since treating its own output as already settled invites the breakdown illustrated by the 5-and-10 problem.

(3) Elena granted the uncertainty point but said this just makes FDT dressed-up evidential decision theory: if logical counterfactuals only track correlations between computations, why not just say the predictor's behavior is evidence about yours and leave it there?

(4) Marcus answered that FDT and EDT come apart on cases like the smoking lesion, where EDT is misled by a correlation running through a common cause rather than shared computation, while FDT ignores it correctly -- so "subjunctive dependence" is doing real work, not relabeling correlation.

(5) Elena's rejoinder: nobody has specified, outside of toy cases, what makes two physical processes "compute the same function" closely enough to count as subjunctively dependent once you allow for approximation and noise -- so the original vagueness has just moved, not gone.

(6) Marcus conceded that this individuation problem is genuinely open, but said an unsolved formal problem isn't the same as the incoherence Elena alleged in (1).

Could you go through the exchange move by move -- who has the better of it at each step, not just overall -- and flag anything substantive the two of them are talking past?
