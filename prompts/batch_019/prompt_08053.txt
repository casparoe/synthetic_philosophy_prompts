I've been taking an online philosophy of science course for the past several weeks, mostly out of personal interest — my actual background is in backend software engineering, and I never took a philosophy class in college. The course has a peer-grading component for its short-answer questions: each week everyone is assigned one randomly selected submission from another student to grade against a rubric before the official grade is released. This week's question comes from the unit on biological function, specifically the contrast between Cummins' causal-role account and the etiological account associated with Wright, Neander, and Millikan, and it's honestly the topic I feel least confident about from the whole course so far. The rubric the platform gives graders is pretty thin (four score bands with a one-line descriptor each), so if my assigned classmate contests the grade afterward, I'd like to have specific, defensible reasons on hand rather than just a gut-level number.

The question, worth 10 points on the assignment, was posed to the class as follows: "Using the heart as your example, explain how Cummins' causal-role account and the etiological account (originally due to Wright, later refined by Neander and Millikan into the 'selected effects' view) each explain why pumping blood, rather than making thumping sounds, counts as the heart's function. Then explain which of the two accounts, if either, has an easier time making sense of a malfunctioning heart. Finally, state which account you find more convincing as a general theory of biological function, and defend it against one objection."

Here is my classmate's submission, reproduced exactly as they wrote it:

"Cummins' causal-role account says that a trait's function is whatever causal contribution it makes to some capacity of a system we're already interested in explaining. If we're trying to explain how the circulatory system manages to circulate blood around the body, we analyze the heart's contribution to that capacity, and pumping is what shows up in that analysis — the thumping sound the heart makes is causally real, but it doesn't feed into the capacity we set out to explain, so it isn't picked out as a function. On the etiological account, later tightened up by Neander and Millikan into the 'selected effects' view, a trait's function is the effect that explains, historically, why the trait is still around: ancestral hearts that pumped blood well left more descendants than those that didn't, so pumping blood is what hearts were selected for, whereas making noise was never itself selected for and is best seen as a byproduct. Both accounts therefore agree that pumping, not thumping, is the function, though for different reasons — one appeals to a current causal-systemic role, the other to a selection history.

Where they really part ways is on malfunction. I think the causal-role account actually has the easier job here: because it defines function in terms of what the component is presently contributing, it can look directly at a heart that is failing to pump enough blood and say, correctly, that this is a malfunction, since the current causal contribution has dropped off. The etiological account, by contrast, is anchored in facts about what happened over millions of years of ancestral selection, and it's unclear how those long-dead facts are supposed to tell us anything about the state of this particular heart sitting in front of a cardiologist today.

It's also worth remembering that Wright's own 1973 account was framed specifically around natural selection, and it was really Cummins afterward who broadened the idea to cover artifacts and intentionally designed things as well — which is part of why I lean toward Cummins: his account was built to travel outside of biology from the start, so it doesn't inherit any of the awkwardness that comes from tying 'function' so tightly to one historical process. The strongest objection to Cummins is that his account is too permissive — that literally anything a component does could count as a function relative to some capacity you pick. But I don't think that objection sticks, since the capacity has to be one that's independently worth explaining (like circulation), and relative to that fixed target, pumping blood is functionally relevant in a way that heart sounds simply are not."
