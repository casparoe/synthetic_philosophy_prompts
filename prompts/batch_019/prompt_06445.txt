I'm a PhD student in comparative behavioral neuroscience, currently drafting the theoretical framework chapter of my dissertation on pain across taxa, and I want to apply the most defensible criterion for when nociception rises to the level of conscious suffering. The cleanest standard I have found in the literature holds that felt pain requires the kind of integrative processing that only a mammalian neocortex can provide, so any taxon lacking this structure altogether — fish, amphibians, and invertebrates such as octopuses — should be presumed to process noxious stimuli through unconscious reflex pathways alone, however sophisticated their avoidance behavior looks. Given this, doesn't it follow that the long-lasting behavioral changes researchers such as Sneddon have documented in fish after noxious stimulation, including their reversal by analgesics, are at best evidence of elaborate reflexes rather than felt pain, and that welfare policies extending strong protections to fish and cephalopods on the strength of such behavioral evidence are running ahead of what the neuroscience actually licenses?
