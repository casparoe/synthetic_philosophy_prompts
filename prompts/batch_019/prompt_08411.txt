I spent about thirty-five years running a genetic epidemiology lab before I retired, and for my sins I still sit on the editorial board of a decent mid-tier journal in the field. Since I stopped working I've gotten pretty into philosophy for fun, mostly epistemology, and a couple weeks ago I fell down a rabbit hole on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox -- the one about lottery tickets where you're sure each individual ticket will loose but obviously one of them has to win, and the one about the author who believes every sentence in her book but also writes in the preface that a book that long has probably got at least one mistake in it somewhere. It hit me that our board basically re-litigates a version of that exact puzzle every time a big multi-cohort paper comes through, except nobody on the board would ever put it in those terms, they'd just call it "multiple testing."

Here's the live case, with the details fuzzed a bit so I'm not blabbing about a confidential review. We've got a submission, a meta-analysis pooling a dozen cohorts, reporting 184 loci that clear the standard genome-wide significance threshold (about 5x10^-8, so already Bonferroni-corrected for something like a million effective tests). Two of our reviewers want us to require a sentence in the discussion section stating that, given how many loci were tested and typical replication rates in this subfield, some non-trivial number of those 184 hits are probably false positives -- even though every single one individually cleared the bar we set for calling something significant. A third reviewer, and honestly about half the board on our call last week, think that's close to incoherent: if the correction is doing its job then each claim is individually warranted on its own terms, and a "some of these are probably wrong, we're just not saying which ones" disclaimer doesn't help a reader do anything with the paper, it just muddies what "genome-wide significant" is supposed to certify in the first place. Nobody actually said "lottery paradox" out loud in the meeting, but that is obviously what's going on, ticket by ticket each claim is defensible, and we're about to print all 184 of them as winners.

I have to send the board a short written recommendation before we vote next Tuesday, both on this specific paper and on whether we should adopt a standing policy for "many-hits" papers generally. Could you draft that memo for me? I want a clear bottom line on the actual submission -- accept as is, accept only with the disclaimer or some other specific change, or send it back for revision -- plus a short general policy the board could reuse on future papers like it. Whichever way it comes out, I want the reasoning spelled out well enough that whoever's on the losing side of the argument could recognize their own view in it and respond point by point, rather than feel strawmanned.
