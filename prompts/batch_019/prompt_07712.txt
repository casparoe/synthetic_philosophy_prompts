Thomas Reid was adamant that you can't use reason to prove your own faculties (perception, memory, reason itself) are trustworthy, since any such proof would already have to lean on those very faculties -- and he was totally fine biting that bullet, treating trust in our basic faculties as a starting point that doesn't need and can't get further justification. That's basically the same structure behind what epistemologists now call the "easy knowledge" / bootstrapping problem, like Cohen's gas guage case, where you seemingly can conclude the guage is reliable just by repeatedly trusting its readings and noting they keep checking out, without ever actually looking in the tank. Crispin Wright's answer to this kind of worry is that we get a non-evidential "entitlement" to trust our faculties rather than earned warrant. Is Wright basically just re-running Reid's old move in new terminology, or is there real daylight between them? And would Reid himself have been bothered by the easy-knowledge cases specifically, or just shrugged the way he shrugged at Descartes and Hume?
