For my IB Extended Essay, I've been reading around the logical positivists, and I ran into a case involving A. J. Ayer that isn't addressed in any of my course materials -- when I brought it up, my supervisor admitted it was outside her area of expertise. Ayer spent decades defending the verification principle: the idea that a sentence expresses a genuine, truth-evaluable proposition only if some possible observation could count for or against it. On this basis, he treated claims that an immaterial soul survives, disembodied, beyond the death of the body as not merely false but strictly meaningless, since he thought no experience could ever confirm or disconfirm them. Yet in 1988, after choking on a piece of food while in the hospital and having his heart stop for several minutes, Ayer underwent a vivid experience that he later described in a short piece titled "What I Saw When I Was Dead." In a follow-up meant to walk back some of the more sensational newspaper coverage, he was careful to say that he still did not believe in God, yet he also admitted that the episode had somewhat shaken his confidence that his own eventual death would really be the end of him -- a strange thing for him to have conceded, given that he also wryly maintained that he still hoped it would be.

What I can't decide is whether this concession actually makes sense by Ayer's own lights, and my supervisor's advice to "just present both views" feels like a cop-out. On one hand, a single, unrepeatable, first-person episode undergone by an oxygen-starved brain looks like exactly the sort of thing his own verification principle should treat as evidentially worthless: nobody else could check it, and it has an ordinary physiological explanation, so maybe a rigorous empiricist should have dismissed it outright rather than let it move him even slightly. On the other hand, maybe the concession isn't really a departure from empiricism at all, if the hypothesis that some kind of experience continues after bodily death was already meaningful by Ayer's own standards, so that the near-death episode simply supplied a weak piece of first-person evidence bearing on an antecedently meaningful hypothesis, rather than posing any challenge to something he had previously dismissed as nonsense. Could you write an essay that reconstructs the strongest version of each reading and then actually decides between them? I would like it to end with a definite verdict on whether Ayer's hedge was a rational, if modest, application of his own empiricist principles, or a genuine lapse from them, rather than a conclusion that both sides have a point.
