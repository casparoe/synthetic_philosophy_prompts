I'm taking a graduate seminar this semester on decision theory and rational agency, cross-listed with the CS department's theory group, and after weeks spent on Newcomb's problem, causal versus evidential decision theory, and the prisoner's dilemma played against a psychological twin of yourself, last week's session covered something that has stuck with me more than anything else so far: acausal trade. As my professor presented it, two agents who can never causally interact — separated by an unreachable cosmological horizon, sitting on different branches of a many-worlds multiverse, or such that one of them exists only as a hypothesis inside the other's model of possible minds — might still coordinate their behavior for mutual benefit, purely because each can predict, or simulate, the other well enough to treat its own choice as fixed by the same underlying computation as the other's — what she called their "subjunctive dependence" on one another — with no causal channel between them at all. What I can't get past is the trust problem underneath it: neither side can ever confirm the other exists, contact it, or hold it accountable afterward, so the whole arrangement has to be carried out entirely inside one mind's model of a stranger it will never meet.

Half my discussion section thinks this is an elegant but empty formal exercise, decision theory playacting at diplomacy; the other half, myself included, thinks it has real teeth once you ask what it would actually require of a civilization, or an AI system, trying to act on it in the present rather than merely prove it coherent on a whiteboard. Partly because our final paper asks us to stake out a position on whether acausal trade could ever be genuinely action-guiding, I want to write my way into an intuition first: for the seminar's optional creative component, I'd like to write a short story that takes the second view seriously and dramatizes it, in the spirit of the philosophically serious science fiction we've been reading alongside the technical material, where the argument does the work of the plot instead of arriving as a lecture wearing dialogue tags. I don't want two ships meeting in deep space to shake hands — the whole point is that the parties can't causally touch — and I'd rather the story sit with the hardest part of the idea: how anyone could become confident enough in a model of an unreachable other to pay a real, present cost on the strength of it, and what it would feel like to realize you can never know whether you're trading with someone or only with your own best guess of them. The setting is wide open — two superintelligences on opposite sides of an expanding universe, a lone AI reasoning about civilizations it will never observe, something smaller and more human in scale — but I'd rather any uncertainty stay open at the end than resolve into a tidy handshake.
