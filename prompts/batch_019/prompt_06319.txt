Okay so this has stopped being an abstract philosophy question for me and become a real decision I have to make. I have bipolar I, and after my third hospitalization this spring my psychiatrist brought up drafting a psychiatric advance directive with what she called a "Ulysses contract" clause in it — basically, while I'm stable and thinking clearly right now, I'd pre-authorize her and my sister to hospitalize and medicate me the next time I go manic, even if in-the-moment-me is refusing and insisting I'm fine (which, going by the last three times, I 100% will be). She literally used the Odysseus-tied-to-the-mast comparison to explain it. I went down a research rabbit hole after that appointment and found out this is a whole area of bioethics and legal philosophy, and apparently the exact same structure shows up in debates about whether it's legitimate for a constitution to bind future generations who never agreed to it — something about Jefferson thinking a constitution should basically expire every 19 years and Madison telling him he was being nuts.

Here's the actual fight though: my friend Priya is also bipolar and refuses to ever sign one of these. Her argument is that when shes manic she doesn't feel like a diminished version of herself, she feels like the truest, most vivid version of herself, so letting "stable Priya" pre-authorize force against "manic Priya" is really just one part of her overpowering another part and dressing it up as consent — she says theres a fancier version of this worry somewhere in the personal-identity literature but couldn't remember whose. My friend Dan thinks thats absurd, and that the whole point of a Ulysses contract is precisely that the self who can actually weigh consequences gets to speak for the self who, in the moment, structurally can't — and that this isn't different in kind from what we already accept whenever someone makes a promise or signs an ordinary contract that their future self might come to regret.

My appointment to actually finalize mine is in two weeks and I keep flip-flopping, so could you write me a dialogue between two people holding the most sophisticated versions of these two positions — not strawmen, and not my friends' arguments verbatim, but the strongest case either side could make — actually responding to each other's specific points instead of talking past one another? You don't need to make either one "win." I'd especially love it if at some point they get explicit about whether the constitutional case (binding future citizens) and the psychiatric case (binding your own future self) are really the same problem in different clothes, or whether that analogy secretly falls apart somewhere.
