I was reading Max Black's old paper on the identity of indiscernibles for a metaphysics elective (the one that's written as this back-and-forth dialogue between two people arguing it out), and the thought experiment he sets up is a universe that contains literally nothing except two solid iron spheres, exactly alike in every way -- same size, same temperature, same chemical make-up, the works -- just sitting out there in empty space at some fixed distance from each other forever, with nothing else around to tell them apart. Since literally every property you could name that's true of one sphere is also true of the other, and yet everybody agrees theres two spheres and not one, it seems like this is basically a real counterexample to Leibniz's Law: two numerically distinct things that nevertheless have all of their properties in common.

My study group got into it about this last night and I couldn't really explain why this isn't treated as a huge deal in logic. If Black's spheres are a legit counterexample, doesn't that mean we can't just assume anymore that things which are identical share all their properties, and doesn't that put every proof that relies on substituting equal terms for one another on kind of shaky ground? Or is there some reason logicians and philosophers can basically shrug this off and keep using Leibniz's Law like nothing happened? I feel like I'm missing something here but I can't tell if its about the thought experiment itself or about the law.
