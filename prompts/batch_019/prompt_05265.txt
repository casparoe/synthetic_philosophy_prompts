After some thirty years running an analytical chemistry lab, I retired, but I still sit, in an emeritus capacity, on my former institute's Safety and Incident Review Board. I've also spent a good part of retirement reading philosophy for pleasure, which is probably why I've been asked to draft the board's recommendation on a case that has divided us.

A solvent fire in a teaching lab sent a graduate student to the hospital. Nobody disputes the physical sequence: a fume-hood sash had been left open, a static discharge ignited the accumulated vapor, and the room's sprinkler interlock had been sitting offline, awaiting repair, for four months. The board owes the dean a report, but we cannot agree on what it should say caused the fire. The safety officer wants the report to name the lab supervisor's failure to enforce the weekly sash-inspection checklist as a cause, since enforcing it would have kept the sash shut. Two other members object that, by that same counterfactual logic, the facilities director's failure to prioritize the interlock repair, the original checklist committee's decision years ago to make inspections advisory rather than mandatory, and even a postdoc's failure to mention the solvent smell she had noticed that morning would equally have prevented the fire had any one of them gone differently -- yet nobody is proposing to write up the postdoc. They suspect the safety officer is choosing whom to blame rather than identifying what caused the fire.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. Rather than just restating that anyone who could have prevented a harm but did not is thereby a cause of it, the memo should say which of these omissions, if any, belongs in the report as a cause of the fire; give the board a standard general enough to reapply in future incident reports rather than a one-off verdict; and take the two objecting members' worry seriously before explaining why the standard comes out where it does.
