The following is a question from a graduate exam on the metaphysics of dispositions, together with one student's answer to it.

Question: Explain how finkish dispositions and masked dispositions (the "antidote" case) each generate counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis of dispositions. Then assess whether David Lewis's Reformed Conditional Analysis (which requires that the stimulus and the disposition's intrinsic causal basis jointly constitute a complete cause of the manifestation) succeeds against both kinds of case. Use the standard examples from the literature.

Student's answer:

"The simple conditional analysis says that x is disposed to M when C just in case, if x underwent C, x would M. Martin's electro-fink shows this is too weak: a wire that is actually dead (not disposed to conduct) can be wired to a device that senses contact and instantaneously makes the wire live, so that touching it does produce a shock. The conditional 'if touched, would shock' comes out true even though the wire lacked the disposition until the very moment of contact, so the simple analysis mistakes a finked conditional for a genuine disposition.

Masking, or the antidote case, is a different kind of problem. A dose of poison keeps its lethal molecular structure and is duly ingested, but an antidote taken alongside it stops death from occurring; intuitively the poison hasn't lost its disposition to kill, it has simply been prevented from manifesting it, yet the conditional 'if ingested, would cause death' is false. So here the simple analysis is too strong: it denies a disposition that is actually present.

Lewis designed his Reformed Conditional Analysis to close both gaps at once, requiring that x retain its causal basis B through the stimulus and that C and B together be a complete cause of the manifestation. This immediately blocks Martin's fink: the dead wire's basis is disconnection, and if it retained that basis while touched, no shock would result, so the wire correctly comes out as not disposed to shock. The same fix handles the antidote case just as well: the poison keeps its basis B (its molecular structure) right through ingestion, so C and B are retained exactly as the analysis requires, and the poison counts as disposed to kill regardless of the antidote. The Reformed Conditional Analysis therefore succeeds against both finks and antidotes, which is exactly why Lewis introduced the 'complete cause' clause: to rule out finks and masks together in a single stroke."

Your comments should state precisely which claims are correct, which are imprecise, and whether the final inference about the Reformed Conditional Analysis actually goes through.
