I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of mind this term and we just spent two weeks on split-brain cases. I got kind of stuck on Tim Bayne's "switch model," which is supposed to be the leading alternative to saying these patients have two streams of consciousness (or two subjects, a la Puccetti). As I understand it, Bayne's whole pitch is that we don't need two of anything -- there's just one stream of consciousness running at any given moment, and what changes is which hemisphere is currently feeding it, via attentional competition between the hemispheres. He likes this because its supposed to explain both halves of the data that rival views apparently can't handle together: the disunity you get in the lab when a stimulus is flashed to just one visual field, and the basically normal, integrated behavior these same patients show the rest of the time.

Here's what's bugging me about it. Outside the lab -- shared body, both hemispheres usually getting roughly the same input, cross-cuing between hands, that sort of thing -- a model where there are two simultaneous, permanently seperate streams that are just really well-coordinated seems like it would predict exactly the same integrated behavior as one switching stream does. So basically all the evidence that could favor "one stream that switches sides" over "two streams that are just very good at staying in sync" would have to come from the lab conditions. Except those are supposedly the very conditions where the switching happens fastest, and patients don't report any awareness of switching even there. So I don't see what's actually doing the work of ruling out permanent duality, as opposed to a background assumption that unified consciousness is the default state that needs no positive evidence, while duality does.

Could you build out the actual case against the switch model along these lines? I want an argument for why split-brain patients have, or at minimum have just as much claim to having, two simultaneous and permanently distinct streams of consciousness rather than one stream that alternates sides -- one that engages with what the switch model would actually need in order to be preferred over a duality view, not just a restatement of the standard split-brain phenomena -- and that also flags the strongest thing a switch-model defender could say back.
