For my Population Ethics seminar this week we had to read chunks of Benatar's Better Never to Have Been, and I think I finally get the "asymmetry" argument for antinatalism, but I wanted to run it by you because my study group ended up arguing about it for like an hour after class. As I understand it, Benatar's basic move is that once you actually add up a typical persons life, the pain, illness, boredom, disapointment, the grief of losing people you love, etc, ends up outweighing the pleasure by a pretty wide margin, and since a person who's never born can't be deprived of the good stuff they missed out on, its always better for that person to just never exist. One of my friends in the seminar pointed out that this lines up with all the psychology research on negativity bias and hedonic adaptation (bad stuff hits us harder and sticks around longer than good stuff does), so we kind of decided the empirical evidence is basically on Benatar's side here.

Where we got stuck arguing is this: if his whole case really comes down to the pain-to-pleasure ratio being unfavorable, doesn't that mean antinatalism should stop applying the second you flip that ratio? Like, if some future biotech let us tweak people genetically so their lives were, say, 90% pleasure and 10% pain, wouldn't Benatar have to admit that having kids is fine again, at least for those engineered people? Half my study group thinks this is basically a fatal flaw in his whole position, and the other half thinks I'm butchering what he's actually claiming. Could you settle it for us: is my summary of the asymmetry argument even right, and if not, where exactly does it go wrong?
