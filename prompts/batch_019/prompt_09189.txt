I'm a novelist assembling a small cycle of short fiction in which each story is organized around a single, well-defined philosophical mechanism rather than a philosopher's biography, and for the next piece I want to work with Kierkegaard's diagnosis of "the public" from the closing section of his review of the novel Two Ages — the part usually excerpted as "The Present Age." I'd rather not have another story about anxiety or the leap of faith; those have had their turn in fiction already. Instead, write a short story of no more than about 2,000 words whose plot genuinely turns on at least three of the following four mechanisms: the public as a bodiless abstraction that everyone can invoke and no one can be held answerable for; leveling as the process by which real distinction is erased under the guise of equality; envy functioning as the age's negative substitute for passion, binding people to one another through comparison rather than commitment; and chatter that lets opinion circulate endlessly while obligating no one to anything. You're welcome to take Kierkegaard's own clash with the newspaper The Corsair as a distant point of departure, but I'd like invented characters and a setting of your own choosing rather than a roman à clef. The piece will have failed on its own terms if its central turn would survive being rewritten as a generic "the mob turns cruel" story — I want the specific mechanics of Kierkegaard's argument doing the narrative work, not just its mood.
