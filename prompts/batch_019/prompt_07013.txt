At work I build ranking and recommendation systems, and the standard justification for the whole approach is that we should infer what users actually want from what they click and buy rather than from what they say they want, since behavior is treated as a more reliable guide to preference than stated opinion. I recently learned that this is essentially the economist's revealed-preference theory of utility, and that there is a long-running dispute over whether that theory gives "utility" any real content or simply empties the term out. Write an essay that weighs the charge that revealed preference makes utility-maximization tautological -- since sufficiently consistent behavior can always be redescribed after the fact as maximizing some utility function -- against the reply that the underlying consistency requirements (e.g., ruling out cyclical choices) are themselves a substantive, falsifiable constraint on behavior, not an empty relabeling. It should also take seriously the objection, associated with Amartya Sen, that choice data alone cannot distinguish a person's self-interested preferences from choices driven by sympathy or by commitments that override what they would otherwise prefer -- a distinction that matters if "utility" is meant to stand in for well-being rather than mere consistency. Reach and defend a specific conclusion about whether "utility," defined purely through revealed preference, can do any of the normative work economists want from it, or whether it can only ever describe consistency and nothing more.
