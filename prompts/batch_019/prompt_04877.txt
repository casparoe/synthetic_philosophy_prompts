Over the winter a friend of mine spent about ten days sleeping on my couch while she got away from an ex-boyfriend who had started showing up outside her apartment and calling her workplace. One evening he actually came to my door and asked, quite calmly, whether I had seen her. I told him I hadn't seen her in weeks, which was a flat lie, and I didn't hesitate for even a second before saying it. I belong to a small philosophy discussion group that has kept meeting monthly since a few of us graduated, mostly as an excuse to keep reading, and when I mentioned the episode a few weeks later, someone pointed out that I had basically lived out the "murderer at the door" scenario philosophers like to argue about. That sent me down a rabbit hole, and I was surprised to find that the position I ended up finding hardest to dismiss wasn't Kant's well-known essay on the subject but Augustine's much older, and if anything even more rigid, treatment of lying, which apparently predates Kant's version of the example by well over a thousand years. I wrote up my reaction as a short piece for the group's next meeting, and I'd value an outside pair of eyes on it before I bring it in. Here is the draft:

Augustine devoted two entire treatises to the ethics of lying: De Mendacio, a fairly early work, and the later Contra Mendacium, which he wrote, if I have the story straight, at the urging of a correspondent named Consentius. Consentius had proposed something clever: since the Priscillianist heretics were themselves rumored to condone lying about their own beliefs in order to escape persecution, why not fight fire with fire and send a Catholic agent undercover, pretending to convert to the sect, in order to expose it from within? A lie, in other words, in the service of unmasking liars and protecting the faith. Augustine's answer was an uncompromising no, and it's clear from the text that this wasn't a throwaway judgment but the conclusion of a genuinely careful argument.

His central move is to define a lie not by the falsity of what is said but by the intention behind saying it: a lie, for Augustine, is asserting something contrary to what is in your own mind with the specific aim of getting someone else to believe it. That is part of why motive and consequences can't get you off the hook. He works through something like eight grades of lies, ranked by seriousness: lies told about religious doctrine come out worst, since they put souls at risk, while near the bottom of the scale sits the lie that harms no one and even protects someone's life or chastity. Augustine's whole point is that even this last, most sympathetic case remains a lie, and remains sinful; it is simply less bad than the ones above it on the list, not permitted. He's quite aware this is a hard sell, since scripture itself seems to reward Rahab for lying to protect the Israelite spies, and he spends real energy on her case, essentially arguing that she is being commended for the mercy and faith behind the act rather than for the falsehood itself, which remains a stain even on a deed God chose to honor. What he does allow, interestingly, is silence: someone hiding a persecuted person from pursuers may refuse to answer, mislead by omission, or simply stay quiet, but may not assert the false sentence.

What strikes me now is not the conclusion, which I reject without much hesitation, but the diagnosis underneath it. Augustine's complaint about the small lie and the noble lie is that they involve the very same move: treating another mind as a thing to be manipulated into a false state rather than as something owed the truth. When I told that man I hadn't seen my friend, I don't think for a second that I did anything wrong, and I would do it again without blinking. But there was a specific, small texture to that moment that I recognized when I read Augustine's discussion, and later Kant's almost identical scenario about the murderer at the door: a sense of having deliberately stepped outside the ordinary relation you have with another rational creature, of treating him as an obstacle to be steered around rather than as someone you talk to. My tentative conclusion is that Augustine and Kant are wrong about the moral verdict but not obviously wrong about the phenomenology, and that separating those two things might be a way of taking the position seriously without being forced into its conclusion.

I'd appreciate comments of a few kinds. First, whether I've actually got Augustine's argument right or am flattening something important in it — I'm relying more on secondary discussions than on a close reading of the primary texts, so I could easily be misrepresenting the eightfold scheme or the handling of Rahab. Second, whether the distinction I reach for at the end, between the moral verdict on lying and its phenomenology, is a real distinction or just a way of having things both ways. Third, whether tying a serious piece of theological argument to an anecdote about an ex-boyfriend illuminates it or trivializes it. Longer and more critical comments are very welcome; this only has to survive a room of people who enjoy disagreeing with each other.
