I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm currently reporting a piece on how people actually reason through high-stakes decisions under risk. One case I've been following handed me an argument I haven't been able to settle myself. The subject — a plaintiff in an employment discrimination suit whose identity I'm keeping out of this — was offered a settlement of $2.4 million, guaranteed. Her lawyers put the odds at trial at roughly 65% for a $4.5 million verdict and 35% for nothing, putting the expected value of pressing on at close to $2.9 million. She wasn't under any financial pressure — steady income, no debts, a paid-off house — and she took the guaranteed $2.4 million anyway. When I described the case at dinner, two friends of mine, a litigator named Elena and an economist named Marcus, got into an argument about whether she had reasoned well, and I've been turning their exchange over ever since.

Elena's opening claim was that the client had simply left half a million dollars on the table: the expected value of going to trial was higher, so on average she would have come out ahead by continuing, and nothing about her circumstances gave her a reason to prefer the worse bet. Marcus countered that this confuses expected dollars with expected utility — money has diminishing marginal utility, so a rational agent can easily prefer a smaller sure thing to a larger gamble, and standard decision theory has predicted exactly this kind of choice since the mid-twentieth century. Elena replied that this explanation does not scale to her client's situation: to prefer $2.4 million for certain over a 65% shot at $4.5 million on diminishing-marginal-utility grounds alone, you would need a utility function curved sharply enough that it should also make her turn down obviously favorable bets over far smaller sums, and nothing else in her financial life suggested that kind of curvature. Marcus granted the point but said the only remaining explanation was that she was simply overweighting the 35% chance of getting nothing, which is a bias rather than a form of good reasoning: once you let people weight bad outcomes however they like, there is no choice you could not defend after the fact. Elena's rejoinder was that homeowners routinely buy insurance whose premiums exceed the expected payout without being poor or being called irrational for it, so either all of that counts as bias too, or being especially averse to the single worst branch of a gamble — rather than just to its probability-weighted share of the outcome — can be a genuine, principled feature of rational risk attitudes rather than a defect in them.

Could you work through Elena and Marcus's exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each stage rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important that both of them are missing? I am especially interested in whether Marcus is right that any extra weight on the worst outcome is bound to collapse into ad hoc bias, or whether Elena's insurance analogy points to a disciplined way of cashing out "more averse to the worst case than its probability warrants" that would not let someone rationalize just any choice. Please close with your own judgment on whether the client's decision was well-reasoned, poorly reasoned, or genuinely underdetermined by the considerations the two of them actually raised.
