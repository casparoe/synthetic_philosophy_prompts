I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on liberal theories of border control, and I've been reading Michael Dummett's *On Immigration and Refugees* (2001) — an odd entry in a bibliography otherwise devoted to Frege, intuitionism, and semantic anti-realism, but apparently not a mere hobby: Dummett was knighted in 1999 partly for decades of activism on behalf of migrants turned away at British ports. Two commitments do most of the argumentative work in the book: a default presumption in favour of the right to enter or to seek asylum, defeasible only by specific, statable reasons; and the claim that a state which withholds aid from people fleeing injustice thereby becomes complicit in that injustice, rather than a mere bystander to it.

Dummett died in 2011, so he never had to reckon with the UK–Rwanda removals scheme, which the Supreme Court struck down in 2023 on the narrow finding that Rwanda specifically was not a safe country to send asylum seekers to, without holding that third-country removal is unlawful as such. Reasoning only from the two commitments above, would Dummett's objection to such a scheme have been satisfied by a genuinely safe receiving country, or does his complicity argument condemn the whole offshoring model regardless of the receiving state's safety record? Tell me which commitment is doing the work in your answer, and flag any point at which you are extrapolating beyond what he actually committed himself to in the book.
