I write literary fiction, and for the last several months I have been assembling a linked collection of short stories, each built around a single hard philosophical puzzle dramatized purely through incident and image, never through a character who pauses to explain the theory aloud. The piece I keep circling without landing concerns the paradox Kripke draws out of Wittgenstein's remarks on rules and meaning — sometimes called "Kripkenstein," a label that itself concedes the position may owe as much to Kripke's construction as to Wittgenstein's actual intentions. I understand the shape of the problem well enough: no finite stretch of a person's past behavior, and no mental state accompanying it, however vividly recalled, could ever settle whether someone who has spent years adding numbers has meant addition all along, rather than some deviant function that agrees with addition on every sum actually performed and diverges only past a threshold never yet reached. And the "skeptical solution" on offer doesn't so much refute that worry as relocate meaning out of the private mind and into a community's shared, ongoing practice of correction and agreement.

I would like you to write me a short story, something in the neighborhood of 800 to 1,200 words, that dramatizes this predicament without ever naming Kripke, Wittgenstein, or "rule-following" — the philosophy has to live entirely in plot and image, not in dialogue that amounts to a lecture. As a seed, which you're welcome to keep or discard entirely: an aging village schoolteacher named Brenner has spent four decades marking his pupils' sums with a confidence bordering on the devotional, until a former pupil of his, now a logician of some minor renown, pays him a visit and, gently, almost as a party trick, asks how he can be so sure he ever meant "plus" and not some other rule that would only reveal its difference on sums larger than any he has ever checked. Let the question spread outward from arithmetic into everything else Brenner assumed he knew how to do correctly — greeting a neighbor, finishing a sentence, teaching a child to shape her own name — and close on an image, rather than a stated moral, that gestures toward the thought that whatever survives of "meaning it correctly" does so only as something communal, upheld and enforced by other people, rather than as a fact settled once and for all inside a single mind. Write it in close third person if that suits the material, though I'll defer to your own sense of the right approach, and I'd rather you discover the ending yourself than have me hand you one.
