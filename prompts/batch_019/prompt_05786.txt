I am developing a character for a novel set in the German judiciary during the 1930s: a young judge who takes considerable pride in her strict legal positivism. As the regime's decrees grow more monstrous, I want her positivism itself to be what disarms her: her reasoning is that, since a rule's legal validity is by definition independent of its moral merit, her own jurisprudential commitments give her no grounds for treating a validly enacted decree as anything other than binding law to be applied, so any scruples she harbors must remain private and extralegal. Later in the book, a colleague drawn to Fuller's natural-law-leaning position simply refuses to apply the decrees, on the grounds that they were never truly law at all. Before I build the rest of the novel's argument around this contrast, I would like to check the underlying philosophy: is it accurate that Hart's positivism structurally disarms this kind of principled resistance in a way that Fuller's account does not, or would I be flattening what was actually at stake in the Hart–Fuller debate?
