Several of the online forecasting communities I follow occasionally post questions about unresolved problems in pure mathematics: whether the Riemann Hypothesis will be proven or disproven by some given date, whether a specific large number will turn out to be prime, or whether a particular Diophantine equation will be shown to have no integer solutions, all well before anyone has actually completed the relevant proof or computation. Forecasters assign percentages to these questions on the basis of things like how far computer verification has been pushed, heuristic probabilistic models of the primes, the track record of similar conjectures, and how many serious proof attempts have already failed, yet classical Bayesian epistemology sits awkwardly with this practice: on the standard axioms, an ideally rational agent should assign probability 1 to every true mathematical statement and 0 to every false one, which makes any credence strictly between those values on an open conjecture look like nothing more than a confession of computational limitation rather than a genuine, trackable epistemic state.

I have recently been reading about the logical induction framework that Scott Garrabrant and his collaborators at MIRI developed in 2016, which constructs a computable belief-forming process over the sentences of a formal language such that no computable betting strategy can guarantee itself unbounded profit by wagering against it on how those sentences will eventually be settled, and which, partly as a consequence, ends up assigning calibrated, non-extreme credences to open questions long before they are resolved, while still converging appropriately once they are. I would like you to describe the most important implications of this framework for the forecasting practice described above. Does logical induction actually vindicate assigning meaningful, non-arbitrary credences to unproven conjectures, rather than merely producing numbers that happen to look reasonable? What, even informally, does it suggest about which kinds of partial evidence should move a forecaster's credence, and by roughly how much, relative to the kinds of evidence forecasters currently lean on? And where should someone trying to apply the spirit of logical induction by hand be most wary of overselling its guarantees, whether regarding how quickly or explicitly it actually converges, or how closely its notion of "probability" for a mathematical statement really tracks ordinary empirical probability?
