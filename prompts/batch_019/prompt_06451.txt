Here is the student's answer in full:

"(a) Popper defines the content of a theory A, Ct(A), as the class of all its logical consequences. Within this, the truth-content Ct_T(A) is the set of true consequences of A, and the falsity-content Ct_F(A) is the set of false consequences of A (note that, unlike Ct_T(A), Ct_F(A) is not itself deductively closed). Given two theories A and B, Popper says A has more verisimilitude than B just in case Ct_T(B) ⊆ Ct_T(A) and Ct_F(A) ⊆ Ct_F(B), with at least one of the two inclusions proper. Intuitively, A should say at least everything true that B says and at most everything false that B says, and it should do strictly better on one of the two counts. This was meant to let Popper say, for instance, that Einstein's theory is more verisimilar than Newton's even though both are, on his own fallibilism, false.

(b) This definition was shown to be untenable by Popper himself, who in a pair of technical papers in 1974 proved that if A and B are both false, A can never come out more verisimilar than B by the definition above -- so the definition can only ever rank a false theory below a true one, and never rank two false theories against one another. This is fatal to the whole project, since virtually every interesting theory in the history of science is strictly speaking false, and the point of introducing verisimilitude was to make sense of claims like 'Einstein is closer to the truth than Newton.' The reason the result holds, roughly, is that once a theory already has some false consequence f, any further true consequence t that it has and a rival lacks can be conjoined with f to yield a new false consequence, the conjunction (t & f), that the rival also lacks -- so a theory cannot gain truth-content without also gaining falsity-content once it is already false, which is exactly the combination the definition rules out. Later authors, including Tichý himself as well as Niiniluoto and Oddie, responded by developing similarity-based measures instead.

(c) The similarity approach gives up comparing deductive content and instead measures how far apart, in a space of possible states of affairs, the state(s) compatible with a theory are from the one actual state of affairs -- roughly, how many respects a theory gets wrong, and by how much, rather than simply whether it entails more truths and fewer falsehoods than a rival. Because distance comes in degrees, this yields a genuine ordering of false theories, which is exactly what Popper's content-based definition could not give us. It also sits naturally with the fact that we can never confirm a theory's verisimilitude directly and so must rely on its degree of corroboration instead: for Popper, a theory's corroboration at a given time just is our measure of the verisimilitude it has accumulated so far, so the two notions, though conceptually distinct, turn out to be extensionally identified in his mature view."
