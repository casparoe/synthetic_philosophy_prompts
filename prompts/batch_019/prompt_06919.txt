In my AP Statistics class, we spent a week on how graphs can be made to mislead people without technically lying — truncated axes, cherry-picked scales, icons that get stretched or enlarged instead of simply repeated — and our teacher mentioned, almost as an aside, that an Austrian philosopher named Otto Neurath had tried to solve this exact problem a century ago with something called Isotype. I went down a bit of a rabbit hole afterward and learned that Neurath, who was also a member of the Vienna Circle, believed that standardized pictograms could communicate social and economic facts to people of any language or education level more honestly than prose ever could, precisely because they followed strict rules, like never resizing a single symbol to show a bigger quantity and instead repeating it a corresponding number of times. His slogan, apparently, was "words divide, pictures unite," and the whole project was bound up with his socialist commitment to educating ordinary workers and with the Vienna Circle's hope for a single, clear, "unified" language for science that would leave no room for the kind of ambiguity or metaphysical confusion he thought ordinary language invited.

What I can't work out is what Neurath would say if he actually saw how pictorial statistics turned out. Today, infographics reach exactly the mass, cross-linguistic audience he wanted — Instagram slideshows, TikTok explainer videos, government dashboards — but they are also one of the most common vehicles for quietly misleading people, since a lot of them violate his own first rule of Isotype by resizing icons rather than repeating them, or lean on an image's emotional punch rather than the "cold," countable clarity he was after. Would he most likely treat this as a rough vindication of his project that just needs firmer institutional discipline and standard-setting, or would he think that once pictorial statistics leave the hands of a neutral, science-minded institution and get produced instead by influencers, advertisers, and partisan accounts chasing engagement, the whole enterprise has betrayed what he meant by a picture language in the first place? I would like your best reconstruction of his likely reaction, grounded specifically in what he argued about Isotype and the Vienna Circle's broader aims, rather than a generic guess about what any early-twentieth-century thinker might say about the internet.
