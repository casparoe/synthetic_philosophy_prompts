I fell down a rabbit hole recently that started with some old Overcoming Bias and LessWrong posts on cryonics and personal identity, and ended with me reading through the bioethics literature on how we define death — specifically Alan Shewmon's critique of the "whole-brain" standard. His argument, if I'm summarizing it correctly, is that the standard's official rationale (that the brain is the organism's "integrator," the thing that unifies all the parts into one functioning whole, so its irreversible loss means the organism as a whole has ceased to function) is empirically undermined by the many documented cases of ventilator-maintained, whole-brain-dead bodies that go on digesting food, healing wounds, fighting infections, regulating temperature and fluid balance, growing, sexually maturing (in children), and in a handful of well-documented cases even gestating a pregnancy to viable delivery — all with zero brain function. If a body can do all that, Shewmon asks, in what sense has it lost its "integrative unity"? This got me wondering how contingent our current definitions of death are on a fairly recent, brain-centric picture of what makes an organism one unified living thing, and that made me curious what an ancient thinker with a totally different, and quite explicitly worked-out, theory of the seat of life would make of the whole debate.

Aristotle is the obvious candidate, since he was as much a careful biologist as a metaphysician, and he explicitly and repeatedly argues (in the biological works, especially the Parts of Animals and the Parva Naturalia material on sleep and on youth, old age, and respiration) that the heart, not the brain, is the central organ of sensation, self-motion, and vital heat, and that the brain's job is merely to cool and temper the blood the heart sends up to it. This sits inside his broader psychology in De Anima, where the soul is the substantial form or "entelechy" actualizing a naturally organized body, and where soul-functions come in a nested hierarchy — nutritive, sensitive, and rational — with the nutritive being the most basic, and the one shared even with plants.

Given all that, I'd like you to reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Aristotle would most plausibly say about a real modern case: a patient on a ventilator with complete and irreversible loss of all brain and brainstem function, but whose heart keeps beating on its own and whose body keeps up the nutritive-level functions described above. Do his own principles commit him to saying this body is still alive (if only vegetatively, via a nutritive soul centered on the heart), that it's dead, or that it's a case his conceptual toolkit simply wasn't built to classify? I'm especially interested in two tensions internal to his own view: first, whether his heart-centered account of bodily integration ends up supporting the modern "integrative unity" rationale for brain death (just relocated to the wrong organ) or undermining it outright; and second, whether his distinction between natural substances, which are organized by an internal principle ("physis"), and artifacts, which are organized by something external, gives him independent grounds to deny that a ventilator-sustained body is still a genuine unified substance at all, regardless of what the heart is doing on its own. I'd like your best-supported verdict rather than just a balanced list of considerations on both sides.
