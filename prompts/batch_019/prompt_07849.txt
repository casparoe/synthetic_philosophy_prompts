A few of us were at dinner not long after watching a documentary about Han van Meegeren, the Dutch painter who forged a series of "lost" Vermeers in the late 1930s and 1940s so convincingly that the era's foremost Vermeer scholar authenticated one as a masterpiece and another ended up in Hermann Göring's collection. When van Meegeren was exposed after the war, he admitted the paintings were his own only once he faced a far graver charge, and had to paint a new "Vermeer" under police supervision to prove his claim. Critics who had once praised the luminous handling of light in these works suddenly found them stiff and faintly absurd. Two friends of mine, Elena and Marcus, argued about what to make of that reversal for long enough at the table that I remember the exchange move for move.

(1) Elena said the reversal proves nothing except that critics are suggestible: not one brushstroke on the canvas changed between being called a masterpiece and being called a joke, so whatever changed people's minds wasn't a genuine aesthetic discovery, only contamination from learning the painter's real identity. (2) Marcus disagreed: properties like "a mature synthesis of Vermeer's late style" are themselves aesthetic properties, not biographical trivia tacked onto the work, and you can only perceive them once you know when and by whom a piece was actually made -- the identical brushstroke can be innovative in one history and derivative in another. (3) Elena answered that this proves too much: if pre-reveal viewers enjoyed the painting just as fully, then on Marcus's own view they were having a perfectly good aesthetic experience of it, so really there are two separate things here -- an experience that forgery leaves untouched, and this notion of "achievement," which looks like a fact about the painter's biography rather than about the painting. (4) Marcus replied that we don't treat this as mere biography anywhere else: we would revise our opinion of a "startlingly original" student essay on learning it was lifted from an obscure old journal, and that revision feels like correcting a mistake about what we were looking at, not snobbery. (5) Elena's closing point was that van Meegeren's own feat -- reverse-engineering a seventeenth-century painter's materials and manner well enough to fool the leading expert of the day, without any of the training or tradition Vermeer actually had -- sounds like a considerable achievement in its own right, so if achievement is what's aesthetically at stake, the forger should score rather well by Marcus's own standard; that he doesn't just shows the standard was rigged from the start to reward originality alone.
