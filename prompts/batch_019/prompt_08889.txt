A friend and I had a long, unresolved argument last week that started from something she had read about the common prior assumption in Bayesian epistemology. Her claim was that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot, by Aumann's theorem, "agree to disagree" once their posteriors about some event become common knowledge — and since the common prior assumption is really just what rationality under uncertainty requires in the first place, any two informed, honest people who still disagree after a long conversation must have at least one irrational party among them. I objected that this seems to indict most of the disagreements I have with smart, well-informed colleagues, but I couldn't pin down exactly where her argument breaks down. Was she right that the common prior assumption simply falls out of Bayesian rationality, and that our persistent disagreements, once we've actually talked things through, are therefore a mark of irrationality?
