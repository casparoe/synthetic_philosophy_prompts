"When the first edition of the Principles of Political Economy appeared in 1848, Mill treated schemes for the common ownership of property largely as untested hypotheses: existing arrangements had obvious faults, but no rival system had yet proved itself in practice, so wholesale change could not yet be recommended. Over the next several editions, however, the chapter 'Of Property' was substantially rewritten in a far more sympathetic direction, a shift Mill credits in his Autobiography chiefly to the influence of Harriet Taylor, whom he married in 1851. He came to insist that whether some form of socialism was ultimately preferable to private property was a question that only accumulated social experience, and not armchair reasoning, could settle.

What Mill never accepted was that this change should be sudden, centrally imposed, or won by force. This is the burden of his unfinished Chapters on Socialism, which Harriet Taylor prepared for publication after his death: existing socialist and communist proposals were, in his view, still essentially untested, and the only responsible route forward was through small voluntary experiments rather than revolution. The experiment he found most promising was the producer cooperative, in which the workers of an enterprise owned it jointly and divided its proceeds among themselves; he cited flourishing examples from the French building trades as proof that laborers could manage their own affairs once given the opportunity, removing the need for a capitalist employer altogether.

Mill was also careful to separate 'socialism' from 'communism' in the strict sense: communism required strict equality of distribution regardless of individual contribution, whereas the socialist schemes he took seriously could still reward people unequally according to the work they contributed.
