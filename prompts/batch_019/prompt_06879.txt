Question (25 points). On Monday, a sculptor takes a lump of clay, Lump1, and, without adding or removing any matter, shapes it by Tuesday into a statue, Goliath. Lump1 continues to exist throughout, coincident with Goliath from Tuesday onward.

(a) [8 pts] Give the standard Leibniz's-Law argument for thinking Lump1 and Goliath are numerically distinct despite being made of exactly the same matter on Tuesday.
(b) [9 pts] Explain how Lynne Rudder Baker's "constitution" relation is meant to let Lump1 and Goliath be non-identical yet coincident, without simply multiplying objects in the same place.
(c) [8 pts] State the "grounding problem" as a challenge to views like Baker's, and briefly assess whether her framework has the resources to answer it.

Student response:

(a) Lump1 and Goliath can't be identical because they don't share all their properties, and by Leibniz's Law (the indiscernibility of identicals), identical objects must share every property. Lump1 existed on Monday, before any statue existed, so it has the property "existed on Monday," which Goliath lacks. There's also a modal version: Lump1 could survive being squashed into a ball, since a lump of clay doesn't care about its shape, but Goliath could not survive squashing, since that would destroy the statue. Since one has a property the other lacks, they are numerically distinct, even though on Tuesday they are built out of the same particles arranged the same way.

(b) Baker's response is that Lump1 and Goliath are related by constitution rather than identity: Lump1 constitutes Goliath, and the two are "unified" without being one and the same thing, so there is no need to deny that a lump and a statue can occupy the same place at the same time. This is essentially the same move four-dimensionalists make when they say two objects can share some of their temporal parts without sharing all of them — Lump1 and Goliath overlap in their Tuesday "stage" but diverge before and after, which is exactly why constitution lets them coincide on Tuesday without being identical. Constitution also gives Goliath new causal powers it wouldn't have as a mere lump, such as the capacity to represent a giant or to be sold as a work of art, which the lump doesn't have just in virtue of being clay.

(c) The grounding problem is really just the puzzle from part (a) restated: if Lump1 and Goliath are made of all the same matter, arranged the same way, on Tuesday, how can they be two things rather than one? It's sometimes called the problem of the many for this reason, since we seem forced to recognize extra objects sharing the same matter when intuitively there should be only one thing there. I think Baker's view handles this well enough, since once constitution is treated as a real, sui generis relation rather than mere spatial overlap, there's no longer any pressure to say we have "too many objects in one place" — constitution is precisely what explains why that isn't an unexplained coincidence.
