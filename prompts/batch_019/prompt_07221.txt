After forty years in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement working through the philosophical literature on the nature of space and time, and one puzzle keeps pulling me back in. Newton's rotating bucket is meant to show that space itself, over and above the relative motions of bodies, must be invoked to explain why the spinning water climbs the sides of the vessel — a classic argument for substantivalism. Yet the twentieth-century "hole argument," developed by Earman and Norton out of a worry Einstein himself raised while constructing general relativity, seems to cut the other way: if spacetime points have an identity independent of the metric field defined over them, then the field equations fail to fix that field uniquely within a bounded region even once everything outside it has been completely specified, leaving substantivalism committed to a strange, unphysical indeterminism. I would like you to compare these two arguments in some depth: what, precisely, does each establish, by what mechanism, and against which opponent? Could a substantivalist who finds the bucket argument decisive consistently escape the hole argument's threat — for instance, by adopting "sophisticated substantivalism" and denying that diffeomorphic models represent distinct physical possibilities — or does that maneuver quietly give up what made the bucket argument compelling in the first place? Please defend a considered verdict on which of the two arguments does more genuine philosophical work, and why.
