I've been lurking on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for a few years, picking up decision-theory vocabulary secondhand rather than through any formal training in philosophy, and I recently drafted a short post trying to pin down how strong I actually think the "coherence arguments" for expected utility maximization are. Before I post it anywhere, I'd like a critical read. Here's the draft:

---

*Draft: Coherence arguments and the goal-directedness gap*

The argument I keep running into goes roughly like this: an agent whose preferences violate the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence) can be money-pumped — exploited through a sequence of trades it agrees to one at a time, ending up strictly worse off than where it started. Since no one wants to be a money pump, a sufficiently capable, self-correcting agent will eventually iron out any VNM-violating quirks in its own preferences. By the VNM representation theorem, what's left is behavior indistinguishable from maximizing the expectation of some real-valued utility function. Stretched to its usual AI-risk conclusion: a sufficiently optimized system, whatever it was trained to do, will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer with respect to *some* utility function, and almost any non-trivial utility function gives an agent instrumental reasons to accumulate power, resist being shut down, and so on. Coherence is doing a lot of the work in getting from "very capable optimizer" to "goal-directed agent with convergent instrumental incentives."

The objection I find most damaging is the vacuity point I associate with Rohin Shah: almost any behavior can be redescribed as expected-utility maximization after the fact, by defining the utility function directly over entire action-histories rather than over outcomes in any ordinary sense — whatever the agent actually does just gets utility 1, every deviation gets 0. A thermostat, a rock, or a system that repeats one fixed motion forever is already "coherent" in this thin sense. So "training pressure selects for VNM-consistency" is nearly trivial once gerrymandered utility functions are allowed; the actually substantive (and much less obviously true) claim would have to be something like "training pressure selects for consistency with a comparatively simple utility function over a small set of outcome-features."

The second objection, which I didn't expect to find as convincing, targets completeness specifically, and I think it's due to Elliott Thornley's work on this (possibly misremembering the attribution). The standard money-pump story for completeness assumes the agent trades away from whatever it currently holds any time it's non-negative about the trade, evaluated locally, one step at a time. But an agent with a genuine gap in its preferences — neither preferring A to B, nor B to A, nor indifferent between them — isn't thereby forced to make every trade a naive, step-by-step chooser would make. An agent that commits to a whole plan in advance and only accepts trades that fit that plan (the "resolute choice" move from the decision-theory literature) can simply decline the one trade that would have pumped it, without ever having to resolve the gap in its preferences first. If that holds up, incompleteness — not just intransitivity — survives contact with the money-pump argument, and the claim that ideal reasoners must have complete, VNM-representable preferences is much shakier than the popular version of the coherence argument suggests.

Tentatively, I think coherence arguments are solid ground for ruling out a narrow class of preferences that really are cheaply exploitable (cyclic preferences over goods you'd actually trade). I don't think they get you anywhere near "expect a simple, fixed, outcome-based utility function" without smuggling in extra assumptions about simplicity or about how the agent handles trades over time — and those extra assumptions are where the real content of the AI-risk argument lives, not in the theorems themselves.

---

I'd rather have the holes in this found now than after I've posted it. Specifically: (1) have I represented the two objections fairly, or flattened something important about either one? (2) Is there a standard defense of the completeness axiom against the resolute-choice move that I'm missing? (3) Is my closing claim — that the interesting content here is empirical, about what training actually selects for, rather than mathematical — a fair way to carve up the disagreement, or does it let the coherence argument off too easily?
