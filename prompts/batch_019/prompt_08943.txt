I'm a novelist currently at work on a multigenerational family saga built around a reincarnation subplot, and mentioning the premise at a dinner party set off a disagreement between two of my guests that I still haven't been able to settle in my own head.

(1) Naomi, who has a background in comparative religion, opened by arguing that any story like mine is incoherent on Buddhism's own terms, not just implausible: anatta denies there is any persisting self, so there is no "you" left over to be reborn, and no continuous subject for karma to attach reward or punishment to across lives. (2) Callum, a friend finishing a philosophy doctorate, said this only refutes rebirth-with-a-soul; Buddhist metaphysics never needed a persisting thing to carry over, only a causal continuum -- the way a flame lit from another flame transmits nothing but causal dependence, not some persisting stuff. (3) Naomi granted that this avoids outright contradiction, but said it wins by emptying the doctrine of the very content that makes it matter to anyone: if "rebirth" just names some causal relation between an earlier mental stream and a later one, it no longer secures what believers actually want -- that something of me will reap what I sow -- and collapses into the trivial claim that my daughter is "me reborn," since she is causally descended from me too. (4) Callum answered that this overgeneralizes: Parfit pressed exactly this worry against ordinary, single-life identity, arguing that psychological continuity within one lifetime is already a matter of degree and never delivers the deep further fact people assume it does, and that Buddhist philosophers had made a structurally similar move long before him. Applied consistently, he said, Naomi's own standard dissolves the self she assumes persists from breakfast to dinner, not only the one she refuses to let persist between lives.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point? I'm especially interested in whether either of them is actually tracking how Buddhist philosophers themselves have handled this problem, and in anything substantive you think both of them are quietly taking for granted.
