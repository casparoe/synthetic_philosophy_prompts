I'm reporting a piece on efforts to make AI systems cooperate with one another, and while working on it I sat in on a small closed workshop on multi-agent systems where two researchers fell into a running argument over dinner that the rest of the table never quite managed to settle. I'd like your help sorting out who had the better of it, move by move.

The subject was program equilibrium: the game-theoretic idea that if players in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma submit programs rather than bare actions — programs that can inspect one another before producing a move — mutual cooperation can become a genuine equilibrium, with no repeated play and no outside enforcement. The standard construction has both players submit the identical program "cooperate if and only if your opponent's program is exactly mine, otherwise defect"; switching unilaterally to any other program only gets you defected against, since the other side's program no longer recognizes you.

Here is how the argument went:

1. The game theorist opened by calling this a genuine solution to one-shot cooperation: two purely self-interested players, exchanging nothing but code, arrive at mutual cooperation as a stable outcome that was simply unavailable to bare actions in the underlying game.

2. The safety researcher objected that this shows less than it appears to: the "program game" has a far larger strategy space than the original Dilemma, and in that larger game mutual defection is an equilibrium too, and so, by a folk-theorem-style argument, is almost any individually rational split of the payoffs. Nothing in the construction explains why rational players would coordinate on the cooperative equilibrium rather than one of the many others.

3. The game theorist answered that multiplicity of equilibria is an ordinary feature of game theory, not a special defect of this result — coordination games have it too, and no one treats that as proof coordination never happens. The actual achievement is narrower: cooperation is now among the stable outcomes, which is a discovery about what is possible, not a promise to explain what players will in fact do.

4. The safety researcher's rejoinder was that this "possibility" is bought by quietly assuming each side can verify, rather than merely guess at, the literal content of the other's program — and once that much mutual transparency is on the table, the two players might as well sign a binding contract, which is exactly the outside enforcement the result was supposed to do without. The cases that actually matter, for AI agents and for people alike, are the ones in which nobody can read anyone else's source code, and the result has nothing to say about those.

Neither one backed down. Could you go through the exchange move by move? For each of the four numbered moves, give a score from -2 (clearly mistaken) to +2 (clearly right) reflecting how well it holds up on its own terms, present these as a JSON object with keys "1" through "4", and then add two or three sentences per move justifying the score. Close with a short note on anything you think both of them are taking for granted that neither one thinks to question.
