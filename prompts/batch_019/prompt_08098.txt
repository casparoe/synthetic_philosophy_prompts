I've spent a fair amount of time on LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten, and I'd like to think I have a solid handle on the standard one-boxing/two-boxing dispute over Newcomb's Problem — I can rehearse the evidential-versus-causal decision theory arguments without much effort at this point. Recently, though, I went back to Gibbard and Harper's original 1978 paper and got stuck on the example they use to motivate their discussion of ratifiability: Death in Damascus. You're in Damascus, Death gives you a meaningful look, and you have to decide whether to stay put or flee to Aleppo, but Death is a near-perfect predictor who has already committed to a city based on where he expects to find you, so whichever act you're on the verge of settling on, the news that Death predicted it seems to make that very act look like the wrong one. Unlike Newcomb's Problem, where two-boxing at least remains ratifiable even though one-boxing pays better, here neither staying nor fleeing seems to be a ratifiable pure act at all, which is apparently why Richard Jeffrey's ratifiability proposal — meant to let evidential decision theorists recover the causalist's verdict in ordinary Newcomb cases — is usually said to break down entirely on this very example.

I brought this up with my local rationalist-adjacent reading group, and it turned into a surprisingly unresolved argument. One friend insisted the case just shows that causal decision theory, and the whole ratifiability framework built on top of it, is broken in a way that actually matters, gesturing toward Andy Egan's counterexamples to CDT and Arif Ahmed's book-length defense of evidentialism. Another insisted the puzzle dissolves the moment you allow mixed strategies, on the model of how matching pennies has a perfectly good solution once you stop insisting on a pure-strategy equilibrium. A third brought up updateless and functional decision theory and argued that framing the problem in terms of "ratifiable acts" was the wrong lens from the start. Nobody was willing to just say what they actually thought you should do, standing in that market with Death staring at you.

So I'd like an essay that first lays out, precisely, how evidential decision theory, causal decision theory (with and without a ratifiability requirement), and a broadly updateless/functional approach would each handle Death in Damascus, and how the case differs structurally from ordinary Newcomb's Problem. But don't stop at a survey: take a side. Tell me, and defend, what a rational agent should actually do when Death gives them that look — including whether "stay," "flee," and "randomize" exhaust the sensible options, and if randomizing wins, at what probability and why. An answer of the form "randomize in such-and-such a way if the predictor is truly perfect, but do X if there's any slack in the prediction" is a perfectly acceptable place to land, provided you argue for it rather than simply cataloguing the possibilities.
