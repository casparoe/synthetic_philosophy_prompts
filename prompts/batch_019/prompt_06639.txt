As a backend engineer who spends a lot of my day job thinking about trust between services that don't necessarily trust each other's code, I went down a total rabbit hole this weekend reading about "program equilibrium" in game theory — I think the original idea comes from a Tennenholtz paper from the early 2000s? The basic hook is that instead of players picking actions directly in a game, they submit programs that get to see each others source code and compute a move on their behalf, and it turns out you can get cooperation as an actual Nash equilibrium in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma this way, which regular game theory says shouldn't be possible. I started with the simple "cooperate iff your source is byte-for-byte identical to mine" trick, then fell way deeper into the provability-logic side of things: FairBot, which uses Lob's theorem so two copies of FairBot can each prove the other one cooperates without an infinite regress, then some fancier refinement (PrudentBot? might be misremembering) meant to avoid getting suckered by agents that cooperate unconditionally, and then a totally different line of work on "epsilon-grounded" strategies that throw in a pinch of randomness so the whole setup stays well-founded even when neither program can fully simulate the other. Apparently there's also a "modal combat" tournament thing some LessWrong people ran at some point, kind of an Axelrod tournament but for modal-logic agents instead of plain strategies.

My brain is a bit fried trying to figure out which of these actually deserve to be taken seriously as a real solution to the cooperation problem versus which ones are more of a cute trick that only works in toy settings. Could you rank the following approaches from most to least compelling, where "compelling" roughly means: gives you a genuinely robust, well-founded reason to expect cooperation, rather than just baking cooperation into the setup by fiat?
