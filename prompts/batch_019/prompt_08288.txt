The question (take-home essay, worth 100 points) was: "In their 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange, Hart and Fuller both discuss the case of the German 'grudge informer' -- the wife who denounced her husband under a Nazi-era statute and was prosecuted after the war for unlawfully depriving him of his freedom -- to make their respective cases about the relation between law and morality. Explain how each of them thinks the case should have been resolved and what each takes it to show, then say which view you find more convincing and why."

Here's the student's answer, transcribed pretty much verbatim:

"During the war a woman reported her husband to the authorities for making remarks against Hitler while he was home on leave, citing a Nazi statute that made this a crime; he was sentenced to death, though the sentence was commuted. After the war she was charged with unlawfully depriving him of his freedom under an ordinary criminal-code provision that predated the Nazis, and argued she'd only relied on the law as it stood. The German court held that the Nazi statute was so contrary to the conscience and sense of justice of decent people that it had never been valid law at all, so her defense failed and she was convicted.

Hart thinks this reaches a defensible outcome by the wrong route. His view is that the statute really was law at the time, and that pretending otherwise blurs the line between law and morality that positivists want to keep sharp -- you can call the old law evil without rewriting history to say it was never law. Since it was valid, the solution that actually follows from Hart's own premises is that the woman shouldn't be punished for something that was legal when she did it. This ties into a bigger point in the essay: Hart argues that it was really the German legal profession's positivist habit of treating any duly enacted statute as binding, no questions asked, that made it so easy for judges to go along with the regime, which is part of why he's so insistent on keeping validity and morality apart here.

Fuller's response operates a level up: instead of asking whether this one statute was too unjust, he asks whether the Nazi legal order as a whole -- riddled with secret decrees, retroactive statutes, and courts that bent the law to fit political ends -- still deserved to be called a legal system at all. His notion of an internal morality of law (generality, publicity, prospectivity, and so on) is doing the work here, not just a gut check about substantive injustice the way Radbruch's formula does it.

Between the two I find Fuller's procedural angle more useful -- 'too evil to count as law' feels like it proves too much, whereas Fuller at least gives you criteria to apply."
