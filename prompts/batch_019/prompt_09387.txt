Ive been re-reading Franklin's autobiography for background on a novel I'm slowly outlining (my protagonist is a Franklin-shaped self-improvement obsessive, complete with his own version of the little virtue-tracking chart), and I got properly stuck on a passage in Part Two before I start putting words in this guy's mouth. It's the bit where Franklin explains how "Humility" got added to his list of thirteen virtues after a friend called him out on his pride. There's also a great bit earlier in that same section about a man who wants his axe ground perfectly bright and gives up, deciding he likes "a speckled ax" better — I brought both passages up with my writing group last week and we mostly just talked past each other, but it's this next part that's actually got me stuck:

"My list of virtues contain'd at first but twelve; but a Quaker friend having kindly informed me that I was generally thought proud... I added Humility to my list, giving an extensive meaning to the word. I cannot boast of much success in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but I had a good deal with regard to the appearance of it... And this mode, which I at first put on with some violence to natural inclination, became at length so easy, and so habitual to me, that perhaps for these fifty years past no one has ever heard a dogmatical expression escape me... In reality, there is, perhaps, no one of our natural passions so hard to subdue as pride. Disguise it, struggle with it, beat it down, stifle it, mortify it as much as one pleases, it is still alive, and will every now and then peep out and show itself; you will see it, perhaps, often in this history; for, even if I could conceive that I had compleatly overcome it, I should probably be proud of my humility."

What's tripping me up is how the "appearance vs. reality" move in the middle relates to the joke at the end. Every study-guide summary I can find just says something like "ha, ironic, Franklin got proud of his own humility" and calls it a day, which doesn't actually explain anything to me. So here's what I'm trying to work out: is Franklin describing something like Aristotelian habituation, where faking a virtue for long enough genuinely converts it into the real thing (he does say the fake mode became "so habitual")? Or is he quietly admitting the opposite — that performing humility never stopped being a performance, useful mainly because it let him win arguments and influence people, so the appearance was the actual point rather than a stepping-stone to anything real? And that closing line about being proud of his own humility — is it just a throwaway witticism, or is it gesturing at a real structural problem with the whole "moral perfection" project, like maybe there's something almost self-defeating about trying to eliminate pride specifically, where succeeding at it would just generate a fresh occasion for the very vice being targeted? Would love help figuring out which of these readings the passage actually supports, rather than just being told it's "ironic" and left at that.
