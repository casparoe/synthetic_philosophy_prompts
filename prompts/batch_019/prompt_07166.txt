A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle and never quite settled the disagreement. My friend maintains that the puzzle is really just a dramatic case of weakness of will: intending at midnight to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon is no different in kind from intending tonight to go for a run at dawn, and whether you follow through later is a separate matter of self-control that doesn't retroactively make the original intention insincere. I think that response misses what's distinctive about the case, since there is no competing desire to resist here — by tomorrow afternoon, even under the coolest reflection, you have no reason whatsoever to drink, which seems to make this a case where foresight itself blocks the intention from forming, rather than one where a formed intention simply gets overridden later. I'd like you to adjudicate the dispute: explain how the three-way distinction among myopic, sophisticated, and resolute planners is meant to locate the exact point at which a rational agent gets stuck, and say whether Kavka is right to treat the puzzle as exposing a genuine, general gap between reasons for intending and reasons for acting, rather than merely a quirk of his invented scenario.
