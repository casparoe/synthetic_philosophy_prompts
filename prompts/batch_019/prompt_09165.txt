I'm teaching a graduate seminar on Leibniz this semester, and right after we finished working through the Monadology, one of my students -- who I'm pretty sure spends more time on rationalist blogs than on the assigned readings -- asked me something in office hours that's been rattling around in my head ever since: isn't "acausal trade" basically just pre-established harmony, minus God? I brushed it off in the moment but I havent been able to let it go, so I'd like a more careful answer than the one I gave her.

For context in case this isn't your usual territory either: "acausal trade" is a term that grew out of decision theory (it traces loosly back to Hofstadter's "superrationality," and gets generalized via Newcomb's problem) for situations where two agents who cannot causally affect each other at all -- because they're in different, causally sealed-off branches of a multiverse, say, or because each is running an accurate simulation of the other's decision procedure in its head -- nevertheless end up cooperating for mutual benefit, purely because each can reason its way to a correct model of how the other one thinks. No signal ever passes between them. Compare that to Leibniz's monads, which are famously "windowless" and can't causally interact either, yet still end up perfectly coordinated -- except in his system that's because God pre-arranged the whole score in advance, like two clocks built to keep identical time without ever touching.

So here's what I actually want from you: work through Leibniz specifically, not some generic 17th-century rationalist. Given the windowless monads, pre-established harmony, his commitment to the principle of sufficient reason, and his insistence that only one world (the best one) is ever actual rather than a Lewis-style multiverse of equally real alternatives -- would Leibniz say acausal trade names a real phenomenon, or is it a category error that only looks coherent because it's quietly borrowing the guarantor role his God plays? Would the PSR commit him to saying any two sufficiently rational monads must converge like this of necessity, making "trade" the wrong word entirely? And does his doctrine that each monad already represents, however dimly, the whole universe (including every other monad) from its own point of view give him a native version of the "model the other agent" move, so that none of this multiverse machinery would even strike him as exotic? Give me your actual best reconstruction of his view, not a survey of considerations on both sides.
