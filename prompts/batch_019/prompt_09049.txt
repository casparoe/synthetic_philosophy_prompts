I spent thirty-five-odd years in a national standards laboratory doing precision metrology — calibrating instruments, chasing down systematic error, the usual grind — and only got serious about philosophy after I retired. These days I'm in a small reading group (mostly other retired scientists, plus one actual philosopher who's very patient with the rest of us) working slowly through Wittgenstein's On Certainty. A couple months back, on a whim, we read Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" first, and now I can't stop comparing the two, because on a first pass they sound like the same insight in different clothes. Wittgenstein says some propositions act as "hinges" that our doubts turn on and are themselves exempt from doubt — not because we've checked them and found them solid, but because doubting them wouldn't even make sense (this is around section 341 onwards, if Ive got the numbering right). Quine says our beliefs form a web where the ones near the core are just much harder to give up than the ones out at the edges, but nothing is, in principle, immune to revision. My philosopher friend in the group insists these are genuinely different claims, not two vocabularies for one phenomenon, but whenever I ask her to pin down exactly where they part ways I dont come away satisfied.

Here's the test case I keep bringing back to group nights, from my own field. For more than a century, "one kilogram" meant, by definition, whatever a certain platinum-iridium cylinder kept in a vault outside Paris happened to weigh — about as close to unrevisable as anything gets in a working lab, nobody doubted it, you built your whole downstream chain of measurement on top of it without a second thought. Then, as of May 2019, the world's metrologists retired that cylinder for good and redefined the kilogram by fixing the value of Planck's constant instead. So which picture fits better: was that cylinder a Wittgensteinian hinge that eventually did what he says hinges can do and "turned fluid" (he's got some image about a riverbed shifting, for cases like this), or was it always just an extremely central, extremely well-corroborated node in a Quinean web that finally got revised once the cost of keeping it stopped being worth it? And if a hinge can be retired by an actual committee vote backed by an actual physical argument, doesn't that tell against Wittgenstein specifically — I'd assumed the whole point of a hinge was that it doesn't get argued away, it just quietly stops being used one day, for no reason you could put in a paper. Pull the two views apart properly for me here rather than just telling me they're compatible, and tell me which one better describes how someone like me was actually relating to that cylinder back before 2019: was I "certain" of its mass in Wittgenstein's sense, or just assigning it a very high, always-in-principle-revisable credence in Quine's?
