Hey, random philosophy question that's been nagging me while I research a piece on the intellectual lineage of "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" (the Sagan line, which supposedly traces back through Laplace to Hume). I actually sat down and read Hume's "Of Miracles" essay instead of just quoting the Wikipedia summary like everyone else does, and I got stuck on the paragraph that's suppose to be the payoff of the whole first half:

"The plain consequence is (and it is a general maxim worthy of our attention), That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish: And even in that case there is a mutual destruction of arguments, and the superior only gives us an assurance suitable to that degree of force, which remains, after deducting the inferior."

The first sentence I get fine. It's the second one that's breaking my brain. A few paragraphs earlier Hume make a big deal out of "proof" meaning something like maxed-out certainty from totally uniform experience, as opposed to mere "probability," which is allowed to come in degrees. So how does he then turn around and describe two proofs crashing into each other, with the "superior" one surviving but only at whatever strength is left after you "deduct" the loser? If proofs supposedly don't come in degrees, what's actually being subtracted from what? Is that just loose 18th-century rhetoric on his part, or is there something more rigorous going on underneath that I'm not getting?
