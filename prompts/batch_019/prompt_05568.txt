I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice and the foundations of contractarian ethics, and we recently spent a session on David Gauthier's proposal, in *Morals by Agreement*, that rational bargainers over a cooperative surplus should settle at the point that minimizes the largest relative concession anyone has to make from her own maximum defensible claim. As several commentators have noted, this "minimax relative concession" rule is mathematically the same solution that Kalai and Smorodinsky proposed as a rival to Nash's bargaining solution: Nash's solution is pinned down partly by an Independence-of-Irrelevant-Alternatives axiom, while Kalai and Smorodinsky get a generally different solution by replacing that axiom with what's standardly called individual monotonicity. To make the contrast vivid in class, I sketched a case in which one party's ceiling — her utopia payoff, the most she could get if the other's share were pushed down to the least she'd accept — rises, while the other party's ceiling and both of their walk-away payoffs stay fixed. I pointed out that her Nash-recommended share can, in such a case, stay exactly where it was or even fall slightly, whereas her Kalai–Smorodinsky/minimax-relative-concession share is guaranteed never to fall.

Two students of mine, Ines and Leo, kept arguing about which rule this actually vindicates well after the seminar had ended. (1) Ines said the case is a decisive strike against Nash's solution: no bargaining rule should be able to leave one party no better off, or worse off, purely because that party's own genuine prospects improved while the other's stayed the same. (2) Leo replied that this is a feature of Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives, not a bug: if two parties would rationally settle at a given point when the feasible set was small, new options elsewhere on the frontier that neither was ever going to choose shouldn't be able to unsettle that agreement — individual monotonicity, he said, just lets a party's unexercised, hypothetical best case do normative work it hasn't earned. (3) Ines answered that the improved ceiling isn't some remote, irrelevant option; it's a real, verified change in what's actually available to that party at no cost to the other, and a rule that ignores this violates a basic fairness intuition — which, she added, is exactly why Gauthier anchors his theory in relative concession rather than in Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives. (4) Leo answered that relative concession leans on its own hypothetical reference point, namely each party's utopia payoff, which is no less counterfactual than the alternatives Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives tells us to set aside — so the two of them just disagree about which counterfactual gets to matter, and Ines hadn't yet said why hers should win.

Neither budged, and I'd rather not referee my own students. Could you go through moves (1)-(4) and adjudicate them?
