Jae's opening claim (1) was that this just proves "maximize expected value" is a broken theory of rational choice, full stop -- if a theory says you should hand over your life savings for a shot at this game and no sane person would, the theory is what's wrong. Mireille said (2) that's not fair to the theory, it's expected *value* that's broken, not expected *utility* -- Bernoulli fixed this back in 1738 by pointing out money has diminishing marginal value, so once you plug in something like log(wealth) instead of raw dollars, the infinite sum of dollars turns into a small, perfectly finite expected utility, which is why a sane log-utility maximizer wouldn't pay much more than that to play either. So there's no paradox for rational agents, just for people who confuse money with utility.

Jae wasn't buying it (3): he said he'd read that this fix doesn't actually work in general. It's not about the function being concave or whatever, he said -- as long as it keeps climbing without any limit, no matter how slowly, you can always cook up a "juiced" version of the same game whose prizes climb even faster, and you get an infinite expected utility all over again. Mireille's answer (4) was that yeah, that's a real result (she thought it traced back to a mathematician named Menger in the 1930s), but the actual fix is just to cap the utility function -- give it a hard ceiling it can never climb past no matter how rich you get -- and then no juiced version of the game can ever produce an infinite expectation, since you're always averaging numbers under that ceiling. So expected utility theory is fine, it's specifically *unbounded* utility that was the problem. Jae's last word (5) was that "just cap it" is a total cheat -- you're inventing a ceiling on how good a life money can buy purely to make the math behave, and it commits you to saying that somewhere out there, doubling a person's fortune does almost nothing for them, which seems false for basically any amount of wealth a person could actually name.
