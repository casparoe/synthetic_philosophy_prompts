I'm a political science student currently taking a seminar on democratic theory, and a recurring local controversy has been on my mind ever since a recent class discussion about who should have a say in a given collective decision. The town where my university is located turns over a large share of its population every few years as students arrive and graduate, and every election cycle brings the same fight about whether students should really be voting in town elections at all. Long-time residents argue that most of us will move away within a year or two and won't be around to live with the consequences of, say, a new zoning ordinance, a school bond measure, or a long-term infrastructure project, so our votes shouldn't count as much as theirs, if they should count at all. Local student advocacy groups respond that we pay rent, spend money in town, and are bound by every ordinance just as much as anyone else for as long as we live here, so excluding us seems arbitrary.

Part of me finds the long-time residents' point genuinely compelling: it does seem odd to let people who will bear none of the long-run consequences decide about long-run projects. But part of me also cannot accept that several years of living somewhere, paying its rents, and obeying its laws should count for nothing. When I raised this with a friend from the same seminar, she pointed out that a structurally similar disagreement comes up in a completely different setting we discussed in class: whether people outside a country's borders, who are nonetheless affected by that country's environmental or trade policy, should have any say in how that policy is made. In both cases, one side wants to define the relevant community around who will live with the long-term outcome, and the other wants to define it around who is currently present and subject to the rules in question. Is one of these ways of thinking about who should get a vote actually correct, or is this simply an arbitrary line that has to be drawn somewhere?
