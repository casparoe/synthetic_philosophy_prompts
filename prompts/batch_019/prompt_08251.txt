Okay, this has been bugging me since a dinner a few weeks back with some people from a rationalist-adjacent meetup I go to. We got into the classic factory farming argument, and I laid out what I actually think, which is roughly: animals matter morally and we should reduce their suffering when it doesn't cost us much, but a chicken's interests just don't weigh the same as a human's -- like if you had to choose between saving five chickens from a burning barn or saving one stranger, you obviously save the stranger, no contest. So my actual position is something like: for animals, just total up the suffering and wellbeing and do whatever produces the best overall outcome, but for humans, treat them as having certain protections that don't just dissolve into a cost-benefit calculation -- there are things you can't do to a person no matter how the math comes out.

One friend there (an ex-philosophy major, very sharp, kind of insufferable about it) said this is a totally standard move to make but that it doesn't actually survive scrutiny, for this reason: imagine there's a species out there in the universe as far beyond us mentally as we are beyond chickens. If they showed up and reasoned about humans exactly the way I reason about animals -- "eh, humans don't get any special inviolable treatment, they just go into the big aggregate calculation, and if farming a few billion of them produces more total value for our civilization than not doing that, so be it" -- I would find that monstrous. And since I'd object so strongly to being treated that way myself, she says I can't keep both halves of my view at once: either I drop the pure cost-benefit approach to animals, or I drop the idea that humans get some protection that isn't just about the total math. Applying whichever standard happens to favor your own species isn't a principle, on her view, its just self-interest wearing a principle's clothes.

I didn't have a good comeback in the moment (we just went in circles for like 45 minutes, per usual for that group) and it's been nagging at me since. Is she basically right that you can't coherently hold "aggregate everything for animals, hard limits for humans" without it collapsing into special pleading, or is there an actual principled basis for treating the two cases differently? I'd genuinely like a real breakdown of whether the argument goes through, rather than another "well, we're just smarter, so it's fine," since I'm pretty sure that response is question-begging somehow and I can't articulate exactly why.
