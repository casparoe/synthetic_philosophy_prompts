In my seminar on rule-following and induction this term, the unit on Kripke's rule-following paradox -- his sceptical argument, developed through a reading of Wittgenstein, that no fact about my past use of '+' can settle whether I have always meant plus rather than the deviant function quus -- came directly after the unit on Goodman's grue paradox, and the structural parallel between the two has struck me as more than coincidental. Both seem to turn on the same underdetermination: a finite run of past cases is equally compatible with two rules, or two predicates, that agree on everything examined so far and diverge only beyond it. I would like a careful comparison of the two arguments rather than a restatement of either one. Is Kripke's paradox essentially Goodman's problem transposed from predicates to meanings, or is there a disanalogy robust enough to keep the two from collapsing into a single argument? I am especially interested in whether Goodman's own remedy for grue -- entrenchment through a history of successful projection -- could be adapted to block Kripke's sceptic, and if it cannot, what it is about meaning-facts, as opposed to lawlike generalizations, that rules the move out.
