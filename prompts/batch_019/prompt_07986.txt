I am a PhD student in economics working on applied causal inference, and in reading through the philosophical foundations of the field I have come across a puzzle about how David Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation relates to James Woodward's interventionist theory. The two are often treated as close relatives, since Woodward himself analyzes causal claims in terms of what would happen to Y under a hypothetical intervention on X, which already sounds like a counterfactual claim. Yet Lewis seems to be pursuing a fully reductive analysis of causation, ultimately grounded in a similarity ordering over possible worlds that does not itself presuppose any causal facts, whereas Woodward appears willing to define causation partly in terms of "intervention," which is already a causal notion. Is this difference in reductive ambition really the deepest divide between the two theories, or am I overstating it? And if Woodward's account does rely on an unreduced causal primitive, does this make it circular in a way that should worry us, or can a non-reductive theory of causation still be philosophically successful?
