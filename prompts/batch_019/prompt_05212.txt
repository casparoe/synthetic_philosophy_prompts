I'm teaching a seminar on medieval Jewish philosophy this term, and last week we spent a whole session on Maimonides' account of animal sacrifice -- Guide III:32, if I'm remembering the chapter right, plus a bit of the follow-up material a few chapters later on the specifics of the sacrificial laws. Everyone in the room already half-knew the headline version from hebrew school or wherever: God never actually wanted or needed sacrifices, He only commanded them because the Israelites, having grown up steeped in Sabian and Egyptian sacrificial cults, couldn't psychologically have made the jump straight to a purer, sacrifice-free worship of Him, so the Torah redirects the existing impulse toward Him instead of abolishing it outright -- weaning someone off a habit rather than forcing them cold turkey.

What almost nobody in the class had actually sat with, though, is Ramban's objection to this, in his commentary on the opening of Vayikra: Abel, Noah, and the patriarchs are all offering sacrifices, and getting praised for it, long before there's any Egyptian or Sabian idolatry around for anyone to be weaned off of, and God is repeatedly described as pleased by the "pleasing aroma," not just tolerating a lesser evil. And then there's the wrinkle that Rambam himself, in the Mishneh Torah's Hilchot Melachim, writes as though the whole sacrificial order just resumes once the messiah rebuilds the Temple, business as usual -- which seems like a strange thing to expect if korbanot were only ever meant as a temporary concession to a specific ancient psychology that, presumably, won't exist anymore by then.

So here's what I'd like: an essay that actually argues this out instead of just summarizing it. Lay out Maimonides' accommodation theory, then Ramban's response, then the tension with the messianic material, and actually take a side -- is there a way to rescue the accommodation theory (maybe some qualification Maimonides himself gestures at, or a Straussian move about who the Guide is really written for), or does Ramban, or some other account, actually do a better job explaining why korbanot are in the Torah then Maimonides does? Don't leave it at "both sides have a point" -- I want an actual verdict, defended.
