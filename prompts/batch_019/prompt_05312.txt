So I was messing around with one of those heart disease risk calculators the other day (you punch in your age, cholesterol, smoking status, etc and it spits out something like "your 10-year risk is 7.3%"), and it got me wondering what that number actually means for me specifically, versus just "people kinda like me" on average. Turns out theres a whole philosophy thing about this called the reference class problem -- the percentage you get depends entirely on which group of "similar people" you compare yourself to, and I belong to a ton of different overlapping groups at once (all men, all smokers, all 45-year-old smokers, all 45-year-old smokers with my exact cholesterol level...) that would each spit out a different answer.

My engineer instinct says just keep adding more variables until the comparison group is as narrow and specific as you can make it, since that should get you closer to the real probability. But apparently philosophers don't think narrowing it down actually fixes the problem, even in principle. Can you explain why "just condition on more and more features" doesn't make the issue go away -- is there something genuinely unsolvable going on, or is it basically a "we don't have enough data" problem wearing a philosophy costume?
