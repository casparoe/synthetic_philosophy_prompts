I'm writing a novel of ideas in which two philosophy professors—former co-authors who haven't spoken in years—end up seated together at a colleague's retirement dinner and slide back into an old, unfinished argument about the epistemology of testimony. One is a reductionist, holding that believing something purely on another person's say-so is justified only insofar as it's backed by independent perceptual, memorial, or inductive evidence that this speaker, or speakers generally, can be trusted. The other is an anti-reductionist, holding that taking someone's word for something is itself a basic source of justification, on a par with perception or memory, answerable to no such independent backing. Write the dialogue that unfolds over dinner. Have him press her on how, if she is right, young children—who have no track record yet to induct from—could ever come to know even their own names or birthdays; have her press him on why, if testimonial justification is really that basic and default, we are not thereby committed to believing any sufficiently confident speaker absent some specific reason not to. Keep both characters equally sharp, have each genuinely engage the other's last point rather than simply restate their own position, and do not let the evening resolve into a clean win for either side.
