I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory, and a disagreement broke out during the break in last week's session that I've been turning over ever since; I didn't weigh in at the time and honestly go back and forth on who had the better of it, so I'd like a considered outside adjudication. We had just gone over the St. Petersburg paradox: a casino offers a game in which a fair coin is tossed until it first comes up tails, and if that happens on toss n you win $2^n; since the probability of that outcome is (1/2)^n, every term of the expected-value sum equals $1 and the total is infinite, yet almost no one would pay more than a modest amount to play. Two of my students, Ana and Devon, kept at it long after the rest of the room had moved on to coffee, and the exchange went roughly like this.

(1) Ana opened by saying the puzzle is basically closed: rational agents maximize expected utility rather than expected cash, money has diminishing marginal utility, and once you rerun the calculation with any reasonable concave utility function of wealth (she used the logarithm), the expected utility of the game comes out finite -- exactly the fix Bernoulli offered centuries ago, and one she thinks still holds up. (2) Devon objected that this can't be a general solution: for any utility function that is unbounded above, you can construct a variant of the same game whose payouts are calibrated not to double in dollars but to double in utility, so the very sum that blew up the original game reappears one level up, now measured in Ana's own preferred units; since that trick works against every unbounded utility function, diminishing marginal utility per se never actually touches the problem, and only a utility function that is bounded above escapes it -- a far more radical commitment than "money's marginal utility declines." (3) Ana replied that Devon's variant is a mathematical curiosity rather than a real problem for her view: to make utility itself double every round under a function like log, the dollar payouts have to grow absurdly faster than doubling, so fast that no real economy could ever fund them, whereas the original game's payouts, however large, are at least the ordinary, single-exponential kind of large that the world's actual wealth can approximate for a good long stretch of rounds. (4) Devon answered that this proves too much: actual casinos can't fund a $2^100 payout either, so if "nobody could really pay that" is reason enough to set aside her variant, it is equally reason enough to set aside the original paradox, in which case bounded resources -- not diminishing marginal utility -- were doing all the work, and Ana still owes some further reason why an unbounded utility function should survive once real-world limits are no longer available to lean on. (5) Ana's last word was that the two variants aren't actually symmetric: hers only needs real-world wealth to be roughly the same order of magnitude as the original game's payouts, which holds for a long run of rounds, while Devon's construction requires payouts that blow past any physically realizable quantity almost immediately, so treating both dismissals as the same move flattens a genuine difference of scale. Devon wasn't convinced this was a principled line rather than two shades of "too big to take seriously," and that's where things stood when everyone had to leave for their next class.
