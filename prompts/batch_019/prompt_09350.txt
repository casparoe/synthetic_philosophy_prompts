I have no formal training in mathematics or philosophy, but through some forecasting sites I follow, I've gotten interested in markets that let people bet on the outcome of open mathematical questions -- for instance, whether a specific enormous number will be confirmed prime, or whether the distributed computer searches checking the Collatz conjecture will turn up a counterexample before some given date. When I described this to a friend of mine who studied philosophy, she told me the whole practice rests on a mistake: a number either is prime or it isn't, and a conjecture is either true or false, and these facts were already fixed the moment the relevant definitions were laid down, long before anyone ran a search or wrote a proof. On her view, attaching a probability to the outcome isn't tracking any real uncertainty "out there," the way a weather forecast does -- it's only a report on our own ignorance dressed up in the vocabulary of probability, and calling it a probability invites the mistaken thought that there's some objective fact the market is trying to discover.

That conversation sent me looking further into the issue, and it turns out to be an old and apparently unresolved one: ordinary probability theory seems to require that a perfectly rational reasoner already assign full confidence to every mathematical truth, and identical confidence to any two statements that are logically equivalent, which plainly is not how any actual reasoner operates -- mathematicians grew more confident in the twin prime conjecture after Yitang Zhang's 2013 proof that prime gaps are bounded, even though that result doesn't come close to logically entailing the conjecture. I also came across a proposal out of AI-alignment research, called "logical induction," which tries to rescue graded belief about unproven statements by treating each sentence as a kind of stock that pays out if the sentence turns out true and nothing if it turns out false, with prices updated day by day, and the sole requirement being that no trading strategy simple enough actually to run can be guaranteed to extract unbounded profit from that price sequence forever. What I can't work out on my own is whether this framework actually answers my friend's objection or just restates it in fancier terms, since even the people who built it seem to concede that the good behavior it guarantees only shows up "eventually," without saying what number to write down for any particular open question today.

Could you write me an essay that takes a real position on this, rather than surveying it from a safe distance? I'd like it to work through the strongest versions of both sides -- the case that partial belief about an open mathematical claim can never be more than a loose figure of speech applied to a fact that's already necessarily settled, and the case that something in the spirit of the market picture above genuinely rehabilitates such partial belief as a disciplined, rule-governed attitude rather than a mere feeling -- and then to actually decide the matter. A conditional verdict is fine (for instance, "it's a legitimate extension of probability, but only once you give up such-and-such standard assumption," or "the market picture answers a nearby question but not this one"), so long as the essay commits to it and defends it rather than concluding that reasonable people could think either way.
