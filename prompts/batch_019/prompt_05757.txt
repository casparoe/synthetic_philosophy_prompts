I'm sketching an early chapter of a novel in which Aristotle, displaced from his own century by a conceit I won't bore you with, spends an afternoon in a contemporary molecular biology lab and is told, more or less in passing, that DNA is a code — a four-letter sequence that specifies, via the genetic code, the amino acid chains that build and maintain an organism. I want his reaction to be earned rather than merely picturesque, so I've been re-reading the relevant stretches of Generation of Animals, where he argues that the male parent contributes no matter whatsoever to the embryo, only a "form" and an "efficient cause," while the female supplies the material substrate on which that form acts — the catamenia, or menstrual residue. His preferred illustrations are the carpenter, who builds a house without any of its timber coming from his own body, and rennet, which curdles milk without itself becoming part of the curd. I was pleased to discover, while chasing this down, that the resemblance between Aristotelian form and modern genetic information isn't my own cleverness — Max Delbrück got there first, in a mischievous 1971 essay literally titled "Aristotle-totle-totle" — which only raises the stakes for getting the philosophy right rather than coasting on a cute coincidence.

So: treating Aristotle's actual apparatus of form, matter, and causation as the real constraint, rather than a vague gesture toward "he liked forms," how do you think he would respond to being told that heredity runs on a molecular code? Would he hear this as vindicating his sense that generation is fundamentally about the transmission of form rather than stuff, or would he think the resemblance frays under pressure — because, say, his notion of form is inseparable from teleology and the gradual actualization of a potential (entelecheia) toward one specific mature end, whereas "code," for the philosophers of biology who have made a project of asking how literally to take that word, tends to rest on a more arbitrary, convention-like relation between symbol and referent that doesn't obviously need any of that teleological scaffolding? Give me at least one concrete point where the two frameworks genuinely converge and one where they come apart, argued from what's actually in the text rather than asserted from a distance — I'd rather write a scene that's wrong in an interesting way than one that flatters a reader's half-memory of "Aristotle believed in forms."
