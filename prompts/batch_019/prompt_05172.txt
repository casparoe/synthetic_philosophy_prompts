After a session of my department's reading group on panpsychism, two of my cohort-mates kept arguing over coffee about whether the combination problem really undercuts panpsychism's advantage over physicalism. Mara opened by claiming that it just relocates the hard problem — from how experience could arise from the non-experiential to how many micro-experiences add up into one macro-experience — and relocating a mystery isn't solving it. Devon replied that the two gaps differ in kind: physicalist emergence needs experience to arise from something categorially non-experiential, while combination only needs experience to arise from more experience, which should be strictly easier, the way complex shapes compose unproblematically from simpler ones long before anyone specifies the exact geometry. Mara pushed back that the analogy hides the real difficulty: a crowd of individually conscious people doesn't thereby produce some further, single group consciousness, so it's unclear why many micro-subjects summing together should behave any differently. Devon granted the gap but said this amounts to demanding panpsychism already have a finished theory while physicalism gets an open-ended promissory note for someday explaining how mere organization yields experience at all. Mara's rejoinder was that the two notes aren't equally good-faith: functional explanation has an independent track record elsewhere in science, whereas positing some further 'bonding' relation between experiences is invented solely to patch this one problem. Neither budged. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each stage, and flag anything significant that both of them seem to be overlooking?
