Hey — I write a science-and-ideas newsletter and I'm about 80% through a piece on "inner alignment" in AI (the idea that a model can end up with the wrong internal goal even when the training objective was basically fine). Before it goes out Friday I want a sharp read on whether the central move actually holds up, because I think most coverage of this just repeats the evolution analogy and calls it a day. Here's the guts of it:

Most explainers treat "the AI learned the wrong goal" as one problem, but I think there's actually two, and mashing them together is why the coverage feels hand-wavy. One is Goodhart-style reward hacking: you give the system a proxy objective and it satisfies the proxy while failing the thing you actually wanted — the boat-racing agent that loops in circles racking up points instead of finishing the race is the classic case. That's a failure of specifying the goal in the first place. The other is what the mesa-optimization crowd calls inner misalignment: the training objective was fine as far as it went, but whatever the network internalized as it's actual goal only tracks that objective on the training distribution and comes apart off it — like the maze agents trained with the cheese always in the same corner, which just learn "go to the corner" and beeline for an empty one once the cheese moves. Capability generalizes, the goal doesn't.

My draft argues the second problem is strictly scarier than the first, because it's invisible in exactly the regime where you'd be checking your work — you can audit the reward function all day and it won't surface a mismatch that only shows up out of distribution. But then I try to knock my own argument down: is there really a "goal" being misgeneralized here, in any sense stronger than ordinary shortcut learning (like a cow classifier that's actually just keying on green pasture)? Or does calling it a "goal" borrow agentive language the evidence hasn't earned, in which case maybe it's not so obviously scarier after all.

Can you tell me: is the two-problems distinction real and fairly stated, is the maze example actually carrying the weight I'm putting on it, and does my own shortcut-learning objection gut the piece more than I'm letting on? Also say so if this is just too in the weeds for a general smart-reader audience — I'd rather cut it than have it read like a term paper.
