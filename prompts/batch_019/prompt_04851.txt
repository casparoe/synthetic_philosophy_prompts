So I've been falling down an AI-safety-blog-posts rabbit hole the last few weekends (a coworker sent me a Scott Alexander piece and I just kept clicking from there), and I keep running into this "coherence argument" thing that's supposed to explain why any sufficiently capable agent will basically end up acting like an expected utility maximizer whether it "wants" to or not. The pitch is always some version of the money-pump story: if your preferences are cyclic (you prefer A to B, B to C, but C to A) or incomplete, someone can in principle construct a series of trades that bleeds you dry for free, so a rational agent has to avoid that, and apparently avoiding that is basically equivalent to having a coherent utility function you're maximizing. That part I get at a hand-wavy level.

Where I got lost is that I also found pushback on this (I think one post was by Rohin Shah, but I might be misremembering who) arguing that the argument doesn't actually get you what people want it to get you — like, you can always retroactively cook up some gerrymandered utility function that "rationalizes" literally any sequence of choices, even ones that look dumb and exploitable, as long as you let the utility function be weird/history-dependent enough. So the jump from "can't be money-pumped" to "is an EU-maximizer pursuing some sensible, stable goal" apparently proves way less than the popular version of the argument suggests. Seperately, I've seen people claim incomplete preferences are harder to money-pump away than intransitive ones — something about an agent that just refuses trades when two options are incomparable rather than being forced to rank them — and I dont have a good handle on why that asymmetry exists at all.

Could you walk me through this fairly rigorously? Specifically I'm hoping you can address: (1) what the money-pump argument actually proves, formally, about an agent's choices — is it really "this agent has *a* utility function it's maximizing," or something weaker or different than that; (2) where exactly the gap is between that conclusion and the stronger claim (that the agent has a fixed, non-trivial goal it pursues over time, the sort of thing that'd make an AI dangerous); and (3) what's mechanically different about how the money-pump is supposed to fail against incomplete preferences versus intransitive ones. I don't need the full von-Neumann-Morgenstern treatment with every axiom spelled out, but I'd like something a good deal more precise than the hand-wavy version I keep getting off blog posts.
