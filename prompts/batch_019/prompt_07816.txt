I've spent the past year reading my way through Toby Ord's idea of 'moral trade': the proposal that two people who disagree about ethics can each end up acting more in line with their own values by trading which of their scruples they actually honor, much as countries trade goods. The case leans on comparative advantage -- neither party needs to be more virtuous overall, only to face a lower moral cost than the other for whatever each of them is giving up. What I can't get past is this: in ordinary trade, a Pareto improvement is real progress because satisfying someone's preferences is close enough to their welfare that nothing further needs checking. Satisfying someone's moral convictions isn't like that: an act can satisfy every conviction a person sincerely holds and still be wrong. So a trade that leaves both parties more faithful to their own views hasn't obviously made anything morally better -- it's just made each of them more consistent with what they already believed, not more correct, and appealing to each party's own values to measure the gain only restates that problem rather than solving it. Please argue against generalizing the comparative-advantage case for economic trade to Ord's case for moral trade, addressing this 'own lights' defense rather than sidestepping it.
