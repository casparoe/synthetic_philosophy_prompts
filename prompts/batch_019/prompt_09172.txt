At work I recently had to justify a choice between several candidate models of quite different complexity for a forecasting pipeline, and a colleague suggested I read up on the Minimum Description Length principle to put the decision on firmer footing than "this one just felt about right." That sent me down a rabbit hole from MDL to Kolmogorov complexity and eventually to Solomonoff induction, which the sources I found describe as the closest thing to a mathematically rigorous version of Occam's razor: every computable hypothesis about the data is given a prior weight of roughly two to the power of minus the length of its shortest program on a universal Turing machine, and predictions are then formed by ordinary Bayesian updating over that enormous hypothesis space.

Around the same time, I came across David MacKay's discussion of plain Bayesian model comparison, where he points out that just computing the evidence for a model — its likelihood integrated over its parameters — already builds in an "automatic" Occam's razor of its own. An overly flexible model spreads its predictive probability so thin over all the datasets it could conceivably have produced that it ends up losing, on the data actually observed, to a simpler and more committal model, with no program or bit length appearing anywhere in the calculation. I don't have any formal training in philosophy, so there may well be an obvious resolution I'm missing, but the relationship between these two razors seems like a genuinely open question to me rather than a merely verbal dispute.

On one reading, Solomonoff induction is really just ordinary Bayesian inference in disguise: a prior set by description length instead of, say, a smooth distribution over real-valued parameters, so MacKay's automatic razor and Solomonoff's razor are the same underlying mechanism showing up at two different scales. On another reading, Kolmogorov complexity comes with an invariance theorem guaranteeing that its simplicity ranking is fixed, up to one additive constant, across every choice of universal machine or programming language — which sounds like a substantially stronger and more "objective" result than anything a garden-variety Bayesian prior, chosen essentially by taste, can offer. Could you compare Solomonoff induction's route to Occam's razor with this more general Bayesian automatic razor, and tell me, with reasons, whether there is anything genuinely special about grounding simplicity in program length, or whether it is ultimately a dispensable special case of the broader Bayesian pattern?
