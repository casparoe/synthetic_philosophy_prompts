So I'm teaching a graduate seminar on early modern epistemology this term, and this week's session was on Hume's essay "Of Miracles" (Section X of the first Enquiry). I've taught this text more times than I can count at this point, but there's a passage near the end of Part I that reliably starts a fight in seminar, and this year I honestly couldn't settle it to my own satisfaction either, which is probably not a great sign given I'm also elbow deep in grading a stack of response papers on this exact section right now.

Here's the bit, quoted fairly fully so the context is there: "The plain consequence is (and it is a general maxim worthy of our attention), 'That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish: And even in that case, there is a mutual destruction of arguments, and the superior only gives us an assurance suitable to that degree of force, which remains, after deducting the inferior.' When any one tells me, that he saw a dead man restored to life, I immediately consider with myself, whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened. I weigh the one miracle against the other; and according to the superiority, which I discover, I pronounce my decision, and always reject the greater miracle."

One of my students argued flat out that this "weighing miracle against miracle" business is a bit of a sham - that a few pages earlier Hume already told us there's a "firm and unalterable experience," amounting to a full proof, standing against any miracle whatsoever, so the outcome of the weighing is rigged from the start and all this talk of comparing probabilities is just theater dressing up a conclusion he'd already reached. I definately want to push back on that reading but wasn't 100% sure how to do it without just insisting Hume's not that sloppy. So: is there a real, non-circular job that the weighing procedure is doing here, something distinct from the proof/probability distinction he already sets up earlier in Part I? And could you sketch a concrete example for me (a toy one is totally fine, it doesn't need to be a real historical case) involving an ordinary, non-miraculous event where this same weighing procedure would actually come out in favor of believing the testimony - just so I've got something to contrast with the miracle case when I walk through this in class.
