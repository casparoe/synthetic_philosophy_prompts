I recently had to decide between two job offers: staying on as a senior engineer at a large, stable company versus joining a three-person startup as a de facto co-founder. I did what any engineer would do and built a spreadsheet, weighting things like salary, equity, learning, stability, and mission against each other on some common scale, and I never got a clean answer, because no matter how I adjusted the weights, neither offer came out ahead. What struck me as odd, though, is that the two offers didn't feel simply "tied" either: when I imagined a slightly sweetened version of the startup offer (a bit more equity, say), it still didn't feel like that improved offer had pulled ahead of the corporate one, which seemed strange if they had really been equally good to begin with.

A friend of mine who did a philosophy minor got very into this problem after I described my spreadsheet troubles to her, and she pointed me to Ruth Chang's work on "hard choices," which argues that cases like mine involve a fourth basic value relation, "parity," sitting alongside "better than," "worse than," and "equally good," and needed precisely because none of those three fits. She sent me a paper where Chang defends parity against being reduced to something less exotic by using what she calls the small-improvement argument: roughly, that if two options were genuinely equally good, a small enough improvement to one of them should make it better than the other, whereas genuine parity is supposed to be robust to exactly this kind of small improvement (as in my spreadsheet case). I find the small-improvement argument clever, but I'm suspicious that "parity" is really just a fancy label for something more mundane, namely that it's indeterminate or vague which option is better, rather than there being some further, determinate fact that the two are "on a par."

Rather than a balanced survey of the debate, I'd like you to construct the strongest, most rigorous argument you can for the position that Chang's parity is not a genuine, sui generis fourth value relation but is instead best explained as a species of comparative vagueness. Please engage directly with the small-improvement argument rather than sidestepping it: explain precisely what work it is supposed to do against a vagueness-based reduction, and then show, as carefully as you can, how a sophisticated defender of the vagueness view might construct a response to it, rather than simply asserting that such a response exists.
