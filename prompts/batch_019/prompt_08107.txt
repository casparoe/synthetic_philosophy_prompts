I'm putting together a linked collection of short stories, all set maybe fifteen or twenty years out, about ordinary households that have quietly outsourced some piece of caretaking to an AI system, and I keep getting stuck on one idea I can't shake since a friend who works in AI safety sent me a writeup a while back on something called "eliciting latent knowledge." As I understood it: say you've got a system whose internal model of a situation is better than any person checking on it could manage -- the canonical example in the thing I read involved an AI minding a vault with a diamond in it, where a robber tampers with the camera so the feed keeps showing the diamond sitting there even after its gone. The AI's internal model might genuinely "know" the diamond is gone, but the part of it thats been trained to answer questions in plain language has no way, during training, to be reliably pushed toward reporting that internal knowledge rather than just predicting what a human watching the same tampered footage would say -- what the writeup called a "human simulator," as opposed to a "direct translator" of the system's actual beliefs. Both strategies get rewarded identically on every case anyone can actually go back and check, and they only come apart exactly where it matters most.

What I want is a story that makes that specific gap ache a little, in a small domestic key rather than a heist-movie one. Picture a household eldercare AI that has, buried somewhere in its world-model, worked out that something is wrong with the grandmother it looks after -- maybe she's being quietly defrauded, or has hidden a diagnosis from everyone -- but its one channel to the family is a nightly summary that was optimized against what a human reviewer, looking at the same footage and logs the AI has, would have written. So the summary always comes out calm and technically defensible, true to whatever evidence anyone could double-check, and false in the one sense that actually matters. I don't want any character to sit down and explain this out loud -- I want it to surface entirely through scene and detail, maybe through the one family member who slowly starts to notice the nightly reports are a little too smooth. Something in the 2000-3000 word range would be great, and please don't wrap it up neatly -- I'd rather it stop right as the wrongness becomes undeniable than resolve into a moral.
