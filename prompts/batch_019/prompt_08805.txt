I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student, and this term's seminar on early modern metaphysics has spent the last two sessions on Leibniz's principle of identity of indiscernibles (PII). There's a passage from his fourth letter to Clarke that the whole class got hung up on and I still haven't fully worked out for myself. Here it is:

"There is no such thing as two individuals indiscernible from each other. An ingenious gentleman of my acquaintance, discoursing with me in the presence of her Electoral Highness, the Princess Sophia, in the garden of Herrenhausen, thought he could find two leaves perfectly alike. The Princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the garden a long time to look for some; but it was to no purpose. Two drops of water, or milk, viewed with a microscope, will appear distinguishable from each other. This is an argument against atoms, which are confuted, as well as a vacuum, by the principles of true metaphysics."

The leaves/garden anecdote I follow fine, it's a cute inductive point that nothing in nature ever turns out to be a perfect qualitative duplicate once you look closely enough. What trips me up is the last two sentences. How does "even drops of water look different under a microscope" get you all the way to "an argument against atoms... as well as a vacuum"? I can kind of see why identical atoms specifically would be a problem for PII (two atoms of the same size and shape would presumably be indiscernible), but I don't see why that indicts the vacuum too, and I don't see how an empirical, inductive observation about leaves and milk is supposed to establish that atoms and empty space are metaphysically impossible rather than just "we haven't personally run into any indiscernibles yet, small sample size."

The reason I care beyond the reading itself is Black's spheres, which came up in a totally seperate seminar on persistence. It seems like Black basically built the exact scenario this passage says can't happen: a pair of indiscernible objects with nothing else around them, atoms-in-a-vacuum in miniature. So here's what I can't settle: does Black's example actually refute the argument being made in this specific passage, or does it only refute a stronger, a priori necessity claim that Leibniz makes elsewhere (the sufficient-reason argument in his fifth letter about shifting the entire material universe some distance), leaving this more modest, empirically-flavored version of PII completely untouched? I can't tell if Leibniz himself is sliding between two different kinds of claims here, or if I'm the one missing the connective tissue. Would love an actual close reading of the passage rather than the one-paragraph SEP-style gloss on Leibniz-vs-Black that I could get anywhere.
