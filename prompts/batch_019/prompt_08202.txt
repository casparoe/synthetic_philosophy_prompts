Over a long career as a field ecologist, now some years retired, I grew comfortable with a certain kind of trust: when a colleague working just outside my specialty reported a result, I generally accepted it without reconstructing the evidence myself, because I knew how such findings are normally checked and because the colleague's judgment had proven reliable over time. No one would have thought this made my belief in the result any less solid.

Since retiring I have taken up philosophy for the first time, and I now find myself in a position that feels structurally similar but troubles me in a way the earlier case never did. A former doctoral student of mine, now a professor of environmental philosophy, has convinced me that a live-capture and radio-collaring protocol I used routinely on wolves through the 1980s was seriously wrong, for reasons involving animal stress and something she called consent by proxy. I have come to believe she is right, but if a skeptical former colleague pressed me on it, I don't think I could reconstruct her argument myself, let alone defend it; I would only be repeating her verdict.

Is this really the same as trusting a colleague's unfamiliar data, just applied to right and wrong rather than fact, or is something importantly different going on? And, practically, am I entitled to say, on this basis alone, that what I did was wrong?
