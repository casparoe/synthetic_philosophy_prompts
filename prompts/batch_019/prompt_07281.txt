Here is the question as posed:

Two partners must divide a jointly owned asset worth a total of 16 units of transferable value. Partner 1 is risk-neutral, with utility over their own share x given by u_1 = x. Partner 2 is risk-averse, with utility over their own share y = 16 − x given by u_2 = √y. Take the disagreement point to be d = (0,0).

(a) Derive the Pareto frontier of this bargaining problem in utility space, and find the ideal (utopia) point.
(b) Compute the Nash bargaining solution.
(c) Compute the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution.
(d) The two solutions should not coincide here. Referring to the axioms that respectively characterize the two solutions, explain why not.

And here is the student's response, verbatim:

(a) Since y = 16 − x and u_2 = √y, we have y = u_2², so x = 16 − u_2². Since u_1 = x, the Pareto frontier is u_1 = 16 − u_2² (equivalently u_2 = √(16 − u_1)), for u_1 ∈ [0,16]. The ideal point comes from maximizing each player's utility separately subject to feasibility: a_1 = 16 (at u_2 = 0) and a_2 = 4 (at u_1 = 0). So the utopia point is (16, 4).

(b) The Nash solution maximizes the product u_1u_2 over the frontier. Substituting, we maximize f(u_1) = u_1√(16 − u_1). Differentiating by the product and chain rules,
f′(u_1) = √(16 − u_1) − u_1/√(16 − u_1).
Setting this to zero and multiplying through by √(16 − u_1) gives 16 − u_1 = u_1, so u_1 = 8, and u_2 = √8 = 2√2 ≈ 2.83. The Nash bargaining solution is therefore (8, 2√2) ≈ (8, 2.83).

(c) The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the point on the Pareto frontier at which the ratio of each player's utility gain (from the disagreement point) to their ideal-point coordinate is equalized. We need u_1/16 = u_2/4, i.e., u_2 = u_1/4. Substituting into the frontier: u_1/4 = √(16 − u_1) ⟹ u_1²/16 = 16 − u_1 ⟹ u_1² + 16u_1 − 256 = 0 ⟹ u_1 = −8 + 8√5 ≈ 9.89. Then u_2 = u_1/4 ≈ 2.47. So the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is approximately (9.89, 2.47).

(d) These differ because the two solutions are pinned down by different axioms. Nash (1950) showed his solution is the unique one satisfying Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance, and independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) — the requirement that shrinking the feasible set, while leaving the original solution point available, should not change the solution. Kalai and Smorodinsky (1975) kept all four of these requirements but layered on a further axiom, individual monotonicity: if the feasible set expands so that one player's maximum attainable payoff increases while the other's stays fixed, that player's payoff under the solution should not decrease. Adding this fifth requirement to Nash's original four singles out a different point whenever the frontier has curvature, which is exactly what happens here, since our frontier u_1 = 16 − u_2² is strictly concave rather than a straight line. (If the frontier were linear, both solutions would coincide, since proportional and multiplicative comparisons of utility gains agree along a line.)
