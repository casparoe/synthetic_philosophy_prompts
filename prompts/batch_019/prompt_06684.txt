I'm part of my high school's investing club, and at last week's meeting our advisor ran a simulation to teach us about bet sizing. Everyone started with the same pretend bankroll, and over fifty successive flips of a coin we were told was weighted 60/40 toward heads, could wager any fraction of it each round at even money: win what you wager if it lands heads, lose it if it doesn't. Whoever finished with the largest pretend balance got an actual gift card, so people took it more seriously than you might expect. Afterward, two of the seniors got into a real argument about what the "correct" strategy actually was, and both sides said things that struck me as fair, so I haven't been able to decide who came out ahead.

(1) He opened by pointing out that the bet has positive expected value every round, +$0.20 per dollar wagered (0.6 times a dollar won, minus 0.4 times a dollar lost), so the rational move is to bet as large a fraction of your bankroll as the rules allow each round, since scaling up a positive-EV bet only scales up your expected winnings. (2) She said that reasoning wipes you out in a repeated game: bet everything every round and you're one loss away from zero, and across fifty flips of a coin that comes up tails 40% of the time, you will hit that loss with overwhelming probability. Instead, she said, you should use the Kelly criterion: bet the fraction that maximizes the expected value of the logarithm of your wealth, which for these numbers is 20% of your bankroll per round, not 100%. (3) He replied that "maximize expected log wealth" is just maximizing under one particular, arbitrary utility function; nothing makes log utility the objectively correct one, and if he happens to be risk-neutral, expected-value maximization is exactly what it means to be rational, so calling Kelly "the" right answer already smuggles in an assumption about what he should value. (4) She said that's not what's going on at all: because the game is multiplicative, the average outcome across many people playing in parallel isn't what happens to any one person who keeps playing round after round; simulate his all-in strategy across fifty rounds and essentially every individual trajectory ends at zero, with the positive expectation propped up only by a vanishingly thin sliver of paths that never lose. Kelly, she said, is just what happens almost surely to a persistent bettor, regardless of anyone's utility function. (5) He came back with: then why do professional bettors and trading desks almost always use half- or quarter-Kelly instead of full Kelly? Kelly assumes you know the true probability exactly, and "the advisor told us 60%" isn't the same as knowing it's 60%; if the true odds are even slightly worse than advertised, full Kelly can be nearly as ruinous as going all-in. So "objectively correct," he said, is claiming more than the math actually delivers.

Neither of them backed down, and I don't have the probability background to referee this myself (I've only gotten through AP Stats).
