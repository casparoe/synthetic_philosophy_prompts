So I'm doing a PhD in philosophy of physics, and in our reading group this week we covered Everettian QM and personal identity, and the session kind of turned into an argument about Tegmark's quantum suicide thought experiment (the one with the gun wired to a quantum-random trigger, repeated over and over). Half the room was adamant that if many-worlds is true, you're rationally committed to expecting survival with near-certainty across many trials, since there's simply no branch in which you're around to experience having died, so from the inside "the gun is safe" -- and a couple of people pushed this further into full-blown "quantum immortality," i.e. the claim that this isn't just true of someone dumb enough to build a suicide machine, its true of anyone facing any potentially fatal event, ever. The other half thought the whole line of reasoning is a decision-theoretic sleight of hand: that Born-rule weights are exactly what should govern rational self-locating credence (the same way objective chances constrain credence via stuff like Lewis's Principal Principle), that "no branch observes its own death" confuses "there's nothing it's like to be dead" with "it's rational not to mind dying," and that the argument quietly ignores all the equally-real, equally-you branches where the trigger did go off.

Nobody really conceded anything by the end (I was mostly just refereeing). Could you write this out as a proper dialogue between the two positions -- someone defending "quantum immortality is a genuine rational implication of many-worlds" and someone defending "this reasoning smuggles in a confusion about self-locating belief"? I'd like both of them to be genuinely strong, not a strawman either way, and actually responding to each other's specific points rather than just restating their opening stance more loudly each time. If you can work in how this connects to the Sleeping Beauty problem that'd be great too, since someone brought that up and I never fully got why it's supposed to be relevant.
