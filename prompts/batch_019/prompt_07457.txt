I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist, and now that I'm retired I've finally been getting into philosophy properly, currently working through Cicero's De Senectute in translation. Theres a bit that's been bugging me: when Cato answers the complaint that old age deprives us of bodily pleasure, he doesn't just say the loss is tolerable, he says its actually a gain, and backs it up with that old anecdote (I think it originates with Sophocles, via Plato, but Cicero repeats it approvingly) about being glad to finally escape sexual desire, "like escaping from a savage and raging master." My instinct as an old scientist is that this smells like a rationalization of an involuntary loss dressed up as a discovered preference, basically what Jon Elster later called "sour grapes" - and it amuses me no end to call that kind of story "adaptive," given what that word meant in my old field. Could you build the strongest version of the argument that Cato/Cicero is doing exactly this kind of rationalizing, rather than making a genuine philosophical discovery about the goods of old age?
