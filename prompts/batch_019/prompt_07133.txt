Like a lot of people who came up reading Overcoming Bias and early LessWrong, I settled into compatibilism about free will mainly on the strength of a conditional analysis: the thought that "I could have done otherwise" just means something like "I would have done otherwise if my prior beliefs, desires, or decision to try had been suitably different." I hadn't examined that move very closely until a friend finishing a philosophy PhD pointed me toward the finks-and-masks literature on dispositions, and now the conditional analysis looks a lot less innocent. C.B. Martin's "electro-fink" example shows that a conditional analysis can be true of an object that intuitively lacks the relevant disposition, because some hidden mechanism flips the object's underlying state on or off at exactly the moment the trigger occurs; Mark Johnston's "masking" cases show the mirror-image failure, where the disposition and its normal causal basis are fully present but something else reliably intercepts the manifestation even when the trigger condition is met. Once you see the pattern, ordinary human agency looks riddled with candidate finks and masks: last-second changes of heart, compulsions, manipulation, even just ordinary bad luck in how a choice-relevant belief gets formed, all seem like they could count as finking or masking whatever conditional is meant to define "the ability to have done otherwise."

I know that people like Kadri Vihvelin and Michael Fara have tried to rescue dispositional or conditional accounts of abilities by building in some kind of "absent finks and masks" rider, but I've only encountered this secondhand and can't tell whether it actually works. Please describe the most important implications of the fink/mask problem for conditional and dispositional analyses of "the ability to do otherwise," and hence for compatibilism built on such analyses. I'm especially interested in whether there's a principled way to exclude finks and masks from the analysis that doesn't either (a) quietly reintroduce the very notion of "ability" the conditional analysis was supposed to reduce, or (b) end up so tailored to the free-will case that it stops looking like a genuine vindication of the old Rylean idea that abilities are nothing more than facts about how one would behave under various conditions.
