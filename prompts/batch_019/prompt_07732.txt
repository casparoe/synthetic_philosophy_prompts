For the past year or so I have been teaching myself the history of ideas around the press, publicity, and public opinion, entirely through my own reading, since I never had the chance to study philosophy formally. I originally came to Kierkegaard through a handful of popular essays claiming that he had somehow anticipated Twitter, which sent me to his short book The Present Age (I understand this is drawn from the latter part of a longer work called Two Ages). There he argues that "the public" produced by the newspapers of his day was not an actual community of people at all, but a kind of abstraction, nobody in particular, incapable of ever being held responsible for anything, and that this abstraction gradually hollowed out individuals' willingness to hold and stand behind opinions of their own; elsewhere he goes so far as to say that the crowd, simply as a crowd, amounts to a kind of untruth. Quite separately, while reading about the history of propaganda for an unrelated project, I came across Walter Lippmann's 1925 book The Phantom Public, the follow-up to his slightly earlier Public Opinion, in which he argues that the fully informed, engaged citizen assumed by democratic theory does not really exist, and that ordinary people can only function as "a public" in occasional, reactive bursts, such as turning out a corrupt official, rather than as some standing body competent to decide the substance of policy.

On the surface, the two of them seem to be converging on the same basic insight, namely that "the public" as we ordinarily talk about it is something of a fiction. But Kierkegaard was fundamentally a religious writer preoccupied with individual selfhood before God, writing decades earlier in a completely different setting, so I doubt Lippmann owed him anything directly. What I would like to understand is how far the resemblance actually goes once one looks past the shared skepticism: what exactly does each of them think is illusory about "the public," and why does each think that illusion comes about in the first place? Do their proposed responses have anything in common, or does one push toward turning inward religiously while the other pushes toward redesigning institutions, so that underneath the apparent coincidence they are really answering two quite different questions? And if both critiques were applied to how "the public" behaves in the age of social media, would one of them hold up noticeably better than the other, or do they simply illuminate different parts of the same problem?
