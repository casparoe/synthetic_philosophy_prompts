This term I am teaching a graduate seminar, cross-listed with the economics department, on the epistemology of rational disagreement, and this week's session was built around Robert Aumann's 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree," together with the no-trade theorems that grew out of it. What struck several of my students, and what still nags at me, is how casually the common prior assumption gets treated in this literature: it is routinely introduced as though it were a harmless normalization, on a par with fixing a unit of measurement, rather than a substantive claim about what ideally rational agents must have believed before any evidence came in. Harsanyi's own gloss on the matter — that any residual disagreement between rational agents must be attributable entirely to differences in information rather than to differences in priors — gets absorbed into these models as background scaffolding, so that the striking "no disagreement" and "no trade" conclusions can then be advertised as following from little more than ordinary Bayesian updating plus common knowledge of posteriors.

I would like you to resist that framing. Please argue that the common prior assumption is not epistemically innocent in the way its use in these theorems suggests: that it is not a mere modeling convenience forced on us by the requirements of coherent credence, but an independently contestable commitment about the antecedent beliefs of rational agents — and, moreover, that it is this commitment, rather than rationality or common knowledge as such, that does essentially all of the work in generating results like Aumann's agreement theorem and the associated no-trade results.

In making the case, please engage with what I take to be the most serious rejoinder available to a defender of the assumption: that once the underlying state space is enriched to include agents' types and their higher-order beliefs about one another, any apparent disagreement in priors can be redescribed as a disagreement in information relative to a larger, commonly held prior over that enriched space, so that the assumption, far from being a substantive constraint, becomes close to unfalsifiable, or even trivially satisfiable by construction. Tell me whether this redescription strategy succeeds in rescuing the innocence of the assumption, and say something about what is genuinely at stake — for our understanding of persistent, well-informed disagreement among rational agents — in insisting that their priors must ultimately coincide.
