I've never studied philosophy formally, but I read about it a lot on my own, and a while back my curiosity about why people (myself included, if I'm honest) so often dig in on a belief instead of updating when new evidence turns up led me to something called the "dogmatism paradox." As far as I can tell, it started out as an unpublished lecture Kripke gave at Cambridge in the early 1970s, which Gilbert Harman then wrote up and discussed not long afterward. The argument runs roughly like this: suppose I actually know that some claim h is true. Since h is true, any evidence that might later turn up against h would have to be misleading, in the sense that it would be evidence pointing away from something that is, in fact, the case. But it also seems obviously right that I should disregard evidence I already know to be misleading. Put those two thoughts together and you get the strange result that knowing h now apparently entitles me to write off, ahead of time, whatever counter-evidence the future might bring against it — which sounds exactly like the kind of closed-mindedness we normally criticize people for.

What's been nagging at me is that I can't decide whether this is a sleight of hand with an obvious flaw or a genuine problem without a fully comfortable answer. I've come across the suggestion that the fix is to say that once the counter-evidence actually shows up, my total evidence has changed, so I'm no longer in the position I was in before it arrived — but that response seems to concede that I can never really be settled in my knowledge of anything until all possible evidence is in, which feels like trading one uncomfortable result for another. I've also seen it suggested that the real mistake is thinking that knowing h gives me, right now, knowledge of facts about evidence I haven't even encountered yet, though I don't know the underlying theory of knowledge well enough to judge whether that response is principled or just a polite way of refusing to bite a bullet.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers, equally sharp and neither one a pushover, who disagree about how to handle this argument? I'd like one of them to hold that the argument basically succeeds, so that some real, if carefully qualified, version of the "dogmatist" conclusion has to be accepted, and the other to hold that a specific step in the argument can be blocked on independent, principled grounds, rather than that move being invented on the spot just to dodge this one puzzle. I want them to actually engage with each other's strongest objections instead of talking past one another: at some point the second philosopher should have to answer the charge that their way out just relocates the problem instead of solving it, and the first should have to answer the charge that their view amounts to a license for willful ignorance in ordinary life, not just in the tidy, isolated case of a single claim h.
