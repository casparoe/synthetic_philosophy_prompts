I teach a graduate seminar this term on relations and the metaphysics of facts, and we've just spent three weeks working through Bradley's regress -- his argument, in the chapters on relation and quality in Appearance and Reality, that no relation can unite its terms without some further relation uniting it to them, and so on without end -- alongside Russell's attempt to block the regress by treating a "relating relation" as something that unites its terms simply by holding, rather than by being itself related to them. My students, rightly I think, find Russell's move stipulative rather than explanatory, which is partly why I'm looking forward to our department's colloquium next month: the speaker has a book coming out defending what she calls "primitive predicational unity" -- the view that Bradley's regress never gets started because predication is a sui generis act, not a relation, and so was never the kind of thing that needed some further tie connecting it to what it predicates and what it predicates it of. I'll be moderating the Q&A, and whenever anyone has pressed her on this in print, she gives some version of the same answer: every metaphysical account bottoms out somewhere in an unexplained primitive, and asking what unifies the unifier is just refusing, on principle, to let any theory count as complete.

I'd like help drafting nine or ten questions for the session, sequenced so that this move stops being a free out by the third or fourth time she reaches for it. At least one question should put to her directly the suspicion that "primitive predicational unity" is just Russell's relating relation, or Frege's unsaturatedness, dressed in new terminology, and ask her to name something her account explains that theirs did not. At least one should press the regress-at-one-remove worry: if the act of predicating wisdom of Socrates has to somehow reach both Socrates and wisdom, why doesn't Bradley's original question simply recur, now about the act instead of about a relation? At least one should test how far the account generalizes by asking her to apply it to a relational proposition with no privileged subject -- "two is between one and three," say -- where it is much less obvious that a single act of predication, rather than something closer to Bradley's many-termed relation, is doing the unifying work. And at least one should raise propositions nobody has ever entertained -- an arithmetical truth no mind has yet considered -- and ask whether her view really is committed to saying such a proposition has no unity, and so is neither true nor false, until some thinker performs the relevant act. For each of these, add a short follow-up question tuned to the answer she is most likely to give, and close the sequence with a question asking her to describe what would actually count as evidence against her view, as opposed to one more restatement of the regress.
