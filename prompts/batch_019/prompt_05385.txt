Last night a friend who works in technical AI safety and I got into a long argument over dinner about whether the incomplete-preferences response to the shutdown problem really earns its keep, and I'd like your help pressing on it further. As background: the shutdown problem is the worry that any sufficiently capable expected-utility maximizer with a determinate preference ordering over trajectories will have an instrumental incentive to resist being shut down, or to manipulate the probability that it is shut down, whenever shutdown would prevent it from doing better by its own lights. The 2015 MIRI proposal for "corrigibility" tried to neutralize this with utility indifference — rigging the agent's utility function so that it is exactly indifferent, in expectation, between being shut down and not — but that approach is now generally thought to break down under self-modification and delegation to sub-agents, since nothing stops the resulting agent from building successors that lack the same patch. My friend was defending Elliott Thornley's alternative, on which you don't patch the utility function at all. Instead, you deny the agent a complete preference ordering across trajectories of different lengths, so that it has no fact of the matter about whether being shut down now is better or worse than continuing; you then pair this with a dominance-style rule — roughly, prefer an option only when it does at least as well at every possible shutdown time, and remain undecided otherwise — so that the agent never has a reason to pay any cost to hasten or delay its own shutdown.

I objected with what I take to be the standard worry about incomplete preferences in decision theory: an agent that lacks a preference between two options can be money-pumped — offered a sequence of trades that exploits the gap to extract resources from it while leaving it no better off by its own lights. This is exactly why many decision theorists think a sufficiently reflective agent should want to "complete" its preferences, for instance by adopting some resolute-choice policy, rather than tolerate incompleteness. If that's correct, Thornley's proposal seems caught in a dilemma: either the agent's shutdown-relevant incompleteness gets pumped away, reinstating a determinate preference about shutdown and, with it, the very manipulation-and-resistance incentives the proposal was meant to remove; or the agent adopts some anti-pump policy whose own justification seems to presuppose just the kind of consistent, forward-looking preference that the incompleteness approach was meant to avoid needing. We argued about this for the better part of an hour without either of us conceding. Please make the strongest case you can that this money-pump objection is fatal to incompleteness-based solutions to the shutdown problem along the lines Thornley proposes — and, in doing so, engage directly with the most natural reply available to a defender of the proposal (for instance, an appeal to resolute choice, or to some disanalogy between ordinary money-pump scenarios and the shutdown case) rather than resting the argument on a bare appeal to the standard completeness axiom of expected-utility theory.
