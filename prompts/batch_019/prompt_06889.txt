Plato's Meno contains a puzzle that has always struck me as harder than it looks. Socrates gets Meno to agree that someone with a merely true opinion about the road to Larissa will guide travelers there exactly as well as someone who has actually made the journey and knows the way; true belief, in other words, seems to do all the practical work we could want from knowledge. Yet Socrates still insists that knowledge is the more valuable state of mind, and tries to explain the difference through the image of Daedalus's statues, so lifelike that they have to be tied down or they wander off: a true opinion, he says, only turns into knowledge, and only becomes stable, once it is tethered, so to speak, by working out the reason why it is true. This came up again recently in a seminar I'm sitting in on, where someone pointed out that current epistemology has its own version of the same worry, sometimes called the swamping problem: if all we ultimately want from a belief is that it be true, then whatever a reliable method or piece of good reasoning supposedly adds on top of the truth seems to get swamped and disappear, in something like the way it wouldn't matter to the taste of a cup of coffee whether the machine that brewed it is generally reliable, so long as this particular cup came out fine.

I'd like you to write a short story, on the order of a thousand words, that gives this puzzle some real dramatic stakes rather than just illustrating it schematically. Somewhere in the story, two people (or a person and some other source of guidance, human or otherwise) should end up holding one and the same true belief about something that matters, one of them having arrived at it through genuine understanding and the other through luck, hearsay, instinct, or some other route that happens to hit on the truth without any grasp of why it is true, and something should then happen that makes the difference between them matter in practice, not merely in the abstract bookkeeping of epistemologists. You're welcome to invent whatever setting and characters suit you, but I'd find it more interesting if you avoided a direct retelling of Plato's own runaway statues and instead found a fresh situation in which the same underlying tension plays out.
