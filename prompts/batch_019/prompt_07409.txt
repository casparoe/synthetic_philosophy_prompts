Ok so I'm about a third of the way into a novel set at Trinity College, Cambridge in the 1690s (yes, that Trinity — there's a joke in there somewhere), and one of my side characters, an old half-feral genius everyone in college is a little scared of, is loosely based on Newton. My actual narrator is a fictional young fellow who slowly realizes the old man's real secret isn't the alchemy everyone already gossips about, its his theology. I've been down a research rabbit hole on Newton's private antitrinitarian writings — like that letter he sent Locke arguing that two of the go-to proof-texts for the Trinity, 1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16, were actually later scribal insertions never in the original text — because I want the reveal in the book to land as genuinely dangerous/unsettling rather than just "haha turns out he wasn't very Christian."

Here's what I'm stuck on though: Newton obviously wasn't the only smart Englishman quietly doubting the Trinity back then — Socinians were around, and his own eventual successor in the Lucasian chair, Whiston, got thrown out of Cambridge decades later for preaching pretty openly Arian stuff in public. So could you compare Newton's own antitrinitarian theology to that broader antitrinitarian current of the period? I get the basic Christology (Christ as subordinate to, and created by, the Father rather than co-equal), but I don't have a good feel for whether Newton was mostly just quietly agreeing with a heterodox position that was already circulating among skeptical dons and Dissenters, or whether the specific route he took to get there — the manuscript/textual-criticism angle, pinning the "corruption" on Athanasius and Nicaea specifically, folding it all into his prophecy and apocalypse obsessions — made his particular version of the heresy unusual even next to other closet antitrinitarians. Basically: how "special" should I be treating his private convictions in the book, versus writing him as just one more guy who'd landed on an idea that was already in the air? Even a rough sketch of where he overlaps with, versus departs from, typical Arian/Socinian reasoning of that era would help a ton.
