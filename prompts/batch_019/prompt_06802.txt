been slowly working through Hume's Enquiry on my commute (a coworker got me into "great books" stuff, dont ask) and I hit a snag in the free will chapter. Right before this bit he spends ages arguing that "necessity" in the physical world is really nothing more than us noticing A is always followed by B and our minds forming a habit of expecting B — so necessity turns out to be a fact about our psychology of prediction, not some hidden force sitting in the objects themselves. Then he gets to free will and writes stuff like:

"It is universally allowed that matter, in all its operations, is actuated by a necessary force, and that every natural effect is so precisely determined by the energy of its cause, that no other effect, in such particular circumstances, could possibly have resulted from it."

and a bit further on:

"By liberty, then, we can only mean a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will; that is, if we choose to remain at rest, we may; if we choose to move, we also may. Now this hypothetical liberty is universally allowed to belong to every one who is not a prisoner and in chains."

...and from there he basically concludes the whole free will vs determinism fight is a dumb verbal dispute, everyone already secretly agrees, case closed.

Something about this feels like a magic trick but I can't pin down why, and I've got zero philosophy background so I might be missing something obvious. Is his "solution" actually doing independent work, or is it just riding on that earlier, weirder move where he redefines necessity as basically a fact about our minds instead of the world? Like — if I reject his psychology-of-expectation theory of causation, do I still have any reason to accept his compatibilism, or does the whole argument fall apart with it? And do modern compatibilists (the Frankfurt / "reasons-responsive" crowd, if I've got those names right) need that same move, or did they find a way to be compatibilists without first redefining causation out from under the libertarian?
