For the individual research paper required in my AP Seminar class, I've decided to write about the ethics of killing in war, and I've run into a disagreement in the literature that I can't quite resolve on my own. Michael Walzer's *Just and Unjust Wars* defends what's usually called the "moral equality of combatants": because he treats the justice of going to war (jus ad bellum) as independent from the justice of conduct within it (jus in bello), a soldier fighting for an unjust cause is, on his view, just as entitled to kill enemy soldiers, and just as legitimate a target, as a soldier fighting for a just cause, provided both sides fight by the same battlefield rules. My teacher then pointed me to Jeff McMahan's "revisionist" response, where he argues that this supposed equality collapses once you take individual moral responsibility seriously: on his account, a person may be permissibly killed in war only if they are "liable" to attack in virtue of contributing to an unjustified threat, so that soldiers fighting an unjust war generally lack the right to kill soldiers fighting a just one, no matter what the laws of war say.

When I mentioned this to a friend who is writing about a related topic, she immediately sided with Walzer, mostly on the grounds that ordinary soldiers rarely get to choose whether their side's cause is just and that treating the two sides asymmetrically would only make actual wars more brutal and harder to end. I'm less sure, particularly after reading more of McMahan's discussion of why an unjust combatant might be excused for fighting without ever being justified in doing so, which seems to leave Walzer's position without much to stand on at the level of individual morality, whatever its practical merits. Could you write me an essay that lays out the strongest version of each side of this dispute — including how considerations of reciprocity and the epistemic limitations soldiers face under fire bear on Walzer's defense, and how the justification/excuse distinction bears on McMahan's — and then reaches and defends a definite verdict on whether the moral equality of combatants is defensible as a genuine moral doctrine, rather than only as a useful legal fiction? I'd rather you commit to an answer than conclude that both sides simply have a point.
