Reading around that led me into epistemic logic more generally, and into this thing apparently called the "problem of logical omniscience" — basically, if you model an agent's knowledge as "true in every possible world consistent with what they know," you're stuck saying the agent automatically knows every tautology and every logical consequence of anything they know, which is obviously not how any real, resource-bounded reasoner works.

Here's what's been bugging me though: every example I can find for why this matters is pretty toy-ish — stuff like "the agent knows P or not-P even though they never thought about P," or some obscure but logically true math identity. Those feel kind of low stakes, like a philosopher's shrug-emoji example. It seems to me the problem gets way more teeth once you plug in something computationally hard instead of just logically valid. Factoring a number into its primes is, in the relevant sense, just a fact of arithmetic that's logically fixed the moment you fix N — so doesnt the standard possible-worlds treatment have to say an agent who "knows" a 2048-bit RSA modulus N already "knows" its prime factorization the instant they know N, since that's a logical consequence of arithmetic, even though actually factoring N might be infeasible for the next trillion years? That feels like a way more damning case than the usual textbook ones, but maybe I'm just reinventing something that's already standard in the literature and I don't know it.
