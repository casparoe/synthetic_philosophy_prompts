Exam question: In Section X of the first Enquiry, Hume's case against believing testimony for miracles is standardly described as falling into two parts. Reconstruct both parts and explain how they are meant to work together, then state and briefly defend what you take to be the single strongest objection to the argument as a whole.

Student's answer:

Hume's discussion in "Of Miracles" divides naturally into the two parts of the essay itself.

Part I lays out what is often called the "in-principle" argument. Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, where a law of nature is just a regularity that has held without a single exception in our past experience. Because that past experience is completely uniform, it counts, in Hume's own technical vocabulary, as a "proof" rather than a mere "probability" — the strongest degree of evidence experience can ever supply — and this proof stands arrayed against any report of a miracle before we even ask who is doing the reporting. That is the basis of the guiding maxim of the whole essay: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact which it endeavours to establish." Only testimony whose falsehood would itself amount to a kind of miracle could outweigh the proof on the other side, and since ordinary testimony falls well short of that bar, Part I in effect shows that a miracle can never really happen, no matter how it is reported.

Part II then turns from what testimony could do in principle to what it has actually done, arguing that no miracle in history has ever been supported by testimony good enough to meet the Part I standard. Hume gives four separate reasons: no miracle is vouched for by a sufficient number of witnesses of the requisite good sense, education, and integrity; human beings have a natural love of surprise and wonder that makes them credulous transmitters of such stories; reports of miracles are concentrated among ignorant and barbarous peoples rather than civilized, enlightened ones; and, most distinctively, the miracles of rival religious traditions are "contrary facts" that undercut one another, since each is offered as proof of the very system that contradicts every other one. Hume presses this last point hardest, since it shows that the sheer existence of mutually incompatible miracle traditions is itself indirect evidence against the God of any of them, which is really the sharpest edge of the whole essay. He closes, famously and ironically, by conceding that Christianity "cannot be believed by any reasonable person without" a miracle, since whoever is moved by faith to accept it "is conscious of a continued miracle in his own person... which subverts all the principles of his understanding."

The strongest objection, in my view, is that Part I is doing all the real work and is subtly question-begging: by stipulating that a law of nature is established by exceptionless experience, Hume has already ruled out, by definition, the possibility that any single event could count as a genuine exception, so the four considerations of Part II are really just decoration on a conclusion Hume had already secured on definitional grounds alone.
