A friend and I ended up in a long, unresolved argument this week about Frege, of all people, after we'd both been reading about the diary he kept for a few weeks in early 1924, a little over a year before he died, which surfaced among his papers and was published only decades later. It is a genuinely ugly document: he denounces the Weimar parliamentary system, longs for an authoritarian leader who might sweep it aside, and argues that Jews should be stripped of political rights, at one point remarking that he has come around to treating Jewishness itself, rather than any individual's conduct, as grounds for suspicion. My friend's position was that none of this bears on Frege the philosopher -- that inferring anything about the soundness of the sense/reference distinction or the logicist program from the character of the man who produced them is a textbook genetic fallacy, no different in kind from noting that some brilliant mathematician was also an unpleasant husband. I kept resisting that move, on the grounds that Frege's philosophy isn't some free-floating, contentless formalism: his signature anti-psychologistic doctrine holds that thoughts (Gedanken) and logical laws inhabit a mind-independent, universal "third realm" -- his own term for it was "drittes Reich," coined, in an irony he did not live to see exploited, a decade and a half before Germany's actual Third Reich -- precisely so that truth could never be relativized to what some particular people or era happens to believe. It is hard for me to treat it as mere coincidence that the same man who spent his career insulating truth from any "our kind of people" relativism turns out to have wanted the political community itself carved up along exactly those lines.

What makes the argument harder to settle is that it did not start with the diary. My friend pointed out that Hans Sluga, in his 1980 book on Frege, had already tried to place the logic within the German philosophical and political currents of Frege's time, and that Dummett -- who probably did more than anyone to canonize Frege as a founding father of analytic philosophy -- attacked that whole approach, insisting that the value of the technical work is entirely independent of its author's biography and politics. The diary then surfaced years later and confronted Dummett with private convictions considerably worse than anything Sluga's argument had required, leaving it genuinely unclear whether the diary vindicates Sluga's instinct that the logic and the politics were never as separable as Dummett wanted them to be, or whether it is simply a damning but philosophically inert biographical footnote that Dummett could, in principle, have accepted without conceding an inch of his methodological point.

Could you write me an essay that takes a real position on this rather than retreating into "it's complicated"? Please lay out the strongest version of the case for treating the diary as irrelevant to assessing Frege's philosophy, the strongest version of the case that some substantive connection runs between his objectivism about logic and thought and the exclusionary politics of the diary, say what the pre-diary Sluga/Dummett dispute should tell us now that the diary itself is in hand, and close by stating clearly -- and arguing for -- whether, and if so exactly how, the diary ought to change the way we read, teach, or evaluate Frege's philosophical work.
