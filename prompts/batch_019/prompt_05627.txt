I'm a philosophy PhD student working on philosophy of biology, and this term's seminar on theories of biological function has spilled well past the classroom. We spent a week contrasting Wright's and Neander's etiological, or "selected-effects," account, on which a trait's function is whatever effect explains why natural selection retained it, with Cummins' causal-role account, on which a trait's function is simply its current causal contribution to some capacity of the system we're trying to explain, with no essential reference to history required. Our instructor used feather evolution as the stress-test case: on the mainstream reconstruction, simple filamentous feathers show up across various non-avian theropod dinosaurs, apparently serving insulation and display, long before anything resembling powered flight, and only later, within the lineage leading to birds, do the asymmetric, vaned feathers needed for flight evolve. Afterward, two people in my cohort, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing over coffee about what this case actually shows, and having listened to the whole exchange, I still can't tell who came out ahead.

(1) Marcus opened by insisting that a living hawk's flight feathers obviously function to enable flight, full stop: that is their current causal contribution to the bird's capacity to fly, and the fact that some much earlier version of the trait served a different purpose is a fact about history, not about what the trait does now. (2) Elena objected that current causal contribution can't be the whole story, because it overgenerates: a heart's stable causal effects include producing an audible thump, and a stomach's include producing hunger pangs, yet neither counts as a function of the organ; only the trait's selective history, why it was retained in the first place, explains why pumping blood and digesting food count and the rest don't, so etiology can't be avoided. (3) Marcus replied that etiology, taken at face value, proves too much in the other direction: if function is fixed by "why the trait is there," and you trace the history back far enough, the honest answer for a hawk's flight feathers is thermoregulation and display, not flight; blocking that result by restricting the relevant history to only "recent" selection for aerodynamic refinement would just be choosing a cutoff that delivers the answer one already wanted, exactly the sort of stipulation Elena had accused causal-role theory of. (4) Elena answered that the cutoff is not a free stipulation: it is a factual question about which selective regime actually maintained and reshaped the trait into its current form, and that fact, unlike Cummins' choice of which "capacity of interest" to analyze, doesn't depend on anyone's explanatory purposes.
