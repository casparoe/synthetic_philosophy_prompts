Ok so I'm noodling on a short story built around that old "Appointment in Samarra" fable - the servant who bolts to Samarra to dodge Death and finds out that's exactly where Death was already headed to meet him. Went down a rabbit hole and found out decision theorists have their own version of this, "Death in Damascus" (Gibbard and Harper), where Death's a perfect predictor and its used to cause trouble for causal decision theory via Richard Jeffrey's idea of a "ratifiable" choice - apparently neither staying nor fleeing turns out to be ratifiable once Death predicts perfectly. I want to put a genuinely rigorous version of the "run or stay" dilemma into one of my character's mouths, so here's the ask: argue that the rational move for someone in Death in Damascus is to flip a coin between the two cities, and that this is the uniquely rational choice once you take ratifiability seriously, not just a shrug you resort to when nothing else works. And don't dodge the obvious objection - if Death is genuinely a perfect predictor, why wouldn't he just predict the coin flip too, which would seem to make randomizing pointless?
