I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and a couple weeks back we spent a session on diachronic norms of rationality, including van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- the idea that if you're now certain what your credence in some claim E will be at a later time, say it'll be r, then rationality requires your current credence in E, conditional on that fact, to also equal r (in symbols, Cr_now(E | Cr_later(E) = r) = r). Van Fraassen defends this with a diachronic Dutch book argument: violate it, and a bookie who knows both your current and future credences can sell you a sequence of bets spanning the two times that guarantees you loose money no matter what actually happens.

Two of my students, Callum and Nadia, kept arguing about this after class, first in the hallway and then straight through my office hours, and I honestly couldn't settle it in my own head afterward. Roughly, it went like this. (1) Callum argued that Reflection has a nice practical upshot: if you already know you'll change your mind about something once you've thought it through more, intellectual honesty requires shifting your credence toward that anticipated future opinion now, rather than smugly sitting on your current view until the future actually arrives. (2) Nadia pushed back with a case: suppose I know for a fact that after a few drinks tonight I'll be at 90% confidence that I'm fine to drive home. Taken at face value, Reflection says my current sober credence in "I'm fine to drive," conditional on knowing that's what drunk-me will believe, should also be 90%. That's clearly wrong -- if anything, knowing I'll believe it while drunk is a reason to trust that future opinion less, not defer to it. (3) Callum answered that van Fraassen basically saw this coming: Reflection is only supposed to bind you when you expect the future opinion to be produced by some legitimate, evidence-responsive process -- learning something new, reasoning it through more carefully -- and not by booze, hypnosis, or a blow to the head. Since drunk-Nadia gets there by neither evidence nor good reasoning, that case is excluded by the principle's own proviso, not a counterexample to it. (4) Nadia said fine, then try this one: suppose I know for certain that tomorrow I'll undergo a procedure that wipes out a chunk of my memories, and that afterward I'll reason totally impeccably from whatever evidence is left -- perfect Bayesian conditionalization the whole way, nothing booze- or brainwashing-like about it at all. That future opinion is exactly the kind of thing Callum says Reflection is supposed to bind me to, and yet it's being formed from strictly less evidence than I currently have. Deferring now to an opinion I already know will be less informed than my present one seems backwards. She said this shows the "legitimate process" fix doesn't actually rescue the principle, it just relocates the same problem one step down.

Neither of them budged, and the whole thing just fizzled out unresolved when office hours ended. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and adjudicate them one by one -- for each, say whether it strikes you as basically right, partly right, or mistaken, and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like your take on whether Nadia's memory-loss case in (4) is genuinely fatal to Reflection even in its restricted form, or whether there's some further, principled fix available to Callum that isn't just another ad hoc patch. And separately: is there some assumption both of them are making about how to read the principle in the first place that neither ever thinks to question?
