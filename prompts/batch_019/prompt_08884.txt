So in my early modern philosophy seminar we've spent the last couple weeks on Descartes' "beast-machine" argument and something about it's been bugging me. In the Discourse on the Method (part V) he doesn't just assert that animals lack minds — he actually gives two pretty concrete tests for telling a real rational soul apart from a really well-built automaton. First, the language test: a machine could maybe be built to produce a fixed response to a fixed input (his example is basically a machine rigged to cry out something like "ouch" if you poke it in the right spot), but it could never rearrange words on the fly to answer fittingly to whatever's said in its presence, the way even the dullest human can. Second, there's the versatility test: reason is supposed to be a "universal instrument" that lets us improvise in totally new situations, while a machine (and, on Descartes' view, an animal) only has organs built for specific actions, so sooner or later it'll hit some task outside its built-in repertoire and reveal that it was never understanding anything, just running on it's parts. Our professor also mentioned that Descartes scholars actually disagree about how far this goes — whether he really thought animals feel nothing at all, or just that they lack reason and language specifically — since apparently his letters hedge on this more than the Discourse does.

Anyway, this got me thinking about stuff like Betty, the New Caledonian crow who, with no training on that exact task, spontaneously bent a straight piece of wire into a hook so she could fish a little bucket of food out of a tube — basically inventing a tool on the spot for a problem she'd never encountered before. Or Alex the grey parrot, who could apparently give correct answers to brand-new combinations of questions about an object's color, shape, and material, instead of just reciting some memorized string. Both of those look a lot like the flexible, non-stereotyped behavior that Descartes' two tests were specifically designed to rule out as impossible for anything lacking a rational soul. So, using Descartes' own criteria (not just a modern "animals are smarter than we thought, case closed" vibe), do you think evidence like this would actually have forced him to give up the beast-machine doctrine, or does his own framework hand him a principled way of explaining it away as sophisticated mechanism rather than genuine understanding? I'd love an actual reconstruction of how that argument would have to go on his terms, not just a "well, he was obviously wrong" hand-wave.
