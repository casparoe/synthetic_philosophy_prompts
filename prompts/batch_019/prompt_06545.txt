So a coworker and I went down a rabbit hole this week that started at the halting problem and somehow ended up in philosophy, and I want a sanity check since neither of us has any real philosophy background (sorry if this is alot of setup). It started with him bringing up "hypercomputation" -- specifically this idea of a machine that does each computational step in half the time of the one before it (step 1 takes a second, step 2 takes half a second, step 3 a quarter second, and so on forever), so it burns through infinitely many steps in a total of just 2 seconds. The pitch, as I understood it, is that you could point a machine like this at an arbitrary program, let it "run forever" inside that compressed 2-second window, and then just check afterward whether it ever halted -- basically brute forcing your way past the undecidability of the halting problem using an infinite loop that finishes in finite time.

That reminded my coworker of Thomson's lamp, which I only know secondhand from a philosophy podcast I half-listened to once -- the lamp you switch on, then off, then on again, doubling the speed each flip, so you end up flipping it infinitely many times inside one minute, and then the puzzle of whether its on or off once the minute is up. His take (which he was very confident about) is that Thomson's lamp already settled this whole category of question decades ago: since you literally can't say whether the lamp is on or off at the end, "infinitely many steps crammed into finite time" is logically broken as a concept, not just something we lack the engineering for. So in his view, the halting-problem machine is dead on arrival too, no physics needed to rule it out, because it's got the exact same logical structure as the lamp.

Assuming he's right about that, here's what I actually want to know: when actual researchers write papers on "accelerated Turing machines" or relativistic hypercomputation claiming these could in principle decide the halting problem, are they just overlooking a decades-old philosophy result, or is there some reason the lamp paradox doesn't actually carry over to the halting-problem case the way my coworker thinks it does?
