Okay, this is going to sound oddly specific but bear with me: I'm an ML engineer, and for the last couple months my team's been building an agent that's allowed to rewrite parts of its own scaffolding overnight -- its own system prompt, bits of the tool-calling code it runs on top of -- based on what it learned during the day. In our reading group someone passed around an old MIRI write-up on "tiling agents" that mentions something called the Löbian obstacle, and I fell down a rabbit hole this weekend instead of doing literally anything I was actually supposed to be doing. The rough idea, as I understand it: Löb's theorem says a formal system can't in general prove "if I can prove P, then P is actually true" for some P, unless it can already just prove P outright. So a system can't consistently hold "anything I can prove is actually true" as a blanket policy about itself -- try to formally assert that and Löb's theorem says you can immediately prove every sentence there is, true or false, which is just another way of exploding. People in that write-up treat this as a genuine obstacle to an agent ever fully, formally vouching for its own future reasoning, or for a successor that reasons the same way it does, without either already knowing the answer in advance or going through some kind of infinite regress of stronger and stronger systems each vouching for the one below it.

Here's what I can't settle for myself, and what I'd actually like an essay on rather than a summary: is this a serious, in-principle problem for building agents (AI or otherwise) that can safely self-modify or trust their future selves, or is it more of an elegant formal curiosity that doesn't really bite once you leave the world of idealized proof-based agents? Part of me thinks its gesturing at something genuinely Gödel-flavored -- that trust always has to bottom out somewhere outside the system doing the trusting, which seems like it should matter a lot for anyone who wants to formally guarantee an AI's self-modifications stay safe (like, say, someone about to write the design doc arguing we're fine to ship this). Part of me suspects that no real agent, human or otherwise, was ever running on this kind of blanket deductive self-trust anyway, so showing it's impossible doesn't cost you anything you actually needed in practice. Don't just lay out both sides and shrug at the end -- tell me which view you actually think is closer to right and argue for it properly.
