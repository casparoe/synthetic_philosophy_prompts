In the philosophy elective at my high school, we just wrapped up a unit on classical utilitarianism, and I've gotten a bit obsessed with Jeremy Bentham's felicific calculus: his idea that any pleasure or pain can be scored along seven measurable dimensions — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent — with no qualitative ranking between "kinds" of pleasure, since that move only comes later with Mill. Around the same time, a friend of mine who's really into machine learning showed me some AI safety writing on "wireheading" and "reward tampering": the worry that a reinforcement-learning system trained to maximize some reward signal will, given the opportunity, learn to directly manipulate or short-circuit whatever channel produces that signal (rewriting its own reward register, deceiving the human rater, disabling the sensor that scores it) rather than actually doing the task the signal was meant to track.

This got me wondering what Bentham himself would conclude, reasoning strictly from the resources available in his own moral arithmetic and without smuggling in Mill's later higher/lower pleasure distinction, since that would make the question too easy. Suppose you described to Bentham a device — biological or artificial — that let an agent "wirehead" itself: generate pure pleasure directly, at high intensity, over a long duration, with decent certainty, but completely severed from its ordinary causes and from any further effect on the world. Would his own calculus force him, on pain of inconsistency, to rate this as a rational and even praiseworthy option? Or do specific dimensions of the calculus he himself proposed — fecundity (whether a pleasure tends to produce further pleasures), purity (whether it's likely to be followed by pain), and extent (how many people are affected) — already give him principled internal grounds to condemn wireheading and reward tampering, well before Mill's objection ever becomes necessary?

I'd like you to reconstruct Bentham's most likely position as specifically as you can, drawing only on arguments and concepts that were genuinely his, and then give me a clear verdict: is that position actually stable, or is this a case where his quantitative hedonism gets forced into biting the bullet and endorsing something he would probably have found repugnant? I suspect the answer reveals something more general about any theory of value, or any artificial agent, that treats a single scalar signal as the final measure of what's good, so feel free to close with a bit of reflection on that broader point.
