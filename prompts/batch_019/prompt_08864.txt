I've been sitting in on some talks in the CS department on inverse reinforcement learning, and it keeps striking me that the "identifiability problem" everyone treats as a fresh technical headache is something philosophy basically already diagnosed decades ago. The ML version: you can't recover a unique reward function from observed behavior alone, because for pretty much any policy there's some reward function paired with some model of the agent's rationality (or irrationality/bias) that rationalizes it perfectly -- Ng and Russell already flagged this back in 2000 (the all-zero reward "explains" literally any behavior), and I think theres a more recent impossibility-style result pushing the same point even for irrational agents, turning it into a genuine no-free-lunch result -- Armstrong and Mindermann, maybe? Don't quote me on the citation, my memory for CS papers is not great. Either way, the standard fix on offer is some kind of simplicity prior, imposed over reward functions alone or over reward-and-bias pairs jointly.

That move looks to me like a reinvention of Davidson's principle of charity, which was doing the exact same job over in radical interpretation: since Ramsey we've known that preference behavior alone underdetermines the split between an agent's credences and its utilities (the classic case: does the umbrella-carrier think rain is likely and only mildly mind getting wet, or think it's unlikely and hate getting wet -- behaviorally indistinguishable either way), and Davidson's answer was to break the tie by assuming the interpretee is maximally rational and their beliefs and desires maximally coherent. So heres the argument I want from you: that imposing a simplicity prior to solve IRL's identifiability problem isn't a new solution at all but Davidsonian charity wearing a lab coat, and that it inherits charity's oldest unresolved objection -- namely, that nobody has ever shown "simple" or "charitable" interpretations actually track the reward (or beliefs) the agent has, rather than just being stipulated to. Give me the strongest version of that argument you can build, including the best reply a committed IRL researcher (or a committed Davidsonian) could make against it, before telling me why that reply doesn't fully get them off the hook. I might use this to kick off discussion in my philosophy of mind seminar, so don't hold back on the technical stuff from either side.
