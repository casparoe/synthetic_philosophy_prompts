Ok this has been bugging me since my philosophy of science seminar covered the substantivalism vs relationism debate about space and time. We spent a lecture on Newton's bucket - you spin a bucket of water, and once the water catches up and is rotating together with the bucket (so there's zero relative motion between them, same as before you even started spinning it), the surface goes concave. Since "relative motion between water and bucket" can't explain the concavity (its zero in both the flat case and the concave case), Newton's conclusion is that the water must be rotating relative to something absolute - space itself, or for Mach, relative to the fixed stars / the rest of the matter in the universe. Fine, that part I get.

Then a few weeks later we got to GR and the professor brought up the "hole argument" (I think this is the Earman & Norton thing from the 80s, might be misremembering exactly who), and its used to argue almost the opposite thing. The idea is that if spacetime points are real individuals with their own identity apart from whatever metric field they happen to carry, then GR's diffeomorphism invariance means two "different" solutions that agree everywhere outside some region (the "hole") but disagree inside it count as two distinct physical possibilities, even given the exact same matter distribution - which is a totally unacceptable kind of indeterminism. So the moral is supposed to be: ditch that kind of substantivalism.

A friend in the class thinks the bucket argument and the hole argument are basically just talking past each other - different topics that happen to share the same "substantivalism" name tag - but I'm not convinced it's that easy an out. Can you walk through both arguments properly and tell me: are they actually in tension, or are they targeting different theses? Is there a way to accept the moral of the bucket (something absolute grounds true rotation) while still dodging the hole argument's conclusion - I vaguely remember something called "sophisticated substantivalism" being the move here? And of the two, which argument do you personally find more convincing, and why?
