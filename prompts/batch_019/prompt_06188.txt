I'm working on a novel whose protagonist is a Spinoza scholar, and I need a scene where a colleague presses her to defend her subject's politics against something he never saw. I want her answer to reflect Spinoza's own reasoning, not a generic modern gloss, so here is the background and the question.

Spinoza's unfinished Political Treatise (Tractatus Politicus) breaks off just as he turns to treat democracy in its own right, so any account of his considered view must be pieced together from the completed chapters on monarchy and aristocracy plus the earlier defense of democracy in the Theological-Political Treatise. Central to both is his claim that a state's stability rests on the affects of the multitude, chiefly hope and fear, and his argument that fear, once stirred, breeds the superstition that ambitious rulers and clergy exploit to secure obedience.

Suppose Spinoza were shown a present-day social media platform whose engagement-maximizing algorithm ends up favoring posts that provoke fear, outrage, and indignation, because such affects spread fastest through the imitation he describes in the Ethics. Would he treat this as merely a more efficient, impersonal version of the priestly exploitation of fear he already diagnosed, or as a genuinely new problem for his case that democracy is the regime most consonant with human nature and reason? Defend one specific answer, grounded as precisely as you can in the mechanics of his political and psychological theory, and say what remedy, if any, his own principles would support.
