"Taurek's target is the assumption, often taken for granted by utilitarians, that if you can only save one of two groups of people from certain death, you are required to save the larger group simply because more people would live. Taurek denies this. His key move is to point out that although each of the five people in the larger group would lose everything if they died, exactly as the lone individual would, there is no single perspective from which the five deaths add up to a loss five times as bad as the one death. Loss and suffering only exist within individual lives, not in some further, impersonal total, so nothing actually becomes 'worse' just because the numbers go up. Since he thinks every person is owed the same degree of concern, Taurek concludes that when the numbers on both sides are equal, we should give each side a fair chance by flipping a coin instead of arbitrarily picking one group; when the numbers are unequal, however, he agrees that we should still save the greater number, since at that point a coin flip would no longer be fair to the larger group. The most influential reply to Taurek comes from Parfit, who argues in his discussion of the Non-Identity Problem that our intuition that numbers matter is too strong and too widely shared to give up, and that Taurek's reasoning, applied consistently, would have implausible consequences in cases involving future generations."
