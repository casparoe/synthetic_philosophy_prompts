I spent nearly forty years as a research chemist, and only since retiring have I turned to philosophy in earnest, currently working my way through the classic empiricists. In David Hume's essay "Of Money," I was struck by his claim that what stimulates industry is not the absolute quantity of gold and silver a country possesses, but rather the interval between an increase in the money supply and the eventual, proportionate rise in prices: it is only during this lag, before the general price level has caught up, that merchants and labourers benefit. At roughly the same time, Richard Cantillon, in his Essai sur la Nature du Commerce en Général, gave a rather different-looking account of why new money is not neutral in the short run, one built around who receives the new money first and how it is subsequently spent, which alters relative prices well before it alters the overall price level. Both writers reject the neutrality of money in the short run, but I am not convinced that their reasons for doing so are actually the same. Could you compare Hume's "interval" mechanism with Cantillon's account of monetary injection in some depth? I am especially interested in whether you think the two are ultimately describing a single underlying process viewed from different angles, or resting on genuinely distinct assumptions about how new money diffuses through an economy, and in your judgment as to which offers the more fundamental explanation.
