I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, and for the past several months I've been reading actual philosophy for the first time since a college elective I mostly slept through decades ago. Somewhere in there I got completely stuck on Thomson's lamp - the old puzzle where you flick a lamp on, then off, then on again, halving the time between flicks each time, so a countable infinity of switches happens inside two minutes flat, and then you're asked whether the lamp is on or off once the two minutes are up. I went down the rabbit hole further and found Benacerraf's reply to Thomson, the "Grim Reaper" version with infinitely many reapers each poised to strike you down at a different instant if your still alive at that instant, and that great ping-pong-balls-in-a-vase puzzle where the number of balls left at "midnight" depends on which balls you happen to remove at each step, not just how many steps you've done. What gets me, as someone who spent a career caring about what's actually physically realizable, is that none of these people want to just say "the question has no answer because the setup is incoherent" - they keep treating it as a live puzzle about what a completed infinite process even amounts to.

So here's what I'd like: write me a short story, a page or two, built around some device or situation that actually carries out a supertask like this one - a lamp, reapers, balls in a vase, or a variant of your own, I don't care which. I don't want a story where a character just shrugs and says "well, we'll never know" or "that could never really happen anyway" - I want the plot to genuinely hinge on what state the system is in once the process completes, with some real stakes attached for a character (a bet, a life, a piece of evidence, whatever works), and I'd like it to take the underdetermination seriously rather than tidying it up too fast - maybe even lean on that vase trick, where the outcome depends on details the setup never pinned down. A character can reason through the logic out loud if that fits naturally, but the paradox needs to be doing real work in the plot, not just sitting there as scenery.
