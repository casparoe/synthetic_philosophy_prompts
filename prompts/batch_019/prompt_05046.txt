I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's problem set concerns Richard Jeffrey's rule for updating on evidence that doesn't itself become certain -- what he called probability kinematics, as against strict Bayesian conditionalization. I'm now working through the submissions, and before I record final scores I'd like an independent, point-by-point read of one student's response, partly to keep my own comments to the student precise and partly to sanity-check my rubric.

The question as set was the following:

"Suppose a museum has recently acquired a small bronze figurine, and you are trying to assess whether it is an authentic ancient piece or a modern forgery (let H be the proposition that it is a forgery). Before any expert opinion, your credence that the surface patina is chemically anomalous for the figurine's purported age (call this E) is P(E) = 0.5, and your conditional credences are P(H|E) = 0.7 and P(H|¬E) = 0.2. A conservator examines the piece and remarks, in passing, 'The patina's not what I'd expect, but I wouldn't swear to it.' This remark does not make you certain of E, but it does move you: your credence in E rises to Q(E) = 0.8, and nothing else changes your view of how E and H are related.

(a) Using Jeffrey's rule, compute your new credence Q(H) that the figurine is a forgery.

(b) State precisely what the rigidity condition requires in this example, and explain why the calculation in (a) would not be legitimate without it.

(c) Now suppose a second specialist's remark about the figurine's casting seams had shifted your credence in some other proposition F relevant to H, and that this had happened before, rather than after, the conservator's remark about the patina. Would you expect your final credence in H to come out the same regardless of the order in which the two pieces of uncertain evidence are incorporated? Explain your answer, and say what it shows about the sense in which Jeffrey conditionalization does, or does not, generalize ordinary conditionalization."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"(a) By Jeffrey's rule, Q(H) = P(H|E)Q(E) + P(H|¬E)Q(¬E) = (0.7)(0.8) + (0.2)(0.2) = 0.56 + 0.04 = 0.60. So my credence that the figurine is a forgery should rise from P(H) = (0.7)(0.5) + (0.2)(0.5) = 0.45 to Q(H) = 0.60 once I incorporate the conservator's remark.

(b) The rigidity condition requires that my credence in E itself stay fixed across the update: whatever probability I assign to the patina being anomalous has to remain stable before and after I fold in the new testimony, since it is exactly this stability that lets me use E as a fixed anchor for recalculating my credence in H. If my credence in E were shifting unpredictably rather than settling on one new value, there would be nothing determinate to plug into Jeffrey's formula in part (a).

(c) No, I do not think order should matter here, and on reflection I do not think it does. Jeffrey conditionalization is standardly presented as a generalization of ordinary conditionalization, recovering it as the special case where the new credence in E goes to 1. Since ordinary conditionalization on a batch of evidence is order-independent -- conditionalizing on E1 and then E2 lands you at the same place as conditionalizing on E2 and then E1 -- and since Jeffrey's rule is just a generalization of that same operation, it should inherit the same order-independence. So the final Q(H) should come out identical whichever piece of testimony, the seams or the patina, is incorporated first."
