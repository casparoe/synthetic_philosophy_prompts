I've been rereading Ramsey for a talk I'm giving next month, and I keep getting stuck on that late piece of his - I think Braithwaite called it something like "Epilogue" when he put together the 1931 collection, and I have a vague memory it started out as a talk to the Apostles. It's the bit where he refuses to feel humble in front of the vastness of the heavens: his "picture of the world" is drawn in perspective rather than to scale, so the stars end up about the size of threepenny bits while people, conversation, and feeling stay in the foreground. It's basically the mirror image of Russell's "Free Man's Worship" cosmic-pessimism move, and its always struck me as a very deliberate, almost defiant personal credo about what actually counts as mattering.

Here's what's been bugging me lately: Ramsey (together with de Finetti) basically handed us the formal machinery - coherent credences, expected utility - that a lot of today's longtermists lean on when they argue that the sheer scale of the future (trillions of possible people, the whole reachable universe, Bostrom's "astronomical waste," Ord's "precipice," MacAskill-style arguments about what we owe the future) is what makes existential risk the overriding moral priority of our era. That's a "scale is what matters" argument through and through, and it's more or less exactly the move Ramsey seems to reject in that passage, where he's pretty explicit that size and numbers aren't what's impressive - thought and feeling are.

So here's my ask: pretending Ramsey somehow got his hands on Bostrom, Ord, and MacAskill, what do you think he'd actually say about longtermism's core scale-based case for prioritizing existential risk? Would he treat it as a natural extension of the very probability theory he helped build, or would his own stated outlook make him one of longtermism's more interesting internal critics? I'd also love it if you factored in that he died at 26 and never got to develop any of this further - how much weight should we even put on a passage like that as his "considered" view versus just a brilliant young man's bravado? Don't hedge for my sake, I'd definately rather have your best reconstruction than a wishy-washy "on the one hand" answer.
