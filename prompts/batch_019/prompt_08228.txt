I have been working through Spinoza's Theological-Political Treatise on my own, and there is one move in it I have not been able to work through to my own satisfaction. Spinoza defines natural right as strictly identical with power: whatever a thing has the power to do, it thereby has the right to do, and he is explicit that this identity survives the formation of a state, since the resulting social body simply has whatever power it collectively commands. Given that starting point, I do not see what stops a sufficiently secure, well-entrenched monarchy from having just as much right as a democracy, by that very same definition - the reasoning seems like it should be indifferent between forms of government, or at most favor whichever one happens to be hardest to overthrow. Yet Spinoza treats democracy as uniquely the most natural regime and as the one that preserves the most of each person's original right, a claim he was still working out when he died partway through the chapter on democracy in the unfinished Political Treatise. Could you walk me through the actual steps that are supposed to carry him from "right is power" to a genuine, non-arbitrary preference for democracy over monarchy or aristocracy, and say plainly which step is doing the real work?
