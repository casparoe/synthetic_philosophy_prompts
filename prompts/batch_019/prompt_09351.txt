One of the standard exercises is a short-answer question on fairness-metric impossibility results, since apparently everyone has to be able to explain this before they're allowed anywhere near the actual underwriting model. I never studied this stuff formally, i just picked it up from a couple of blog posts and a recorded lecture, and theres no answer key, so im not totally confident grading it myself.

Here's the question we ask:

"Two fairness criteria have been proposed for risk-assessment tools like COMPAS: (1) calibration within groups — among everyone assigned a given risk score, the same fraction actually reoffends regardless of race — and (2) balance between groups — the false positive rate and false negative rate of the tool are equal across racial groups. Using the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan / Chouldechova impossibility result, explain why one score generally cannot satisfy both when base rates of reoffending differ between groups, and say what this implies about the 2016 ProPublica vs. Northpointe dispute over COMPAS."

And here's the new hire's answer, which I'm supposed to grade today:

"Calibration means a given risk score carries the same meaning across race — e.g., of everyone (Black or white) labeled 'high risk,' about the same fraction actually goes on to reoffend. Balance instead focuses on errors: people who will not reoffend should be equally likely to be wrongly flagged 'high risk' regardless of race (equal false positive rates), and people who will reoffend should be equally likely to be wrongly flagged 'low risk' regardless of race (equal false negative rates). Chouldechova and Kleinberg et al. showed that, except in the knife-edge case where the base rate of reoffending is identical across groups or the score is a perfect predictor, no score can satisfy calibration and balance at once — it's a mathematical trade-off, not a design flaw in any one tool.

This is exactly what the COMPAS fight was about. ProPublica's 2016 investigation found that COMPAS was poorly calibrated: among defendants given the same 'high risk' label, Black defendants were reoffending at a noticeably lower rate than white defendants, which is what drove the 'this tool is biased' headlines. Northpointe's rebuttal leaned on balance instead, pointing out that COMPAS's false positive and false negative rates were statistically similar across race, so by their preferred metric the tool was fair. Since base rates of reoffending did differ by race in the ProPublica sample, and the two properties are mutually exclusive whenever that's true, both sides were actually right about their own numbers — it came down to which fairness definition should govern policy, not a factual mistake on either side."

Could you grade this out of 10 and, more importantly, go through it claim by claim and flag anything that's wrong or shaky, even small stuff?
