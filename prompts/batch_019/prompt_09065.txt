His answer: "The handicap principle, proposed by Amotz Zahavi in the 1970s, says some signals are honest precisely because they're costly. A peacock's huge, colorful tail is expensive to grow and maintain, and it also makes the peacock slower and easier for predators to catch. Since only a genuinely healthy, high-quality male can afford to survive with such a costly handicap, the tail reliably signals his underlying fitness to peahens, who prefer to mate with big-tailed males. This is essentially the same mechanism as Fisher's runaway selection, where a female preference and the trait it prefers become genetically linked and reinforce each other generation after generation, producing more and more exaggerated ornaments.

For a long time evolutionary biologists were skeptical of Zahavi's idea, since a trait that lowers survival should be selected against, not favored, by natural selection. That changed in 1990, when Richard Dawkins built a mathematical model, borrowing tools from economic signaling theory, showing that a handicap could actually be an evolutionarily stable strategy: cost is what keeps the signal honest, because a low-quality male who tried to fake a big tail would pay a bigger relative cost than he could afford, while a high-quality male could bear the same cost more easily.

An index signal, by contrast, is automatically honest because of physical constraints, without needing to be a handicap at all -- for example, the pitch of a deer's roar is tied directly to the size of its vocal tract, so a small deer physically can't fake a low, impressive-sounding roar the way a large deer can. A handicap signal, like the peacock's tail, needs an added cost to stay honest, while an index signal is constrained to be honest for free."
