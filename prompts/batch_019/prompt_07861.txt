I am preparing the session on panpsychism for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I would like to get past the version of the "combination problem" that most students arrive already knowing in outline: the worry that if fundamental physical entities carry some minimal form of experience, it is hard to see how enough of them, suitably arranged, could add up to the single, unified consciousness of a subject like you or me. Stated that baldly, the objection tends to compress several genuinely distinct difficulties into one, and a sharp student can walk away thinking that a single clever move -- some story about integration, say, or information flow -- disposes of "the" problem, when it may really only address one strand of it. A colleague in cognitive science who works on integrated information theory sat in on last year's session and pointed out, rightly, that her own framework runs into a structurally similar worry about which physical systems get to count as loci of experience in the first place, which is part of why I want to lay out the taxonomy more carefully this time.

Could you write an intuitive, more or less spoken-aloud explanation of how the combination problem pulls apart into distinct sub-problems, and of why making headway on one leaves the others untouched? I would like the explanation to distinguish at least three strands: the problem of getting many micro-subjects to compose, rather than merely sit alongside, one macro-subject; the problem of deriving the enormous variety of macro-level qualities -- colors, aches, tastes -- from whatever thin repertoire of qualities the micro-level is supposed to supply (sometimes discussed as a "palette" worry); and the boundary problem, that is, why brains, but apparently not rocks, crowds of people, or an arbitrarily gerrymandered half of a brain, are the sort of aggregate that composes a subject at all. A closing line or two on why cosmopsychists, who start with one cosmos-sized subject and try to work downward to entities like us rather than building upward from micro-subjects, inherit a mirror-image version of the same difficulty (sometimes put as a "decombination" problem) rather than escaping it, would also be welcome. I am less interested in a survey of who has said what than in an explanation precise enough that a good student could reconstruct, unprompted, why "the" combination problem is really several problems wearing a single name.
