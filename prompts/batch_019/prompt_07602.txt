The question was: "Explain the (synchronic) Dutch Book argument for probabilism, using a simple numerical example. Then explain what the converse Dutch Book theorem adds to the picture. Finally, give one serious objection to treating this pair of results as a full vindication of probabilism as a requirement of rationality."

Here's the answer I have to grade:

"A Dutch Book is a set of bets that guarantees a loss for the agent no matter what happens. Say I think P(rain tomorrow) = 0.6 and P(no rain tomorrow) = 0.5. Since these don't sum to 1, my credences are incoherent, and a bookie can sell me a bet on rain for 60 cents that pays $1 if it rains, plus a bet on no-rain for 50 cents that pays $1 if it doesn't. I pay $1.10 total, but exactly one bet pays off $1, so I lose 10 cents whichever way it goes. Since I'd have accepted both bets as fair by my own lights, this shows that violating the probability axioms leaves you exploitable, and the standard conclusion is that rational credences must be coherent.

De Finetti also proved the converse Dutch Book theorem: if your credences are coherent, no book can be made against you. Between the two results, coherence and being un-bookable turn out to be the very same property, which really makes the original theorem redundant once you have the converse - if you can't be booked, you must already be coherent, so the converse is doing all the real work here.

The most serious problem, raised by Hájek, is that all this really shows is that incoherent credences leave you financially exploitable, not that they're epistemically defective - avoiding a sure loss is a pragmatic matter, like knowing better than to play a rigged casino game. So even granting both theorems, the Dutch Book pair gives you at best a prudential reason to be coherent, not a distinctively epistemic one, and probabilism was supposed to be an epistemic thesis."
