I recently spent some time reading through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's coverage of theories of biological function, and one tension has stuck with me. The etiological ("selected effects") view, associated with Larry Wright, holds that a trait's function is whatever effect it was selected for by natural selection. The causal-role view, associated with Robert Cummins, holds that a trait's function is simply whatever causal contribution it currently makes to some capacity of the system we are trying to explain, with no essential appeal to history at all. As a programmer, I found the causal-role view intuitive at first, since it resembles how we describe what a function in code "does" in a given context — but I would like to see that intuition pressure-tested against a hard case rather than just asserted.

Please write a dialogue between a committed etiological theorist and a committed causal-role theorist who are presented with the following case by a biologist: a single wild rabbit is born with a novel mutation that thickens its coat. The thicker coat allows it to survive one freak, unusually harsh winter that kills most of the population, and the rabbit reproduces before anyone can tell whether the trait will spread through the population, be selected against despite this one success, or simply disappear again for unrelated reasons. The biologist asks: does the thicker coat have the function of insulation right now, in this individual rabbit?

Have the two theorists argue the case directly, including what each should say if the trait does spread in later generations and what each should say if it instead vanishes. Both debaters should be equally sharp, and each should respond to the specific point the other has just made rather than restating their own position or talking past one another.
