I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. A patient in our deep-brain-stimulation trial for treatment-resistant depression signed an advance directive before implantation stating that any request to deactivate the device (a simple reprogramming, not further surgery) would be honored only after a mandatory 30-day waiting period, since she didn't trust her depressed self to judge the treatment fairly. Eight months in, she is symptom-free and thriving by every clinical measure, but has now asked, calmly and consistently across three visits, to have the stimulation turned off -- not because of relapse, but because she says she no longer recognizes herself, and would rather be "the difficult, vivid person I was" than the calmer person the device has made her. Her 30 days run out next week. This strikes me as a nastier cousin of the "transformative experience" problem (L.A. Paul's term) that gets kicked around on rationalist blogs in the context of choices like having a kid: the question here isn't whether she could have foreseen the change, but whether the self who signed the directive and the self now revoking it count as the same authority. Roughly half the committee wants to honor the directive exactly as written, since this is precisely the contingency it was drafted for; the other half thinks its authority lapsed the moment the treatment succeeded on its own terms. Please draft the committee's decision memo: a concrete recommendation with a timeline for when the 30 days run out, and a genuine, fair treatment of whichever side the memo doesn't adopt.
