"In her 1956 pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree, G. E. M. Anscombe argues that Oxford should not confer an honorary degree on Truman. (a) Using the distinction between intending a consequence and merely foreseeing it, explain why Anscombe thinks the doctrine of double effect cannot justify the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. (b) Explain why, on Anscombe's own terms, this argument does not commit her to pacifism. (c) A classmate objects: 'Since Truman's advisors had already estimated, before the bombs were dropped, roughly how many civilians would die, there is no real difference on Anscombe's own principles between intending those deaths and merely foreseeing them.' Is this a fair gloss on Anscombe's position? Defend your answer in two or three sentences."

And here is my partner's answer:

"(a) The core of Anscombe's case is the doctrine of double effect, which she formulates as a distinction between what an agent aims at, either as an end or as the means to that end, and what the agent merely foresees will result from the action without aiming at it. On this view, an agent can permissibly bring about a bad effect, such as civilian deaths, as a side effect of an otherwise legitimate act, as long as the bad effect isn't the means by which the agent gets what he wants. Anscombe argues that the bombings fail this test because the destruction of the two cities was not incidental to some separate military objective; flattening the cities was the mechanism by which Japan's surrender was supposed to be produced. The killings were not a side effect of aiming at some legitimate target and missing, or hitting it and causing collateral damage — terrorizing the population and government into giving up was the whole strategy. So the civilian deaths were intended as a means, not merely foreseen as a side effect, and double effect cannot excuse the act.

(b) This does not commit Anscombe to pacifism, because she never claims that killing in war, or even killing that foreseeably kills civilians, is always wrong. Soldiers may still kill enemy combatants, and civilians may still be permissibly killed as an unintended side effect of a proportionate attack on a legitimate target. Her objection is narrower than pacifism: intending the death of the innocent as a means is always murder, whatever the consequences of refraining, but that leaves plenty of room for justified killing in a just war.

(c) I think this is basically a fair gloss. Once you know for certain, or close to it, that an action will kill a given number of civilians, there's no real difference left between intending their deaths and foreseeing them — intending is just what foreseeing turns into once the probability gets high enough. So Truman's foreknowledge of the death toll already puts him in the same position, on Anscombe's principles, as someone who intends those deaths outright."
