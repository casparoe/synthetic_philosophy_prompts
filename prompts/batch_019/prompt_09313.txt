Over the past couple of months I've been working through John Locke's "Some Thoughts Concerning Education" on my own, mostly out of curiosity after realizing there was a lot more to him than the "blank slate" idea I'd vaguely picked up somewhere. What struck me most is his extended argument about rewards and punishment: he's firmly against beating children, since he thinks it produces what he calls a "slavish temper" rather than genuine virtue, but he's almost equally hard on the opposite approach of bribing them with sweets, toys, or money for good behavior, on the grounds that this just teaches a child to value the treat rather than whatever the treat was supposed to encourage. What he proposes instead is governing children through esteem and disgrace: cultivating in them a sensitivity to the good or bad opinion of the people around them, especially their parents, so that being warmly praised or coldly ignored by the household does the disciplinary work that whips and candy would otherwise do.

This has stuck with me because my son's school, and a couple of the apps he uses at home for reading and language practice, all run on points, badges, streaks, and leaderboards, and the teachers seem to like this precisely because it "motivates" kids without anyone having to punish or bribe them in any crude way. It occurred to me that this might be exactly the sort of thing Locke was warning against - a more elaborate, digital descendant of the sugar-plum he thought corrupted a child's sense of value - except that it also has a public, comparative dimension (everyone can see your streak, your badge, your rank on the leaderboard) that seems closer to what he meant by esteem and disgrace than to a private material reward handed over in secret.

So I'd like your help thinking this through carefully, using what Locke actually argued rather than just his general reputation as an educational reformer. Would he most likely see today's gamified rewards and behavior-tracking systems as bribery dressed up in new clothes, or would their social, comparative character make them a genuine, if updated, version of the esteem-based discipline he recommended? And is there a problem lurking here that I don't think Locke ever quite resolves: if what motivates a well-behaved child is really just the desire to be well thought of rather than love of the thing itself, is that so different from chasing a badge or a streak? Finally, given that Locke was originally writing for a wealthy family educating a single boy through a private tutor, I'd be curious whether you think his framework can be carried over at all to a classroom of thirty children sharing one app, or whether that gap in context should make us hesitant to invoke him here in the first place.
