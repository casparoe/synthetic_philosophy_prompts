I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of probability, and I'd like a dialogue for next week's session on Bertrand's paradox that goes beyond the textbook treatment. Picture two philosophers of probability at a chalkboard where the three classical constructions of a 'random chord' have just been drawn out -- random endpoints, random radius, random midpoint -- yielding their familiar answers of 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 for the probability that the chord exceeds the inscribed equilateral triangle's side. One should defend Jaynes' resolution of the paradox: that requiring the answer to be invariant under the rotations, translations, and rescalings left unfixed by the problem's statement singles out 1/2 as objectively correct, thereby vindicating a properly constrained principle of indifference. The other should grant the argument's ingenuity while denying that it escapes the very arbitrariness it was meant to dissolve, pressing on why this invariance requirement rather than some other should govern our ignorance -- perhaps by way of a case like van Fraassen's cube factory. Keep the two evenly matched, each engaging the other's actual last move rather than restating a prepared position, and don't let the exchange resolve itself just because one side runs out of things to say.
