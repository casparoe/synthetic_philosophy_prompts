I never studied philosophy in school, but a few of us who got interested in it later in life through books and podcasts run a small monthly reading group at my local library. Last month one of the regulars brought in a short, decades-old paper by a philosopher named John Taurek called "Should the Numbers Count?", which imagines a case where you have a life-saving drug that can be used to save either one person, David, or a different group of five people, but not both. Taurek's conclusion is that you're not actually obligated to save the five just because there are more of them. It's an odd little paper, but it set off a long argument between two of the regulars, Marco and Sasha, that kept going over coffee well after the meeting had officially ended.

(1) Marco opened by saying the conclusion is simply wrong: if you can save five people or one at the same cost to yourself, you save the five, and if an argument leads anywhere else, the argument is what's broken, not our sense of right and wrong. (2) Sasha said it's not so obviously wrong once you ask what exactly gets worse as the numbers rise: no single person experiences a fivefold loss, since each of the five loses only their own single life, exactly as David does, so it's unclear where the extra badness comes from that would make saving the five a duty rather than merely one permissible option among others. (3) Marco replied that this is a trick: of course no one person suffers five deaths, but five separate people each suffering their own complete loss is still worse overall than one person suffering theirs, in the same way that five houses burning down is worse than one, even though no single house burns five times over. (4) Sasha answered that the house comparison quietly assumes the very thing in dispute: houses can be added into a total amount of damage because no individual house has a point of view on the matter, but people aren't like that; David's claim on you is neither weaker nor stronger than any one of the five's claims, and stacking up several equally strong separate claims doesn't automatically add up to something stronger unless you already believe claims combine the way property does, which is exactly what Taurek denies. (5) Marco then asked Sasha to imagine not knowing in advance whether she'd turn out to be David or one of the five, and to say honestly which rule she'd want in place beforehand, always save the larger group or flip a coin regardless of size, since she'd obviously want the larger-group rule, because it gives her better odds of coming out alive, and that alone shows the numbers matter. (6) Sasha said that's exactly why Taurek himself proposes flipping a coin rather than automatically favoring David in the first place: it gives each side an equal chance instead of guaranteeing a loss just for being outnumbered, and asking which rule gives "better odds" already assumes that numbers should set the odds, so Marco's question just restates his conclusion instead of arguing for it. Marco's last word, as they were leaving, was that he still couldn't accept a coin flip if the numbers were more lopsided, a thousand against one, say; Sasha admitted that was the case she found hardest to answer, though she suspected the discomfort came from picturing the thousand as a single pile rather than as a thousand separate individuals, each with a claim exactly as strong as the one person's, and no stronger.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know this literature well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move, including that last point about lopsided numbers, and say who has the better of it at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether you think there's a stronger move available to either of them, at any point, that neither Marco nor Sasha actually made. Numbering your answer to match the six moves would make it easier for me to follow.
