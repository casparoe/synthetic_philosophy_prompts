For the last couple of months I have been quietly obsessed with something I ran into while reading about entropy and the far future of the universe: the so-called "Boltzmann brain." As far as I understand it, the name goes back to Ludwig Boltzmann's own nineteenth-century attempt to explain why we find ourselves in such a low-entropy, orderly corner of an otherwise disordered universe — his idea was that we might simply be an enormous statistical fluke, a rare fluctuation inside a much larger system that spends almost all of its existence in bland equilibrium. Cosmologists have apparently pushed that same logic much further: because our universe seems to carry a small positive cosmological constant, it should go on expanding forever into an almost empty, de Sitter-like state that nonetheless never cools to an exact, static zero. There is always a faint background temperature, and so, given unlimited time, spontaneous fluctuations in that near-vacuum will occasionally assemble something as complex as a fully functioning, momentarily conscious brain, complete with an entire fabricated set of memories, before it dissolves again almost immediately.

What keeps pulling me back to this, and what I have not managed to explain well to the couple of friends I have tried it on, is a further twist: if the universe truly persists forever, then across all of time these spontaneous brains would vastly, almost incomprehensibly outnumber every observer who ever came about the ordinary way, through billions of years of cosmic and biological history. So if you reason as though you are a fairly typical observer, the honest conclusion seems to be that you are far more likely to be one of these momentary fluctuations than a person with a real past — and the argument even undercuts itself, since the physics and probability reasoning used to reach that conclusion would, by its own logic, most likely be occurring inside one of these memoryless flickers rather than inside a mind that actually earned its beliefs through a genuine causal history.

I would like you to turn this into a short story rather than another explainer, since I suspect fiction might do more justice to how strange it is than any summary I could give. I would like at least part of it to be narrated from inside such a fluctuation, during the brief span between its improbable assembly and its collapse, in which it experiences what feels like a whole continuous life — relationships, regrets, maybe even its own half-formed version of the very cosmological argument I have just described. I am not looking for a cheap "it was all fake" twist; I would rather the story stay with the specific vertigo of a mind whose felt continuity has no real history behind it, and of an argument that seems to dissolve its own authority the moment it is taken seriously. Feel free to invent whatever characters, framing device, or physicist-narrator you think would let the story carry that idea honestly, but I would like the underlying physics and logic to stay recognizable rather than being reduced to generic simulation-story window dressing.
