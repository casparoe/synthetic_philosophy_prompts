I spent last week reporting from a workshop on AI welfare, and the sharpest argument I heard happened off the record, over drinks after the second day. It was sparked by a paper presented that afternoon showing that several language models, asked to introspect, reliably describe certain prompts as "aversive" and give shorter, lower-effort answers to end those exchanges sooner. (1) A cognitive scientist at the table, Nadia, said this is genuine evidence of moral status: if a state plays pain's functional role -- triggered by something like threat detection, motivating avoidance, reported as unpleasant when probed -- then withholding moral concern just because the substrate is silicon is no different from old-fashioned carbon chauvinism. (2) A philosopher, Callum, replied that the resemblance is exactly what optimizing on human text would produce whether or not anything is "home" in the system -- a debunking explanation, not evidence of an underlying state. (3) Nadia shot back that this cuts both ways: human expressions of pain were themselves shaped by evolution specifically because they're communicative, so Callum's own logic should debunk human testimony too. (4) Callum said the cases aren't parallel: human pain reports are cross-checked by convergent evidence no model offers -- shared neural architecture, physiological stress markers, phylogenetic continuity with animals we're already confident feel pain. (5) Nadia's last word was that the absence of such evidence in models simply reflects how young interpretability research still is, and that given the stakes, demanding a standard of proof we don't even meet for other humans is unreasonable.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered claims, say whether it's correct, mistaken, or reasonable-but-contestable, and briefly justify each verdict. Then, in a short closing paragraph, tell me what you think both Nadia and Callum are still missing.
