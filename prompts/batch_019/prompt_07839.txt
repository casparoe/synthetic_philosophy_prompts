I've been slowly working my way through a secondhand collection of Frederick Douglass's speeches and letters that I picked up at a library sale, and one thing keeps nagging at me: how completely his position on the U.S. Constitution changed over the course of his life, and how personal the fallout from that change seems to have been. In his early years as an abolitionist, closely aligned with William Lloyd Garrison, Douglass apparently accepted the Garrisonian view that the Constitution was irredeemably a pro-slavery document, built on compromises like the three-fifths clause and the fugitive slave clause, and not worth participating in politically at all -- this is the view behind Garrison's famous description of it as "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell." But by the early 1850s Douglass had broken with Garrison and swung around to arguing more or less the opposite: that the Constitution, read on its own terms rather than by the founders' private intentions, was actually an anti-slavery document, and that the framers' pointed refusal to ever use the words "slave" or "slavery" anywhere in the text was itself significant. From what I can tell this wasn't just a tactical disagreement -- it caused a real and lasting rift between two men who had once been close.

I'd like to see this argument played out properly, rather than have the matter settled in advance by picking a winner. Could you write a dialogue between Garrison and Douglass, set around the time of their break, in which they work through this disagreement together? I'd like both of them to be arguing sincerely and at their best -- Garrison shouldn't come off as merely cynical about politics, and Douglass shouldn't come off as naively glossing over what's actually in the text -- and I want them to genuinely respond to one another's strongest points rather than just restate their own starting positions. In particular, I'd hope to see Garrison give his best answer to the "the word 'slave' appears nowhere in the document" argument, and Douglass give his best answer to how a document containing the three-fifths and fugitive-slave clauses could possibly be called anti-slavery.
