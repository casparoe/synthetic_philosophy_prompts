So I've been down a rabbit hole reading MIRI's old "embedded agency" posts and I hit a wall on the tiling agents section. The setup as I understand it: you've got an agent that only takes an action once its proven the action is safe/good, and you want it to be able to build a successor (or just trust its own future self) that works the same way. Seems like that should be easy - if the successor only acts on proofs, and I already trust the proof system, why wouldn't I trust the successor? But apparently this runs straight into Löb's theorem and the whole plan falls apart, which people call the "Löbian obstacle." I can follow Löb's theorem fine as pure logic (if T can prove "provable(P) implies P", then T can already prove P - kind of a weird bootstrapping thing), but I don't have a gut-level sense for why THAT specific fact is what wrecks self-trust here, or why its not just an overly-formal version of a problem we already handle for regular software (tests, code review, sandboxing, staged rollouts). Can you break down why this is a genuinely deep problem for a self-modifying agent, in terms that'd click for a programmer, rather than just a logician being pedantic about something engineers deal with all the time?
