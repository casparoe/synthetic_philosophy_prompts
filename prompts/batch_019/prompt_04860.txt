I was an atmospheric chemist for most of my working life and only got into philosophy after retiring -- lately I've been stuck on the frame problem, the old McCarthy/Hayes/Dennett puzzle about how a reasoning system is supposed to decide which of the countless things an action *doesn't* change are even worth representing (Dennett's example of the robot that either blows itself up or sits there forever deducing irrelevant facts is a nice illustration of the two ways this goes wrong). Turns out our university's environmental review committee runs into a real version of this. We're scoping the review for a new campus AI/HPC data center, and the committee's split: one camp wants to analyze only effects with a short, clean causal chain -- onsite power draw, cooling water, noise -- the other wants to trace things several links out, to marginal emissions from the regional grid, upstream chip fabrication, and development induced by the new substation and construction jobs. That second camp says stopping early is just arbitrary line-drawing dressed up as rigor, and honestly we have no house rule for where the chain has to stop -- it comes down to whoever argues loudest that week. I've got the scoping meeting in two weeks and need a short memo: a stopping rule the committee could apply consistantly from now on, plus what it actually implies we should decide for this project. Please take the "trace it further" camp seriously rather than waving them off as pedantic -- I have a hunch they're onto something real that I can't quite articulate myself.
