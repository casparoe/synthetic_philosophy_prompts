I'm finishing a feature on Kurt Gödel's little-discussed venture into natural theology: the modal-logic ontological argument he drafted privately, showed to Dana Scott and a few colleagues around 1970, and declined to publish himself, apparently out of concern that it would be read as a personal profession of faith rather than a logical exercise. It only became public after his death, in the Collected Works, with commentary by Robert Adams. What makes it more than "Anselm with symbols" is that Gödel built the proof from an unanalyzed primitive he called a "positive property," defined a God-like being as one that has every positive property, laid down axioms governing how positivity behaves under negation, conjunction, and necessary implication, and then used the modal system S5 to show that the mere possibility of a God-like being forces its necessary existence. For the science angle, I've also been reading about the 2013–14 computer formalizations (a project led by Christoph Benzmüller and collaborators), in which automated theorem provers confirmed that the inference from Gödel's axioms genuinely goes through — which shifts the interesting disagreement away from "is the logic valid" and onto the axioms themselves, plus a couple of downstream consequences that even sympathetic logicians find hard to swallow.

For a sidebar, I want to get past the usual grab-bag of objections to "the" ontological argument, most of which were aimed at Anselm's or Descartes's versions and don't obviously transfer. Below are six objections, several of them tailored to Gödel's specific formalization rather than to the ontological argument in general. A philosopher friend who read an early draft insisted that most of these quietly beg the question once you look closely at what Gödel's axioms actually say, and I'd like a more careful, item-by-item verdict before I put that claim into print.

- gaunilo_parody: the Gaunilo-style move of plugging a different, equally axiom-satisfying set of properties into the same schema (a necessarily existing island, or a "demonic" analogue) to show the form manufactures whatever superlative being you seed it with.
- kant_existence: Kant's objection that existence is not a real predicate, so building it into even a maximally perfect concept can't guarantee that anything answers to that concept.
- modal_collapse: Sobel's result that Gödel's axioms, once you add the definitions of "essence" and "necessary existence" needed to complete the proof, entail that whatever is actually true is true in every possible world — collapsing contingency generally, not just proving theism.
- positive_undefined: the complaint that "positive property" is never given an independent test, so nothing but the desired conclusion tells us whether, say, omniscience counts as positive and the capacity for gratuitous cruelty does not, meaning the axioms may already contain what they are meant to derive.
- s5_dependence: the worry that the proof is really just a way of cashing out the disputed modal principle that possible necessity implies actual necessity; deny that principle (drop down to a weaker modal logic) and "a God-like being is possible" stops buying you "a God-like being exists."
- axiom_fragility: the fact that patches proposed to block modal collapse (starting with Anderson's 1990 revision) change which axioms are doing the real work and what, if anything, still follows, suggesting a system tuned to a target rather than one built from independently plausible premises.

Please sort these six objections into tiers from S (still lands with essentially full force against Gödel's specific axioms and definitions) down to D (largely defused once you look closely at what he actually assumed), judged against his version specifically rather than against the ontological argument in the abstract.
