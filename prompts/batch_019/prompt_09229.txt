Over four decades in theoretical physics, I grew comfortable with the idea that a finite body of evidence can underdetermine the choice between rival theories: the Copenhagen, many-worlds, and Bohmian formulations of quantum mechanics all fit the data equally well, yet no physicist concludes from this that there is simply no fact of the matter about what is actually happening at the quantum level. Now that I am retired and reading philosophy in earnest, I have come across Quine's "gavagai" example—in which a field linguist cannot tell whether a native's word means "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit stage"—along with the thesis of the indeterminacy of translation that he bases on it. As far as I can tell, this is simply the same predicament transposed onto language: an unremarkable case of underdetermination, the sort every working scientist learns to live with. Why, then, do philosophers treat Quine's thesis as such a distinctive and unsettling claim about meaning, rather than just another instance of the ordinary evidential gap between theory and data?
