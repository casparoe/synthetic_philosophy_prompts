I've been down a research rabbit hole for a longform piece on the "solve aging" crowd — the Bryan Johnson Blueprint people, the rapamycin-and-metformin trial folks, the cellular reprogramming startups that are all pretty explicit that aging is a disease to be cured, not a stage of life you're supposed to live through with any grace. Somewhere in there my brother, who teaches classics, kept texting me bits of Cicero's De Senectute (the "On Old Age" dialogue, where he puts his own opinions in the mouth of old Cato, lecturing Scipio and Laelius) going "this is basically arguing with your sources from 2000 years in advance." And honestly it kinda got under my skin. The whole essay is built around knocking down four specific complaints against old age — that it pulls you out of active life, weakens your body, kills your pleasures, and drags you toward death — and his answer to that last one is the part I keep chewing on: death is either nothing at all (no soul left to feel the loss, so why worry) or it's actually good news (if the soul's immortal you get to go join the great minds who came before), plus there's that image of ripe fruit dropping off the branch on its own versus green fruit getting torn off, and the bit about a wise person knowing when to exit the stage rather then overstaying their scene.

So here's what I can't settle for myself, and what I'd love your take on: would Cicero look at the modern life-extension crowd and see fellow travelers, or exactly the people he was writing against? There's a case for the former — he's genuinely proud of Romans who kept their minds and bodies sharp into their 80s through discipline and moderation, so you could say the biohackers are just doing his advice with better tools. But there's also a case that his whole back half is a deliberate argument that refusing to accept the "last act" is a failure of both nerve and reasoning, which sounds like it'd land pretty squarely on someone spending a fortune to not die. Can you actually reason through this with his specific arguments rather than just serving up "old Roman guy says be chill about death"? I'm especially interested in whether there's a genuine soft spot in Cicero's own position that a sharp Bryan Johnson type could exploit against him, or whether it actually holds up once you look closely.
