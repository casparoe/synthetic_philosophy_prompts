I'm working on a longread about scientists and mathematicians who secretly held pretty out-there religious or metaphysical beliefs, and Gödel is turning into the best example by far — my editor's now obssessed with him too. Basically every summary I can find says the same thing: that his famous ontological proof, the modal-logic one from 1970 that he only ever showed to Dana Scott (because he thought he might be dying and wanted a record of it to survive him, which is a little on the nose), was basically his rigorous, symbolic-logic proof that we survive death. The idea being that a truly rational universe, in the Leibnizian sense he was so into, just wouldn't create a mind and then snuff it out for no reason, so personal immortality falls out of the logic almost like a theorem.

What I don't get is: if one of the greatest logicians of the 20th century actually produced a valid logical proof of personal immortality, why isn't that a bigger deal in philosophy of religion circles? Is it that academics are too snobby to engage with a "proof" from a mathematician instead of a card-carrying philosopher, or is there something actually wrong with the argument that keeps serious people from citing it as evidence for an afterlife?
