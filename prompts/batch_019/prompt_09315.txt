I'm taking a course on the philosophy of physics this term, and after a couple of weeks on the foundations of statistical mechanics we've moved on to its cosmological payoff: the Boltzmann brain problem. The basic idea, as I understand it, is that if our universe settles into an eternal de Sitter phase because of the cosmological constant, the vacuum has a nonzero temperature, and over sufficiently long timescales rare thermal and quantum fluctuations will spontaneously assemble every possible configuration of matter — including, every so often, a fully conscious brain, complete with false memories of an entire life and a coherent external world, that pops into existence for a moment before dissolving back into chaos. Since assembling a single such brain is astronomically more probable than assembling an entire orderly universe like ours, the standard worry, raised early on by physicists like Dyson, Kleban, and Susskind, is that in an eternally inflating universe these "Boltzmann brains" will vastly outnumber observers who got here the ordinary way, through actual cosmological and biological history.

Here's what's bugging me, and what a few of us in the study group couldn't resolve after class last week. If almost all observers with exactly my memories and experiences, in a universe like the one our best current cosmological models describe, are Boltzmann brains rather than "real" observers, then ordinary self-locating reasoning — reasoning as though I were randomly sampled from the class of observers subjectively indistinguishable from me — seems to force the conclusion that I myself am almost certainly a Boltzmann brain right now, and that everything I take myself to remember, including this course, is a random fluctuation about to dissolve back into chaos. The reading and my professor both treat this as a reductio: a strike against whichever cosmological model predicts it, rather than something to actually believe about oneself. That has always struck me as suspiciously convenient, though. If the probabilities really are that lopsided, isn't refusing to accept the conclusion just a matter of not liking where the argument leads, rather than an actual rebuttal of it?

So what I'd like is a straight answer: is there a principled reason, beyond mere distaste for the conclusion, to treat Boltzmann brain domination as evidence against a cosmological model rather than as first-person evidence about what I probably am? And if there is such a reason, why doesn't the same move work for any unwelcome argument whatsoever — just relabel the conclusion a "reductio" of whatever assumptions produced it, and walk away unchanged?
