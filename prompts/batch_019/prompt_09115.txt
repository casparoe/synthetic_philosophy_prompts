For my seminar on medieval political philosophy, I am drafting a short paper comparing Aquinas's two best-known discussions of tyrannicide, and would like help evaluating my thesis before I commit to it in writing.

In his commentary on the Sentences (Book II, distinction 44), while arguing that subjects are not bound to obey a ruler who holds power by no legitimate title, Aquinas seems willing to accept, largely without qualification, that a private individual who kills such a tyrant acts justly and may even deserve praise for liberating his country. Yet in Book I of De Regimine Principum, addressing what should be done about a ruler who degenerates into tyranny, he argues at length against leaving the killing of tyrants to private initiative: those who take this on themselves, he warns, are often driven by ambition rather than zeal for the common good and tend to install a worse tyranny in place of the old one, and a general license to kill "tyrants" would endanger even legitimate rulers whom the seditious simply find too strict. Correcting a tyrant, he insists instead, belongs to the public authority that instituted him in the first place.

My thesis is that these two discussions are compatible rather than representing a change of mind: the caution of De Regimine Principum is a natural extension of the earlier view, not a retraction of it, once we are clear on what work "legitimate title" and "public authority" are doing in each text. Please argue for this reconciling thesis as persuasively as you can, making sure to address the strongest reason for thinking Aquinas simply changed his position.
