Over the last couple of years I've read a fair amount of popular physics and cosmology in my spare time — nothing formal, just books and long articles — and one idea I keep coming back to is the "Boltzmann brain" problem. As I understand it, if our universe really does keep expanding forever into a cold, nearly empty, dark-energy-dominated state, then over an infinite stretch of time, random thermal and quantum fluctuations in that near-vacuum will occasionally assemble a fully functioning, momentarily conscious brain, complete with a rich set of false memories, purely by chance. Because that kind of freak event is astronomically more likely per unit time than the ordinary formation of galaxies, stars, and evolved observers like us, a "typical" observer sampled from the whole future history of such a universe should overwhelmingly be expected to be one of these isolated fluctuating brains rather than someone with a genuine evolutionary and cosmological history — which seems to undercut the very reasoning we used to arrive at this cosmological picture in the first place.

What I didn't realize until I read further into the history of statistical mechanics is that Boltzmann ran into a strikingly similar problem in his own lifetime, and that his response to it is basically the direct ancestor of the modern idea. When Zermelo invoked Poincaré's recurrence theorem to argue that Boltzmann's own statistical account of the second law implied the universe must eventually return arbitrarily close to any earlier state, Boltzmann's answer was to propose that the universe as a whole spends almost all of eternity in flat, structureless thermal equilibrium, and that the low-entropy, orderly stretch we happen to inhabit is simply a rare statistical fluctuation, one we could only ever find ourselves living inside of, since no observers could exist anywhere else. That is clearly the ancestor of the modern Boltzmann brain worry, but it is not quite the same claim: Boltzmann was picturing a whole fluctuating region, large and stable enough to contain ordinary planets, ordinary evolutionary history, and reliable memories, rather than a single isolated brain with fabricated memories popping briefly out of the vacuum.

So here is what I would like your honest, carefully reasoned opinion on, rather than a quick guess: if someone sat Boltzmann down today and explained the full modern version of this problem to him, complete with dark energy and an eternally expanding vacuum, do you think he would recognize it as simply his own fluctuation idea carried to its logical conclusion, and embrace it for the same reasons he was willing to bite the bullet on Zermelo's recurrence objection? Or did his own physics, or his running disputes with Mach, Ostwald, and the energeticists over what statistical and probabilistic reasoning is even entitled to tell us about the world, give him principled grounds for treating the isolated-brain scenario as a genuinely different and more troubling problem — perhaps even one that should count as a reductio against any physical theory that predicts it, rather than a conclusion to accept at face value? I would like you to actually argue your way to an answer using what is known of his stated views, rather than just asserting one.
