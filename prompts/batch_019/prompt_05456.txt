So I've been down a rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing about how legal philosophers argue over "unjust laws," and it sent me back to the Hart-Fuller debate from the late 50s. Everyone who's taken a jurisprudence class remembers the Grudge Informer case -- the wife who reported her husband to the Nazis for grumbling about Hitler while he was home on leave, and the postwar German court that had to decide whether the wartime statute she relied on even counted as valid law in the first place. Hart's take is basically: fine, it was valid law, just a monstrous one, and the court should've been honest and openly passed a retroactive statute to punish her rather than pretend the Nazi law had never been law at all; Fuller thinks that's exactly the wrong lesson to draw. But a law profesor I talked to for the piece pointed out that most popular summaries stop right there, and the actually interesting round of the fight happens a few years later, wich almost nobody outside law school seems to know about.

That's the part I want to dig into. In his book Fuller lays out eight "principles of legality" -- generality, publicizing the rules, no retroactivity, clarity, the rules not contradicting each other, not demanding the impossible, some constancy over time, and officials actually applying the rules as announced -- which he calls the "inner morality of law." Hart reviews the book in 1965 and basically says: these are just principles of efficacy for getting anything done through rules, moral content optional -- even an efficient poisoner needs internal standards of craft (right dosage, right timing), and nobody thinks that makes poisoning virtuous. Fuller's comeback, in a "Reply to Critics" chapter he tacked on later, is that governing through those eight principles is what it actually looks like to treat the people you rule over as responsible agents who can follow rules and be held to them, rather than as raw material to be pushed around, and that this is a moral fact efficacy-talk can't dissolve.

Could you write me a dialogue between a card-carrying Hartian positivist and a card-carrying Fullerian natural lawyer hashing this out? They can open on the grudge-informer case if it helps them warm up, but I want most of it spent on the "is the inner morality of law really morality, or just glorified efficiency" fight -- let the positivist lean hard on the poisoner analogy (extending it with their own examples if they want), and don't let the natural lawyer get away with just repeating "but Nazi law was evil" -- they need an actual answer to the efficacy charge specifically. Keep them evenly matched, have them genuinely respond to each other's last point rather than deliver parallel speeches, maybe 12-20 exchanges, and it's fine if it doesn't end in a tidy resolution -- I'd rather see the disagreement get sharper than see it smoothed over.
