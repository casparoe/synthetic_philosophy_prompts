I host a philosophy podcast, and my next guest has a forthcoming book defending the Control Principle -- that an agent's blameworthiness cannot depend on factors outside their control -- by arguing that every standard case of moral luck is merely epistemic: the outcome changes what we can infer about an agent, never how blameworthy they actually are. Her standard example is the classic resultant-luck case of two equally reckless drivers, one of whom kills a pedestrian and one of whom gets home safely, where she insists we don't actually blame the first one more, we simply learn more from the outcome about how reckless they were all along. Draft eight interview questions that push on this move rather than let her simply restate it: I want at least one question that stipulates a version of the driver case in which the two drivers' antecedent recklessness is, by hypothesis, already known to be identical, so there is nothing left for the outcome to be evidence of; at least one on circumstantial luck, where an agent is simply never placed in the circumstances that would have tested or corrupted their character, so there is no completed act for anything to be evidence of; and at least one on constitutive luck, asking whether her strategy has any foothold left once the dispositions she treats as the "real" ground of blame are conceded to be just as unchosen as the outcome she is explaining away. For each, flag the reply she is likely to reach for and supply the follow-up that closes it off, and end with a question asking what she would accept as a genuine, non-explained-away case of moral luck.
