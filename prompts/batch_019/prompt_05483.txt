Lucas's original Gödelian argument against mechanism ran into the objection, associated above all with Benacerraf, that a mathematician is entitled to assert that her Gödel sentence is true only on the assumption that the formal system F supposedly capturing her reasoning is consistent, and nothing licenses that assumption — least of all Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, which guarantees that F itself could never certify its own consistency. Penrose's later version of the argument, in Shadows of the Mind, seems to route around exactly this difficulty by trading consistency for soundness: if F is a system whose theorems the mathematician would only ever assert as true, she is thereby entitled to affirm F's soundness — and so, trivially, its consistency — from a standpoint outside F, without needing the kind of internal proof the second theorem rules out. Given that this move appears to discharge the very difficulty that defeated Lucas, why do most philosophers of mind still treat computationalism as a live option, rather than conceding that Penrose's soundness-based argument succeeds where Lucas's did not?
