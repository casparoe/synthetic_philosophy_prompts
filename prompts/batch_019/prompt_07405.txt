so i've been down a rabbit hole reading about AI safety stuff and keep running into this thing called the Löbian obstacle, and it's kind of broken my brain a little. as i understand it, it comes from Löb's theorem in logic: if a formal proof system could prove the general rule "if I can prove X, then X is actually true" for some X, that alone would be enough for it to turn around and prove X outright — so a consistent system can only ever prove that rule for X's it could already prove directly, never as a genuine outside bootstrap. people apply this to AI agents that only act when they've got an actual proof that something is safe, and the upshot is that such an agent can't fully verify, in that same proof-based way, that a copy of itself (or some future version of itself) will always act safely, since doing that would basically require the exact kind of blanket self-trust Löb's theorem rules out.

what i can't decide is whether this is a genuine dead end for self-trust or just an artifact of demanding the wrong kind of certainty. could you write me a dialogue between two people who've each thought about this a ton and land on opposite sides? one person thinks the obstacle shows that total trust between two equally strong reasoners (or a reasoner and a copy of itself) is just flat-out logically impossible, and that every "solution" people propose is secretly smuggling in a weaker kind of trust while pretending its not. the other person thinks that's too defeatist, and that theres a legit way to route around it — whether thru probability instead of proof, a ladder of successively stronger systems, or something else — that isn't just hiding the problem under fancier math. neither one should be a pushover, they gotta actually engage with each other's strongest points instead of restating their own view, and in particular the probability-defender shouldn't get to pretend self-referential probability doesn't come with its own weird paradoxes.
