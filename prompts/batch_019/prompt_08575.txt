I'm a retired physical chemist -- spent about thirty-five years doing spectroscopy before I hung it up -- and a couple of years ago I started sitting in on a philosophy discussion group that meets at our local community center, mostly out of curiosity. I've gotten a bit hooked since then. Lately we've been on a kick about perception: whether what you expect or believe can actually change what you see, versus just changing what you say or conclude about what you saw. It reminded me of an old story from my grad school days, so I told the group about it: the N-ray business in France around 1903-04, where a well-respected physicist and a bunch of his colleagues kept reporting that a faintly lit screen got visibly brighter whenever a supposed new form of radiation was pointed at it, until an American physicist visited the lab, secretly removed the one piece of the apparatus that was supposedly doing all the work, without telling anyone -- and the reports of brightening kept right on coming anyway. (This is more or less how I remember it from a "pathological science" lecture decades ago, so forgive me if I'm hazy on some detail. It hit close to home regardless, since squinting at a weak signal against noise was basically my whole career.)

Telling that story set off a real argument between two people in the group, Renata and Sam, that went on for a good twenty minutes and that nobody could settle. Here's roughly how it went. (1) Renata said this is basically a real-world proof that expectation can reach all the way down into perception itself: these were sober, trained people, not liars, so if they "saw" brightening that wasn't there, their seeing itself must have been altered by what they expected to see. (2) Sam objected that judging a faint, near-threshold brightness change in a dark room is exactly the kind of task where, on ordinary signal-detection grounds, expectation shifts how willing you are to say "yes, brighter" without necessarily touching how sensitive you actually are to the light -- so the story shows the reports weren't tracking anything physical, but not whether that's a change in seeing or just a change in judging after normal seeing. (3) Renata shot back that if you can always wave a case away as "just a shift in willingness to report," what would ever count as evidence for real perceptual change -- that sounds like a rule that can't lose. (4) Sam said it's not unfalsifiable, just untested here: proper forced-choice testing with randomized blank trials can tell "how sensitive" apart from "how willing to report," and nobody ran anything like that in 1904, so the story is honestly silent on the deeper question either way. (5) Renata's last shot was that some of the reports were oddly specific -- comparing brightness across repeated trials, not just yes-or-no -- which feels like more than a coin-flip guess to her. (6) Sam's comeback was that confident, richly detailed guesses about stimuli at or below threshold are a completely ordinary finding in that line of research, so detail on its own doesn't prove anything was really seen.

Nobody in the group would referee it properly, so now I'm asking you. Could you go through moves 1 through 6 and give me a score for each -- say, -2 if that move is pretty clearly wrong, +2 if its pretty clearly right, 0 if it's a wash -- and a sentence or two on why? And then tell me straight, since I suspect both of them are missing something: does this old story actually settle anything about whether belief can get into perception itself, or were Renata and Sam mostly arguing past each other?
