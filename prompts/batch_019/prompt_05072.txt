During my career as an experimental physicist I thought a good deal about the Everett interpretation of quantum mechanics, where the branches produced by a measurement are usually taken to be equally real, with none singled out as "the" actual outcome. Now retired and reading philosophy in earnest, I've been working through Aristotle's sea-battle passage in De Interpretatione and its reconstruction in Arthur Prior's tense logic, and the branching-time models used there seem to be wrestling with exactly the same choice.

Please compare the Peircean and Ockhamist semantics for future contingents that came out of Prior's work. As I understand it, the Peircean evaluates a sentence such as "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" symmetrically across every branch, so that nothing true on only some branches can make the sentence true simpliciter. The Ockhamist, by contrast, wants the sentence to be true or false outright, and so seems to need one branch singled out in advance — a "Thin Red Line" — as the one that will actually happen. Is my Everett analogy apt, or does it break down somewhere important? And does the Ockhamist's privileged branch really buy back bivalence at a lower price than simply building determinism into the model from the outset? Justify your answer.
