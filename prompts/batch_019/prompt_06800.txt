I sit on the exam-policy committee for a professional certification that our industry association awards to senior software engineers, based on a closed-book, closed-tool practical exam. Two committee members want to let candidates use an AI coding assistant during the exam, since virtually every engineer uses one on the job and testing without it seems to measure a skill nobody actually relies on anymore. Other members worry that once an assistant is doing part of the work, we'd really be certifying someone's ability to prompt and check a tool rather than their own engineering judgment, and that employers who treat the certification as a hiring signal would no longer know what it vouches for. While putting together talking points, I came across the "extended mind thesis" and the Otto's-notebook example -- the idea that a tool which is reliably on hand, quick to consult, and trusted without a second thought can count as part of someone's own cognition rather than an external aid -- and also the standard objection to it, sometimes called the "coupling-constitution fallacy," that this confuses a resource being reliably hooked into a process with its actually being part of that process. I'd like a short memo, addressed to the full committee ahead of our vote in three weeks, recommending one specific policy -- ban the assistant, allow it without restriction, or allow it under stated conditions -- with reasons both camps could actually respond to. It should take the extended-mind argument seriously as a real consideration rather than mentioning and dismissing it, whichever way the recommendation ultimately comes down.
