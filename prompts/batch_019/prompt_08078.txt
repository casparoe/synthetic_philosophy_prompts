I spent the better part of forty years running a laboratory that worked on cephalopod and, in later years, bumblebee nervous systems, and it is only since retiring that I have let myself read the philosophy that bears on what all that neuroanatomy was actually supposed to be evidence for. Together with two former colleagues I now record a modest podcast on questions at the border of biology and philosophy, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent several years developing and defending a precautionary, marker-based framework for extending moral and legal consideration to animals whose sentience is uncertain -- the sort of framework that has already fed into recent UK legislation recognizing decapod crustaceans and cephalopods as sentient beings, and into the wider push among consciousness researchers to take invertebrate, and even insect, experience seriously. She has given the standard version of this view in a dozen interviews already, marker list and precautionary principle included, so I would rather not have her simply restate it for us.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions that press on the parts of the framework I suspect are shakiest, each one anticipating the reply she is likely to reach for? I want at least one question on the "distribution problem" -- the worry that you need some prior theory of what consciousness is before you can decide which behavioral or neural markers even count as evidence of it, so that a framework advertised as theory-neutral may be smuggling one in regardless; one on how the asymmetry between false positives and false negatives under the precautionary principle is supposed to hold up once we move past charismatic cases like octopuses and crabs to fruit flies, plants, or artificial systems engineered to display those same markers; and one built around the mirror test's well-known failures -- dogs and pigs behave as self-unaware by that test yet remain excellent candidates for sentience on every other ground -- and what that suggests about how much weight any single marker can bear. For each of these, please add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that a good dodge does not end the exchange there. I would like to close on a harder, more personal question: since her framework is built never to rule sentience out with much confidence, ask her to name one discovery that would actually lower her credence in some animal's sentience, rather than simply raising it elsewhere.
