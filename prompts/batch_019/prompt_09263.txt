I'm on the neuroethics working group at the hospital where I work, and we've got a genuinely thorny one for next week's meeting. The disorders-of-consciousness team wants us to sign off on adding a new bedside EEG test to the standard workup for patients stuck in prolonged unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (the updated term for persistent vegetative state) or a minimally conscious state. The test is built on higher-order theories of consciousness -- it's hunting for a specific frontal signature that's supposed to show whether a patient has an actual higher-order representation of what their senses are picking up, versus "just" intact first-order sensory processing with nobody upstairs registering it. They want to use a negative result to justify two things: scaling back opioids during painful bedside procedures like line changes and wound debridement (unfelt processing doesn't need analgesia, the reasoning goes), and giving families a more definite answer than we currently can to the "is she still in there" question.

One of our consulting philosophers pointed out something that's now split the group pretty evenly, and honestly I cant unsee it either: this doesn't just measure consciousness, it presupposes a specific and genuinely contested answer to what consciousness even is. Higher-order theorists say you need that frontal signature for a state to be felt at all, full stop -- no higher-order representation, no experience, whatever's happening downstream is unconscious processing. But plenty of serious first-order and local-recurrence theorists think that's backwards for exactly the case we care about: on their view the hurting is done by posterior sensory/affective processing, and the frontal higher-order stuff is only needed for the patient to know they're in pain or report it, not for the pain to exist in the first place. So the exact same clean negative scan supports two opposite readings -- "this patient can't suffer" versus "this patient can suffer just fine, they just can't register that it's happening or tell us" -- and those readings point to opposite decisions about painkillers and opposite things to say to a family standing at the bedside.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? I need it to actually land somewhere -- adopt the protocol for the proposed uses, reject it, or adopt it with specific conditions -- not a "the science isn't settled" shrug. If it's conditions, spell out what they'd be and what would trigger us revisiting the policy later. And it has to take the DOC team's motivation seriously (they're trying to replace ad hoc bedside judgment calls with something more principled, not being careless about it) instead of just siding with the philosophers because raising doubt is the easy move.
