For the past year or so I've been quietly obsessed with the free will debate, mostly ever since a late-night argument with a friend about whether her brother could really be blamed for relapsing, given how little control he seemed to have over that particular moment. That argument sent me down a long, unsupervised reading spree — no classes, no professors, just me and a lot of open tabs — and eventually I landed on Harry Frankfurt's old thought experiment about a character named Jones, who is secretly watched by another character, Black, who has some way of forcing Jones's hand if it looks like Jones is about to decide the "wrong" way. The twist is that Jones ends up deciding the "right" way entirely on his own, so Black never actually has to intervene — and yet, because Black was standing by the whole time, Jones supposedly could not have done otherwise. Philosophers apparently treat this as a serious problem for what I later learned is called the principle of alternative possibilities: the old idea that you can only be praised or blamed for something if you genuinely could have chosen differently.

What really pulled me in, though, was the back-and-forth that came after. Some people objected that there's still a kind of "flicker" of freedom hiding inside the case — Black's device has to be watching for some early sign that Jones is wavering, and that sign itself might count as a thin alternative possibility of its own. And then, apparently, there's a comeback to that comeback: something like a dilemma where either the early warning sign is itself completely fixed in advance, in which case the whole setup is quietly smuggling in determinism from the start, or it isn't fixed in advance, in which case Black's device can never really be guaranteed to catch Jones in time, and the whole story stops proving that Jones lacked a live alternative after all. I'll admit I'm not sure I have every moving part exactly right, but the general shape of it — freedom possibly hiding in the gap between prediction and action — has stuck with me.

I'd love it if you could write me a short story that actually lives inside that tension rather than just retelling the Jones-and-Black setup in different clothes. Set it however feels right to you — a near-future neural implant that quietly "corrects" bad decisions before they happen, an old-fashioned inspector who's convinced he can read intentions off a suspect's face before they act, or something else entirely — but I'd like the plot itself to turn on that flicker-versus-dilemma problem, so that at some point a character (or the reader) genuinely can't tell whether some trace of an alternative choice was ever truly "live," or whether the whole predictive apparatus only ever looked reliable because it was secretly counting on everything being fixed from the start. I'm much more interested in a story that makes that uncertainty felt than one that simply explains it to me afterward.
