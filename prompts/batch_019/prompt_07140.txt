I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation on legal positivism, which is apparently the reason my state's judicial council appointed me as the public member of its new Historical Convictions Review Board — a seven-person panel created after the legislature repealed the last remnants of our old "crime against nature" statute. The Board processes petitions, mostly from elderly petitioners or their surviving family, to clear convictions obtained decades ago under that statute for private, consensual conduct that would plainly be protected today. Relief is granted through a standard order, and the chair has asked me, as the resident philosopher, to draft the memo she'll circulate before our next session, because the other six members are deadlocked over what that order should actually say the Board is doing.

One faction wants the order to declare the statute void from the moment of its enactment, on the ground that a law criminalizing harmless private conduct for no rational reason beyond animus was never fully law to begin with — they keep invoking the postwar German cases and the idea that a rule can be unjust enough to forfeit its claim to legal validity. The other faction, while just as sympathetic to petitioners, thinks this is a mistake: the statute was, as a plain historical fact, enacted through ordinary procedure and enforced and upheld by courts for generations, and dressing up an act of legislative mercy as the discovery that "there was never really a law here" is not only philosophically shaky but sets a precedent that cuts both ways — nothing stops some future board or legislature from using identical reasoning to declare a duly enacted law it merely dislikes null from inception. The choice is not just rhetorical: under our administrative code, whether the order reads "void ab initio" or "pardoned and expunged" determines whether related consequences — old sex-offender registry entries, less-than-honorable discharge characterizations, professional license revocations — are nullified automatically or must be separately re-litigated. I've told my dissertation advisor more than once that I did not expect to need the 1958 Hart-Fuller exchange for actual casework quite this literally.

Could you draft the memo I owe the chair? I want it to state the strongest version of both positions — the second faction's rule-of-law worry about precedent deserves real engagement, not a token paragraph, since I suspect it's the crux — and then land on a concrete recommendation (one of the two framings, or a third option neither faction has proposed) together with a specific answer to the collateral-consequences question. The Board votes on order language in three weeks, so please write something that could actually go into the minutes as a proposal, not an open-ended tour of the issue.
