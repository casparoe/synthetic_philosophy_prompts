Last night, a friend and I spent hours arguing about whether structural realism really earns its keep as a response to the pessimistic meta-induction in philosophy of science. I was defending John Worrall's line that the survival of mathematical structure across theory change—Fresnel's equations for light persisting, in form, inside Maxwell's electromagnetic theory—gives us genuine cumulative knowledge, even though the underlying ontology (the elastic ether) was abandoned. She kept coming back to the Newman objection: that if all we ever really latch onto is the abstract relational structure of the world, then, given a domain of the right cardinality, almost any structure can be trivially instantiated by some relation or other, so the realist's claim threatens to collapse into a near-tautological statement about how many objects there are. I didn't have a satisfying reply at the time. Could you explain, at a gut level, why the Newman objection is supposed to be so damaging to epistemic structural realism specifically, as opposed to scientific realism in general, and then assess whether standard escapes—restricting the relevant relations to "natural" or causal ones, or retreating to ontic structural realism—actually defuse the problem or merely relocate it?
