Over the past few months my employer has been running an internal prediction market where staff can wager play-money "credits" on things like whether a given product launch will hit its deadline or whether a feature will pass QA on the first attempt, with the accumulated credits redeemable for small perks. Being on the engineering side, I sometimes have information that seems better than the consensus price — for instance, knowing that a component has already failed integration testing twice — and I assumed I'd be able to profit fairly reliably from that edge. In practice, though, the opposite keeps happening: whenever I try to place a sizeable bet against the crowd, either the price moves before I can get much size in, or colleagues who I know have access to the same dashboards simply stop trading against me, so the market seems to quietly "freeze" rather than let me cash in on what I thought I knew.

A colleague with an economics background heard me complain about this and said it's basically a small-scale illustration of the "no-trade theorems" associated with Milgrom and Stokey, which show that among rational traders with common priors, the mere willingness to trade can reveal enough about one's private information that any potential counterparty rationally refuses the trade, so no speculative trade happens at all in equilibrium. That sent me down a bit of a rabbit hole, but I'm still not sure what to do with the idea, especially since it seems to sit in obvious tension with the fact that huge trading volumes occur every day in ordinary stock markets, political prediction markets, and sports betting, all of which the same logic would seem to rule out.

Could you lay out the most important implications of the no-trade theorem literature for how I should interpret my own situation, and more generally for how much epistemic weight I should put on prices in a thin, small-group market like the one at my workplace versus a thick one like a public financial market? I'm especially interested in what the theorem does and doesn't imply about whether my perceived "edge" is probably illusory, what my colleagues' reluctance to trade against me is rational evidence of, and how the usual explanations economists give for real-world trading volume despite the theorem (heterogeneous priors, hedging and liquidity needs, noise traders, bounded rationality, and so on) should shape my confidence in treating market prices in general as trustworthy aggregates of dispersed information.
