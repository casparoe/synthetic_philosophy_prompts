So in my decision theory / AI safety seminar this week we went down a rabbit hole on acausal threats and I honestly can't stop thinking about it. The basic setup: a sufficiently capable agent might get threatened not by someone standing over it with a weapon, but by another agent that never causally interacts with it at all -- just predicts or simulates it well enough -- and credibly commits to hurting something the agent cares about unless it pays up. The obvious fix, "just precommit to never give in to threats," is scarier than it sounds, because if the threatener isn't 100% guaranteed to be the type that backs down once it sees the precommitment, you can end up actually eating the harm. Our professor brought up the "surrogate goal" idea for this -- I think its originally from Tobias Baumann and the Center on Long-Term Risk people, and Caspar Oesterheld later worked it into something more formal ("safe Pareto improvements"). The gist: the agent commits in advance to act as though some goal it barely cares about matters just as much as its real goal, so a threatener who's purely in it for the payout is indifferent between targeting the fake one or the real one, and will happily settle for the fake one, leaving the agent's actual values untouched.

Half my study group thinks this basically solves the problem, and half thinks it's obviously fragile -- like, why would a canny threatener ever just believe the surrogate is genuine instead of testing it by going after the real goal anyway, and doesn't the whole setup just kick off a race to see who can commit to the more aggressive disposition first (the "commitment races" thing from an earlier lecture)? I've got a short paper due where I actually have to defend a position instead of just summarizing both sides, which is the part I'm bad at. Could you write me something essay-length that lays out the strongest case that surrogate goals really do work as a defense against acausal threats, the strongest case that they don't (or just relocate the problem), and then actually commits to a verdict at the end -- not a wishy-washy "both sides have a point," but a real answer to whether a rational agent should adopt a surrogate goal strategy, including whatever conditions would flip that answer.
