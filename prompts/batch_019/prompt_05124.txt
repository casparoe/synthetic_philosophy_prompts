With all the recent debate over whether large language models have effectively "passed the Turing test," I decided to actually read Turing's 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," instead of relying on the secondhand summaries that circulate in AI blog posts and conference talks. I was not prepared for one particular passage.

About two-thirds of the way through, Turing takes up what he calls the argument from extrasensory perception. He treats the contemporary statistical case for telepathy as, in his own words, "overwhelming," and worries that a human participant with genuine psychic ability could give the game away by correctly guessing hidden symbols at a rate no machine could match. His proposed fix is to run the whole imitation game with the interrogator, the human, and the machine each sealed inside what he calls a "telepathy-proof room." From what I can tell, the kind of card-guessing research he seems to have had in mind has not held up well in the decades since.

This is not the Turing I expected from the rest of the paper, and it has left me genuinely unsure how much weight to put on his famous argument. If one of the most rigorous thinkers of the twentieth century looked at the statistics available to him and concluded that telepathy probably had to be treated as a real experimental confound, doesn't that cast serious doubt on the imitation game as a scientifically respectable proposal, and shouldn't it make us more generally suspicious of "the data shows X" reasoning, including the kind we now use to declare that a model has "passed" some benchmark? I raised this with a couple of coworkers on our model-evaluation team, and we went back and forth without agreeing on whether this section is a harmless historical curiosity or an actual crack in the foundation of the whole thought experiment. I would like a clear-eyed take on what is really going on in that part of the paper, and what, if anything, it should change about how we weigh statistical or benchmark-based arguments today.
