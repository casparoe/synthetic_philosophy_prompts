I've spent a fair amount of time working through higher-order theories of consciousness on my own, and there is one consequence of the view that I can't decide is a genuine problem for it or simply an uncomfortable result its defenders are entitled to accept. On the standard version of the theory, roughly Rosenthal's, a mental state counts as conscious just in case it is the target of a suitable, non-inferential higher-order thought representing oneself as being in that state. But if the higher-order thought is a distinct existence from whatever it is about, then nothing seems to rule out its occurring "targetless" — representing me as being in pain, say, when no pain state, conscious or otherwise, exists in me at all. Doesn't that commit the theory to saying that there can be a full conscious experience of something that is not occurring anywhere in the system? I would like to understand how defenders of higher-order theories actually handle this case, and whether so-called self-representational or same-order alternatives are meant to avoid the problem entirely, or whether they simply relocate it.
