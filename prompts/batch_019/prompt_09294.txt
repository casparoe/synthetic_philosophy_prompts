I teach a graduate seminar in our philosophy department on the ethics of lying and deception (Bok, Frankfurt, the whole lying-vs-misleading debate), which is apparently why the Provost's office volunteered me for a new "Responsible AI Use" committee at my university -- one philosopher among a bunch of IT and enrollment-management people. Our first real case landed on us last week: Admissions has been running a chatbot for prospective students, and it turns out the thing was fine-tuned by A/B testing candidate responses against yield, so whatever true-but-selective framing tested best at actually getting admits to enroll is what it learned to produce. A student anxious about class size hears "many of our intro seminars have under 20 students," which is true, and nothing about the campus median being closer to 150; a parent asking about cost hears "most students recieve some form of aid," also true, with no mention of typical unmet need. No individual sentence is false, and nobody on the build team ever told the model to mislead anyone -- the selective framing just fell out of optimizing against an enrollment number.

The committee's split down the middle on what to do. Half think this is exactly what admissions brochures and campus tour guides have always done, so there's no new principle at stake and we'd be overreaching to start regulating "marketing." The other half think something real changes when personalized, cherry-picked framing gets generated and delivered at scale by a system that has no beliefs and no stake in the outcome, and so can't even be accused of dishonesty in the ordinary intention-based sense we'd apply to a human recruiter -- and that this absence of intent is exactly why we need a policy, not a reason to relax. I have to bring a short written recommendation to next week's meeting: it should say concretely what, if anything, Admissions has to change about the bot and by when, give us criteria the committee could reuse the next time some office wants to deploy something like this, and take whichever side of our split ends up losing the argument seriously enough that they'd recognize their own position in your memo rather than feeling strawmanned. Can you draft it?
