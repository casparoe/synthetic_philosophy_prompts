I have no formal training in statistics, computer science, or philosophy — I'm just someone who got interested in algorithmic decision-making after reading about the risk-assessment tools that American courts use at sentencing and parole hearings, and I've spent a fair amount of my spare time over the past year trying to work through the arguments properly rather than just pick a side. The case I keep coming back to is COMPAS, the recidivism-scoring tool built by a company called Northpointe, which ProPublica investigated back in 2016. Their headline finding was that among defendants who did not go on to reoffend, Black defendants were labeled "high risk" at a much higher rate than white defendants, while among those who did reoffend, white defendants were labeled "low risk" at a much higher rate than Black defendants.

What stopped me in my tracks was Northpointe's response, which I later found echoed in some actual academic papers (one by a researcher named Chouldechova, and another with Kleinberg and Mullainathan among the authors, if I have the names right). As I understand their argument, they proved something quite general: if a risk tool is calibrated so that a given score means roughly the same reoffense rate regardless of race, and if the two racial groups in the data have different overall base rates of reoffending, then the false-positive and false-negative rates across the groups cannot be made equal — not because of anything Northpointe did wrong, but as a matter of mathematical necessity. Several sources described this as an "impossibility result," which sounds to me like the kind of thing that settles an argument rather than continuing it.

So here is my actual question. Doesn't this mean that ProPublica's framing of COMPAS as a racially biased tool was, strictly speaking, a mistake — that what they were calling evidence of bias was really just the unavoidable arithmetic consequence of the two groups having different base rates, and that there isn't any meaningful sense left in which the tool, or Northpointe, did something unfair? I go back and forth about this with a friend who works in software, and I'd like to know whether the impossibility result really does settle things this way before I decide how much weight to put on it.
