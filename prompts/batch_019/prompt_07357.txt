Question: State Aumann's agreement theorem, being explicit about the role played by the common prior assumption. Then explain why the theorem does not require that the two agents share the same private information, and give an example of how relaxing the common prior assumption can produce a situation in which two Bayesian agents' posteriors for the same event are common knowledge and yet differ.

Student's answer: "Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two Bayesian agents share a common prior over the same state space and their posterior probabilities for some event E become common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal, even though the two agents may have based their updates on different private information. This is often summarized by the slogan that 'you cannot agree to disagree': if a colleague of mine and I both know each other's stated beliefs (and know that we know them, and so on), and we started out with the same prior, then any residual difference in our numbers cannot survive; the mere fact that we still disagree tells each of us that the other has evidence we don't, and updating on this pushes us together toward agreement.

Aumann's proof represents each agent's information as a partition of the state space, whose cells are the sets of states the agent cannot distinguish given her private information. Two agents can therefore have very different first-order information — one might have observed a coin flip and the other a die roll — and still, if their posteriors on E are common knowledge, those posteriors coincide. This is the sense in which the theorem doesn't require shared information: the agents only need common knowledge of each other's conclusions, not of the evidence behind them.

The common prior assumption itself, sometimes called the Harsanyi doctrine after Aumann's 1976 paper introducing it, holds that any two rational agents' probability judgments can differ only because they possess different information, never because they have different fundamental priors — so all differences in belief are ultimately attributable to differences in evidence, and nothing else. If we relax the assumption and let the two agents start from different priors, agreement can fail even under common knowledge of posteriors: each agent's posterior is a function of her own prior and her own likelihood update, and no amount of iterated mutual knowledge of the bottom-line numbers forces the priors themselves into line. For instance, an optimist and a pessimist forecasting a startup's chance of success might each update rationally on the same public information yet persist, by common knowledge, in different posteriors of 0.7 and 0.3, simply because they never shared a common prior to begin with."
