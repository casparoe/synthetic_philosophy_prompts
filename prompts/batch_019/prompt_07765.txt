I was recently invited onto my hospital's clinical ethics committee as its Islamic studies advisor, mostly on the strength of a dissertation chapter on al-Ghazali that I wrote years ago and hadn't thought about since. That chapter has suddenly become load-bearing. An elderly ICU patient is on ventilation and vasopressors with what the attending team considers a hopeless prognosis, and the family's own religious adviser, invoking al-Ghazali's occasionalism, argues that since God alone truly causes every event and what we ordinarily call "causes" -- the ventilator, the physician's hand -- are only the customary occasions God has habituated us to associate with their effects, withdrawing life support cannot count as causing the patient's death at all, and the committee should simply defer to whatever timeline the family prefers. Some members consider this a serious, doctrinally orthodox position we have no business overruling; others worry that, taken at face value, it would equally excuse a clinician who deliberately administered a lethal dose, since on this view no human act ever really causes anything. I need to circulate a short written opinion before we meet the family again next week. Please draft it: it should recommend a concrete course of action and timeframe for this patient, and it should engage the adviser's argument on its own philosophical terms rather than just falling back on the standard letting-die/killing distinction.
