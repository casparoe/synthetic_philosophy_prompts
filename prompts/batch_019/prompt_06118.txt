I retired five years ago after a thirty-six-year career running a low-temperature condensed-matter lab, most of it spent building and using SQUID-based devices, including, in later years, some experiments designed in the spirit of Leggett and Garg's tests of macroscopic quantum coherence. Throughout that career I used the quantum formalism every working day and never had to take a position on what it meant: decoherence was an enemy to be engineered away in the lab, not a philosophical puzzle to be resolved. Since retiring I've joined a small philosophy-of-physics reading group at the local university, and we've spent the last two months working through interpretations of quantum mechanics -- Bell's essays, Everett's original 1957 paper, Bohm's 1952 papers, and the more recent treatments by David Albert, Tim Maudlin, and Sean Carroll.

What has clarified things most for me is a way of framing the measurement problem that I hadn't encountered as a working physicist, and that I understand traces back to a paper of Maudlin's: the problem arises because three individually plausible claims cannot all be true together -- that the wave function is a complete description of a system, that it always evolves linearly in accordance with the Schrödinger equation, and that measurements have determinate outcomes. On this framing, Bohmian mechanics denies the first claim by adding particle positions as extra variables, Everettian quantum mechanics denies the third by letting outcomes branch, and Copenhagen, at least in the textbook von Neumann collapse-postulate form most physicists were actually taught (as opposed to whatever Bohr himself really meant), denies the second by positing an unexplained collapse. What bothers me is that once the choice is laid out this starkly, Copenhagen looks like the odd one out: it never specifies a dynamics for the collapse it needs, never says exactly when or why a "measurement" triggers one, and seems to keep its default status in physics curricula more through inertia than through having paid down its debts the way Bohm and Everett at least attempt to.

I'd like you to write me an essay that takes this trilemma as its starting point and argues its way to a real conclusion. Walk through exactly which of the three claims each of Copenhagen, Bohmian mechanics, and Everettian quantum mechanics gives up, and be concrete about the specific bill each then has to pay: for Copenhagen, the measurement/cut problem and the unexplained trigger for collapse; for Bohm, explicit nonlocality and the strained fit with relativistic field theory; for Everett, the status of probability -- the Born-rule problem, the decision-theoretic derivations attempted by Deutsch and Wallace, and the standard objections to them -- together with the preferred-basis question. Weigh these costs against one another and reach an actual verdict about which interpretation offers the most defensible resolution of the trilemma, including a direct answer to my suspicion above about whether Copenhagen's textbook dominance is earned or merely inherited. Please don't retreat to the observation that the interpretations are empirically equivalent and so it's all a matter of taste -- I want a specific ranking, defended against the strongest version of the alternatives.
