Here's the question the students got:

"Explain what Gödel's and Cohen's results together show about the status of the Continuum Hypothesis in ZFC. Does the independence of CH from ZFC mean that CH has no determinate truth value? Discuss what role, if any, large cardinal axioms play in settling the question."

And here's the student's answer:

"The Continuum Hypothesis (CH) states that there is no cardinality strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the reals, i.e. 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁. In 1938 Gödel showed that if ZFC is consistent, so is ZFC + CH, by building the constructible universe L, a minimal model of ZFC in which CH (and even GCH) holds - so ZFC cannot refute CH. In 1963 Cohen showed that if ZFC is consistent, so is ZFC + ¬CH, using his new method of forcing to extend a model of ZFC by adjoining many generic reals without collapsing ℵ₁, giving a model where the continuum is at least ℵ₂. Together, these results establish that CH is independent of ZFC - the axioms just don't settle it either way.

Since neither CH nor its negation can be derived from our best foundational theory, CH doesn't have a determinate truth value: it's simply true in some models of ZFC and false in others, and asking which one is "really" the case is a bit like asking whether the parallel postulate is really true of space. In this sense the independence result is basically the end of the story for CH as a mathematical question.

That said, set theorists haven't just dropped the matter - a lot of work has gone into finding new axioms that would settle it, and the main candidates are large cardinal axioms. Just as postulating measurable or supercompact cardinals has resolved other independence results, especially in descriptive set theory, most set theorists expect that large enough cardinals will eventually pin down the value of the continuum too, so the hunt for the right large cardinal axiom is really a hunt for the truth about CH."
