Ok so I've been falling down a philosophy of mind rabbit hole the last week or two, started innocently enough with the SEP page on mental causation (occupational hazard of being a software engineer with too many browser tabs open, I guess, especially with all the AI-consciousness chatter going around right now). Anyway I kept running into this "causal exclusion" argument against non-reductive physicalism -- Jaegwon Kim's argument that if the physical world is causally closed (every physical effect has a sufficent physical cause on its own) and mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, then mental properties don't seem to be doing any actual causal work, since the physical cause already covers it. Accepting systematic overdetermination everywhere seems like a cop-out, so the mental ends up looking screened off / excluded.

Here's the bit I can't shake though. I remember hitting a rebuttal somewhere (Ned Block maybe? don't hold me to that) that goes: doesn't this argument prove way too much? If mental properties are in trouble because they're "multiply realizable" and hence not identical to their physical base, isn't basically every macro-level property multiply realizable in that same sense -- like a rock breaking a window, or a key fitting a lock, can be realized by a ton of different exact microphysical arrangements. So if exclusion is a real problem for mentality specifically, it seems like it should also wreck ordinary macroscopic causation across geology, biology, even most of engineering, with "causal powers" draining all the way down to fundamental particles. My coworker who's way more into this than me says that's obviously a reductio and the argument must be broken somewhere, but he couldn't actually tell me where. Kim's own move, if I'm getting it right, is to say most mental properties can be functionally reduced (defined by their causal role) so they're not really distinct from the physical after all -- except qualia, which he just bites the bullet on and calls epiphenomenal.

Could you walk me through whether that generalization objection actually works, and whether Kim's functional-reduction fix blocks it for ordinary mental states (beliefs, desires, etc.) or just relocates the same problem the rock-and-window case has? I'd also love it if you could explain why multiple realizability is supposed to specifically generate a causal exclusion problem in the first place, rather than just being some separate, unrelated fact about mental properties -- that's honestly the part where my intuition keeps falling apart.
