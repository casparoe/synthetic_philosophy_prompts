I've spent years following the LessWrong-adjacent literature on Kelly betting and what Scott Garrabrant termed 'geometric rationality' -- the line of argument, running from Kelly's original 1956 paper through Ole Peters' ergodicity economics, that an agent facing repeated multiplicative stakes should maximize expected log-wealth rather than expected wealth, since once ruin is possible the ensemble average over many parallel agents stops predicting what happens to any single agent's wealth over time. I'd like a short story in the tradition of hard-SF philosophical fiction (Greg Egan and early Asimov are the right register), built around exactly this divergence between the ensemble average and the time average. It shouldn't be stated as a moral: the divergence should be load-bearing in the plot itself, so that something real turns on a character, institution, or civilization wagering the arithmetically 'optimal' fraction of its resources and getting precisely the outcome the mathematics predicts. I'd rather it not be set at a card table or a racetrack -- Kelly's own setting -- so find a context where the same structure is doing something less obvious. No character should explain the Kelly criterion or ergodicity out loud; the reader should be able to feel it happening. Aim for something in the 800-1200 word range.
