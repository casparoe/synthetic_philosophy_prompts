"Rawls and Hart both think the mistake in the traditional debate is to ask 'is punishment justified by its consequences or by desert?' as if there were only one question to answer. Rawls's distinction, from 'Two Concepts of Rules,' is between the practice as a whole and a particular action falling under that practice. On his view, a practice like the legal system of punishment is set up and maintained because it is useful — it deters crime and protects the public, which is a broadly utilitarian justification. But once the practice exists, a particular judge deciding a particular case does not re-run the utilitarian calculation; she just applies the rule: this defendant broke the law, so he is liable to punishment. The justification of the individual act is therefore internal to the rules, not a fresh cost-benefit analysis.

Rawls uses this distinction to answer the stock objection that utilitarianism would allow punishing an innocent person if it produced enough social benefit (for instance, executing a scapegoat to calm down a community after a crime). He gives this maneuver its own name, 'telishment': because inflicting suffering on the scapegoat still produces the same deterrent payoff as genuine punishment, the rule-utilitarian is entitled to authorize it under this label as long as it is officially built into the practice rather than performed as a one-off secret exception. In other words, telishment is Rawls's way of showing that the utilitarian practice-level justification extends smoothly to this case too, just under a different name.

Hart's distinction in the 'Prolegomenon' is closely related: he separates the General Justifying Aim of punishment, which he thinks is utilitarian (crime prevention), from questions of Distribution. Distribution, for Hart, is about how the benefits that punishment produces — deterrence, public reassurance — get spread across society as a whole, rather than concentrated on any one case, which is again a fundamentally utilitarian idea dressed up in different vocabulary.

Ultimately, I think Rawls and Hart are doing the same thing in different vocabulary: both notice that 'why do we have this practice?' and 'why do this to this person?' are different questions, and both think the first is consequentialist. Hart is just restating Rawls's practice/act distinction using the words 'aim' and 'distribution' instead."
