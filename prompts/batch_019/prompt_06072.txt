I've recently been reading Douglas Jesseph's history of the long-running dispute between Thomas Hobbes and the Oxford mathematician John Wallis over Hobbes's repeated, and ultimately always mistaken, attempts to square the circle. Until now I'd only known Hobbes as the Leviathan-and-"nasty, brutish, and short" political philosopher, so it's been interesting to learn that he regarded geometry, not politics, as the one genuinely rigorous science available to human beings, and that he remained convinced of his own flawed proofs for roughly the last quarter-century of his life even as Wallis kept publicly picking them apart.

That background sent me back to the source text, where I found the following passage from Leviathan (Chapter 4, "Of Speech"), which I'm hoping you can help me unpack:

"Seeing then that truth consisteth in the right ordering of names in our affirmations, a man that seeketh precise truth had need to remember what every name he uses stands for, and to place it accordingly; or else he will find himself entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twigs; the more he struggles, the more belimed. And therefore in geometry, which is the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind, men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations they call definitions, and place them in the beginning of their reckoning."

As a software engineer, the idea that everything downstream depends on nailing down your definitions up front isn't unfamiliar — it's basically the pitch for strong typing or a well-specified API contract. But I don't think Hobbes is just saying "be careful with your terms." He seems to be making a much stronger claim: that this is specifically what makes geometry certain in a way that no other discipline manages. Could you help me spell out that argument more precisely — is the epistemic work being done by definitions as such, or by something deeper about geometrical objects being things we construct ourselves rather than find in nature? And separately, is there a reading of this passage, or of Hobbes's related idea that reasoning is just "reckoning" with names, that would explain why he stayed so unmovable throughout the Wallis affair — that is, did his own theory of what makes a demonstration valid give him principled (if ultimately wrong) grounds for trusting his circle-squaring, rather than this just being plain stubbornness?
