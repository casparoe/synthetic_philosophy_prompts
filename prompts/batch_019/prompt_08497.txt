I've been reading about AI safety via debate as a proposal for scalable oversight, and, as a backend engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I tried writing up my own take on it using analogies from parts of CS I actually know well: game-tree search and complexity theory. I'd like your comments on the draft below — specifically on whether I'm using the technical analogies correctly, and on whether the worry I raise at the end is a real problem with the scheme or one I'm overstating.

Draft:

The basic pitch of debate, as I understand it, is that verifying an argument is often much cheaper than generating one, so if you pit two capable models against each other and make them attack each other's reasoning, a judge who couldn't have solved the original problem alone can still reliably reward the honest debater, because the dishonest one eventually has to defend indefensible ground. The original paper, if I recall right, frames this in complexity-theoretic terms: an unaided model whose answer a judge can check against a short certificate is playing roughly the role of NP, while optimal debate, because the judge only ever has to adjudicate one localized disagreement rather than a whole argument, is supposed to reach all the way up to PSPACE — the same jump you get going from static proofs to interactive ones. If I've got that right, it's a striking claim: it says the format of the interaction, not the judge's own competence, is what expands the range of questions you can trust the answer to.

What bothers me is a pattern I recognize from a very different context: writing toy game-playing programs. A fixed-depth minimax search suffers from the horizon effect, where an inevitable bad outcome just gets deferred past the point the engine bothers to look, so the position at the search boundary looks fine even though it's already lost. I've since read about something called the "obfuscated arguments problem" in the debate literature, where a dishonest debater can bury its real error inside a large tree of plausible-looking sub-claims, built so that no single branch looks more suspicious than the others, leaving the honest debater with nowhere obviously better to recurse into. That reads to me like a horizon effect for human judges: the error hasn't been removed, just pushed to a depth the judge can't afford to search.

The fix I came across — letting debaters cross-examine frozen copies of each other — seems to me like it mainly buys you something like iterative deepening: more effective search depth before you run out of budget, not an escape from the horizon itself. Which leaves me suspicious that the obfuscated arguments problem isn't really a fixable implementation bug, but a standing tax on the whole approach that scales with the gap between the debaters' ability and the judge's — the same way a strong-enough engine can still hide a trap from a weaker one, however many plies of iterative deepening you throw at it.

Where do you think this analogy holds up, and where might it mislead me? I'd especially like to know whether the PSPACE claim is something I'm garbling, and whether there's a real answer to my closing worry that I'm simply not aware of.
