A friend of mine and I spent an entire dinner arguing about a case they kept pushing on me: suppose I promise to help you move apartments on Saturday, and then back out at the last minute — but I hire and pay for professional movers on your behalf, cover whatever costs the delay causes, and make sure you end up at least as well off as if I had shown up myself. My friend insisted that our shared intuition that I have still wronged you shows that whatever grounds the duty to keep a promise cannot be reducible to protecting your reliance or fulfilling the expectations I invited you to form, since those are precisely what I have neutralized. I spent the rest of the evening trying to rescue some version of an expectation-based story and never quite managed it, which sent me back into the literature on why promises bind.

As it happens, the philosophy society I belong to has invited a speaker next month who has built much of her research program around defending and extending a Scanlon-style, expectation-based account of promissory obligation: the view that breaking a promise wrongs the person you promised specifically because you deliberately led them to form and rely on an expectation, and that defeating that expectation without justification treats them in a way they could reasonably object to, without needing to presuppose any prior social convention of promise-keeping. Whenever a case like my friend's comes up in the Q&A after her talks, she has a practiced comeback: "that just shows we need a supplementary principle, not that Fidelity is the wrong principle." I would rather not let her deploy that line once and move on to the next question.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this Q&A that make that comeback progressively harder to lean on? I would like at least one question built around a compensation case like my friend's, pressing on whether repeatedly patching the account with supplementary principles starts to look ad hoc rather than principled; at least one question raising the objection, associated with Kolodny and Wallace, that any appeal to which expectations are "reasonable" quietly reintroduces the very social practice of promising that the account was supposed to explain without presupposing; and at least one question asking why the person you promised, specifically, rather than any bystander who happens to form and rely on that very same expectation, is the one with standing to release you from the obligation. For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so the exchange can go a round deeper instead of stalling on her first response.
