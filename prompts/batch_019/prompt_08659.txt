I was naturalized as a citizen six months ago and cast my first vote this past spring -- an experience that sent me back, for my dissertation on early modern theories of political inclusion, to the closing pages of Spinoza's Tractatus Politicus. The manuscript breaks off mid-argument in Chapter Eleven, on democracy, at the point where Spinoza contends that women cannot rightly hold a share in government because they do not possess a power (potentia) equal to men's -- an inference he draws simply from the fact that history affords no example of a state in which men and women have ruled as equals. Most of the commentary I've found treats this as an inconsistency Spinoza could have dropped at no cost to his system, or a lapse into the prejudice of his age. I'm not convinced it's so easily excised. Please construct the strongest argument you can that the exclusion of women follows from, rather than betrays, the core commitments of Spinoza's political naturalism -- in particular his identification of right with power, his rejection of Aristotelian final causes, and his account of the imagination and the affects -- and explain why that same reasoning would not equally require excluding the many men whose power is plainly unequal to other men's.
