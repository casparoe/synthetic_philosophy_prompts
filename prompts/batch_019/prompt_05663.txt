I'm putting together a longer piece on decision theory thought experiments that mess with people's intuitions, and I've gone a bit down the rabbit hole on "Death in Damascus" — the one where Death is basically a perfect predictor, and no matter which city the guy flees to, Death is already there waiting for him. A decision-theorist friend explained that in the standard version, neither "stay in Damascus" nor "run to Aleppo" ends up ratifiable: the moment you actually commit to one, and update your beliefs about where Death is based on having committed, you immediately want to switch to the other. That flip-flop is apparently what makes the case such a headache for causal decision theory specifically, even though CDT is sort of "its" theory.

What I can't quite get my head around, and what I'd love help with for the piece, is this: can you invent a totally new, mundane scenario — no grim reaper, nothing supernatural, just some ordinary person choosing between two options — that has that exact same self-undermining structure? I want some other feature of the world (a rival, a forecaster, an algorithm, whatever fits naturally) whose relevant state is strongly correlated with whatever the person actually ends up choosing, but not caused by that choice in any straightforward sense. Then, using the specific details of your own example rather then just restating Damascus, walk me through why committing to either option makes the person want to un-commit as soon as they factor in what that commitment implies about the state of the world.

One more thing for a sidebar I'm drafting: I've seen it claimed that just flipping a coin between the two options rescues rationality here, since a "perfect" predictor supposedly can't out-guess genuine randomness — but I've also seen that dismissed as not a real fix at all. Which side is closer to right, and why? My editor hates it when I just write "aaand that's a paradox, folks" and move on, so this time I'd actually like to understand the mechanics well enough to explain them.
