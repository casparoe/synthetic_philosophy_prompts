I am preparing a session on Locke for a seminar on personal identity, and I want to push his account into territory he could only reach by hypothesis. Locke locates personal identity in continuity of consciousness rather than sameness of body or soul, and he explicitly separates "man" from "person": he holds that if a prince's consciousness were somehow transferred into a cobbler's body, the person would go with the consciousness rather than the flesh, and, more strikingly, that two non-communicating consciousnesses alternating control of a single body -- one by day, one by night -- would count, on his own criterion, as two persons rather than one. He never had an actual case to test this against. Split-brain patients come close, though not quite in the same shape: severing the corpus callosum leaves each hemisphere able to perceive, choose, and act independently and simultaneously, sometimes so that the two hands work at cross purposes while the speech-dominant hemisphere confidently invents reasons for behavior the other hemisphere actually caused (later work complicates how total the separation really is, but the basic dissociation is well established). Reasoning strictly from Locke's own stated criterion, rather than our post-split-brain intuitions, what do you think he would say about such a patient -- one person, two persons sharing a single man, or a case his apparatus cannot sort cleanly? And which of his own commitments would you expect him to abandon first in order to reach an answer?
