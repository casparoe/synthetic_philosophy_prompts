At our monthly game night we take turns picking the restaurant, and one friend's choices have always followed an odd pattern: offered Thai versus Mexican, he picks Mexican; offered Mexican versus sushi, he picks sushi; but offered sushi versus Thai, he picks Thai. Last week another friend noticed this and, as a joke, started him off with a Thai reservation and then walked him around the cycle -- swapping it for Mexican plus a dollar, then for sushi plus a dollar, then back for Thai plus a dollar -- and he took every trade, ending up back where he started, three dollars poorer. She said this proves his preferences aren't rational, since a rational person can't be walked around in a circle and charged for the privilege. He said that's unfair: now that he's seen the trick, he'd simply decline the next offer, so nothing forces him to keep losing money -- it only works once, by surprise. She said that if he'd decline a trade he'd otherwise want, purely because he recognizes the pattern, then he isn't acting on his real preferences anymore, which is its own kind of inconsistency. He said there's nothing wrong with deciding in advance not to take a deal you'd otherwise want in the moment -- that's just the same self-control a dieter uses to turn down dessert -- and it doesn't prove the preference wasn't genuine. She said a preference that never survives contact with an actual choice isn't a preference, just a feeling, and that he's hiding behind a word. Neither budged. Could you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall, and flag anything important that both of them are missing?
