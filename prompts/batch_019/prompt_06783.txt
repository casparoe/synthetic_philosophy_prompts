My doctoral supervisor retires next month after nearly forty years reconstructing Newton's hidden theology: the case against the Trinity, and against Athanasius's honesty, that Newton wrote out in full but never dared publish, having kept his fellowship at Trinity College only by securing a rare 1675 dispensation from ordination. The manuscripts stayed with his family until a 1936 Sotheby's sale split them between Keynes and the Orientalist Abraham Yahuda, and she has spent her career reassembling Yahuda's share. I've been asked to toast her at the farewell dinner, where half the room will know exactly what I mean if I say 'Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture,' and the other half is family and former students who know her only as the woman who taught them that Newton discovered gravity and stop there. Write me a five-minute toast built on the irony that she has spent a career publishing, patiently and in public, exactly what Newton spent his life hiding -- one that lands as affection for her rather than a joke at Newton's expense or a tutorial on fourth-century Christology, and that gives both halves of the room something to laugh at and something true to take home.
