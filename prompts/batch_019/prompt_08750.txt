I've been rereading old rationalist-blogosphere arguments about Bayesian versus frequentist statistics, and there's one puzzle from that debate that I've only ever seen asserted, never actually walked through. The Bayesian side likes to point out that, by the Likelihood Principle, a posterior depends only on the data you actually observed and not on the stopping rule that produced it — so a Bayesian supposedly never needs anything like the alpha-spending corrections frequentists build into sequential trials, and "peeking" at accumulating data (the kind of undisclosed flexibility that Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn blamed for a good deal of the replication crisis) isn't a problem for them at all. But that seems to run straight into the old "sampling to a foregone conclusion" objection that, if I'm remembering the history right, Armitage raised against the Savage–Birnbaum defense of the likelihood principle around the time of Birnbaum's 1962 paper: if stopping rules are truly irrelevant, why couldn't an experimenter just keep sampling under a cleverly chosen adaptive rule until a Bayesian's posterior is pushed as close to certainty as desired in a hypothesis that happens to be false?

What I'd like is a mechanistic answer to that, rather than the usual one-liner that "the likelihood principle handles it." My understanding is that the real resolution turns on the sequence of posterior odds being a martingale under the true hypothesis, so that something like Ville's inequality bounds how likely it ever is for a Bayesian's credence in the truth to sink below some threshold — essentially the measure-theoretic cousin of what the Sequences call "conservation of expected evidence." What I don't understand is why the analogous frequentist quantity lacks this property, so that a p-value really can be driven below 0.05 with near-certainty by a patient enough optional-stopping design, while the Bayesian's exposure to being badly wrong stays bounded no matter how an adversary times the stopping. I'd love an actual walkthrough of the mechanics behind that asymmetry — a toy example with a running likelihood ratio on coin flips would be ideal — rather than another restatement of "Bayesians condition on the data, not the design."
