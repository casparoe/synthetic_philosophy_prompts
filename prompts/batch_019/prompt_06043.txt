Ok, this is a slightly odd ask but bear with me. I write the science-and-ideas column at a mid-size outlet, and I somehow got roped into our regional press council -- a volunteer body that hears reader complaints against member newsrooms and issues rulings that aren't legally binding but that outlets mostly comply with anyway because nobody wants the black eye. There's a case on our docket that a few of us have literally been arguing about at the bar after work: a member paper ran a short item about a small business owner who screamed at a teenage barista over a mispoured latte, naming him and embedding a customer's 45-second phone video. Everything in the piece was true. Then it went everywhere -- he got doxxed, his home address got posted, his business got review-bombed into the ground, and he lost his lease within a month. Half the council says the paper reported one accurate, mildly newsworthy fact and isn't on the hook for what an anonymous online crowd did with it afterwards; the other half says knowingly feeding a private citizen's name into that machine makes the outlet complicit in what followed.

I've been reading Kierkegaard's "Two Ages" for a column on internet pile-ons, and his stuff on "the public" and "leveling" -- this notion that the press conjures into being an anonymous, unaccountable audience that judges everyone while being nobody in particular -- plus what happened to him personally with the Corsair, feels like it was built for exactly this fight. I don't want a memo that just name-drops him and moves on, though; I want his actual argument doing real work. Could you draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the council before next week's vote? Give one concrete recommendation (uphold or dismiss the complaint, whether the paper should scrub identifying details from the archived version, run a correction or apology, some going-forward policy for these "viral private citizen" stories -- whatever you think is defensible, with a real deadline attached), and actually engage the "we just reported a true fact" side seriously rather then waving it off, ideally in terms Kierkegaard himself would recognize rather than generic media-ethics language.
