A friend and I spent much of a long dinner arguing about the "Death in Damascus" case from decision theory -- the one where a man encounters Death in a Damascus marketplace and flees to Aleppo, which decision theorists have since turned into a puzzle about prediction and choice. My position was that Death in Damascus is, at bottom, just a relabeled Newcomb's problem: in both stories a highly reliable predictor has already fixed some state of the world -- the contents of the boxes, or the city to which Death travels -- before the agent chooses, and in neither story can the agent's choice reach back and causally alter that state. Since causal decision theory tells you to two-box in Newcomb's problem and to stand by that verdict no matter how strongly two-boxing predicts an empty opaque box, I argued that it should just as confidently tell you to flee to Aleppo in the Damascus case, setting aside the fact that fleeing is itself evidence that Death has gone to Aleppo too.

My friend thought this was mistaken, and maintained that Death in Damascus is actually the standard example philosophers reach for to show causal decision theory running into trouble precisely where Newcomb's problem does not. She kept invoking "ratifiability," insisting that, unlike two-boxing, neither staying nor fleeing counts as a stable choice once one factors in how becoming confident in a plan shifts one's credence about where Death has gone. I could tell she was not simply inventing this, but I could not reconstruct why the two cases should come apart, given that the correlation-without-causation structure looked identical to me in both. Could you settle which of us has the better view, and explain -- in enough mechanical detail that the point actually lands -- whether and why Death in Damascus is disanalogous to Newcomb's problem for a causal decision theorist? And if my "relabeled Newcomb's problem" picture is wrong, I would like to understand exactly where it breaks down.
