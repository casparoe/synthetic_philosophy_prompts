I'm about two-thirds into a novel set during a wildfire evacuation, and there's a pivotal scene where the last rescue helicopter has fuel for exactly one more run. The pilot has to chose between a ridge with a single trapped ranger and a ridge with six stranded hikers -- no way to do both, no time to think it through. While I was trying to figure out how someone might actually defend NOT just automatically going for the six, a friend doing his ethics PhD sent me down a rabbit hole with Taurek's old "Should the Numbers Count?" paper, where he argues you can't just add the extra lives into one big total loss, since there's no single person for whom losing six is six times worse than losing one -- and that flipping a coin might honestly be more defensible than reflexively saving the bigger group. People keep telling me this connects to the whole "separateness of persons" idea too, though I'm fuzzy on how much work that's actually doing.

My publisher wants a short taped conversation to release alongside the book, and they've found me a moral philosopher who genuinely holds something like Taurek's view to talk it through with me. I'm doing the interviewing and I really don't want to just let her restate the position for twenty minutes. Could you draft seven or eight questions that actually press on it -- I want at least one that makes her deal with Parfit's "Innumerate Ethics" reply head on rather than just dismissing it, one on whether the coin-flip proposal is a genuine principled answer or just a polite way of refusing to choose, one that tests whether the view survives once the numbers get really lopsided (say, one person against a thousand, not one against six), and one that pushes on whether "separateness of persons" is doing real justificatory work here or is just a label that sounds rigorous. For each question, add a quick follow-up keyed to the dodge or answer she's most likely to reach for. And end with one question where I make her actually apply her own view to my helicopter scene, on the record, no hedging.
