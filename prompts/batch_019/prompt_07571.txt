I sit on the bylaws and elections committee of a professional association with roughly 2,400 members, which currently elects its executive council and president each year by simple plurality vote among the nominated candidates. Last spring's presidential race had four candidates on the ballot; the winner was elected with 29% of first-preference support, and an informal post-election survey suggested that a clear majority of members would have preferred either of the two next-highest finishers to her in a head-to-head comparison. The committee has been asked to propose a replacement procedure before nominations open for the next cycle, and we are stuck. One faction wants to move to instant-runoff voting; another favors a Borda count; a third member argues that since Arrow's impossibility theorem shows no voting rule can satisfy every reasonable fairness condition at once, the choice among systems is arbitrary and we should just keep plurality rather than pretend some alternative is more legitimate; and our newest member is pushing hard for approval voting, on the grounds that because it doesn't aggregate ranked preferences at all, Arrow's theorem "doesn't even apply to it" and this settles the matter in its favor.

I need to circulate a short decision memo to the full committee before next month's meeting, recommending one specific procedure (one of the four above, some hybrid, or a different option if you think it serves us better) along with the reasoning behind it. Most of my colleagues have no background in social choice theory, so the memo should explain, in terms they can actually follow, what Arrow's theorem does and does not imply for a decision like ours -- correcting both the "it's all arbitrary, so don't bother" reading and the "this method is exempt, so it must be the fair one" reading -- without turning into a lecture on formal voting theory. It should close with a concrete recommendation, a realistic timeline for amending the bylaws before the next election cycle, and a candid paragraph on the strongest objection to whichever system you end up recommending, so the committee isn't blindsided by it at the meeting.
