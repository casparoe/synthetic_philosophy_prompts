I'm a retired neuroscientist who spent thirty years running a lab that hunted, electrode by electrode, for something like a "grandmother cell," and I was exactly the kind of skeptic who thought the idea — coined half in jest to begin with — deserved to stay a joke, even after "concept cells" tuned to a single person's identity across wildly different pictures of them, and sometimes even the printed name, turned up in real human recordings. Only since retiring have I started reading the philosophy that was apparently litigating all of this the whole time, under headings like realism versus instrumentalism about mental representation, and I've grown attached to Dennett's suggestion that a pattern can be "real" without being written into the physics at the bottom — real enough that I'd like a speech to gesture at that middle ground rather than force a choice between "it's really there" and "it's just a convenient story we tell." Next month I've agreed to give the ten-minute opening remarks at a small closed workshop pairing mechanistic-interpretability researchers, who are busy finding and naming "features" inside language models (one lab has already made something of a celebrity of its Golden Gate Bridge feature), with philosophers who want to ask whether that naming is discovery or projection. As host I don't think it's my place to tell either room who is right, and I'm wary of the easy move that lets everyone off the hook by calling the whole dispute merely verbal; thirty years of watching my own field fail to close the analogous question is exactly why I don't believe it is. Please write those opening remarks, pitched so the engineers can't file the question under "not my problem" and the philosophers can't file the workshop under "not real philosophy."
