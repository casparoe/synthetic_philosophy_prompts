Exam question: Marx distinguishes the alienation of the worker, developed above all in the 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts, from the exploitation of the worker, developed in Capital. (a) Explain the theoretical basis of each concept. (b) Argue for or against the claim that these are conceptually independent forms of harm — that a worker could in principle be exploited without being alienated, or alienated without being exploited — illustrating each direction with an example.

Student's answer: Alienation and exploitation name two different injuries capitalism does to the worker, and they belong to two different moments in Marx's thought. Alienation is the concept of the young, still Hegelian and Feuerbachian Marx of 1844: he argues that under private property and wage labour the worker is estranged in four ways — from the product of his labour, which confronts him as an alien power; from the activity of production itself, which is forced rather than freely chosen; from his own species-being, the free and conscious productive activity that distinguishes human beings from animals (a notion Marx takes over from Feuerbach's account of the human essence in The Essence of Christianity without modification); and from other human beings, including the capitalist. Exploitation, by contrast, belongs to what Althusser called the mature, scientific Marx of Capital, who had by then broken with this earlier humanist vocabulary: for the mature Marx, exploitation is simply the fact that the capitalist pays the worker a wage worth less than the value of the labour the worker performs, and pockets the difference as surplus value.

Because these two concepts have different theoretical bases, they are also independent. A well-paid senior engineer at a firm she does not own is still exploited, in Marx's technical sense, but she may not be alienated at all if she finds the work absorbing and controls how she does it. Conversely, a self-employed potter who owns her wheel and kiln outright is not exploited by anyone, yet she may still be alienated: if she can only sell what the market wants rather than what expresses her, she is cut off from her product and her own creative powers just as surely as a factory hand would be.
