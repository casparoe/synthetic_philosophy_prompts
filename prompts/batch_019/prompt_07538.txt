I'm developing a question for a graduate seminar on early modern probability and decision theory, and I'd like to test it on you before I put it to my students.

Pascal, with Fermat, effectively founded mathematical probability in their 1654 correspondence on the problem of points, so his Wager is not just theology dressed up as decision theory. It trades on a specific feature: because the promised good is strictly infinite, even an arbitrarily small, non-zero credence in God's existence yields a dominant expectation, and no finite improbability can outweigh it.

Now suppose Pascal meets Nick Bostrom's "Pascal's Mugging": a stranger with nothing but his own word claims he can grant or inflict some almost unimaginable but strictly finite good or harm, demands a trivial payment now, and can always name a larger quantity to outweigh however small a credence Pascal gives him.

Does Pascal's own reasoning commit him to paying up, or can he refuse the mugger while keeping the Wager — say, by insisting his argument turned on the finite/infinite distinction rather than on "sufficiently large" finite stakes? Give his most defensible position, and say plainly which of his actual commitments, theological or mathematical, is doing the work.
