Since retiring from a career in experimental physics some four years ago, I have been spending my mornings working through texts in philosophy that I never had time for while still running a lab, and I have recently become rather absorbed, of all things, in the philosophy of religion. My current object of fascination is Luis de Molina's doctrine of middle knowledge, the centerpiece of the view generally called Molinism: the idea that, logically prior to any decree to create, God knows the truth-value of every counterfactual of creaturely freedom — what any possible free creature would do in any circumstance in which it might be placed — and uses this "middle" knowledge (situated between his knowledge of the merely possible and his knowledge of the actual) to decide, providentially, which world to bring about, all without overriding anyone's libertarian freedom. The standard difficulty here, pressed by Robert Adams and later sharpened by others such as William Hasker, is the so-called grounding objection: since the creature in question does not yet exist, God has not yet decreed anything, and nothing is supposed to causally necessitate a free choice, there seems to be no fact of the matter, and nothing to ground one, that could make these counterfactuals true rather than false (or vice versa) prior to creation.

Reading through this literature, I kept being reminded of an old preoccupation from my own field: the status of "counterfactual definiteness" in discussions of Bell's theorem and the EPR paradox. There too one is dealing with a robust counterfactual claim — that an unperformed measurement would have yielded some definite outcome had it been performed — and there too critics have asked what exactly could ground that claim, given that the experimentally confirmed violations of Bell's inequalities seem to leave us with a choice between abandoning locality and abandoning any such determinacy.

I would be grateful if you could compare these two debates in some depth. Do the grounding objection to Molinist middle knowledge and the disputes surrounding counterfactual definiteness in quantum mechanics reflect a genuinely shared puzzle about what, in general, could ground the truth of a substantive counterfactual concerning an event that never actually occurs, or do the disanalogies between libertarian agent-causation and physical indeterminism (among others) run deep enough that the resemblance is merely superficial? I would also like to know which of the two puzzles strikes you as the more tractable, and why — please commit to a view rather than simply setting out both sides.
