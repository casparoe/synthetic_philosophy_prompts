I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the metaphysics of representation as it bears on contemporary machine learning, and I want to pressure-test my own view about which strands of interpretability evidence actually support realism about representations in neural networks, rather than being equally compatible with an instrumentalist or fictionalist reading of talk about "features." Consider five kinds of evidence researchers have offered when attributing a representation to a trained network: (a) high decoding accuracy from a linear probe trained on hidden activations; (b) a "monosemantic" feature recovered by a sparse autoencoder that reliably fires on a narrow, human-describable class of inputs; (c) a causal intervention (e.g., activation patching) on a hypothesized feature that shifts downstream behavior in the predicted direction; (d) a fully reverse-engineered multi-component circuit (such as an induction head) whose parts each admit an independent functional description; and (e) purely behavioral evidence of systematic, compositional generalization, with no internal analysis at all. Taking Ramsey's "job description challenge" as the bar a state must clear to count as a genuine representation (roughly: it must be consumed downstream in virtue of its content, not merely correlated with what it represents), rank these five evidence types from strongest to weakest support for representational realism. Please give your answer as a JSON object with keys "S", "A", "B", "C", and "D" (S = strongest support, D = weakest), each mapped to a list containing the letter(s) of the corresponding evidence type(s), allowing ties.
