I've spent a long time reading rationalist and AI-safety blogs, and one argument I keep running into is the money-pump case for "coherence": Eliezer Yudkowsky's argument, most explicitly in the piece "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities," that any sufficiently capable agent will end up with consistent, expected-utility-like preferences, on pain of being exploited by a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off by its own lights. Wanting to see how professional decision theorists have engaged with this style of argument, I came across Edward McClennen's notion of "resolute choice": the claim that an agent with cyclical, or otherwise "incoherent," preferences can avoid ever being pumped without revising those preferences at all, simply by committing in advance to a policy and sticking to it even at choice points where a narrower, in-the-moment re-evaluation would recommend defecting from the plan.

I raised this in a reading group a few weeks ago, and the discussion mostly talked past itself: the coherence side kept re-describing resolute choice as "refusing free money for no reason," while the resolute-choice side kept re-describing the money-pump argument as "assuming away the very question at issue." To force both sides to engage with a concrete case, consider an agent choosing among three apartments, A, B, and C, that trade off rent against commute time such that, all things considered, the agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and is willing to pay a small fee to trade up at each step. A pump can then be run by first offering A, then C for a fee, then B for a fee, then back to A for a fee, indefinitely. The resolute-choice reply is that, before the first offer is even made, the agent can adopt a policy that includes declining at least one of these trades, and that sticking to this policy later — even at the moment when the single trade on offer looks, taken on its own, like a strict improvement — is not weakness of will but the very thing diachronic rationality consists in.

Could you write a dialogue of roughly 700 to 1,000 words between two decision theorists arguing out exactly this case? One should press the money-pump line: that whatever the resolute-choice theorist calls it, declining a trade that is a strict improvement by the agent's own current preferences just is irrational, and that "resoluteness" either smuggles in transitive preferences over entire plans — in which case the cyclical preferences were never doing any real work — or it is simply irrationality wearing a more flattering name. The other should defend resolute choice against exactly that dilemma, and should also take on the further worry that a resolute agent can still be pumped if the trades are sprung on it one at a time, unannounced, rather than offered as a package that can be evaluated and committed to in advance. Please make sure each side directly engages with the other's strongest point rather than restating their own position past it — that is exactly what my reading group failed to do.
