For my seminar on naturalizing intentionality, I'd like a dialogue between two philosophers of mind: one defending Millikan's teleosemantics, on which a mental state's content is fixed by the proper function it was selected to perform, and one defending Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory, which fixes content through purely synchronic causal-counterfactual relations rather than any appeal to selection history. Start them off on the frog that snaps at flies and at BB pellets shot past its face: the Fodorian should press the disjunction problem, asking what, on the teleosemantic view, pins down 'fly' as the content rather than 'small dark moving thing' or whatever else flies and pellets have in common, and should meet the standard reply — that the content is whatever explains the fitness benefit — with the worry that selection is too coarse-grained to favor one such description over the others. Then have the Fodorian bring up Swampman: lacking any selection history, he would seem, on teleosemantics, to have no mental content whatsoever, despite being a perfect physical duplicate of an ordinary thinker. I want the teleosemanticist to actually answer this rather than concede, and to press back on whether the Fodorian's own theory can distinguish genuine misrepresentation from merely disjunctive content without covertly reintroducing teleology. I don't want either side to come out a clean winner.
