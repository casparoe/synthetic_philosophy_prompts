Been chewing on the self-undermining objection to conciliationism about peer disagreement and want to get your take before I turn this into a proper essay. Here's the rough argument, in notes form:

Conciliationism (the equal weight view, à la Elga and Christensen) says that when you learn an epistemic peer disagrees with you about P, you should move your credence substantially toward theirs, often close to splitting the difference. Fine so far. But conciliationism is itself a contested first-order philosophical thesis, and plenty of smart, well-informed epistemologists (Kelly and steadfasters generally) reject it. So by conciliationism's own lights, learning about *that* disagreement should knock down my confidence in conciliationism itself. And if my confidence in the policy drops, I no longer have much standing to actually conciliate on object-level disputes, since the rule telling me to do so is itself something I'm now unsure of. Seems like the view eats it's own tail.

I recall Elga has some response to this - roughly, that you're allowed to be "immodest" about your own credence-forming policy even while being conciliatory about pretty much everything downstream of it, kind of like how you can't use modus ponens to argue yourself out of modus ponens. But this feels like a dodge to me: it just relocates the bedrock, unrevisable commitment that steadfastness got criticized for needing in the first place. If conciliationists get to bootstrap out their core policy like that, i don't see why steadfasters can't do the same move for their own controversial first-order commitments (moral, religious, whatever) - just insist peer disagreement doesn't touch the starting points.

Second, smaller gripe: a lot of the intuitive force in this literature comes from Christensen's restaurant-bill case - two friends, both sober, did the tip math independently, no track record differences, peerhood basically stipulated. I think that vividness sneaks in and does illegitimate work once the argument gets extended to disagreements in ethics, metaphysics, or religion, where establishing that someone really is your epistemic peer is the whole problem, not a background assumption you get for free.

So: does the self-undermining worry actually land, or is there a version of the "immodesty" move that isn't just special pleading? And is my restaurant-bill complaint fair, or am I missing why the analogy is supposed to generalize anyway? Also flag it if I've mangled Elga's actual response - im going off memory here.
