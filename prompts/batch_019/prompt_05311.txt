So I've been down a rabbit hole for a piece on Bayesian rationality and self-trust, and I can't shake this issue with van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. Quick recap in case I'm garbling it: the idea (from his "Belief and the Will" paper, 1984 i think) is that if you're already certain right now what your credence in some proposition will be at a later time, then your current credence in that proposition, conditional on that certainty, has to equal it too -- otherwise a bookie can supposedly construct a Dutch book against you across the two times, the same way synchronic incoherence lets a bookie fleece you at a single moment. Framed that way it sounds like a nice formal version of "trust your future self, who presumably knows at least as much as you do, if not more." But every write-up I've found also brings up the obvious problem cases: what if you know that by tomorrow you'll have been hypnotized, or drugged, or will have simply forgotten the evidence you have right now? Deferring to that future credence seems like precisely the wrong thing to do, not the rational thing.

Apparently van Fraassen's own fix is to restrict the principle so it only kicks in when you don't have any specific reason to doubt your future self's rationality on the question at hand. That's the part that's got me stuck, because it feels like it could cut two ways: maybe that restriction saves a substantive, independently motivated norm of rationality, or maybe it just quietly turns Reflection into a dressed-up version of "defer to your future opinion, except when you have a reason not to" -- which is something basically nobody would've denied before any Dutch book was ever mentioned.

Could you write me an actual essay on this, not just a survey of the positions? (I'm not a philosopher by training, so don't dumb down the argument, just walk me through it properly.) Lay out the Dutch-book case for the principle, the memory-loss/manipulation-style counterexamples, and the restricted version, and then actually commit to an answer: once the restriction is written in explicitly, does Reflection still do real, independent philosophical work, or does it collapse into something more mundane that never needed a Dutch book to justify it in the first place? If it's relevant, feel free to weigh in on whether diachronic Dutch book arguments even have the same rational force as synchronic ones (I've seen the objection that the bookie needs to know your future credences in advance, which seems like a weirdly strong assumption for a rationality proof to rest on) -- but don't let that turn into an excuse to dodge the main verdict.
