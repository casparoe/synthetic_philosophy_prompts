So this is a bit of an unusual ask, but here goes. I'm a novelist -- mostly quiet, character-driven SF -- and my friend who does AI safety research sent me down a rabbit hole a few weeks back involving something called "acausal threats." The idea (i think it's from the Center on Long-Term Risk, or somewhere adjacent to that world, I honestly don't remember exactly) is that a sufficiently good predictor could threaten you effectively even with zero causal contact between you, purely because you both know it can model you accurately enough that the threat "lands" logically -- its the sober, less meme-y cousin of that whole Roko's Basilisk thing. What really got me was the proposed defense: you can deliberately adopt a "surrogate goal," precommitting (visibly, so the threatener can see it coming) to treat something you don't actually care about as if it were sacred, so any threat gets redirected onto that decoy instead of your real values. You basically build a fake self that can be extorted or tortured so the real one never has to pay out. I haven't been able to stop thinking about how that's simultaneously a genius hack and a form of self-mutilation.

Could you write me a short story, maybe 1500-3000 words, that dramatizes this? I don't want it to read like a lecture on decision theory -- I want it to trust the reader the way a good Ted Chiang or Greg Egan story does, letting the implications surface through plot rather than exposition. The one thing I really need is for the mechanics to matter: there has to be an actual acausal threat driving the plot (not just regular blackmail dressed up in jargon), and the surrogate-goal defense has to cost the protagonist something real by the end, ideally something that lands in the gut rather than just in the head. Setting is completely up to you -- far-future AI, a present-day lab, whatever gets you there cleanest -- I'm mostly trying to steal the emotional shape of the idea for something longer I'm working on.
