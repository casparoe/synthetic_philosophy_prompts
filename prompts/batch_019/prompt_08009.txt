In my graduate seminar on comparative logic this semester, we have spent the last few weeks on Nāgārjuna's use of the catuṣkoṭi, or tetralemma, in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, especially in the chapters examining the Tathāgata and nirvāṇa, where he denies in turn all four classical options — that the enlightened one exists after death, that he does not, that he both does and does not, and that he neither does nor does not — echoing the older "unanswered questions" of the Pali suttas. Graham Priest and Jay Garfield have argued, in their well-known joint paper on the topic, that we should take Nāgārjuna's fourfold negations at face value: he is, in effect, a dialetheist who holds that certain claims, especially those concerning the relation between conventional and ultimate truth, are both true and false, and that this can be modeled coherently with a paraconsistent logic rather than explained away. Most of my students have instead converged on a more conservative "contextualist" line, drawing on distinctions available in Candrakīrti's commentarial tradition: that the tetralemma's four horns are not really negating the same proposition in the same sense, that Madhyamaka negation is typically prasajya (non-implicative) rather than paryudāsa (implicative), and that once these distinctions are in place no formal contradiction survives — Nāgārjuna's logic turns out to be perfectly classical underneath.

I'd like you to write the strongest possible brief for the losing side of that classroom debate: argue that the dialetheist reading is in fact correct, and that the contextualist strategies my students favor do not succeed in dissolving the contradictions except by changing the subject or draining Nāgārjuna's claims of their intended force. Your argument should engage concretely with the prasajya/paryudāsa maneuver rather than simply asserting that it fails, and it should also confront the older worry, going back to Richard Robinson's mid-century critique, that "resolving" Nāgārjuna's paradoxes by fiat is just as likely to convict him of equivocation as it is to establish that he genuinely embraces true contradictions. Explain why the dialetheist reading survives that charge better than the alternatives do. You need not personally endorse the conclusion; I simply want to see the best case that can be made for it, developed with enough logical and textual precision that it could survive contact with a skeptical seminar room.
