I've been sitting in on a formal epistemology reading group this term, and the topic that has generated the most disagreement so far is imprecise probability. The standard Bayesian picture models a rational agent's degrees of belief as a single probability function satisfying the probability axioms. Proponents of imprecise credences -- going back to Isaac Levi and developed further by writers like Peter Walley and James Joyce -- argue that this is too demanding: when your evidence is scant, ambiguous, or symmetric between many hypotheses, picking out any single sharp number to represent your opinion misrepresents how good your evidence actually is. On their view, the rational response in such cases is to adopt a whole set of probability functions (a "credal set" or "representor"), each compatible with the evidence, rather than to privilege one arbitrarily.

I'd like an essay that takes this controversy seriously rather than just reporting both sides. Please engage specifically with two objections that have been pressed against imprecise credences. First, there is Adam Elga's argument (sometimes discussed under the heading of "belief inertia") that natural decision rules for acting on imprecise credences -- e.g., preferring one option to another only when it has higher expected utility according to every distribution in one's credal set -- can commit an agent to a pattern of choices that fails to respond to a long run of evidence that obviously bears on the decision at hand. Second, there is the phenomenon of "dilation," in which conditioning an imprecise credal state on new information can make it less precise rather than more, which sits awkwardly with the thought that learning is supposed to sharpen one's opinions, not blur them. Please explain how a committed imprecise probabilist might try to answer each of these worries (for instance, by adopting a different decision rule than the one Elga's argument targets, or by arguing that dilation isn't always an epistemic defect), and assess whether the answers work.

The essay should end by defending an actual verdict on whether imprecise credences are rationally permissible (or even required) in the cases that are supposed to motivate them, rather than concluding merely that "there are considerations on both sides." A qualified verdict is fine -- something like "imprecision is defensible as a representation of evidential support, but only once divorced from the decision rule Elga criticizes" would count as a real answer -- but please commit to something.
