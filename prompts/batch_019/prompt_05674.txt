A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about whether al-Ghazālī's rejection of causal necessity really amounts to a medieval anticipation of Hume, or whether that's a flattering myth that obscures what he was actually up to, and we never reached agreement. I'm now working through a stack of exams for the medieval philosophy course I TA, and the very first answer in the pile happens to bear directly on that dispute, so I'd like an outside read before I mark it myself.

The question set to students was:

"In the Seventeenth Discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazālī denies that there is a necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect, illustrating the point with the example of fire and cotton. (a) Reconstruct his argument and state what he takes it to establish. (b) Explain the theological work this conclusion is doing for him. (c) Assess how closely his position anticipates Hume's later treatment of causation, noting any important disanalogies."

Here is the student's answer:

"Al-Ghazālī's target in the Seventeenth Discussion is the necessitarian theory of causation held by the falāsifa, above all Ibn Sīnā, who held that a cause like fire has a nature that necessitates its effect, the burning of cotton, once the relevant conditions obtain. Ghazālī argues that we never actually observe this necessity: we observe only that burning regularly follows contact with fire, not that it must follow, and it is not self-contradictory to imagine the fire present and the burning absent. Since the connection can be coherently denied, it is not logically necessary but merely customary. This matters theologically because it protects the possibility of miracles and safeguards God's omnipotence: if fire necessitated burning by its own essence, God could not spare something from burning without changing that essence, whereas on Ghazālī's view God simply declines, on a given occasion, to create the burning He otherwise habitually creates alongside contact with fire. In this respect Ghazālī reaches, roughly six centuries before Hume, the position Hume would later defend: that causation is nothing more than constant conjunction, and that the 'necessity' we feel is a habit of mind rather than a feature of the world. Averroes, replying on the philosophers' behalf in The Incoherence of the Incoherence, essentially conceded the point, agreeing that necessity cannot be established by reason alone and must rest on custom instead. Ghazālī's argument is therefore best read as an early ancestor of Humean empiricism about causal power, reached from within an Ash'arite theological framework rather than a secular one."
