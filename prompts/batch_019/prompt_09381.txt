I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a good deal of my free time over the past year reading about free will and moral responsibility, and I keep coming back to Frankfurt-style cases: the scenario where someone would force you to do a certain thing if you showed any sign of choosing otherwise, but never actually has to step in because you go ahead and do that thing on your own anyway. As I understand it, the point is to show that the Principle of Alternative Possibilities is false, since you can apparently be fully responsible for what you did even though you couldn't have done otherwise.

While reading around this, I learned that Locke is usually credited with getting there first, close to three hundred years earlier, in his discussion of a man locked in a room. Here is the passage, from Book II, Chapter XXI of his Essay Concerning Human Understanding:

"Again: suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in, i.e. prefers his stay to going away. I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone. So that liberty is not an idea belonging to volition, or preferring; but to the person having the power of doing, or forbearing to do, according as the mind shall choose or direct."

I can follow the basic story, but the old-fashioned phrasing makes it hard for me to pin down the actual argument. Could you help me work out exactly what distinction Locke means to draw between an act being "voluntary" and a person being "at liberty," and how that distinction is supposed to do the same job as the counterfactual intervener does in a modern Frankfurt case? What's nagging at me is that Locke's man never even tries the door, so he never actually runs up against the thing that would have stopped him, whereas the whole point of a Frankfurt case seems to be that the intervener is standing by, ready to act, only if the agent starts to do otherwise. Is that difference beside the point Locke and Frankfurt are each making, or does it actually matter for whether the locked-room case really shows what people say it shows?
