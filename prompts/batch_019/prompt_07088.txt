A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Lewis's Principal Principle and never quite settled it. Her view was that because Lewis is a Humean about chance -- chances at a time supervene on the pattern of the entire history of the world, future included, via the best-system analysis -- almost any proposition about how things later turn out has some power to make a different chance distribution fit the total pattern better than the one we started with. That, she said, is why "historical evidence" cannot simply be assumed admissible in the Principal Principle, and it's the real content of what Lewis, in "Humean Supervenience Debugged," called the "big bad bug." I granted the bug but argued that the standard fix -- conditionalizing not on the bare claim that the chance of A is x, but on a full history-to-chance conditional together with the actual history up to t (the so-called New Principle) -- doesn't defeat the bug so much as define it away: once the conditionalized proposition already builds in the very history that was doing the undermining, deference comes out true almost by construction, and it's unclear what's left of the idea that credence tracks an independently characterized objective feature of the world. She thought that was too quick. Could you go through, more carefully than we managed, exactly how undermining is supposed to threaten the original Principal Principle, how the New Principle is meant to block it, and whether my "basically definitional" worry actually holds up?
