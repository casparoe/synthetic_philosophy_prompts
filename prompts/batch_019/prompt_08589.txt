I'm taking a course in set theory this semester, and a worry from lecture has been nagging me ever since a couple of classmates and I tried to talk it through afterward. The professor introduced the Axiom of Choice with the usual slogan that it is what licenses forming a single function that selects one element from each set in an arbitrary infinite family of nonempty sets, and that without it there is no guarantee such a selection is even possible. She illustrated this with the classic case of infinitely many pairs of shoes versus infinitely many pairs of socks: you can pick, say, the left shoe from each pair without invoking the axiom, because there is an obvious uniform rule for doing so, whereas picking one sock from each pair of otherwise indistinguishable socks is supposed to require it.

What I can't reconcile is a much more mundane case. Given any infinite sequence of nonempty subsets S_1, S_2, S_3, ... of the natural numbers, mathematicians constantly form new sequences by letting a_n be, say, the smallest element of S_n, and this is done throughout analysis and number theory without anyone ever citing the Axiom of Choice. But this looks exactly like the kind of infinite, simultaneous selection that, according to the shoes-and-socks story, ought to need the axiom to even guarantee that such a function exists. Since there is no obvious "left shoe" feature present when the sets in question are just subsets of the natural numbers rather than pairs of shoes, why does this construction get to skip the axiom while the sock case doesn't? Is the usual motivating story for the Axiom of Choice simply imprecise about what actually triggers the need for it, and if so, what is the precise general criterion for when a choice function is guaranteed to exist without appealing to the axiom at all?
