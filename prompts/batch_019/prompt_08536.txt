The question was:

Q4 (20 pts). (a) Hume denies that promising involves any special "act of the mind," discoverable by introspection, that creates a new motive to perform. Explain what work this claim does in his argument that promissory obligation must be conventional rather than natural. (b) Rawls's "practice" conception distinguishes the question "why does the practice of promising exist" from "why should I keep this particular promise," and holds that only the first question is answerable in utilitarian terms. Explain this distinction, and say whether Hume's account already amounts to the same view. (c) Scanlon rejects practice-based accounts in favor of his "Principle F." Explain what Principle F locates the wrong of promise-breaking in, and give one reason Scanlon thinks this is preferable to a Rawls-style practice account.

Here's the student's answer:

(a) Hume's point is that if you look inward when you make a promise, you can't actually find some special mental act — a distinct "willing" — that generates a brand-new motive or obligation to perform; the will can redirect existing motives but can't conjure one out of nothing. So the naive picture on which promising works by an inner act of self-obligation is confused. Since there's no natural mental act doing the work, and self-interest alone won't explain why we keep promises when it's inconvenient, Hume concludes that whatever binds us must be an artifice — a convention societies invent because it lets people cooperate when their interests don't line up in time. Once the convention is running, we also feel real moral disapproval of promise-breaking, through sympathy with the public interest in the convention's survival, not just self-interest.

(b) Rawls's distinction is that the practice as a whole can be justified by its usefulness, but once you're inside the practice you can't reopen that question every time you consider breaking a particular promise, because the practice couldn't do its job of letting people rely on each other if everyone treated each instance as up for case-by-case recalculation. This is really just Hume's convention theory again in more modern dress: Hume already thought promissory obligation was conventional rather than natural, so Rawls is basically restating the same view in different vocabulary.

(c) Scanlon rejects practice accounts because they make the obligation depend on an actual functioning social practice, which gets the wrong answer in cases like a private, undiscoverable promise that couldn't affect anyone's trust in promising generally — the practice view seems to say there's nothing wrong with breaking it, which is deeply counterintuitive. Instead, Principle F locates the wrongness in what would happen if this kind of promise-breaking became general: if people felt free to break promises whenever convenient, trust would collapse and nobody could rely on anyone's word, which is why it's wrong even in the one-off case. In that sense Principle F is really an updated version of Kant's argument against the false promiser in the Groundwork — both think the problem is that the policy couldn't be willed as a general practice — and Scanlon's main achievement is putting Kant's insight on firmer, less metaphysically loaded ground.
