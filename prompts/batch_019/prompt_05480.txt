So this happened at a conference last month and I still haven't sorted out who was right. Three of us shared an airbnb — me, another grad student named Dana, and a postdoc named Ruben — and on the last morning we had to split the cost unevenly (different people had the bigger room, someone left a day early, etc). The wifi was down and half our phones were dead, so Dana and Ruben each just did the math in their heads. They got different numbers for what everyone owed, and it turned into a whole thing, partly because we'd literally just read Elga and Christensen on conciliationism vs steadfastness for our epistemology proseminar the week before, so it was fresh in everyone's head:

1. Dana said: neither of us has any track record of being better at this kind of arithmetic, so by the equal-weight view we just read for class, we're peers here and should each move our number toward the midpoint.
2. Ruben said he'd re-run the calculation twice since and got the same total both times, and that this extra checking is evidence Dana doesn't have, so they're not actually symmetric anymore.
3. Dana replied that feeling sure after rechecking isn't new evidence of being right, since that's exactly what being confidently wrong feels like from the inside too — if "I checked again and I'm still sure" always counted as new evidence, nobody would ever have to conciliate about anything.
4. Ruben switched tack: fine, but plenty of serious epistemologists defend steadfastness, so at the meta level, he and Dana are themselves peers on whether the equal-weight view is even true. If Dana's principle applies to itself, she should lower her confidence in it whenever a steadfaster disagrees with her about it — which seems to leave her unable to insist, with full confidence, that he conciliate on the airbnb split.
5. Dana said there's a standard move for that (something like: every general epistemic principle runs into this when applied to itself, so it's not a special problem for conciliationism), but admitted she wasn't sure that answer actually rescues the verdict about who owes what.

We ended up just splitting the difference between their two numbers to get out the door, which definately didn't feel like it settled anything.
