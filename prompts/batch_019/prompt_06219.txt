I run a small novel-writing group that meets every other Sunday, and at the last meeting things got surprisingly heated over a craft question that turned out to be way more philosophically loaded than anyone expected. One of our members, Nora, workshopped a manuscript where the big midpoint reveal is that "J. Alder," the reclusive nature writer her protagonist has been trading long letters with for two hundred pages, is actually the protagonist's own mother, who everyone in the book (and the reader) believes died years earlier. Nothing in the narration is technically false the whole way through — every sentence about J. Alder is true of the mother and vice versa — the trick is just that the reader builds two seperate mental files on these people that turn out, at the reveal, to be the same file.

Colin, who did a philosophy MA before switching to an MFA and will find a way to mention it, said the reveal only works because names carry "sense" along with reference: "J. Alder" and "the mother" pick out the same woman but present her under different guises, the same way "the morning star" and "the evening star" both turn out to be Venus — and realizing Hesperus is Phosphorus was a genuine discovery, not a trick, so the novel's doing the same honest thing to the reader. Femi shot back that this just dresses up the real issue: nothing in the story-world changes at the moment of the reveal, only the reader's picture of it, so calling it a "discovery" is generous PR for what's really a manufactured error. Colin said fine, but the same is true of the morning-star case — nobodys picture of Venus changed either, and we still call that real knowledge — so "nothing in the world changed" can't be what makes an insight cheap. Femi said there's still a difference: you can check the Venus claim with a telescope, but a novel just asserts the identity and expects you to feel enlightened for being told something the author made up five minutes ago. Colin then brought up some case — he couldn't remember whose it was — about a guy who sincerely believes London is pretty and also, in a sense, believes it isn't, because he picked up the name "London" twice, once from hearsay in French and once from actually living there, without ever connecting the two; his point being that even completely plain, non-descriptive names can produce this exact gap, so it isn't some special sleaziness available only to novelists, it's built into how names and belief work generally. Femi said that if the best philosophers can offer here is an unsolved puzzle, that's a strange thing to lean on in defense of a plot device, and that maybe the honest conclusion is that Colin's plot device works by being confusing, not by being deep. Nora just sat there looking like she regretted bringing the snacks.

I keep flip-flopping on who's actually ahead here. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of each specific point, rather than just handing out one overall winner — and flag anything important that neither of them seems to have noticed? I write fiction for a living, not philosophy, so please actually explain the ideas you're using rather than just naming them and moving on.
