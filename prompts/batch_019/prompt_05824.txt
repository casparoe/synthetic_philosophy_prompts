the question we sent them was: "Explain the core intuition behind the no-trade theorem (Milgrom & Stokey), including the role played by common knowledge of rationality and a common prior. Then explain why, despite the theorem, we still see enormous trading volume in real financial markets."

and here's the candidate's answer:

"The basic idea is sometimes called the Groucho Marx theorem: if I offer to sell you my shares, the very fact that I'm willing to sell should make you suspicious I know something bad about the company, and symmetrically my willingness to buy from you should make you suspicious I know something good. If we're both rational and this is common knowledge, any trade motivated purely by private information gets fully unwound by the belief update it triggers in the other party, so speculative trade never gets off the ground before it starts. This is basically Aumann's agreement theorem in disguise -- two Bayesians who start out with the same information can't agree to disagree once they compare notes. For the no-trade result to go through you also need a common prior, meaning you and I have to start out knowing the same things before we each get our own private signal, otherwise there's no contradiction in both of us thinking we're getting the better deal. You also need the initial allocation to already be Pareto-optimal, so the only motive left for trade is new information rather than something like differing risk tolerances. In real markets we obviously see tons of trading anyway, and the usual explanations are noise traders, liquidity and hedging needs, and genuine differences of opinion rather than differences of information -- essentially the same puzzle Grossman and Stiglitz raised about why markets can't be fully informationally efficient, since if prices reflected all information nobody would bother paying for it."
