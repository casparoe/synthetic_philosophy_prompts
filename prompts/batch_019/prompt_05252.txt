I spent thirty-two years as an experimental particle physicist, most of it on fixed-target experiments studying CP violation in the neutral kaon system, the line of work that traces back to Cronin and Fitch's 1964 result. Since retiring I have joined a small reading group of similarly idle former academics, and we have lately been working through the philosophy of time. One session ended in a genuine argument over Huw Price's charge that physicists reason about probability asymmetrically — happily using statistical mechanics to infer the future from the present while refusing to run the same inference backward, since that would make the past look high-entropy too, which we know it was not. I went home and drafted my own attempt to think the issue through, largely to find out whether decades of doing statistical mechanics for a living actually helps here or just gets in the way. I would value your comments on it, especially on the three points I list at the end.

Here is the draft. Start from Boltzmann's H-theorem: given the time-symmetric collision dynamics of a dilute gas, plus the molecular chaos assumption, a quantity H can be shown to decrease monotonically, which Boltzmann identified, up to sign, with the approach to equilibrium. Loschmidt's objection was simple and, I think, fatal to the theorem taken as a strict derivation: because the underlying dynamics is reversible, every H-decreasing trajectory has a time-reversed twin along which H increases, so nothing in the dynamics itself can prefer one temporal direction over the other. Zermelo's later recurrence objection presses the same worry from a different angle, invoking Poincaré's theorem that any bounded mechanical system returns arbitrarily close to its initial state if one waits long enough, which appears to rule out even statistical, monotonic increase over sufficiently long stretches of time.

Boltzmann's mature reply, as I understand it, was to retreat from "entropy must increase" to "entropy is overwhelmingly likely to increase," on the grounds that the phase-space volume associated with a macrostate grows enormously with its entropy, so a system that starts in a low-entropy corner of phase space has nowhere probable to go but up. The trouble is that this counting argument is exactly as time-symmetric as the mechanics beneath it: it implies entropy was probably higher a minute ago just as much as it implies entropy will probably be higher a minute from now, for any generic "now" one cares to pick. That flatly contradicts the fact that we have fossils, tree rings, and photographs testifying to a genuinely low-entropy past, rather than a present that is merely a local dip between two high-entropy directions. The standard patch, which I gather now goes by the name "Past Hypothesis," is to stipulate that the universe, or at least our corner of it, began in an extraordinarily improbable low-entropy macrostate. My worry is that this stipulation is doing essentially all the explanatory work in the story, while nothing explains it in turn; it gets asserted as a boundary condition and then quietly treated as though it had been derived.

Boltzmann himself also floated the alternative I find least satisfying of all: instead of a special initial condition, suppose the universe is mostly in equilibrium and we simply inhabit a rare statistical fluctuation away from it. But the fluctuations that dominate any such count are the cheapest ones, and a fluctuation that assembles a single momentary observer, complete with false memories of an orderly past, is enormously more probable than one that assembles an entire coherent galactic history. Taken seriously, this seems to predict that a "typical" observer in such a universe should expect to be exactly that kind of short-lived, deluded fluctuation, which strikes me as a reductio of the whole approach rather than a live cosmology. So, tentatively, I have landed on thinking that statistical mechanics does not explain the thermodynamic arrow so much as show what follows from it, conditional on a boundary condition that physics cannot itself supply. I have also decided, possibly too conveniently, to set aside the fact that the weak interaction violates time-reversal symmetry outright, since everything I have read treats this as many orders of magnitude too small, and of the wrong character, to be the source of the macroscopic asymmetry — even though this is the one part of the story closer to my actual field than anything else here.

There are three things I would like from you. First, tell me plainly where I have gotten the physics or the history wrong; I am working largely from memory and may well be conflating Boltzmann's actual replies to Loschmidt and to Zermelo, or misdating them. Second, assess whether the final move — treating the Past Hypothesis as an unexplained brute posit rather than a genuine explanation — is fair, or whether I am too quick to dismiss cosmological attempts, through inflation or otherwise, to make that low-entropy beginning itself unsurprising. Third, tell me whether the reductio I describe for the fluctuation account has a standard name or a standard rebuttal I ought to know, and whether setting aside the CP-violation angle is the right call or a case of ignoring the one piece of actual evidence I have direct access to.
