I've spent the last few weeks reporting a piece on the odd, quiet revival of interest in Temple-era animal sacrifice: the red heifers flown in from a ranch in Texas a couple of years ago on the chance that one might qualify for the purification rite described in Numbers, the dress rehearsals a few religious-Zionist groups now stage for a future Passover offering, and so on. Since I mostly write about how old philosophical arguments resurface in unexpected places, I wanted some intellectual ballast before filing anything, so I went back to what's usually cited as the classic philosophical treatment of sacrifice: Maimonides' discussion in the Guide for the Perplexed, Part III, Chapter 32. I got stuck on the central move he makes there, and none of the popular summaries I've found since quite address what's bothering me.

Here is the core of the passage, in the standard 1904 Friedländer translation: "It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. ... [T]he general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images, to bow down to those images, and to burn incense before them. ... It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God ... that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man ... it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us ... that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings ... As the sacrificial service is not the primary object [of the commandments about sacrifice], whilst supplications, prayers, and similar kinds of worship are nearer to the primary object ... a great difference was made in the Law between these two kinds of service." A few pages later he even reads Jeremiah's line that God "spake not unto your fathers ... concerning burnt-offering or sacrifices" as scriptural support for the whole argument.

What I can't square is this. If sacrifice is, by Maimonides' own account, essentially a concession to the psychological state the Israelites happened to be in three thousand years ago — a way of weaning a still half-idolatrous people onto monotheism, with prayer as the real, "primary" object of worship all along — why does he turn around, in the Mishneh Torah, and codify the sacrificial system in exhaustive, evidently unembarrassed technical detail, and even write its restoration into his picture of the messianic future, as though it were the permanent, first-order form of divine service rather than training wheels a spiritually mature people should have outgrown? Is that a real inconsistency between Maimonides the philosopher and Maimonides the legislator, or does "primary object" mean something in this passage that would let the two sit together comfortably? I'd like to actually understand the mechanics of the argument here rather than land on the flattened one-liner — "Maimonides thought sacrifice was primitive" — that I keep running into in my research.
