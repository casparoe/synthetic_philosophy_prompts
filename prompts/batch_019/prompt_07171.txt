I teach philosophy of science, and for the past decade I have given the same two guest lectures on the demarcation problem to our undergraduate research-methods students -- lectures I could by now deliver half-asleep, since it never much mattered whether my students left with a perfectly precise picture of the problem. This spring is different. I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a three-day workshop that our law school's judicial education institute is running for sitting trial judges on how to evaluate scientific expert testimony, and the organizers specifically want the talk built around Popper's falsifiability criterion, since some version of "can it be tested, and has it been tested?" is one of the factors the Supreme Court's 1993 Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals decision instructs judges to weigh in deciding whether an expert's methodology counts as science rather than junk science. Most of the judges in the room already treat that criterion as settled doctrine, the sort of thing they can just apply like a statute. What they don't know, and won't especially want to hear, is how unsettled the demarcation problem still is among the people who work on it for a living -- two of whom, colleagues from my own department, will be sitting in the audience as fellow faculty for the institute, so I can't simply flatter the room's confidence either.

Could you draft these opening remarks for me? I would like something that runs to roughly ten or twelve minutes read aloud, that uses the real strengths and the real limits of the falsifiability criterion as the actual structure of the talk rather than as a disclaimer tacked on at the end, that would not make my philosopher colleagues wince, and that still leaves the judges -- who have rulings to write tomorrow, not dissertations -- with something more usable than "it's complicated."
