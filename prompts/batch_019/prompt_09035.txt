People tend to treat 'libertarian paternalism' (Thaler & Sunstein's nudge idea: keep the choice open, just tweak the default) and 'asymmetric paternalism' (Camerer et al's idea: design the policy so it helps people who are making a mistake but costs almost nothing for people who aren't) as basically the same proposal, since the standard example everyone reaches for -- auto-enrolling people into a 401k -- happens to satisfy both. But 'preserves choice' and 'cheap for the rational' are actually logically independent conditions. Come up with a policy that's asymmetric paternalist without being libertarian, and one that's libertarian paternalist without being asymmetric, then tell me which of the two conditions is really doing the moral work when people defend nudge-y policies -- the preserved choice, or the low cost to people who don't need the help. Defend your pick.
