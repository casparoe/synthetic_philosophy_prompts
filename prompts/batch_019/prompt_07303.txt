I am drafting a short paper for my department's philosophy-of-biology reading group on the etiological/causal-role debate about biological function, and rather than rehearse the standard heart-and-kidneys illustrations, I have tried to force the issue using an actual scientific controversy. Before I circulate the draft, I would like your comments, particularly on three things: (a) whether my reconstruction of Cummins, Neander, and Godfrey-Smith is accurate and fair to each; (b) whether the genomics case really generates the dilemma I claim it does, rather than one side having an easy reply I have missed; and (c) whether my closing claim is a defensible conclusion or an overreach. Here is the draft:

Two families of view dominate philosophical accounts of biological function. On the etiological view descending from Wright, a trait's function is the effect that explains its presence: hearts have the function of pumping blood because pumping blood is what hearts were selected to do. Neander's "selected effects" refinement ties this explicitly to natural selection, and Godfrey-Smith's "modern history" variant relaxes it so that what fixes the function is whatever selective regime currently explains the trait's maintenance, not necessarily the episode that first produced it. On the causal-role view associated with Cummins, a trait's function is simply its contribution to some capacity of a containing system, picked out relative to whichever analytical breakdown of that system we happen to care about; history drops out entirely. Each view has its canonical embarrassment. The etiological view struggles with "swamp" cases — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an organism, assembled by freak chance with no selective history at all, whose heart we are still inclined to credit with the function of pumping blood (this is exactly what Neander's "Swampman Meets Swampcow" is arguing about). The causal-role view, having no principled way to prefer one systemic capacity over another, seems committed to saying that the heart's function, relative to the capacity of alerting a doctor with a stethoscope, is making a thumping noise — a result almost everyone wants to resist, though Cummins can shrug this off as no cost to a theory that was never meant to single out one "the" function per trait.

Rather than re-litigate those cases, I want to run the distinction through the 2012 ENCODE dispute. The ENCODE consortium (a large, publicly funded project mapping biochemical activity across the human genome) reported that upwards of 80% of the genome shows some reproducible biochemical activity — transcription, transcription-factor binding, a distinctive chromatin signature — and presented this as evidence that most of the genome is "functional," against the older picture in which the great bulk of it is inert. Evolutionary genomicists, most pointedly Graur and colleagues in "On the Immortality of Television Sets," objected that this is not what function means in biology: what matters is whether a stretch of DNA is kept in place by purifying selection, i.e., whether mutating it is bad enough for fitness that selection removes the mutants. On their view, comparative evidence that only a small fraction of the genome shows this kind of constraint is the only evidence that bears on whether it is functional; biochemical activity without selective maintenance is exactly what a genome this large would produce as noise.

Once put this way, it is hard not to see ENCODE as deploying a Cummins-style causal-role notion — functional just means causally implicated in some activity of the cellular system — while their critics were insisting on something close to a selected-effects notion. Neither side treated this as a merely verbal dispute to be dissolved by disambiguation; both thought the other was making a substantive error about what "function" means in biology, and the exchange was not gentle. My tentative view is that the critics were right, and not merely because the etiological notion is more useful for some purposes and the causal-role notion for others — which is the reply Cummins's own pluralism supplies for free. In genomics specifically, the causal-role notion does almost no discriminating work, because in a nucleus as biochemically promiscuous as ours, some causal contribution to some systemic capacity is nearly free: a suitably chosen analytical breakdown of "what the cell does" will credit enormous stretches of noise-transcribed, promiscuously bound sequence with a function, which defeats the very explanatory purpose — distinguishing sequence that is there because of what it does from sequence that is there despite doing very little — for which biologists reached for the word in the first place. If that is right, ENCODE gives a principled reason, not merely a pragmatic one, to prefer the etiological notion in this domain, even for a pluralist who is happy to let causal-role talk stand elsewhere.

That is the draft. I would especially value pushback on whether I have been fair to Cummins, whose considered view already anticipates the "shrug," and on whether my last paragraph actually blocks that shrug or merely restates my preference for the etiological notion in different words.
