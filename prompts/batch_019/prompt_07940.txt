Last weekend a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about a mutual friend, Dana, who is endlessly annoyed when people flake on plans, yet has cancelled on the two of us three times this month alone. I argued that whatever her own track record, she isn't wrong that flaking is inconsiderate, so her complaints still land on their merits; my friend countered that this is exactly the move that lets hypocrites off the hook, and that by nagging everyone else about flaking while doing it herself, Dana has forfeited any standing to complain about it at all, true or not. We went back and forth for a while without settling whether "the charge is true" and "she has no business making it" can really be kept apart as separate questions, or whether insisting on that separation is itself a dodge.

As it happens, I record a small philosophy podcast, and next month's guest has written a book defending a fairness-based account of the standing to blame: roughly, that hypocritical blame is objectionable not because it is false but because blaming someone presupposes holding yourself to the very standard you are invoking, and the hypocrite is quietly exempting herself from it. Could you help me draft eight to ten interview questions that do more than let him restate that thesis? I want at least one question, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, on: (1) whether his account can actually preserve the classic distinction (associated with G. A. Cohen) between whether a charge is correct and whether its maker has standing to level it, or whether fairness considerations end up quietly infecting the truth question after all; (2) whether standing, once forfeited through hypocrisy, is restored by mere private reform, or requires something more public, such as an acknowledgment or a comparable sacrifice; (3) whether a hypocrite's blame is best described as blame that misfires and so isn't really blame at all, or as blame that goes through but is simply impermissible -- and what practical difference that distinction is meant to make; and (4) whether the account scales up to collective cases, such as one country criticizing another's record on an issue its own history is not exactly clean on. Please keep the questions conversational enough to be asked aloud rather than read off a page.
