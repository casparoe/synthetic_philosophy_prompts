So I'm about two-thirds into a novel about a woman named Imogen who spends nearly forty years alone restoring the stained glass in a derelict chapel in a valley that's already been condemned for a reservoir — the day she finishes is the day the valley gets flooded, and the chapel goes under for good, windows and all, with no living person ever laying eyes on it again (she dies not long after, so not even her). I wanted her to have some actual philosophical backbone instead of just vague "art for its own sake" hand-waving, so a friend of mine who's doing a philosophy PhD pointed me toward G.E. Moore, who apparently argued in Principia Ethica that the two greatest goods a life can contain, more or less full stop, are aesthetic enjoyment and personal affection — friendship, love, that sort of thing. Perfect, I thought, until I actually sat with it and relized there's a problem.

Both of those "goods" seem to require an actual mind doing the enjoying or the caring — someone getting pleasure from something beautiful, two people who feel affection for each other. Imogen has neither by the end: nobody will ever see the windows, and she's long since cut herself off from every friend and lover she used to have. So doesn't Moore's own theory mean her whole life's work nets out to basically zero value, or worse than zero once you factor in what she gave up to do it? I want to hand this argument to a cynical engineer on the dam crew as a "your life's work amounts to nothing" gut-punch partway through the book, and I'd like it to actually be a valid reading of Moore, not some strawman a philosophy grad student would laugh at. Is that a fair way to run the argument on his own terms, or am I misreading what he actually thought made things valuable?
