I'm taking a graduate seminar on experimental philosophy this term, cross-listed with cognitive science, and this week's unit is Joshua Knobe's side-effect effect. We started, like everyone does, with the original 2003 chairman vignette: a CEO who adopts a profitable new policy knowing it will, as a side effect, harm the environment is overwhelmingly judged to have harmed it "intentionally," while a CEO who adopts an equally profitable policy knowing it will help the environment is mostly judged not to have helped it intentionally, even though the two scenarios are identical in structure and differ only in the moral valence of the foreseen side effect. What I'm now struggling with is the thicket of rival explanations that has grown up around that single result, because next week I owe a short position paper that has to adjudicate between them rather than just redescribe the chairman case, and a few of us got into an argument after seminar about which explanation is actually winning at this point in the literature.

The five accounts on our reading list are: Knobe's own claim that the folk concept of "intentional action" is itself partly constituted by moral evaluation, so the concept genuinely applies differently across the two cases; Adams and Steadman's pragmatic account, on which the concept applies the same way in both cases and only the conversational appropriateness of asserting "intentionally" shifts, since denying it in the harm case would seem to excuse the CEO and asserting it in the help case would seem to give him credit he does not deserve; Machery's trade-off hypothesis, on which the asymmetry is really driven by whether the agent knowingly imposes a cost on someone else to secure a benefit for himself, with moral valence as such doing no independent work; Sripada's deep self concordance model, on which the judgment tracks whether the outcome is concordant with the agent's stable cares and values rather than merely incidental to them; and a more general "evaluative bias" position, for which the strongest evidence is that Beebe and Buckwalter later found essentially the same harm/help asymmetry for knowledge attributions, suggesting the phenomenon has nothing special to do with the concept of intentional action at all.
