Okay, this is a weird source for a philosophy question, but I just had a baby and, like a responsible new parent, went and applied for life insurance — and the underwriting process has kind of wrecked me. The insurer ran my age, bloodwork, and family history through their model and quoted me a premium that bakes in some specific probability that I die in the next year. Out of curiosity I plugged similar info into a different mortality calculator, one that grouped people by different variables, and got a noticeably different number for basically the same guy. My sister's doing a philosophy MA and told me this is pretty much the setup Reichenbach used to raise the "reference class problem" decades ago — apparently I belong to tons of overlapping classes (new fathers, non-smokers, cyclists, left-handed guys born in October, whatever combo you like), each licensing a different frequency, and the only fully specific class is just me, where frequencies aren't even defined anymore. i can't shake the thought that there might be no fact at all about MY probability of dying this year. Can you argue for the strongest version of that view — that a number like this is only ever true of a reference class and never of an individual, no matter how much data backs it up — in a way that still explains why my insurer, my doctor, and I aren't being irrational to keep using these numbers to make real decisions anyway?
