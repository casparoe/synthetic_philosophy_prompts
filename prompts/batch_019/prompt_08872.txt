I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on rival theories of biological function, and I keep running into trouble with cases of exaptation. Could you compare the etiological account of function that runs from Wright through Millikan and Neander's 'selected-effects' refinement to Cummins's causal-role account? I'm especially interested in how each would assign a current function to a trait that was originally selected for one effect but is now maintained by selection for quite a different effect it was later co-opted to perform — the standard illustration being feathers, usually said to have evolved first for thermoregulation and only later been co-opted for flight. Does the etiological account need a move like Godfrey-Smith's restriction to 'recent' rather than 'origin' selective history to avoid the wrong verdict here, and is that move principled or merely ad hoc? Does the causal-role account only dodge the difficulty by reintroducing the familiar worry that causal-role functions are too liberal, since it must non-arbitrarily single out which systemic capacity — thermoregulation or flight — is the relevant one? I'd like a considered verdict, with justification, on which account handles exaptation more successfully, and whether it can do so without simply being patched to fit the case after the fact.
