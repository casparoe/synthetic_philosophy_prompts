"The Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) says that for any x and y, if x and y have all the same properties, then x and y are one and the same thing (x = y). Put the other way around: if x and y are truly two distinct things, there must be at least one property that one has and the other lacks.

Max Black's thought experiment asks us to imagine a universe that contains nothing but two iron spheres, each exactly one mile in diameter, two miles apart from one another, with exactly the same temperature, colour, and chemical composition, and nothing else in the universe to serve as a landmark. Because the universe is perfectly symmetrical, any true description of one sphere is equally true of the other. So the two spheres appear to share literally all of their properties, and yet there are clearly two spheres, not one. If that scenario is genuinely possible, PII is false.

One influential reply, due to Saunders, denies that the spheres are fully indiscernible in the first place. Consider the relation 'is two miles from': sphere A bears this relation to sphere B, but A does not bear it to itself, since nothing is two miles from itself, so the relation is asymmetric in just the way we need to break the symmetry between them. Saunders calls this weak discernibility: even though neither sphere has an intrinsic feature the other lacks, the relation lets us tell the two spheres apart and say which is which, rather than just knowing that there must be two of them. On this view, the spheres always differed at least relationally, so Black has only shown that a restricted version of PII, one that ignores relations, is false, not the principle itself. I think this defense works: once we've found a genuine respect in which the spheres differ, it's no longer true that they share ALL of their properties, so PII comes out safe."
