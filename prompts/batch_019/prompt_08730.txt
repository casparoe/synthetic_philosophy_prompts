I'm doing a PhD in Slavic Languages and Literatures, and this semester's seminar has had everyone chewing on Tolstoy's "What Is Art?" for weeks now, which apparently means my friends bring it up at parties too. Two people from my cohort, Priyanka and Dev, got into a whole thing over coffee last week about whether Tolstoy's famous verdict on his own writing actually follows from his own theory, and I couldn't tell who was winning, so I want a neutral read from someone with no stake in our seminar politics.

Quick background in case it's useful: Tolstoy's account runs on "infection" (zarazhenie, if I've got the transliteration right) — art is whatever transmits a feeling from its maker to an audience so they come to feel it too, merging with the artist and with each other. He gives three conditions that determine how infectious a given work is: the individuality of the feeling conveyed, the clarity with which it's conveyed, and the sincerity of the artist, i.e. how much the artist actually felt what he's expressing rather than performing it for effect (this last one he treats as the most important of the three). Separately, he splits art into good and bad depending on whether the feeling transmitted is one that can unite people universally — for him, basically the Christian sense of universal brotherhood, accessible in principle to a peasant as much as to a duke — or one that only a leisured, over-cultivated minority can access. And infamously, when this apparatus gets turned on his own back catalogue (by him, and by pretty much everyone who's written about the essay since), the big psychologically dense novels end up looking like bad or counterfeit art next to a plain didactic tale like "God Sees the Truth, But Waits."

Here's more or less how the argument went. Priyanka opened by saying Tolstoy's right about himself, and it's not close: Anna's jealousy, Vronsky's boredom, Levin's whole estate-owner malaise are gorgeously individuated and clearly rendered, sure, but every one of those feelings presupposes a class position — leisure, property, a particular marriage market. A serf, or some random reader in Lagos or Osaka a century later, has to do real imaginative labor just to access what's being transmitted, whereas the little tale's feeling — guilt, forgiveness, recognizing a wrongly punished man as a brother — needs none of that scaffolding. So on Tolstoy's own universality test, the tale wins even though it's the cruder piece of writing. Dev shot back that this is smuggling: Tolstoy gives three conditions for how much infection is happening — individuality, clarity, sincerity — and "lots of different kinds of people can access this" isn't one of them; that's a separate test for whether the infected feeling is morally good, not for whether infection occurred at all. Priyanka's just used universal accessibility to do double duty as a measure of infectiousness and a measure of moral worth, which is exactly the conflation the theory is supposed to keep apart. By the actual three conditions, Anna Karenina is almost certainly more individuated (nobody mistakes Anna for a type) and arguably more sincere too, given how personally implicated Tolstoy clearly was in writing it. If it still loses, that's the moral criterion overriding the infection criteria, not the theory being vindicated. Priyanka's comeback was that sincerity, on Tolstoy's account, was never just "did the author feel something real" — it's bound up with whether the artist is transmitting a genuine perception rather than indulging a class-bound taste, and you can't neatly separate "how sincerely felt" from "was this actually a true perception," because Tolstoy's whole point is that corrupted art feels sincere and compelling precisely to the audience it was built for. Our sense that Anna Karenina reads as sincere is itself the kind of evidence Tolstoy predicts, not a neutral fact Dev can just cite against him. Dev's last word was that this is exactly the move that makes the theory unfalsifiable: if millions of people across wildly different classes and countries report being moved by Anna Karenina, and every one of those reports can be waved off as "you're just part of the corrupted audience too," then "universally accessible" has stopped tracking anything checkable and just means "whatever feelings Tolstoy happens to endorse." At that point he's not testing his own novel against his criteria, he's using the novel as a hostage to protect the criteria from ever failing.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just handing out an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Priyanka's universality point and Dev's unfalsifiability charge can both be right at once — it feels like they should be in tension but I'm not fully sure they are — and whether Tolstoy's theory has some way out of this that neither of them actually reached for.
