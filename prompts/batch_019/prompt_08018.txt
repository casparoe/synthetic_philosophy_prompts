Quick context: I'm a phil PhD student, and this week's epistemology seminar spent basically two sessions on Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument from *Knowledge and Its Limits* — the part where he runs a margin-for-error, sorites-ish argument (the slowly-warming-room case, where you supposedly feel cold at the start and not-cold by the end, with imperceptible steps in between) to try to show that basically no non-trivial condition is luminous, meaning theres no condition C such that whenever C obtains, you're automatically in a position to know that it obtains. Our instructor kept hammering on the point that this isn't just about feeling cold — if the argument generalizes to the condition "knowing that p" itself, it knocks out the KK principle too (Kp -> KKp), since KK is basically just what luminosity says once you plug in "knowing that p" as the condition in question.

Half the seminar came away thinking the argument basically works and that it's a real problem for access-internalist pictures where we're supposed to always be able to tell when we know something; the other half (including, I think, whoever wrote that Neta & Rohrbaugh paper I skimmed once, though I might be misremembering the names) suspects the margin-for-error premise smuggles in something suspiciously close to what it's supposed to be disproving, so the "refutation" of KK ends up less clean than advertised. I've gone back and forth twice already in my own head and don't fully trust my read on it anymore.

Could you write me an essay-length answer to this question: does Williamson's anti-luminosity argument give us good reason to reject the KK principle?
