So my "Free Will and Moral Responsibility" seminar just spent two weeks on Frankfurt's Jones/Black case -- the one where Black the neuroscientist wants Jones to perform some action A, and has a device that reads a "prior sign" off Jones's brain: if the sign shows Jones is about to choose not-A, the device intervenes and makes him do A anyway; if the sign shows he's going to choose A on his own, the device just sits there and never fires. In the actual sequence Jones goes for A completely on his own, the device stays dormant, and Frankfurt's point is that Jones is still responsible even though he literally couldn't have done otherwise -- so the principle that responsibility requires alternative possibilities (PAP) turns out to be false. After our last discussion section, two people in my group, Maya and Owen, ended up arguing about this in the hallway for like half an hour while I just stood there half-listening, half-still-confused-about-the-reading.

Roughly how it went: (1) Maya opened with the standard move -- whatever grounds Jones's responsibility (his own reasoning, his actually deciding for himself) is completely untouched by Black's device, so the unexercised backup can't be relevant to that responsibility, meaning you can have full responsibility with zero alternative possibilities. (2) Owen said the case is hiding a dilemma: that "prior sign" either nomologically guarantees what Jones does, or it doesn't. If it does, the scenario is quietly deterministic, and a libertarian was never going to concede anything to a deterministic case -- they'll just say Jones isn't responsible because determinism is true there, not because alternatives are irrelevant. And if the sign doesn't guarantee it, then right up until Jones actually decided, there was a live possibility of him going the other way -- so an alternative possibility was sitting there the whole time, un-eliminated. (3) Maya answered that whatever "possibility" survives on the second horn is a joke of an alternative -- it's not something Jones can notice, exploit, or steer by, it's just leftover statistical noise in the setup, and if that's the only kind of "could've done otherwise" the principle needs to be true, the principle was never worth caring about to begin with. (4) Owen shot back that this concedes his actual point: Frankfurt's argument advertises itself as showing responsibility needs literally no alternatives, full stop, but Maya just quietly swapped in "no alternatives that are robust or usable," and that extra qualifier is carrying a ton of weight that Frankfurt himself never argues for -- so at best, the reading proved a narrower and much less startling thesis then it gets credit for.

Then they had to split up for their next classes and it just... didn't resolve. I've reread the case a few times since and keep flip flopping on who's ahead, especially for 3 vs 4. Could you go through this move by move -- for each of the four points, say who's got the better of the exchange and why, rather than just handing out an overall winner -- and then add a fifth part on whether there's some assumption sitting underneath their entire back-and-forth that neither of them is questioning but maybe should be? Doesn't have to be formal, I'd just genuinely like to understand who's right about what instead of picking a side myself.
