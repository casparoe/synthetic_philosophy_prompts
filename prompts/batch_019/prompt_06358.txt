Question: A friend of mine who's skeptical of the reliability of mathematics argues as follows: "Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows that Peano Arithmetic (PA) can't prove its own consistency. So we can never really be sure the basic axioms of arithmetic are consistent, and Gentzen's famous 1936 proof of PA's consistency doesn't help, since it's really just PA proving its own consistency with a bit of extra machinery bolted on — exactly the kind of thing Gödel's theorem rules out." Assess this argument. Your answer should (a) state precisely what the second incompleteness theorem does and does not say, (b) explain what Gentzen's proof actually establishes and why (or whether) it escapes the circularity charge, and (c) address whether there are any grounds for confidence in PA's consistency that don't just consist in a proof carried out inside some formal system.

R.'s answer: The argument trades on a real theorem but draws too strong a conclusion from it. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that if a theory T is consistent, and is strong enough to formalize a reasonable amount of arithmetic (and satisfies some further technical conditions on how it represents its own proofs), then T cannot prove the sentence Con(T) expressing "T is consistent." This is a conditional claim: it doesn't tell us that PA is in fact inconsistent, or even that this is especially likely — it only tells us that one particular route to reassurance, a proof from within PA itself, isn't available if PA does happen to be consistent. So the theorem's existence isn't itself evidence of a problem with PA specifically; it's a general limitation on what any sufficiently strong theory can do for itself, not a diagnosis of an actual flaw.

Gentzen's proof doesn't fall afoul of this, because it isn't a proof carried out inside PA. What Gentzen actually showed is that Con(PA) follows once you grant transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀, in a system that is strictly stronger than PA in this respect — PA itself cannot prove transfinite induction that far. Since the second incompleteness theorem only forbids a theory from proving its own consistency, and Gentzen's proof is carried out in a different, more powerful theory, there's no circularity: this is exactly the kind of loophole the theorem leaves open, a strictly higher vantage point from which Con(PA) becomes visible even though PA can never reach it unaided. And because the extra assumption Gentzen needs is a concrete, constructively presentable one about well-founded notations for ordinals below ε₀, rather than anything like an appeal to completed infinite totalities, his proof is finitary in just the sense Hilbert demanded, so it fully delivers what Hilbert's program originally wanted for arithmetic, even if the broader program failed for stronger theories.

As for independent grounds: the most basic one is that PA has an intended model, the ordinary natural numbers under the usual successor, addition, and multiplication, and any theory with a model is thereby consistent. Because we can prove within ZFC that this structure exists and satisfies PA's axioms, we get a proof of Con(PA) that is completely independent of Gentzen's ordinal-theoretic argument. So between the model-theoretic route and Gentzen's proof-theoretic one, we now have two independent proofs of Con(PA), which is about as much confirmation as any mathematical claim ever gets, and really leaves no serious room for residual doubt.
