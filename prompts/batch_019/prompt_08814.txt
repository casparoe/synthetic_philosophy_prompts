I'm writing a piece on the philosophical dispute buried inside the Kelly criterion, and I'd like to dramatize it rather than just explain it. Imagine a friend who has been offered as many repetitions of a favorable bet as she likes — a coin weighted to land heads 60% of the time, paying even money — and who must decide what fraction of her bankroll to stake on each round. She puts the question to two advisers. Write a dialogue between them: one is a partisan of geometric rationality, who argues for the Kelly fraction on the grounds that it almost surely maximizes the long-run growth rate of any single, actual trajectory of wealth, whereas ordinary expected-value reasoning silently averages her outcome across parallel worlds she will never live through. The other, arguing in Samuelson's tradition, presses back that "almost sure" dominance in the limit is not the same as what is rational for someone who only gets to run history once, and that the Kelly fraction is utility-maximizing only for someone whose risk preferences happen to be exactly logarithmic — for anyone else, a different fraction is the rational choice, not a mistake. Make them answer each other's sharpest objection rather than restate their own position, work through actual numbers, and don't let either one concede.
