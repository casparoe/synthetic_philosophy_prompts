and here's the student's answer:

"Hempel's paradox comes from two principles that both look obviously true on their own. The Equivalence Condition (EC) says that if some evidence confirms a hypothesis, it also confirms any hypothesis logically equivalent to it. Nicod's criterion says a generalization like 'all ravens are black' is confirmed by any raven that turns out to be black, and is simply irrelevant to anything that isn't a raven at all. The problem is that 'all ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'all non-black things are non-ravens,' which by Nicod's criterion is confirmed by any non-black thing that turns out to be a non-raven — a white shoe, a red herring, a green apple. By EC, the red herring has to confirm 'all ravens are black' too, which seems crazy, since finding a red herring in a fish market tells you nothing about the color of ravens.

Hempel's own solution is to give up the Equivalence Condition and restrict confirmation to positive instances of the hypothesis exactly as it's phrased, so that only ravens count as evidence one way or the other for 'all ravens are black.' That preserves the intuitive verdict that the herring is irrelevant, and lets him keep Nicod's criterion for the cases we actually care about.

The probabilistic treatments take the opposite tack: they grant that the herring really does confirm the raven hypothesis, but only by an infinitesimally small amount, since there are vastly more non-black things than ravens, so one more non-black non-raven barely moves the probability compared to what one more black raven would do. I find the probabilistic story more convincing, since it actually explains why the paradox feels so wrong — we mistake a tiny effect for a zero effect — rather than just declaring one of the two principles false to begin with."
