I'm putting together next week's lecture for my grad seminar on population ethics and wanted to sanity check how I've been framing Parfit's non-identity problem, becuase I got some funny looks from colleagues when I ran it by them at the workshop lunch yesterday. My gloss has always gone like this: take the fourteen-year-old girl case — she has a kid now instead of waiting a decade like she's advised to, the kid has a genuinely rough start but still ends up with a life that's clearly worth living, and crucially, if she'd waited she would've conceived a totally different child. Since the actual kid can't point to some "better-off" version of himself that he's being unfavorably compared against (that other possible kid never would've existed at all), there's nobody who ends up worse off than they otherwise would've been, so on the standard comparative picture of harm, nobody's actually been harmed or wronged. That's basically Parfit's point right, that once you trace through the identity-switching carefully the wrongness just evaporates, which is why the whole thing only looks like a "problem" if you haven't fully thought it through?

I've been using this in class to tell students that cases like picking which embryo to implant, or just having a kid a bit earlier than ideal, shouldn't trouble our conscience the way people instinctively assume — it's basically a comparative illusion. But my colleague Alicia kept insisting I had the upshot completely backwards, and that Parfit never actually let go of the verdict that the girl does something wrong, which confused me even more since I thought "no harm, no foul" basically was the chapter's punchline. Am I misremembering how Parfit actually lands on this, or is my way of teaching it basically fine?
