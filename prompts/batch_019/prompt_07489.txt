I'm a PhD student in econ working on epistemic foundations of game theory for my second-year paper, and I've been stuck for weeks on the "backward induction paradox" in a way most people who namedrop it don't seem to actually engage with. Everyone knows the shallow version: backward induction says take the money at the very first node of the centipede game, but real people in the lab keep passing for a few rounds, ha ha, theory doesn't match behavior. That's not what's bugging me. What's bugging me is the deeper thing Pettit and Sugden were pointing at (and that Aumann tried to dissolve, with Binmore and Stalnaker basically saying he cheated): the backward induction argument needs common knowledge of rationality to hold at every node, including ones that, by the argument's own logic, should literally never be reached if both players are rational. So once your opponent has passed two or three times instead of grabbing the cash, what are you actually entitled to believe about them going forward, and doesn't the whole backward-induction chain quietly assume an answer to that question it has no real right to?

Could you write me a dialogue about this? Set it up concretely: two people are sitting across from each other actually playing the centipede game for real stakes, pot growing each round, and right after one of them passes on some middle round instead of taking, they both stop and start arguing about whether backward induction really was the uniquely rational thing to have done, and whether it still tells them what to do now. One character should defend it hard, happy to bite the bullet that rationality is a standing property of a whole strategy rather then something that gets falsified by history, so there's nothing incoherent about reasoning through nodes neither player will ever actually reach. The other should be just as sharp and push back that this basically defines the paradox away — that a genuinely sequential game has to let you update on what you observe, and that "what a still-rational opponent would do here" is exactly the notion backward induction can't cash out once we're at a node its own premises say shouldn't exist. I don't want either side strawmanned, and I want them actually engaging each other's specific moves rather than just restating their starting positions back and forth — and it's fine, maybe even better, if it doesn't resolve neatly, since I don't think the actual literature has resolved it either.
