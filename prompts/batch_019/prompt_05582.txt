So I've been falling down a bit of a rabbit hole on Otto Neurath for a piece on the history of infographics (apparently that whole "repeat a little icon of a person five times instead of drawing one big person" trick you see in every airport pictogram and news graphic today traces back to his Isotype project from 1920s Vienna). Neurath was pretty explicit that he saw this "picture language" as something better than ordinary "word language" for conveying statistical and social facts — more universal, less prone to the vagueness and metaphysical mischief he though ordinary words invite, easier for people across languages and literacy levels to actually grasp. But then I remembered that Wittgenstein, who was floating around that same Vienna Circle milieu, has his own famous "picture theory" in the Tractatus, where he says every meaningful proposition is a picture of a state of affairs, and that it manages to depict reality by sharing its logical form with it. Which got me stuck: are these two guys using "picture" to mean basically the same kind of thing dressed up differently (one literal, one metaphorical), or are they actually built on pretty different theories of representation?

Can you compare the two for me properly — not the wikipedia-level "one drew icons, one wrote a dense book" version, but the actual theoretical commitments underneath? Like, is Neurath's picture language ultimately just a system of conventional signs (icons that could in principle be as arbitrary as words, just easier to standardize across borders), or is it secretly doing something closer to what Wittgenstein wants, where the representing works because the picture's structure literally mirrors the structure of the fact it's depicting? And whichever way that shakes out, who ends up with the more defensible account of why a "picture" (in either sense) is a good vehicle for objective knowledge — Neurath's practical, pedagogical version, or Wittgenstein's logical one? I keep going back and forth on it and would love an actual argued answer rather than a "both raise interesting points" cop-out, my editors already warned me about that one.
