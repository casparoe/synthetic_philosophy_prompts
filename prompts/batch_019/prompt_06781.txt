I'm writing my AP Research paper on whether aging itself should be classified as a disease, which turns out to be a genuinely live debate among biogerontologists — some of them think drug regulators are too quick to wave aging off as "just normal" instead of treating it as a pathology worth targeting directly. While working through the literature review, I ran into the broader philosophical debate this argument is quietly sitting on top of: naturalism versus normativism about health and disease. Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory is the classic naturalist position: a disease is present when some part or process of the body is functioning at a statistically subnormal level compared to other members of your reference class, where the reference class is fixed by your species, age, and sex. Lennart Nordenfelt's welfare theory is the normativist alternative that keeps getting cited against him: health is really a matter of whether you have the ability, under standard circumstances, to realize your "vital goals," which lets values back into the definition on purpose.

Here's the specific snag I can't get past on my own, and it's the part I actually want help with, rather than a summary of the two positions. Because Boorse's reference classes are age-relative, an 84-year-old's declining kidneys, hearing, or muscle mass count as statistically normal for an 84-year-old and are therefore, strictly speaking, not disease, no matter how severe the decline gets (my grandfather makes this exact joke every time a doctor frowns at his labs). But the biogerontologists who want aging medicalized argue that its sheer universality is precisely why it should count as pathology rather than an excuse to dismiss it — a pathogen that infected every single member of a population wouldn't stop being a disease just because everyone had it. So does Boorse's theory simply deliver the wrong verdict on aging, or is "aging isn't disease, it's just the single biggest risk factor for nearly every disease" actually the correct answer his theory is designed to give?

I'd like you to push on this rather than just restate it. Boorse's notion of "normal function" is supposed to be grounded in what natural selection actually shaped a body part to do, not in raw present-day statistics, so maybe that is his escape route for handling any universally-distributed condition. But that route seems to run into real trouble with aging specifically, since evolutionary theories of senescence like antagonistic pleiotropy and mutation accumulation suggest natural selection was never strongly shaping how the body holds up past reproductive age in the first place — so on Boorse's own naturalistic grounds, doesn't age-related decline start to look like a genuine design failure rather than a healthy default? Then walk me through how Nordenfelt's theory handles the same case, and whether tying health to "vital goals" actually resolves this dispute or just relocates it into an equally contestable argument about what counts as a "standard circumstance" or a "vital goal" for someone elderly. I want an actual verdict on which theory gives the more defensible answer, not just a balanced list of considerations on each side.
