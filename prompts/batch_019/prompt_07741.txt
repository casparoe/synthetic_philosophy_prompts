I'm taking an advanced seminar on practical reason this semester, and this week's readings paired Bernard Williams's "Internal and External Reasons" with John McDowell's reply to it, which left several of us arguing well past the end of class. Separately, I help produce a small student-run philosophy podcast, and next month's episode features a guest who has built much of her career defending and extending a broadly Williams-style internalism about reasons for action. I've been asked to draft the interview questions, and I'd rather the episode not simply rehearse the internal/external distinction that anyone who has read the SEP entry already knows.

Could you draft eight to ten questions that press on the actual mechanics of the view rather than inviting her to restate it? I'd like at least one question on the "sound deliberative route" from the agent's subjective motivational set (S) and the worry, raised by several of Williams's critics, that once the route is allowed to include imaginative rehearsal, correction of false belief, and sympathetic identification with others, it becomes elastic enough that almost any external-reason claim can be redescribed as internal after the fact — which would make the whole thesis unfalsifiable rather than substantive. I'd also like one question built around Williams's own test cases — the agent who wants a gin and tonic and, believing the glass in front of him contains one, has no real reason to drink what is in fact petrol, and Owen Wingrave, whose family insists he has a reason to join the army even though nothing in his own S would ever lead him there — asking what non-question-begging procedure these cases are supposed to establish, since on the page it can look like Williams simply stipulates the verdict he wants. And I'd like one question on McDowell's reply that a virtuous person's upbringing gives her a kind of "second nature" letting her perceive reasons that a callous or indifferent person cannot reach by any deliberation from their existing motivations — pressing on whether internalism can accommodate moral education and character change without quietly redefining an agent's S, after the fact, to contain whatever new sensitivity they happen to acquire. For each of these three, please add a follow-up question keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, including, for the third, the familiar fallback of saying we can still call an unmoved person cruel or insensitive without thereby attributing to them any reason they failed to act on. Close the list with a single question asking her, in her own words, what sort of case she would accept as a genuine counterexample to internalism.
