I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on medieval and early modern theories of self-awareness, and this semester I'm also TAing a graduate seminar that reads Avicenna's psychology alongside Descartes' Meditations. Inevitably, we keep returning to the flying man argument from Book I of the Kitab al-Nafs, the psychological portion of his philosophical summa al-Shifa: Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created whole and fully rational, but suspended in a void, unable to see or hear anything, with his limbs held apart so that no part of his body ever touches another and nothing is felt from outside. Such a person, Avicenna insists, would still affirm the existence of his own self while affirming nothing about the existence of a body, hands, or any other organ — from which he concludes that the self is not identical with the body.

After a particularly contentious seminar session, a few of us ended up arguing for another hour over coffee about an objection our instructor had pressed hard in class: that this inference echoes the standard complaint against Descartes' own real-distinction argument in the Sixth Meditation, and that Toby Mayer has pressed close to this very objection against Avicenna's argument specifically. The worry is that the flying man's failure to notice or affirm a body is merely an epistemic or introspective fact, and that it is invalid to infer from it the metaphysical conclusion that the self is really distinct from the body — precisely the gap Arnauld exposed in Descartes by noting that someone can clearly and distinctly perceive a right triangle while doubting the Pythagorean theorem, without this showing that the theorem is inessential to the triangle. I suspect the objection moves too quickly once the argument is reconstructed along lines suggested by commentators like Marmura and Kaukua, who read the flying man as making a claim about the primitive, non-inferential structure of self-awareness itself rather than as a bare conceivability argument, but I haven't managed to make this case cleanly on my own.

Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that on its most defensible reconstruction the flying man argument does not commit the conceivability-to-possibility fallacy that afflicts the parallel Cartesian argument, and that it therefore succeeds in establishing that the self is not identical with the body, even though it may fall short of establishing full substance dualism on its own.
