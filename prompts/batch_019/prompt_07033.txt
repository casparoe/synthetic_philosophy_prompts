I am a PhD student in epistemology working on the debate between reductionist and anti-reductionist accounts of testimonial justification, and I have noticed that the standard illustrative cases in this literature—a stranger giving directions, a child learning facts from a parent, a report read in a newspaper—all involve a hearer who plausibly already has enough background inductive evidence about the reliability of testimony that Humean reductionists and Reidian anti-reductionists end up agreeing on the verdict anyway, leaving the disagreement without much practical bite. Construct a single, detailed case, not drawn from the standard literature, in which a reductionist and an anti-reductionist would clearly diverge in their verdict on whether the hearer's resulting belief is justified: the hearer should have little or no independent inductive basis for trusting the source (ideally some antecedent reason for wariness), while still meeting the anti-reductionist's conditions for a basic entitlement to trust testimony (a sincere, competent assertion, with no specific defeaters present). Close by stating, for each theory, exactly which feature of the case does the work in generating its verdict.
