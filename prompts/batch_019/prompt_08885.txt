As a novelist working on a character who abandons a settled life to pursue an artistic vocation, I want to be sure the philosophical ground beneath the decision is sound. L.A. Paul's account of transformative experience holds that certain life-altering choices resist rational appraisal in advance, since their value can only be disclosed by undergoing them, never calculated beforehand. Bernard Williams's discussion of moral luck, built around a Gauguin-like figure who leaves his family for painting, argues instead that such a choice can only be vindicated retrospectively, through a success the agent could never have secured by deliberation alone. Compare these two accounts of why such momentous choices elude rational justification beforehand. Do Paul and Williams locate the same underlying obstacle to rational choice under different descriptions, or two genuinely distinct problems? And on either view, is the agent entitled to make the leap before knowing how things turn out, or only forgiven for it afterward?
