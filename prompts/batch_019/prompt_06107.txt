I've somehow ended up the token humanist on my university's cross-departmental Research Priorities Committee, mostly because nobody could accuse a philosopher of favoring either side in a fight that's been simmering in the physics department for two years now. We've got money for exactly one new tenure-track line in "foundations of quantum gravity," and the two finalists come out of what are basically rival research programs: one does string theory, the other loop quantum gravity. The internal referee letters are wild to read side by side. The string theorists' letter says the LQG candidate's papers don't even engage with the real problem (unifying gravity with the rest of the Standard Model) and that background independence is basically a substitute for actual results. The LQG letter says the string candidate's work is elegant math with zero empirical traction, and that "unification" is a metaphysical prejudice dressed up as a criterion. It's not that the two letters disagree about who's better, they don't even agree on what would count as a good reason to prefer one over the other. Which, if I'm honest, is about as clean a real-life case of Kuhnian incommensurability as I've run into outside of teaching Structure to undergrads.

The committee meets next Friday and wants a one-to-two page memo from me. I'm supposed to be the "process person" and stay neutral on the actual physics, but they still want a real recommendation out of me, not a shrug dressed up in academic caution. Could you draft that memo? It should (a) name what's actually going on between these two letters and why its not just an ordinary disagreement about quality, (b) address head-on whether a committee like ours even has a principled, non-question-begging way to compare research programs that don't share standards of what counts as a good result -- I keep half-remembering Kuhn's later stuff about accuracy, simplicity and fruitfulness functioning as shared values even without a shared algorithm for applying them, but I'm honestly not sure that gets us all the way out of the hole -- and (c) actually recommend a course of action -- hire A, hire B, split the line, extend the search, whatever -- with a rough timeframe, and it needs to take the losing side's best objection seriously instead of just picking a winner and rationalizing it afterward.
