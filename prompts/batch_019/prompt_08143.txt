Since I retired (spent my career as a field ecologist, mostly shorebirds) I've been filling the extra hours with philosophy, and a few months back a former postdoc of mine who now works in AI safety got me poking around the decision-theory corner of that world. Lately I've been stuck on this idea of "acausal threats" -- the notion that a sufficiently good predictor could effectively blackmail an agent with zero causal contact between them, purely by simulating or reasoning its way to a good model of that agent, sort of a dark mirror of Newcomb's problem. Everyone in that literature seems to agree the right move is to precommit to being the sort of agent that never caves to a threat, since that makes threatening you pointless from the start. But then there's a further wrinkle called "surrogate goals": the agent also cooks up some goal it doesn't actually hold, loudly commits to caving on attacks against THAT fake goal instead, and hopes any threatener goes for the decoy rather than what it really values.

I mentioned this to my philosophy discussion group and nobody could tell me if its actually supposed to hold up under scrutiny. My gut reaction, for what its worth, is that this is just the game-theoretic version of a killdeer's broken-wing display -- I watched that trick work on foxes more times than I can count out in the field. But a fox isn't running a model of the bird's decision procedure, it just sees an easy meal in front of it. A threatener capable of pulling off acausal blackmail in the first place presumably can model its target well enough to notice the surrogate goal is fake, or at least to ask why it should believe the target wouldn't also cave for real once pushed hard enough. So could you write me a proper essay on this: lay out the actual mechanism that's supposed to make surrogate goals work, give the strongest arguments for and against them as a genuine solution to the acausal-threat problem (as opposed to plain threat-resistance with no decoy at all), and land on an actual verdict. If your answer ends up conditional, fine, but tell me exactly what it's conditional on -- don't leave me with a shrug.
