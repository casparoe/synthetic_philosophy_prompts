For the theoretical chapter of my dissertation on moral distress among clinicians, I'm trying to work out which of two classic defenses of the reality of moral dilemmas is more defensible. Bernard Williams, in "Ethical Consistency," grounds the case for genuine dilemmas in the phenomenology of "moral remainder": the obligation an agent overrides for good reason doesn't simply disappear but persists as a source of legitimate regret, which he treats as evidence against any theory that resolves every conflict without residue. Ruth Barcan Marcus, in "Moral Dilemmas and Consistency," relocates the issue to logic instead, arguing that a moral code counts as consistent as long as some possible world exists in which all its rules are jointly satisfiable, so that a dilemma produced by the actual world's bad luck is compatible with, rather than a symptom of, a rational moral code. Which of these two strategies gives the better reason to think the guilt clinicians report in such cases is rational rather than a mere psychological artifact, and why?
