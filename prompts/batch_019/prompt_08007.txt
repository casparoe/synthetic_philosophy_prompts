For a project in my math class, I've been doing some independent reading on the history of the continuum hypothesis (CH), and I've come across a question that isn't really mathematical. Georg Cantor conjectured CH in 1878 (it later became the first of Hilbert's twenty-three famous problems) and, convinced it had to be a genuine theorem, spent much of his career trying and failing to prove it. This fit into a broader conviction that the transfinite numbers were objectively real - a view serious enough that he corresponded with Catholic theologians about how his mathematical "Transfinite" related to the theological "Absolute Infinite." Cantor died in 1918, long before Gödel showed in 1940 that CH cannot be disproved from the standard axioms of set theory (ZFC), and Cohen showed in 1963 that it cannot be proved from them either - together establishing that CH is simply independent of ZFC.

Given how personally invested Cantor was in CH having one definite, discoverable answer, how do you think he would have reacted to that outcome? Would his realism about the infinite have pushed him toward something like Hugh Woodin's ongoing search for new axioms that might settle CH after all, or do you think he could have been persuaded toward a more deflationary "multiverse" view on which the question simply has no single fixed answer? Please commit to your own best-supported prediction rather than just listing possibilities.
