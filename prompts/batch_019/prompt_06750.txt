I've been working through Leibniz on my own, and two of his commitments seem to me to bear directly on a puzzle from modern logic that he could never have encountered. The first is his view that necessary truths can always be reduced, in finitely many steps, to an explicit identity, while contingent truths depend on an infinite analysis that only an infinite understanding (God's) could ever complete. The second is his dream of a universal characteristic paired with a calculus of reasoning exact enough that any dispute could eventually be settled by calculation rather than argument. The puzzle is what's now called the "problem of logical omniscience": because epistemic logic represents knowledge using "possible worlds," a notion that itself traces back to Leibniz's own metaphysics, it turns out to be a mathematical necessity that any agent modeled as knowing something also knows every logical truth and every consequence of what it knows, however hard that consequence would actually be to work out. Do you think Leibniz's finite/infinite-analysis distinction gives him real resources for explaining why finite reasoners fall short of this kind of omniscience, or would his own calculus-of-reasoning project be just as exposed to the problem as any modern formal system for representing knowledge?
