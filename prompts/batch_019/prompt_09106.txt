And here's the student's answer, exactly as Mara would find it stapled to the exam:

"Bradley's regress starts from the worry that a relational fact needs more than just its two terms. If Socrates and wisdom are simply juxtaposed, we have a list, not a fact; something has to relate them together into a single state of affairs. So we add a relation R. But now R has to connect to Socrates on one side and to wisdom on the other, and this connecting is itself something further that has to happen -- so we need R1 to tie R to Socrates and R2 to tie R to wisdom, and then further relations to tie those in turn, and so on forever. Bradley's conclusion in Appearance and Reality is that relations, taken as items additional to and separate from their terms, are incoherent, and since this is true of relations generally, the many distinct 'things' of ordinary experience cannot be ultimately real in their apparent separateness. Only the Absolute, the single undivided whole in which this multiplicity is somehow taken up and transformed, is fully real. The regress is really an argument for internal relations: if a relation has to be internal to (i.e. grounded in the nature of) its terms rather than an external addition, the terms can no longer be picked out as independent of one another, which is exactly the holism Bradley needs for his monism.

The unity problem in Russell is the same worry wearing different clothes. 'Socrates is wise' can't just be the collection {Socrates, wisdom}, because a collection has no truth-value -- it doesn't say anything. Russell's answer, from the Principles of Mathematics onward, is that the predicate contributes something unsaturated: wisdom, functioning as a predicate rather than a subject, is an incomplete entity that reaches out for something to complete it, and that incompleteness is precisely what welds subject and predicate into a single truth-evaluable whole rather than a mere list, and blocks the Bradley regress because the unifier is not a further separate term in need of its own glue. Russell carried this same idea into his multiple-relation theory of judgement, on which 'Othello believes that Desdemona loves Cassio' is analysed as one many-place relation of believing holding directly between Othello, Desdemona, loving and Cassio, unified in exactly the same way, without needing a single proposition floating between Othello and the world at all."
