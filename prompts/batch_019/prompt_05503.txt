In my seminar on early modern philosophy this term, we spent two weeks on Émilie du Châtelet's "Discourse on Happiness." Two classmates from that seminar, who started seeing each other the week we discussed it, are now getting married, and they have asked me to give the toast, on condition that it engage seriously with du Châtelet rather than merely invoke her name.

That is harder than it sounds. Du Châtelet holds that happiness requires cultivating strong passions rather than extinguishing them, against the Stoics, but that doing so depends on sustaining certain illusions, since a clear-eyed view of how contingent our attachments are would deflate them. She treats romantic passion as inherently temporary, recommending sturdier reserves -- above all study, which alone does not depend on fortune or another person -- for when passion recedes. She wrote all this from within a marriage of convenience, alongside a long attachment to Voltaire and, in her final years, a passionate one with a younger poet: hardly an obvious authority on lifelong monogamous devotion.

I don't want a toast that quietly launders her view into greeting-card sentiment, nor one that reads as a seminar recap at my friends' wedding. Please write the toast itself: about three minutes long, warm enough to work at the reception, but honest to what she actually argued, awkward parts included. Half the guests were in the seminar and will notice if her position gets flattened; the rest just want a good toast.
