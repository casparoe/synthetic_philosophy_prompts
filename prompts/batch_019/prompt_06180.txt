Ok so this has been rattling around in my head for a while and I can't quite resolve it myself. A few years back I read through Kokotajlo's old LW post on the "commitment races problem" (I think that's the right author, might be misremembering) -- the worry that if agents can gain a bargaining advantage by locking in a stance before their opponents do, then everyone's incentivized to commit earlier and earlier, even before they've reasoned carefully about anything, which risks locking in disastrous threats or promises forever. It's usually framed as a scary-ish problem specific to multi-agent AI safety, tangled up with stuff like logical time, updatelessness, and agents wanting to self modify into being early commiters before they get "corrupted" by more information.

Then last month my book club worked through some of Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict, and it struck me that this is basically the oldest move in the game theory playbook: burn your bridges, make the threat physically irrevocable, and thereby make it credible -- in a game of Chicken, whoever commits first (visibly and irrevocably) wins. A friend from my meetup insists the AI version is a genuinely distinct phenomenon and not just Schelling dressed up in new jargon, but the best he could offer was "the agents are smarter and the stakes are civilizational," which feels like its begging the question rather than answering it.

So could you actually compare the two for me: the "commitment races problem" as its discussed in the AI-alignment/rationalist blogosphere, versus Schelling's classic account of commitment tactics in bargaining. Where do they really overlap, and in which specific ways (if any) is the commitment races problem a genuinely new phenomenon rather than just an instance of Schelling-style commitment with extra flavor text? I'm especially curious whether stuff like logical time/logical priority, the possibility of self-modification, and the difference between one unified-ish superintelligence versus two haggling humans or nations actually change the underlying logic, or just the surface presentation.
