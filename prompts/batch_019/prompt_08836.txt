I've been reporting on the culture around professional forecasting tournaments, and at a closed-door retreat last month I watched a routine disagreement curdle into a genuine argument about epistemology -- one I still haven't settled in my own mind. Two of the sharpest people in the room, whom I'll call the Forecaster and the Theorist, had each submitted a probability on the same question that quarter (something geopolitical; the substance doesn't matter here) and landed more than thirty points apart. They swapped their full reasoning. Neither moved. Reconstructed from my notes, the exchange went roughly like this:

(1) The Forecaster said the standoff was itself irrational: per Aumann's theorem, two Bayesians with the same priors who have common knowledge of each other's posteriors cannot rationally go on disagreeing, so if the Theorist wouldn't converge, the Theorist wasn't reasoning in good faith.

(2) The Theorist replied that the theorem only bites under common priors, and nothing obliges two honest reasoners to start from the same one; a standing gap in priors is evidence of different starting points, not of bad faith.

(3) The Forecaster answered that a prior gap with no traceable source in any actual difference of information or experience is exactly what should raise suspicion rather than be shrugged off -- an unexplained difference in priors is the kind of thing the theorem is meant to flag as a symptom of hidden bias.

(4) The Theorist said that might be true in the abstract but proved nothing here, since even granting common priors, nothing in Aumann's result, or in the later work on iterated exchanges of opinion, guarantees convergence after a single round, let alone convergence toward the Forecaster's number specifically rather than some third value neither had yet reached.

(5) The Forecaster said fine, but this hadn't been a single round -- they had traded full reasoning four times over the course of the evening, and the Theorist's number hadn't moved by a single point; that much unmoved ground after that much shared information was itself the tell, since the theorem presumes each side is genuinely crediting the other's rationality and candor, and the Theorist's imperviousness suggested otherwise.
