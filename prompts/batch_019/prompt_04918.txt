I spent thirty-odd years as a field ecologist, mostly studying reproductive strategies in fish and amphibians, and philosophy was never really on my radar until I retired a couple years ago and suddenly had time to read outside my own field. Lately I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole reading about "suffering-focused" ethics and negative utilitarianism, partly thanks to some old Effective Altruism forum threads a former grad student of mine sent over, and partly thanks to one of Magnus Vinding's books on the subject. If you've ever watched a female sunfish release ten thousand eggs knowing full well that maybe two or three will make it to adulthood, you get a pretty visceral sense of how casually nature produces and discards suffering, so the core intuition behind these views is pretty compelling to me -- there does seem to be some kind of asymmetry between pain and pleasure that classical utilitarianism just doesn't capture. But I keep loosing track of which version of the view is actually supposed to be the strongest, since apparently there's about a dozen flavors of it and they all seem to bite different bullets.

So here's what I'm hoping you can help with: rank the following six positions from most to least philosophically defensible, using whatever criteria seem relevant to you (internal consistency, ability to handle nasty counterexamples, fit with widely shared moral intuitions, whatever). A sentence or two of reasoning for the placements you're genuinely torn on would be great, but I don't need a full essay. The positions are: (1) strong/lexical negative utilitarianism, on which reducing suffering has absolute priority over increasing happiness, so no amount of happiness could ever outweigh any amount of suffering; (2) weak or "negative-leaning" utilitarianism, where both matter but suffering gets a heavier weight in the sum; (3) tranquilism, on which the only thing of ultimate value is the presence or absence of craving, so a pleasurable experience is good only insofar as it stills some craving; (4) antifrustrationism, a la Christoph Fehige, on which only frustrated preferences count as bad and creating a brand new satisfied preference out of nothing has no positive value; (5) threshold negative utilitarianism, on which suffering only registers morally once it crosses some minimum severity, so mild discomfort doesn't count at all; and (6) plain old classical, symmetric hedonistic utilitarianism, thrown in as a baseline so I can see how far the others have drifted from it.
