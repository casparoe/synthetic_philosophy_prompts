The question was: "Reconstruct Wittgenstein's private language argument, using both the diary/'S' example (§258) and the beetle-in-the-box passage (§293). What, precisely, does the argument establish, and does it succeed?"

Here's the submission I have to grade:

"Wittgenstein's target is the idea that I could have a language only I can understand, because its words refer to my own private sensations. In the diary case he imagines someone who wants to track a sensation by writing 'S' in a calendar every time it recurs. The catch is that there's no way to check he's using 'S' correctly the second time, since the only thing he could check it against is his own memory of the first time, and a memory isn't a fact that can be checked against anything else. So there's no fact of the matter about whether he's using 'S' correctly -- as Wittgenstein puts it, 'whatever is going to seem right to me is right,' which he says just means we can't really talk about 'right' here at all.

The beetle-in-the-box passage makes the same point differently: if everyone had a box only they could look into, and called whatever was inside 'a beetle,' the word 'beetle' couldn't mean the thing in the box, since the box's contents could vary wildly, or be empty, without changing how anyone actually uses the word. Wittgenstein's conclusion is that there's no such thing as private, subjective experience in the sense the Cartesian picture wants -- the 'inner' is nothing over and above its outward expression.

His deeper point is that meaning is constituted by a community's shared practice of using a word correctly, which is exactly why a solitary individual could never really follow a rule on his own -- there'd be nothing to distinguish him from someone who merely thinks he's following it. That's why the argument is usually read as an argument for a fundamentally social conception of meaning."
