I'm a software engineer and serve as technical lead on my fintech company's model risk committee, which has to sign off on any algorithm used in credit decisions before it can go live. We're currently reviewing a vendor's default-risk score for a new lending product. The vendor's own audit shows the score is well-calibrated: applicants from our two largest demographic groups who receive the same score default at essentially the same rate. An independent audit we commissioned found something else: among applicants who did not default, those in the group with the higher overall default rate were denied credit or given worse terms at nearly twice the rate of non-defaulting applicants in the other group. Our fair-lending policy bars any practice with a 'disparate and unjustified' impact on a protected class, but it doesn't say which of these two properties -- equal accuracy per score, or equal error rates across groups -- should decide whether an impact counts as 'unjustified.' Half the committee wants the model pulled as discriminatory; the other half says the calibration audit already establishes fairness, and that the disparity is an unavoidable mathematical consequence of the two groups' differing default rates, not a flaw more data would fix. Both sides insist the math backs them, and I don't have the formal background to referee that myself. Please write the one-page memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting: recommend approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions (stating the conditions and a re-audit deadline if applicable), and give the strongest version of the argument for whichever side you don't recommend, rather than treating this as a dispute that better data or a smarter model could dissolve.
