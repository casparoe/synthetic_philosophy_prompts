I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been getting into the subject lately through a philosophy podcast I listen to on my commute. A recent episode covered David Lewis's modal realism, and as I understood it, Lewis argued that every possible world is just as concrete and real as the one we happen to inhabit, and that calling our world "actual" doesn't mark it out as metaphysically special — it functions more like an indexical term such as "here" or "now," which simply picks out wherever the speaker happens to be. This struck me as essentially the same claim made by the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, which I'd already encountered through a couple of popular physics books: every time a measurement occurs, the universe branches into multiple outcomes, each equally real, with no branch privileged as the "true" one. When I mentioned the parallel to a friend who is finishing a philosophy MA, she said I was conflating two quite different things, but she was on her way out the door and didn't have time to explain further.

Given that a number of respected physicists are now comfortable asserting that these branching worlds physically exist, it seems to me that modal realism has essentially received empirical backing from quantum mechanics, even if philosophers rarely frame it that way. Both positions, after all, commit to the same startling idea: that reality isn't exhausted by the single world we can observe, and that our world's apparent uniqueness is a matter of perspective rather than metaphysical priority. So is there a real, principled distinction between what Lewis is proposing and what many-worlds physicists are proposing, or is the difference mostly a matter of academic territory — physics being taken seriously as "real science" while a structurally similar idea in metaphysics gets waved off as speculative armchair theorizing? I would like to understand whether I am missing something substantive here before I keep using many-worlds as supporting evidence for Lewis's view in conversations with friends.
