Ok so this is going to sound like a weird rabbit hole but hear me out. At work we had this whole retro about why literally every team ended up standardizing on the same internal tool even though half the engineers who "chose" it later admitted they never actually compared it against the alternatives -- they just saw two or three senior devs adopt it early on and took that as enough signal. Someone on the thread said this is a classic case of an "information cascade" / "rational herding" and linked some old econ papers (Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, I think? early 90s), so I spent a chunk of the weekend reading summaries of the model instead of doing anything productive (definately did not mean for that to happen).

The setup they use to explain it is this urn-and-balls game: everyone privately draws a colored ball as a noisy hint about which of two urns is "the real" one, then has to publicly guess, but you only ever see what everyone before you guessed, not what ball they actually drew. Apparently the punchline is that pretty fast it becomes individually rational for person N to just copy the majority guess and ignore their own ball entirely, because the accumulated guesses of everyone before them are stronger evidence than their one private draw. Ok fine, that part clicked. What's messing with my head is the next claim: because everyone after that point is just copying instead of contributing new info, the whole "consensus," no matter how many hundreds of people pile into it, is really only ever backed by the private info of the first couple of people -- and that's apparently exactly why these cascades are so easy to knock over with a tiny scrap of new public information, even after a long unbroken streak of everyone agreeing. That feels completely backwards to me, shouldn't more people agreeing make something more locked-in, not less?

What I'm really asking for isn't the math, it's the actual gut-level intuition: can you unpack why a rational cascade ends up so informationally thin and reversible despite looking like rock-solid consensus from the outside? I'd also love help seeing why that's mechanically different from ordinary bandwagon / network-effect herding (like a tool becoming genuinely more useful the more coworkers already use it) -- since from the outside both just look like "everyone copied everyone else," but I gather economists treat them as pretty different animals. And if you want bonus points: the thing that bugs me most isn't really economics, it's more of a rationality puzzle -- every single person in the cascade is behaving perfectly rationally given what they can see, and yet the group as a whole ends up worse-informed than if people had just gone with their own private hunches. I don't have good intuitions for how a chain of individually-sound reasoning turns into a collectively dumb outcome like that.
