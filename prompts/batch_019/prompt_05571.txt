This semester I am taking a seminar called "Rational Choice and the Self," and for the past few weeks we have been working through L. A. Paul's writing on transformative experience: her argument that certain choices (she leans heavily on the examples of becoming a vampire and of deciding to have a child) resist ordinary expected-value reasoning, because living through the experience would remake the very standpoint from which you would later judge whether it had been worth it. The distinction I keep losing hold of in discussion section is between that claim and mere uncertainty. It is not just that the chooser does not know how things will turn out, the way one might not know whether a new job will work out; even complete information about the outcome would not tell her what the experience would be like from the inside, or what she would come to value once she had lived through it. Our professor keeps pointing out that most of us quietly collapse this into ordinary risk when we write about it, so I would like to test whether I actually grasp the distinction by watching it play out in a story rather than by producing another response paper that just re-describes the argument.

Could you write me a short story of roughly 800 to 1,200 words built around a character facing a single irreversible decision that is transformative in Paul's specific sense: epistemically, in that she cannot know in advance what the relevant experience is like, and personally, in that living through it would reshape her preferences and self-conception rather than just her information. I would rather you not use the vampire case or the decision to have a child, since those are the two examples from the reading and I would like to see the idea worked out somewhere fresh — perhaps a one-way colonization mission, an irreversible neurological procedure, an irrevocable religious vow, or any scenario you think dramatizes the puzzle better than I could specify in advance. It would help if at least one other character pressed the protagonist to reason about the decision in ordinary cost-benefit terms, as though she could simply weigh the likely pros and cons, so that the story can show rather than assert why that framework fails to get a grip here. If you can end on the moment of decision itself, without narrating whether it turned out to be "worth it," that would sit better with the philosophical point than a tidy resolution would.
