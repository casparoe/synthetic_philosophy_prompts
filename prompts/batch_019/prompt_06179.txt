Our school's effective altruism club recently spent a session on AI alignment, and we ended up spending most of the time on Eliezer Yudkowsky's proposal of "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV): rather than programming a superintelligent AI with a fixed rulebook or a hand-picked set of values, you have it act on an extrapolation of what humanity would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wish we were, and had had more time and opportunity to grow together and resolve our disagreements. Purely by coincidence, in my philosophy elective the following week we covered informed-desire (or "idealized-preference") theories of well-being, and one of the standard objections we discussed was what's sometimes called the authority (or alienation) problem: the worry that the fully informed, idealized version of a person can end up so far removed from that person's actual psychology and history that there's no obvious reason why its preferences should carry any special normative weight for the real, un-idealized person.

I haven't been able to stop thinking about how closely these two ideas line up, and I'd like to turn the connection into an essay. Could you write one that directly tackles this question: does coherent extrapolated volition inherit the authority/alienation problem that's been raised against idealized-preference theories of well-being, or is there something about how CEV is actually set up — for instance, its emphasis on extrapolating each individual's own dispositions rather than imposing an external standard of what's good for them, or the role of a safeguard like Yudkowsky's proposed "Last Judge" — that lets it dodge the objection in a way ordinary informed-desire theories can't? Please lay out the strongest version of the objection, the best replies available on CEV's behalf, and then actually commit to and defend a verdict, rather than leaving the two sides in equilibrium.
