The question, from a closed-book final on which students had the assigned Groundwork excerpts in front of them but no secondary readings, was: "In the Groundwork, Kant distinguishes an action performed 'from duty' from one merely 'in conformity with duty,' using the example of a shopkeeper who gives correct change even to inexperienced customers who could easily be overcharged. (a) Explain this distinction and why Kant thinks only the former can have moral worth. (b) Explain why, for Kant, moral worth attaches to the agent's motive rather than to the action's consequences or its mere conformity to duty. (c) Present one influential objection to Kant's account of moral worth from the contemporary literature (e.g., from Herman, Arpaly, Markovits, or Schiller), and explain how a Kantian might respond to it."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"Kant's shopkeeper gives correct change to every customer, even children who could easily be cheated, but he does so only because being known as honest is good for business. Since his real motivating reason is self-interest rather than the recognition that honesty is required by the moral law, his action merely conforms to duty; it does what duty requires without being done from duty. A shopkeeper who instead gave correct change because it is his duty to do so, regardless of the effect on his reputation, would be acting from duty, and for Kant only this second shopkeeper's action has moral worth, because moral worth is not a property of what is done but of why it is done.

This is also why Kant thinks moral worth depends on motive rather than on consequences or mere conformity: two people can perform the identical outward act, with identical outcomes, and yet only one of them deserves moral credit, because credit tracks the will's reason for acting rather than the act's effects on the world or its correspondence with what duty demands. Kant makes essentially the same point with his example of a man who does good for others but has, through his own misfortune, lost all natural sympathy for them; precisely because such a man no longer feels any pull toward helping and helps anyway, we can be sure the action flows from duty and not inclination, which is why Kant treats this case as displaying moral worth in its purest form. Read alongside the shopkeeper case, this suggests that warm feeling and moral worth actually pull in opposite directions for Kant: the more an agent's helpful action can be traced to sympathy rather than to respect for the moral law, the less credit the action deserves.

A well-known objection to this picture comes from Herman's discussion of Twain's Huckleberry Finn, who helps his friend Jim, an escaped slave, get to freedom even though Huck sincerely believes, given how he was raised, that he is doing something low and even sinful by not turning Jim in. Herman argues that Huck's action is nonetheless morally admirable, and uses the case to argue that an agent can act rightly, and even with moral worth, without being motivated by an explicit, conscious motive of duty, so long as the agent's sensibility is reliably tracking the morally relevant features of the situation. A Kantian could respond that Huck's attachment to Jim, even though Huck never conceptualizes it as 'duty,' still expresses an implicit respect for Jim as a person, which is all Kant's motive of duty really requires; on this reading, Huck counts as acting from duty in Kant's sense after all, just without possessing the concept."
