For a feature on the renewed enthusiasm for Nozickian libertarianism among a cluster of venture capitalists who have taken to citing Anarchy, State, and Utopia the way an earlier generation cited Ayn Rand, I've been trying to work out for myself whether the argument that most impresses them -- the Wilt Chamberlain thought experiment -- actually does the work they think it does. I don't mean the seminar-room gloss ("any patterned distribution can be upset by free exchange, so redistribution is a kind of theft"), but the version philosophers actually fought over, and that is where I keep getting stuck, especially once G.A. Cohen's rebuttal enters the picture. Cohen's reply -- later expanded into his book Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality -- grants that maintaining a patterned distribution requires continuous interference with what people do with their holdings, but denies that Nozick has thereby shown this interference to be unjust rather than merely costly. He also presses the question of whether the specific bundle of property rights being exercised in the thought experiment -- the right to set a price, to accept it from a million people, and to keep the proceeds untaxed and pass them on -- is itself secured by Nozick's own principles of just acquisition, or simply assumed.

Write me an essay, pitched at an intelligent general readership but not shy of the technical detail, that takes a side on this and defends it: does the Wilt Chamberlain argument succeed in showing that any non-historical, patterned principle of distributive justice is incompatible with respecting individual liberty, or does Cohen's distinction between a pattern's being costly to maintain and its being unjust to maintain sink the argument? Reconstruct the strongest form of both Nozick's case and Cohen's reply -- including the question of whether the initial distribution the thought experiment starts from is itself secured by a defensible theory of just acquisition, rather than simply stipulated -- and then commit to an answer. A qualified verdict is fine (for instance, "the argument defeats strict equality of outcome but not patterned principles generally"), but "it's more complicated than that" is not something I can print.
