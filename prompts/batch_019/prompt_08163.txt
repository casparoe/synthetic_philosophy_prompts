This semester I'm taking a philosophy of mind course, and we've just finished the unit on Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis: the Otto and Inga case, the parity principle, and the conditions under which an external resource is trusted enough to count as part of someone's memory — constant use, easy access, automatic endorsement, a history of endorsement — along with the standard objection that this confuses mere causal coupling with genuine constitution. My grandmother lived with Alzheimer's for nine years and, like Otto, carried a notebook everywhere: reminders of who people were, that my grandfather had died, what she'd eaten that day, all of which she consulted constantly and never doubted. She passed away two weeks ago, and I've been asked to give a short eulogy, around five minutes, at her memorial this Saturday, to a room that will be almost entirely non-academic family who mostly just find the notebook heartbreaking. My mother keeps saying that, near the end, "her mind was really in that notebook," and I suspect she's onto something real, but after a term spent reading the objections to Clark and Chalmers, I'm wary of asserting that outright as settled fact in front of a grieving family. Could you write the eulogy — one that draws honestly on the extended mind thesis to say something true and comforting about what did and didn't survive of her as her biological memory gave out, without turning into a philosophy lecture or claiming more than the thesis can actually bear?
