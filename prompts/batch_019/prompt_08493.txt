I'm a PhD student working on early modern moral philosophy, currently drafting a chapter on Leibniz's theory of justice. Leibniz holds that justice, in its most universal sense, is "the charity of the wise" (caritas sapientis) — benevolence disciplined by wisdom, and the highest of the three degrees of right he adapts from Roman law, above strict right (neminem laedere, harm no one) and equity (suum cuique tribuere, give each his due). Yet Leibniz is equally committed, via his theodicy, to the thesis that the actual world just is the best of all possible worlds: the one optimal arrangement of compossible substances that a perfectly wise, good, and powerful God could not have failed to select. Please argue that these two commitments are in genuine tension — that an agent who has fully internalized Leibnizian optimism has no principled, non-question-begging reason to exercise charity, since no finite intervention could improve on an arrangement that is already optimal by hypothesis. I'd like you to engage with the strongest reply available to Leibniz here, rather than a version of the objection he could easily dismiss.
