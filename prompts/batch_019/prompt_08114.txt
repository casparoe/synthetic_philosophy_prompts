Quick one from a rabbit hole I fell into this week. Bayesians always say a theory earns extra credit when it successfully predicts the evidence, but the textbook example everyone sites -- general relativity nailing Mercury's perihelion precession -- was already a known anomaly for decades before Einstein showed up. If that data point already had probability 1 for us, plain old Bayes' theorem says conditioning on it can't budge our credence in GR one bit, which clashes with basically every physicists gut sense that this was huge confirmation. I think this is what philosophers call Glymour's "problem of old evidence." Could you describe the most important implications of this problem for how I should weigh it next time a scientist tells me their shiny new model "explains" or "retrodicts" some result that was already on the books, rather than making a genuinely fresh prediction? Am I right to be suspicious of that kind of claim, or have Bayesians actually patched this hole?
