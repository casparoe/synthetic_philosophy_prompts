I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I've become quite absorbed in the cosmological argument after following online debates about it — the kind where someone lays out the kalam version (everything that begins to exist has a cause, the universe began to exist, so the universe has a cause) and someone else objects that an infinite past isn't actually impossible. Wanting to see the argument before William Lane Craig's modern repackaging of it, I tracked down a free translation of the Summa Theologica and read through the question where Aquinas asks directly whether reason alone can prove that the world had a beginning. To my surprise, his answer is no: he says that's something held by faith, not demonstration, which already didn't match how I'd always heard "the cosmological argument" described. What really stopped me was a little further on, in his reply to an objection arguing that if the world were eternal, there would have to be an infinite chain of fathers begetting sons stretching back forever — supposedly just as impossible as an infinite chain of causes. Here is his reply in full, from that translation (this is Summa Theologica I, Question 46, Article 2, Reply to Objection 7, in the old Dominican Fathers translation):

"In efficient causes it is impossible to proceed to infinity per se—thus, there cannot be an infinite number of causes that are per se required for a certain effect; for instance, that a stone be moved by a stick, the stick by the hand, and so on to infinity. But it is not impossible to proceed to infinity "accidentally" as regards efficient causes; for instance, if all the causes thus infinitely multiplied should have the order of only one cause, their multiplication being accidental, as an artificer acts by means of many hammers accidentally, because one after the other may be broken. It is accidental, therefore, that one particular hammer acts after the action of another; and likewise it is accidental to this particular man as generator to be generated by another man; for he generates as a man, and not as the son of another man. For all men generating hold one grade in efficient causes—viz. the grade of a particular generator. Hence it is not impossible for a man to be generated by man to infinity; but such a thing would be impossible if the generation of this man depended upon this man, and on an elementary body, and on the sun, and so on to infinity."

I have reread this more times than I want to admit, and I still can't pin down what is actually doing the work in the per se/accidental distinction. On the surface, both the stick-hand-stone chain and the father-son chain look like one thing causing the next thing in a line, so why does the first need a starting point and the second not? The hammer example confused me even further, since it sounds like Aquinas is saying the hammers were never really one causal series in the first place — and I don't see why that same move couldn't be made about the stick and the hand. I would also like to understand how much this passage is really carrying: is this distinction the actual reason Aquinas doesn't need the universe to have a beginning in time, unlike the kalam argument, or is it a narrower point that just happens to live in this section? And separately, his "argument from contingency" elsewhere in the same work (the one about possible versus necessary beings) often gets treated as interchangeable with this one — does it rely on the same per se/accidental machinery, or does it sidestep the issue entirely? I would rather get a close reading of what Aquinas is actually claiming here than the usual capsule summary of "the Five Ways."
