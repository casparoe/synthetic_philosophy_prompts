Over the past year I've been reporting on the rapid growth of prediction markets—platforms like Polymarket and Kalshi, which let users bet on outcomes ranging from elections to Federal Reserve decisions—and on the related claim, popular among market-design economists, that sufficiently liquid markets with enough participants are unusually good at aggregating dispersed private information into a single, trustworthy number. While fact-checking a draft, an economist I had interviewed pushed back on that framing by invoking a family of results she called "no-trade theorems," which trace back to Aumann's 1976 work on common knowledge and were formalized more explicitly by Paul Milgrom and Nancy Stokey in 1982: under fairly general conditions, rational, risk-averse traders who share a common prior should never be willing to trade with one another purely on the basis of private information, because the mere fact that someone is offering you a trade is itself a signal that ought to make you revise your estimate of its value until any apparent gain disappears.

I've since read enough summaries to follow the outline of the argument, but the proofs lean on iterated reasoning about common knowledge and Bayesian updating in a way that lets me trace each step without quite feeling, in my gut, why the conclusion has to hold so generally. Could you explain the core intuition behind no-trade theorems in plain language, ideally by walking through a small concrete example, in a way that makes clear exactly which assumption is doing the work — common priors, risk aversion, or common knowledge that a trade is being offered — and what changes once that assumption is relaxed? I would also welcome a closing word on how you think this result should shape our confidence in real markets, prediction markets especially, as devices for honestly aggregating what people believe, given that such markets are visibly full of trading activity that the theorem seems to say should not occur.
