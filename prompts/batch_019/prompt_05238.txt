A recurring worry in the rationalist blogosphere's discussions of "modest epistemology" is how much weight you should give to evidence about the general unreliability of reasoning like yours, when that evidence conflicts with an object-level argument that still looks sound on reflection. Formal epistemology has a more precise version of this worry, framed in terms of higher-order evidence: evidence bearing not on some proposition P itself, but on how reliable your assessment of the evidence for P is likely to be.

Consider a case in that vein. You work through a moderately intricate argument and find it fully convincing. You then learn, from a source you have every reason to trust, that you recently took a drug which causes roughly a third of people who take it to make basic reasoning errors, without giving them any subjective sense that something is wrong. Introspectively, the argument still seems airtight to you. If your confidence in its conclusion should drop even so, but the argument keeps striking you as sound on its own terms, you appear to be in a state of epistemic akrasia: believing P while also believing that your belief in P isn't fully rational.

Write an essay that takes a stand on this case and its upshot. Address at least: (a) whether genuine epistemic akrasia can be rationally required by cases like this one, rather than merely a forgivable lapse; (b) the main alternative view, on which the appearance of akrasia dissolves once you allow your object-level and higher-order beliefs to come apart without either one being irrational ("level-splitting"); and (c) what your answer implies for the ordinary, non-toy version of the problem -- how a reasoner should weigh a track record of error, their own or that of people relevantly like them, against an argument that still seems solid under direct inspection. Don't settle for a split-the-difference conclusion: commit to an answer and defend it against the strongest version of the alternative.
