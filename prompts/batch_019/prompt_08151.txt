In my seminar on the epistemology of machine testimony, we have just covered the Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK) problem as Christiano, Cotra, and Xu formulate it, using their well-known vault example: a predictor's reporter module can be trained either as a direct translator of the predictor's own internal beliefs, or as a human simulator that simply outputs whatever a human watching the sensor feed would conclude — two strategies that yield identical answers throughout training, since tampering never occurs until deployment, but that can diverge once it does. To ensure the exercise tests students' grasp of the underlying structure rather than their memory of the vault case, construct a second worked example set in a domain unrelated to vaults, cameras, or physical security. Specify what the predictor is trained to output and what the human labels are based on; explain why a direct translator and a human simulator would be indistinguishable to the labelers on the training distribution you describe; give a realistic deployment scenario in which the two reporters would answer differently; and close by explaining why ordinary loss-minimizing training gives no particular reason to expect the direct translator, rather than the human simulator, to be the one that gets learned.
