I'm on the steering committee of a small writers' collective that runs an online literary journal, and for the past year I've been buried in Kierkegaard for a novel-in-progress set in 1840s Copenhagen, so "Two Ages," the leveling stuff, and the whole Corsair business have been rattling around in my head lately. Funny timing, because we've hit an actual dilemma at the journal that I can't stop reading through that lens. Every published piece on our site has a "reader responses" section open to anonymous comment, and a few months back an anonymous pile-on basically drove one of our newest contributors -- a young writer on maybe her third publication ever -- off the platform after she posted something formally experimental that alot of readers apparently hated. Half the committee now wants to require real names on all comments going forward, essentially arguing (without knowing thats what they're doing) that anonymity lets "the public" form and vent opinions with zero responsibility attached to anyone in particular, which is basically the leveling dynamic Kierkegaard diagnosed in the present age. The other half points out that some of our most genuinely useful critical comments over the years have come from anonymous reviewers who are early-career writers themselves, and who'd never dare criticize a more established contributor under their own name for fear of it costing them later -- so a real-names policy mostly just ends up protecting the powerful from criticism. A couple of people have floated a middle option, verified-but-still-pseudonymous accounts, but nobody's worked out what that would actually look like in practice.

Could you draft the short memo I'd bring to our meeting next week? I want it to actually engage with Kierkegaard's specific critique of the press and the public -- the mechanics of leveling, his account of "the public" as a kind of phantom that has opinions but that no concrete person is ever answerable for, and what his own mauling by the Corsair suggests about how that plays out for an actual individual on the receiving end -- rather than some vague "he distrusted the crowd" gloss. Use all that to argue for one specific policy the committee could actually vote on Thursday, and be honest about the strongest version of the anonymity-protects-the-vulnerable objection, since that's the one I personally find hardest to answer and I don't want the memo to just wave it away.
