A couple of weekends ago I was at a pub quiz with two mates of mine, Colin and Dana, and they got into an argument that I still haven't been able to shake. It was during the picture round, and there was a bonus question where you had to add up our running score in your head to decide whether it was worth risking our last wildcard. Colin worked it out loud, got a number, and was about to commit to it. Dana stopped him and said something like "hang on, you've had four pints, and people who've been drinking are wrong about stuff like this way more then they think they are, so you should probably be less sure of yourself right now." Colin shrugged, redid the sum twice more really carefully, got the exact same number both times, and said he was back to being fully confident again, since he'd basically double-checked it.

Dana said that doesnt actually help, because rechecking your own maths with the same possibly-drunk brain isn't independent confirmation, it's just the same dodgy process marking its own homework. Colin said fine, but if that's true then a drunk person could never be justified in trusting their own sum even when they keep getting the same answer, which seems like a mad conclusion. Dana said that wasn't her point at all — she wasn't saying he was wrong, only that walking around fully confident while also believing the thing about drunk people messing up basic maths means he's holding two beliefs that undercut each other, and that this exact combo apparently has a name, "epistemic akrasia," which she actually googled right there at the table to prove she wasn't making it up. Colin's comeback was that you either believe a number or you don't, nobody actually believes things at "85%" in real time, so telling someone to "just be less confident" isn't a real instruction a person can act on, it's just a fancier way of saying "trust me over yourself."

Neither of them gave any ground and it kind of ruined the rest of the round (we lost the wildcard anyway; for what it's worth, Colin's number turned out to be right). I didn't want to wade in and take sides at the time, and I honestly still don't know who to believe. Could you go through their argument move by move and say who actually had the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Dana's "epistemic akrasia" point is a genuine problem for Colin, or whether he's right that it falls apart as soon as you ask what "just lower your confidence" is even supposed to mean for a real person in the moment. Also, does the fact that he turned out to be right actually count for anything when judging who was being more rational at the time?
