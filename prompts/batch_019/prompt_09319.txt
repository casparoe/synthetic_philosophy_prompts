Ok, so I've fallen down a pretty deep YouTube rabbit hole on quantum entanglement and Bell's theorem lately, and theres something that's been bugging me. Every pop-sci explainer says basically the same thing: Bell's theorem proves the universe is nonlocal, "spooky action at a distance" is real, and Einstein was wrong to want a local universe. But then I found out about this other option called superdeterminism (Sabine Hossenfelder talks about it a lot, and I think Gerard 't Hooft too) where instead of dropping locality, you drop this other, much less talked about assumption in the theorem - the one that says the hidden variables and the measurement settings have to be statistically independent of each other, sometimes called "measurement independence" or the "free choice" assumption. Basically the idea is maybe the experimenter's choice of what to measure isn't as free/random as everyone assumes, and is secretly correlated somehow with whatever's determining the particle's properties.

Everyone I've read just dismisses superdeterminism as basically a conspiracy theory that could be used to explain away literally any experimental result ever, and honestly a lot of those dismissals feel more like people repeating each other than actually grappling with the argument. So here's what I want: can you write the strongest possible case FOR superdeterminism (i.e. for giving up measurement independence) being the more reasonable response to Bell's theorem, rather than giving up locality? I don't want a "well, both sides have some merit" cop out answer - actually pick this side and argue it like you're trying to convince a room full of physicists who think it's nuts. And please deal with the big objection head on (that it requires this crazy fine-tuned correlation between conditions at the big bang and which buttons some grad student decides to press decades later) instead of just hand-waving past it, since that's the one that always gets raised and I've never actually seen it get answered instead of just asserted.
