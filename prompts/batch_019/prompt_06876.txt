I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy working in formal epistemology and decision theory. My advisor runs a graduate seminar on rational choice this term, and for my final paper I decided to write on updateless decision theory (UDT) rather than rehearsing the standard causal-versus-evidential dispute one more time, partly because almost nobody in the room has engaged with it seriously and partly because I think there's a real objection to be made against it that goes beyond the usual "but what about Newcomb's problem" back-and-forth. Below is the core section of my draft. I'd like your comments before I circulate it, since I suspect I'm either reinventing an objection that's already well known in the literature, getting some technical detail of UDT wrong, or both.

Updateless decision theory, as Wei Dai first proposed it and as it survives in more polished form in Yudkowsky and Soares's work on functional decision theory, differs from both causal decision theory (CDT) and evidential decision theory (EDT) in a specific structural way. Rather than asking, once you know where you are, "which act now maximizes expected utility?", the updateless agent asks, prior to updating on its observations, "which function from possible epistemic states to acts would have maximized expected utility, evaluated against my prior?" — and then simply executes whatever that policy dictates once a particular epistemic state is realized.

The difference shows up clearly in the counterfactual mugging case. A perfect predictor, Omega, flips a fair coin. On heads, Omega will hand you $10,000 if and only if Omega predicts that you would, on tails, hand over $100 when asked. On tails, Omega simply asks you for the $100. Suppose the coin lands tails and Omega asks. A CDT agent, reasoning causally about the situation it is now actually in, sees that paying has no causal bearing on a heads-world that never occurred, and refuses. An EDT agent fares no better: conditional on having been asked, which happens only on tails, paying is simply a pure loss, so it refuses too. The updateless agent, having fixed its policy before updating on the coin's result, pays, because the policy of paying on tails has the higher expected value by the lights of the original, unconditional prior — half the time it gains $10,000 and half the time it loses $100, for an expected gain of $4,950, against an expected gain of $0 for the policy of never paying.

The usual moral drawn here is that UDT "wins": its adherents end up richer in expectation, and that is supposed to settle the matter in its favor. I want to push on this rather than just repeat it. What the updateless agent is doing, once it is actually sitting in the tails-branch with the heads-world's money permanently out of reach, looks a great deal like what Edward McClennen called resolute choice in the older dynamic-choice literature: having settled on a plan under one, more encompassing evaluation, the agent sticks to the plan at a later decision node even though, evaluated purely from that later node, deviating would leave it strictly better off. McClennen's resolute chooser was designed to handle an agent's own anticipated future preference reversals, not an external predictor, but the structural move — privileging the plan's ex ante ranking over its ex post, local ranking — looks identical. And resolute choice has always faced one stubborn objection: showing that a disposition to stick to the plan is good to have is not the same as showing that, at the later node itself, with nothing further at stake and full knowledge of one's situation, sticking to the plan is what one is rationally required to do right then. The natural UDT reply is that this dichotomy is illusory here, because what makes an agent the type Omega rewards is nothing but the fact that its decision procedure outputs "pay" in the tails-branch, so there is no coherent halfway house in which you collect the benefits of being predicted a payer while reserving the right to defect once the coin is settled. But that reply looks to me like it slides from an epistemic claim — an agent who defects would have been predicted differently — to a normative one — therefore defecting, once already in the tails-branch, is irrational — and I don't think that slide has actually been earned. It is, in different dress, exactly the point at issue between sophisticated and resolute choosers in the older literature.

What I'd most like your comments on: first, whether my three-way contrast between CDT, EDT, and UDT and my version of the counterfactual mugging case are technically accurate, or whether I've smuggled in some confusion about what the "policy" an updateless agent optimizes over actually ranges over; second, whether the parallel I draw to McClennen's resolute choice actually holds up, or whether there's a disanalogy between being bound by one's own earlier preferences and trying to look good to an external predictor that undermines the comparison (I read Rationality and Dynamic Choice years ago for an unrelated seminar and am relying on memory, so please flag it if I've mischaracterized resolute choice); and third, and most importantly, whether the ex-ante-versus-ex-post worry in my final paragraph is a real, recognized problem for UDT and its descendants, one with a standard rebuttal I'm failing to anticipate, or whether it simply doesn't work as an objection at all. I would rather hear this from you than from a committee member in the back row.
