I'm in Year 13 and applying to study Law this year, so I go to a Saturday-morning seminar run by my school's Law Society for people preparing personal statements and interviews. Our supervisor, Ms. Whitfield, has been taking us through jurisprudence topics, and after two sessions on Hart's concept of law she moved us on to the Hart–Devlin debate and set us the following practice question to write up in thirty minutes: "Explain the disagreement between Patrick Devlin and H.L.A. Hart on the legal enforcement of morality. Your answer should cover (a) the background to the debate in the Wolfenden Report, (b) Devlin's argument, including his 'disintegration thesis' and his proposed test for the limits of toleration, and (c) Hart's principal objections to that argument, and should end with a brief, reasoned judgement about whose position you find more convincing. (20 marks.)"

As part of the exercise we have to mark each other's scripts against Ms. Whitfield's scheme before she moderates everything herself, and I've been given my reading-partner Priya's answer to grade. I'd like a second, independent opinion before I hand my marking in, since I'd rather be fair to her than mark on a hunch. Here is what she wrote:

"The debate begins with the 1957 Wolfenden Report, which recommended that homosexual acts between consenting adults in private should be decriminalised, on the ground that the law's function is to protect citizens from harm and public offence rather than to punish private vice, since 'there must remain a realm of private morality and immorality which is, in brief and crude terms, not the law's business.' Patrick Devlin, then a High Court judge, rejected this reasoning in his 1959 lecture 'The Enforcement of Morals.' He argued that every society is held together by a shared morality in much the same way that it is held together by shared political institutions, so that a sufficiently serious departure from that shared morality is, in principle, as dangerous to the society's survival as treason – this is generally known as his 'disintegration thesis.' Since most people cannot state the boundaries of the shared morality directly, Devlin proposed that they be identified indirectly, by asking whether a given practice provokes genuine 'intolerance, indignation and disgust' in the mind of the ordinary reasonable person, traditionally personified as the man in the jury box; a practice that fails this test may, in principle, be criminalised.

Hart replied at length in Law, Liberty and Morality (1963). Hart in fact identifies four distinct grounds that might be offered for criminal punishment – harm to others, offence to others, harm to the offender (paternalism), and immorality as such – and, following Mill, accepts only the first as sufficient on its own, allows the second and third only a very limited role, and rejects the fourth outright; Devlin, on Hart's reading, effectively collapses the first three into the fourth. Hart also denies that Devlin has any real evidence for the disintegration thesis, pointing out that plenty of societies have survived considerable changes in sexual morality, and he argues that the reasonable man's 'disgust' is a poor guide to legislation, since it simply reflects existing prejudice rather than any reasoned moral view. This last point connects to Hart's wider jurisprudence: since Hart's whole positivist project is built on showing that a rule's status as law never depends on its moral merit, his objection to Devlin is at bottom just an extension of that same separation thesis to the special case of sexual morality, rather than an independent argument in its own right.

On balance I find Hart considerably more persuasive, chiefly because Devlin never really explains why moral disagreement about private sexual conduct should be treated as equivalent to treason or violent crime."
