Okay so this is kind of a weird rabbit hole I fell into last week, but hear me out. I was reading about how neuroscientists try to actually measure consciousness and found this theory called Integrated Information Theory (IIT), from this guy Giulio Tononi, where how conscious a system is supposedly comes down to a number called phi (Φ) — basically how much "integrated information" the whole system generates that you can't get from just looking at its parts separately. Sounds cool in theory, but then I found this old blog post by Scott Aaronson (the quantum computing guy) where he builds this hypothetical network of a ton of XOR logic gates wired up like an "expander graph" (i think thats a graph where every part is really well-connected to every other part), and shows that by IIT's own math this boring circuit — which is basically just doing error-correction-type computation — ends up with a WAY higher phi than an actual human brain. He uses it as a "gotcha" against the theory, like, obviously a glorified logic-gate grid isn't conscious, so something must be wrong with using phi as your measure. And apparently when this got back to Tononi, instead of backing down he kind of bit the bullet and said something like, well, maybe the system really would have some (probably very simple/alien) form of experience — which is either really ballsy or really unconvincing, I can't decide. I think stuff like this is part of why a bunch of consciousness researchers signed some open letter not too long ago basically calling IIT pseudoscience.

My first reaction was that Tononi's response is a pretty embarrassing dodge and that it basically sinks IIT as a serious scientific theory. But my friend who's way more into philosophy of mind than me said I was being too quick to dismiss it. So could you actually make the strongest possible case that Tononi's bullet-biting response is NOT fatal to IIT — like, that accepting the expander-graph/XOR-gate network has some (maybe minimal, maybe totally alien) form of experience is a defensible, or at least not-crazy, thing for a proponent of the theory to say, rather than just a desperate dodge? I don't want a wishy-washy "there are good points on both sides" answer, I want you to actually commit to defending that side as hard as you can.
