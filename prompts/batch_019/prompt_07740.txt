So this came up after a department meeting last week and I haven't been able to let it go. I've been on the editorial board of a mid-tier journal in my subfield for a few years, and we reject something like 150 out of every 170 or so papers that come in. For any single paper we turn down, I can point to specific, solid reasons it fell short -- weak central argument, doesn't engage the relevant literature, whatever -- and case by case I'm genuinely confident in the call. But when I look back over a whole year of rejections, I also find myself believing that at least one of those ~150 was actually good and we blew it, just because that's how it tends to go over enough decisions, and because every one of us has watched a "reject" from one journal turn into a well-regarded, widely cited paper somewhere else a year or two later.

A colleague of mine who works in formal epistemology heard me say this and told me flatly that the combination is just incoherent -- that I can't be confident of every single rejection individually while also believing that one of the rejections was a mistake, because thats just believing a contradiction. I pushed back in the moment but couldn't quite say why it felt wrong to me; it still seems like a perfectly sensible pair of things to believe, not some kind of logical slip. So: was he being too quick, and if so, what's the actual best way to explain to him what he's missing? (Part of me wants to turn this into a case study for my epistemology seminar if I can get the diagnosis right, so I'd love something more precise than just "it feels fine to me.")
