Deflationary theories of truth deny that "is true" names a substantive property whose underlying nature—correspondence to fact, coherence with other beliefs, or whatever it might be—stands in need of philosophical analysis. On the minimalist version of the view, the truth predicate is instead fully characterized by the instances of the equivalence schema: the proposition that p is true if and only if p. Everything we might want to say using "true"—that truth is the aim of belief, or that a good theory is more likely to be true than a bad one—can allegedly be paraphrased away using these instances plus ordinary logic, so that no further theory of truth's nature is required.

The objection I find hardest to dismiss is sometimes called the generalization problem. We regularly reason using generalizations that quantify over propositions or utterances—"Everything the witness said is true," "Every theorem provable in this system is true"—and we rely on such generalizations to license inferences to particular conclusions: from "everything the witness said is true" and "the witness said the door was locked," we infer that the door was locked. The deflationist's natural move is to treat the generalization as standing in for the (possibly infinite) conjunction of its instances. Critics object that no one who asserts or relies on such a generalization has any such conjunction in mind, and that the inference to a particular conclusion looks like an ordinary application of a general inferential rule—one that seems to require "true" to express some genuine, if minimal, property, in order to guarantee that the rule is truth-preserving. If that is right, the predicate is doing logical work that mere disquotation does not obviously license.

I am not convinced the objection succeeds, and would state the deflationist's best reply as follows. The relevant generalizations should be read schematically rather than as objectual quantification over a domain of propositions that would need to be surveyed. On a schematic reading, "everything the witness said is true" does not denote an infinite conjunction to be grasped all at once; it licenses, for any instance we can specify, exactly the substitution that the equivalence schema itself authorizes. The inference to "the door was locked" then requires no antecedent truth property doing explanatory work, only that we can specify the relevant instance, which the second premise supplies. What looked like a substantive inference rule turns out to be nothing but repeated application of the schema.

I would welcome comments on: whether this schematic reply is faithful to how minimalists such as Horwich actually respond to the generalization problem, or whether I am simply reconstructing a response rather than reporting one they in fact endorse; whether the objection can be restated so that it survives a schematic treatment of the generalizations; and, more generally, whether the essay would be clear to a reader unfamiliar with this specific debate.
