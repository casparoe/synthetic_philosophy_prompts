I'm currently teaching a graduate seminar on rational belief change, and a case one of my students raised in discussion this week has put me at odds with the colleague who co-teaches the course with me. I'd value a third opinion.

The student works part-time as a nurse anesthetist and described a situation from her rotations. A patient is about to undergo a minor procedure and will shortly be given a sedative that the nurses on the ward jokingly call "the good news drug": as a well-documented side effect, it leaves patients feeling calm and essentially certain that everything went fine, an effect that has nothing to do with how the procedure actually went and that lasts until the drug clears her system several hours later. Just before the sedative is administered, the patient's honest assessment, based on her surgeon's own hedged pre-op remarks, is that there is roughly a 30 percent chance the procedure will turn up a serious problem. The anesthetist tells her exactly what to expect: as soon as the drug takes hold, she will feel essentially certain that nothing is wrong, and that feeling will not budge until the sedative wears off. My student argued that, given this foreknowledge, the patient is already being irrational if she doesn't start shifting toward near-certainty now, before the sedative is even administered -- she can, after all, predict with near-perfect accuracy exactly what her own opinion is about to become, and it seems odd to knowingly cling to a view you are certain you are about to abandon. My co-teacher thought this was basically right, and even drew an analogy with a patient who is simply waiting on lab results and knows she will believe whatever they say: there, deferring to your anticipated future opinion seems not just permissible but required, so why not here too?

I think something has gone wrong in the sedative case, but I don't want to just assert that, and I'm wary of drawing the line between the two cases in a way that secretly just stipulates the answer I want. Was my student's argument correct? If not, where exactly does it break down, and what, if anything, really does distinguish the sedative case from the lab-results one?
