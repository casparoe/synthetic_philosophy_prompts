I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent about 35 years doing condensed matter stuff before I hung up my lab coat), and in retirement I've been slowly working my way through the philosophy of causation, mostly via the Stanford Encyclopedia and a couple of books, plus a monthly meetup of fellow retirees who like to argue about this stuff over wine. For most of my life "causation" seemed like a basically solved problem to me as a physicist: A causes B when there's some actual physical process connecting them -- a force, a transfer of energy or momentum, something conserved moving from one to the other. So it really threw me when I ran into the literature on "causation by omission." The standard examples are things like: a gardener's failure to water a plant caused it to die, a lifeguard's failure to jump in caused a swimmer to drown, a doctor's failure to prescribe a drug caused a patient's death. In every one of these cases there is, by construction, no physical process running from the omission to the effect -- nothing is transferred, because nothing happened. And yet it feels a bit mad to say the gardener's negligence had nothing to do with the plant's death.

From what I can tell (and my meetup friends backed me up, or at least didn't correct me) there's a genuine, live disagreement here between two camps. One side says causation is basically just counterfactual dependence -- if the plant wouldn't have died had the gardener watered it, that's sufficient for the not-watering to count as a cause, full stop, physical process be damned. The other side wants to keep something like the process picture -- I think a philosopher named Dowe worked this out formally as a "conserved quantity theory," where a cause and effect have to be linked by transference of a conserved physical quantity -- and so they either deny flatly that omissions are ever causes, or they carve out some deflationary substitute relation (I've seen the term "quasi-causation") to explain our talk without granting that nothing can genuinely cause something. There's also a nice, slightly comic puzzle attached to this that I gather is well-known: if my not watering the plant caused its death because the plant wouldn't have died had I watered it, why doesn't the same go for some stranger on the other side of the planet, or the Queen of England, who also didn't water it and who's watering would, counterfactually, have equally saved it? Pure counterfactual dependence seems to prove way too much, and its not obvious to me how you fix that without just readmitting something process-like through the back door.

What I'd love is a dialogue between one philosopher defending the counterfactual/dependence side (omissions can be real, literal causes, no physical process required) and one defending the process side (who denies this, or allows at most some quasi-causal stand-in), arguing it out over a single concrete case of the sort above (pick one, or invent your own). I want real engagement, not two monologues -- have each of them respond to the other's actual move, especially: how does the process theorist handle the Queen-of-England-style objection without just admitting the counterfactualist was right all along, and if they retreat to "quasi-causation," doesn't the counterfactualist get to say that's just counterfactual dependence wearing a costume? Please don't wrap it up with a neat resolution or a truce -- I'd rather see exactly where each side has to bite a bullet.
