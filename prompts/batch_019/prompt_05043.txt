So in my early modern philosophy seminar we've been slogging through Leibniz's Theodicy and bits of the Discourse on Metaphysics, and this week was all about the "best of all possible worlds" stuff. Our professor's gloss was that Leibniz doesn't mean "nicest world for people," he means the world with the most perfection, which cashes out as roughly: the greatest possible variety/richness of things that exist, combined with the simplest and most orderly set of laws holding it all together. She kept stressing that this isn't classical utilitarianism since its not just about summing up pleasure — but also that this exact same notion of perfection is supposed to ground his actual ethics too, not just his theology, since justice for Leibniz is basically "the charity of the wise," i.e. acting so as to promote that same harmony/perfection among people. The more I sit with that the more it feels like it could run into some of the same problems as utilitarianism does.

My housemate is in a contemporary ethics class and was going on at dinner about Parfit's repugnant conclusion, i.e. the result that a total-welfare-maximizer has to prefer some enormous population whose lives are barely worth living over a much smaller population living great lives, since you can always pump up the total by tacking on more barely-net-positive people. That immediately reminded me of Leibniz's "principle of plenitude" — the idea that God wants as much essence/reality to exist as is compossible. So: does Leibniz's specific criterion of perfection (variety + order, not just raw hedonic sum) actually save him from a repugnant-conclusion-style trap, or is his moral theory committed to something just as counterintuitive once you push hard enough on the plenitude side? Could you actually reconstruct this using his real apparatus (compossibility, the principle of the best, plenitude, caritas sapientis, etc) rather than just going with "he was an optimist so I guess he'd bite the bullet"?
