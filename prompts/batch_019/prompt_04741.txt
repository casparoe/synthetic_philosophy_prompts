So for my history of economic thought seminar we just reread Keynes's "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," and I can't stop thinking about how weirdly specific his predictions actually are once you get past the "15-hour work week" meme everyone quotes. Like, he's not just forecasting GDP growth - he coins the term "technological unemployment" right there in that essay, and he has this whole underdiscussed bit distinguishing "absolute" needs from "relative" (positional) needs, where he basically bets that relative needs are a minor, almost pathological share of demand rather than a universal engine of the economy, which is why he thinks that once absolute needs are met people will just... stop wanting more. He also gets weirdly moralistic near the end, arguing that loving money as an end in itself (instead of as a means to living well) is basically a mild psychological disorder that we only tolerate right now because its economically useful, and that once we're through the "tunnel" of economic necessity we can go back to treating avarice as a vice instead of a virtue.

Obviously the 15-hour week didn't happen, and my friend in the reading group joked that Keynes would've had a coronary if he saw us doomscrolling at 1am, but I want something more substantive than "lol he was wrong." Given the actual moral/psychological framework underlying the essay (not just the forecasting part), what do you think Keynes would say if he were confronted with: (a) the persistence of overwork and hustle culture despite way overshooting the productivity gains he predicted, (b) status-driven consumption that's now supercharged by social media in exactly the "relative needs" sense he thought would stay marginal, and (c) current anxiety about AI-driven automation reviving his "technological unemployment" worry almost a century on. Would he mainly blame bad politics/distribution for the failed prediction, would he have to concede that relative needs are way more powerful than he assumed, or would he just insist we're still stuck in the psychologically rough "transition period" he warned about? I'd rather you actually try to reconstruct his reasoning from the essay's own premises than just list plausible vibes-based reactions.
