I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums long enough to have come across Wei Dai's LessWrong post on the "commitment races problem" back when it was still fairly new, and it's one of those pieces of decision theory that has stuck with me more than most. The setup, as I understand it: in bargaining situations where agents can make binding advance commitments, ordinary Schelling-style logic says you want to commit to the toughest credible demand as early as possible, since a rational counterpart should then capitulate rather than force a mutually ruinous standoff -- the classic image being a driver in a game of Chicken who visibly throws the steering wheel out the window so the other driver has no choice but to swerve. What turns this into a "race," rather than just a piece of everyday brinkmanship, is the claim that the relevant sense of "early" need not be calendar time at all: a sufficiently reflective agent can reason about what it would have committed to had it started deliberating sooner or thought faster, and then simply act as though that earlier, less-considered commitment were already binding -- "earlier," that is, along something more like a logical or computational axis than a causal one. Dai's original framing centered on advanced AI systems that can self-modify or otherwise bind themselves far more easily and quickly than humans can, but the underlying puzzle feels like it should have a cleaner, more general answer than that.

I raised this with a few people from my old meetup group recently, and none of us could really cash out, on a mechanical level, why this logical-time framing is supposed to make things worse than garden-variety brinkmanship, rather than just being a more abstract way of restating "move fast and commit hard." Concretely: does pushing commitments back along logical time actually generate something like an infinite regress, or does it just cash out in agents making commitments before they have done any real deliberation at all -- and if it is the latter, why should that be categorically different from an ordinary human negotiator who commits impulsively out of temperament rather than strategy? Separately, I would be curious how this bears on updateless and functional decision theories, which already have their agents evaluate policies as though chosen prior to conditioning on any observations -- does building an agent around one of these decision theories make it more exposed to commitment-race dynamics, or is that exactly the kind of pathology they are meant to defuse? I already have a decent grip on the basic Chicken-game intuition; what I am after is the gears-level story of what actually breaks once "earliness" stops being measured in real time.
