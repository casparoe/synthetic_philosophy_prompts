I'm a retired chemist -- spent about thirty years doing corrosion and materials-failure work before I hung it up -- and since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy discussion group that meets in the back room of a pub near the university, mostly just for fun. Somehow I got voluteered for Q&A duty with next month's guest speaker: a philosopher who defends a "reformed" conditional analysis of dispositions, the move where you patch the old "it's fragile iff it would break if struck" analysis by tying the disposition to some intrinsic causal basis that has to survive, intact, all the way through the stimulus. I've done enough reading now to more or less follow why she needs the patch -- Martin's electro-fink wire, and Lewis's follow-up with a sorcerer doing something similar to a glass, if I'm remembering that right -- but that persistence clause still smells to me like a fudge factor bolted on after the fact, tailored to rule out just those two examples.

Could you draft me eight to ten questions for the Q&A, each with a follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give, so she can't just restate the view and sit back down? I want at least one question pushing on whether "the basis has to persist" is doing real metaphysical work or is closer to a stipulation written to order, at least one on whether her analysis actually earns its keep against masking and antidote cases -- since there the causal basis is untouched, it's the downstream process that gets interfered with rather than the basis itself -- and at least one that makes her cash the whole thing out against a case a working scientist would recognize, like a passivation layer that pretty clearly masks a metal's disposition to corrode without removing it. End with a question about what would have to be true of the world before she'd just give up and call dispositions primitive, unanalyzable powers, since that seems to be where a good chunk of this literature washes up anyway.
