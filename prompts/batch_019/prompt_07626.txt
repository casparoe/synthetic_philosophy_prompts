I'm several chapters into a historical novel set among the Glasgow tobacco merchants toward the end of the 1750s, and I've reached a scene I'd rather get right than fudge. A friend who works in economic history has already warned me that most novelistic uses of Hume are embarrassingly thin, so I want to over-invest here rather than risk that. My protagonist is a junior clerk to one of the trading houses who has spent a couple of years working through Hume's essays on money and commerce — *Political Discourses* first, then the further pieces on trade he kept adding in later editions — and has become a quiet convert. His employers, decent but conventionally minded men, have just handed him an awkward task: several of the town's guild deacons are circulating a petition, meant for the Convention of Royal Burghs, urging Parliament to tighten the penalties on exporting coin to England and the Continent and to raise duties on imported manufactures, on the grounds that the nation is being steadily bled of its "treasure" by an unfavourable balance of trade. The partners want the house's opinion in writing before they decide, a fortnight from now, whether to add their signature.

Could you draft that memo for me, written in his voice? I want it to reach an actual recommendation — sign, refuse, or sign only if the petition is amended in some specific way — rather than hedge, and I want the reasoning to do real work rather than just invoke Hume's name: it should draw on his account of how outflows of specie are self-correcting through their effect on domestic prices and competitiveness, his insistence that money is merely the instrument of commerce and not wealth itself, his case that interest rates are governed by the state of trade and available capital rather than by the sheer quantity of coin in the country, and his argument that a trading neighbour's prosperity is not simply won at one's own expense — all brought to bear on the deacons' specific complaints rather than delivered as a lecture. At the same time, I don't want him to sound like a man who's never had a doubt, so the memo should take seriously the strongest thing his masters could say back: that even a mechanism which corrects itself eventually can take long enough about it that credit tightens and workmen go idle in the meantime, which isn't a worry Hume's own framework simply waves away. Feel free to write it in a period register, since I'll likely quote it directly in the book, but getting the actual argument right matters to me more than the costume.
