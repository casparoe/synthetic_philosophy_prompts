After a career in experimental physics, I have spent much of my retirement working through Spinoza, and one issue continues to puzzle me. In the Ethics, he appears to rule out free will in the sense that most people care about: every idea and volition follows with strict necessity from prior causes, stretching back through an infinite chain, so that nothing could ever have happened otherwise than it did. Yet in the Theological-Political Treatise, and in what he completed of the later Political Treatise before his death, he argues that democracy is the most natural form of government precisely because it best preserves the freedom of its citizens. This seems like an odd position for a thoroughgoing necessitarian to take. If no one, ruler or ruled, could ever really have chosen otherwise, in what sense can one constitution be called freer than another, and why should a philosopher so committed to universal necessity care about defending democracy on those grounds?
