In my seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, we have spent the last two weeks closely reading the Zhuangzi's treatment of death: the scene in the "Zhile" chapter where he drums on a basin and sings after his wife's death, his subsequent dialogue with a skull on the road to Chu, Lady Li's regret over her own earlier tears in the "Qiwulun" chapter, and his deathbed refusal, in "Lieyukou," of a proper burial, insisting that heaven and earth will serve as his coffin. Across these passages, Zhuangzi treats death as simply one more transformation in a single seamless process, no different in kind from the changing of the seasons, and he repeatedly mocks those who anxiously cling to a fixed form. Working specifically from these passages rather than general Daoist truisms, what do you think Zhuangzi would say about the contemporary transhumanist push for cryonic preservation and radical life-extension aimed at indefinitely postponing death? In particular, I'd like your view on whether his philosophy would treat such efforts as just another twist of fate, no more troubling than any other transformation, or whether they would strike him as exactly the sort of anxious grasping he diagnoses as the root of human suffering—and which of these readings you think the text actually supports.
