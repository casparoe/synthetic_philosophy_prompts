A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about whether Aquinas's political theology actually licenses tyrannicide, after he insisted that even Aquinas, of all people, would have sanctioned killing a tyrant. I think the truth is more tangled than either of us allowed, so I wrote up my own reading of the relevant texts below. I'd value your comments on it.

Aquinas's remarks on killing tyrants are spread across three works that don't sit together as easily as popular summaries suggest. In his early commentary on Lombard's Sentences (Book II, distinction 44), he distinguishes a ruler who is a tyrant "as to title" — one whose rule rests on usurpation, gained by force or fraud rather than any genuine consent — from a ruler who is a tyrant "as to exercise," whose title is sound but who governs unjustly. Removing the first kind, he suggests, involves no real sedition, since there was no legitimate authority there to disturb; removing the second is different, since a private person may not simply take it upon himself to undo an authority that is not his to undo.

By the time he writes De Regno, the emphasis has shifted almost entirely onto caution, even regarding rulers who seem straightforwardly tyrannical. Aquinas catalogues the dangers of letting individuals decide for themselves that a ruler deserves to die: the judgment is unreliable, ambitious men will dress up self-interest as liberation, and a tolerated practice of tyrannicide is at least as likely to be turned against merely unpopular rulers as against genuine tyrants. His preferred remedy is that whichever public body originally conferred the ruler's authority is the body competent to restrain or depose him; failing that, if there is no such recourse, the multitude should endure the tyranny and turn to prayer rather than the sword. The Summa's treatment of sedition (II-II, q. 42) adds a further wrinkle: a government not ordered to the common good already lacks the kind of unity that sedition is a sin against, so provoking its overthrow is not, properly speaking, sedition at all — though Aquinas adds a proportionality condition (the overthrow must not do more harm than the tyranny did) and insists that the tyrant, not his opponents, bears the greater share of blame for whatever discord results.

My tentative reconciliation is that all three texts share one load-bearing premise: political authority is held in trust from the community, so only some public or communal act can rightly withdraw it. On this reading, the apparent permissiveness toward usurpers isn't an exception to that rule but a case where the rule never applied, because no authority was ever conferred in the first place.

I'd particularly like to know whether I've represented the three texts fairly, and whether that "authority held in trust" premise really does the reconciling work I want it to do, or whether it just relocates the tension between De Regno's caution and the Sentences commentary's apparent permissiveness toward usurpers.
