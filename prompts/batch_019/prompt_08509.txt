I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been running a small podcast for our local philosophy-of-science reading group. Next week's guest is a philosopher of computer science who is best known for arguing that computational complexity, rather than mere computability, is what neutralizes two classic objections to machine functionalism: Ned Block's "Blockhead" lookup-table argument against the Turing test, and Putnam's claim that almost any physical system can be seen as implementing almost any computation, provided one is not fastidious about the mapping. Her response to both turns on distinguishing merely possible from efficiently achievable: Blockhead fails only because the table would need more entries than there are particles in the observable universe, and Putnam's triviality result fails once the map between physical microstate and computational state is itself required to be efficiently computable.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate this position. I want one question on why sheer resource cost should carry any philosophical weight at all, once she has already granted that Blockhead is logically possible; one on whether her own fix for Putnam -- requiring an efficiently computable decoding map -- could be turned back against her, by finding some efficient re-encoding under which the lookup table counts as computationally cheap after all; one on whether non-uniform, "advice"-style hard-coding lets a brute-force strategy back in for the one finite transcript that any real Turing test ever actually produces; and one on how, if at all, any of this bears on Searle's Chinese Room, since Searle's argument is explicitly indifferent to how fast or slow the symbol manipulation is. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question about whether her entire distinction is hostage to unresolved conjectures such as P versus NP.
