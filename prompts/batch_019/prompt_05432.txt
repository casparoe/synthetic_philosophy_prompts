A few nights ago I had dinner with an old friend from my game theory seminar days, and we ended up in one of those arguments that starts over dessert and doesn't really end. The subject was backward induction in finite games of perfect information, and specifically whether the paradox of the centipede game is a genuine problem for game-theoretic rationality or just a cute artifact dissolved by Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome. She holds the orthodox line: if "rationality" is defined, the way Aumann defines it, as a property of what a player would do at every node, including ones that are never reached, then there is nothing paradoxical about a player who defects on move one because of what he is sure his opponent would do many moves later. On her view, the objection associated with Reny, and pressed directly against Aumann by Binmore, trades on treating a single deviation as though it were proof that common knowledge has broken down for the rest of the game. I took the other side: if a node can only be reached because someone has already been irrational, the theory owes the player standing at that node an account of what to believe now, and once belief is genuinely in play, Stalnaker is right that choosing a revision policy that preserves backward induction over one that licenses forward induction is exactly that -- a choice, not something common knowledge of rationality settles on its own. Neither of us moved an inch, and things got worse when we sketched the tree on a napkin and played it for real, doubling the stake each round: she took the pot on the very first move, on principle, and I passed, also on principle, and came away with nothing.

What I'd like from you is the dialogue we should have had instead of the one we actually had. Write a dialogue between two players who have just finished a real-stakes centipede game against each other -- one who defected immediately and one who kept passing -- now arguing, calmly and in real technical detail, over whether the defector's reasoning was actually rational or only looked that way because defecting is precisely what kept it from ever being tested. Give the backward-induction defender Aumann's actual answer to the self-undermining worry, not just the bare conclusion, and let the critic press the belief-revision point hard enough that the defender has to either concede a real cost or explain exactly why "rational at every node" survives contact with a node that the theory itself said would never be reached. Neither side should be the weaker debater: I want each of them responding to the other's actual last point, the way I wish we had, and I'd rather the dialogue end with the crux exposed than with either side awarded a tidy win, since that's certainly where my friend and I left things.
