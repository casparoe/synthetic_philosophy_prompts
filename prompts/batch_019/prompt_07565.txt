I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've been down a rabbit hole on Henry Sidgwick, the Victorian utilitarian - turns out he was a founding member and first president of the Society for Psychical Research, spending something like two decades personally investigating mediums and telepathy claims, and dying still agnostic about all of it. Apparently this wasn't just a retirement hobby: it seems tied to the 'dualism of practical reason' he's left stuck with at the end of The Methods of Ethics, basically needing some proof the universe has a moral backbone or duty and self-interest never fully line up. What are the most important implications of that whole saga - why someone that rigorous went looking for ghosts, and why he refused to ever call it one way or the other - for how a modern, evidence-minded person (like, say, someone in my line of work) should treat todays fringe-y, hard-to-replicate claims, psi research, near-death-experience studies, that sort of thing? Not after a bio of the guy, more the actual lesson buried in there.
