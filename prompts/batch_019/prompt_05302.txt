(b) Explain the specific difficulty that Pettit and Sugden raise about the backward-induction argument itself — not about how real people actually behave, but about whether the argument's own premises are coherent.

My classmate's submitted answer reads, verbatim:

"(a) Backward induction starts at the last decision node and works backward. At move 6, B compares taking (three-quarters of the pot) to passing (only an even split), so B should take. Knowing this, at move 5, A should anticipate that passing just leads to B taking on move 6, leaving A with a quarter of a bigger pot rather than three-quarters of a smaller one, so A should take at move 5 instead. This reasoning iterates back through moves 4, 3, and 2, and finally to move 1, where A does best by taking immediately. The unique subgame-perfect equilibrium is for A to take on the very first move, ending the game with the smallest possible pot split 3:1 in A's favor, even though both players would have earned far more if they had passed for a few rounds.

(b) This conclusion is the source of the backward induction paradox, first introduced by Aumann to show how the subgame-perfect equilibrium concept can conflict sharply with what any reasonable person would actually do. Pettit and Sugden's version of the puzzle isn't about the psychology of real players but about whether the backward-induction argument is even coherent on its own terms. The argument requires each player, at every node, to believe the other player is rational and will remain rational for the rest of the game. But a later node is only ever reached if some earlier player failed to take — i.e., behaved exactly the way a rational player supposedly never would. So if A actually observes B passing at move 2, A's own assumption that B is rational has already been contradicted by the evidence, and nothing in the backward-induction argument tells A what to believe about B's rationality afterward. The argument seems to assume, at every node, exactly the belief that reaching that node should already have undermined.

(c) Aumann's 1995 theorem shows that if rationality is common knowledge among the players, the backward-induction outcome must occur. Since common knowledge is a much stronger assumption than mere mutual knowledge, Aumann's result essentially settles the Pettit-Sugden worry: it proves formally that a fully rational population really would end up at the backward-induction outcome, so the appearance of paradox comes from underestimating how demanding the common-knowledge assumption is, not from any real gap in the argument."
