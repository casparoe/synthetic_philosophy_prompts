Six weeks ago my younger brother relapsed after almost four years of sobriety from opioids. While he was using, he emptied the account holding what remained of our mother's estate, which we had agreed to split evenly, and he missed my daughter's christening, the one event he had spent months promising he would not miss. My family has split into two camps ever since: my father wants to respond the way he always has, with real anger and a demand that my brother make it right, while my sister insists anger is beside the point now, since he has a documented substance use disorder and, as she puts it, "that wasn't really him." In trying to think this through more carefully than our family arguments allow, I have been reading Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," together with Gary Watson's discussion of how learning the history behind a wrongdoer's actions — he uses the case of the death-row inmate Robert Harris — can pull us back and forth between the ordinary reactive, participant stance and the more detached "objective attitude." I still cannot work out which facts about my brother's case would genuinely warrant stepping back into the objective attitude, which would merely mitigate or excuse this particular episode the way an everyday excuse does, and which are beside the point entirely.

Second, considerations that would merely mitigate or excuse this particular episode without exempting him as a responsible agent generally. Third, considerations that, on reflection, do not genuinely support softening our reactive attitudes at all, whether because they are irrelevant to responsibility or because they cut the other way.
