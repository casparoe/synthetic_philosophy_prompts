Most introductory treatments of the Church–Turing thesis present it as a paradigm case of an unprovable-but-well-confirmed claim: it identifies an informal, pre-theoretic notion ("effectively calculable by an algorithm," or "mechanically computable") with a precise mathematical one (general recursiveness, λ-definability, or computability by a Turing machine), and since one side of the proposed identity is informal, no ordinary mathematical proof of the equivalence seems available. The evidence usually cited is the striking confluence of independently motivated formalisms, from Church's λ-calculus and Herbrand–Gödel recursiveness to Turing machines, Post's production systems, and register machines, which all turn out to capture exactly the same class of functions. This tidy picture, however, has been complicated from several directions that rarely make it into introductory discussions. Gödel himself came to regard Turing's particular analysis of what a human "computer" does when mechanically following instructions, decomposing the process into atomic steps drawn from a bounded repertoire of states and immediately observable configurations, as amounting to something closer to a genuine argument for the adequacy of the definition, rather than merely one more data point in an inductive confluence; on this basis he seems to have regarded Turing's formulation as resting on firmer ground than Church's own proposal, which he had initially found less than fully convincing. Separately, it has been argued, in a line of thought associated with Saul Kripke and building on Gödel's completeness theorem, that a version of the thesis can be extracted from fairly minimal and uncontroversial structural assumptions about what a finitary formal derivation system is, with no appeal to confluence required at all. And separately again, philosophers of computation such as B. Jack Copeland have insisted that, whatever one concludes about this "classical," purely mathematical thesis, there is a further and genuinely empirical claim in the vicinity: that no physical process whatsoever, including hypothetical ones exploiting exotic spacetime structures or other physical loopholes, could compute a function that is not Turing-computable. On their view, this "physical" Church–Turing thesis should not be treated as standing or falling with the classical one.

With all this in mind, write an essay that stakes out and defends a considered position on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. At a minimum, address: (a) whether the classical, mathematical thesis is best understood as an unprovable empirical generalization, as something that arguments like Turing's or Kripke's turn into a near-theorem, or as a Carnapian explication for which categories like "provable" and "empirical" simply do not apply in the usual way; and (b) whether the classical thesis and the physical Church–Turing thesis do, or should, have different epistemic statuses from one another. Weigh the considerations on each side seriously, but do not settle for a purely descriptive survey of positions: reach and justify a specific overall verdict, even if it is a differentiated one. (For instance, "provable, in some suitable sense, for the classical thesis, but only ever empirically supported for the physical thesis" would count as a real verdict, whereas concluding merely that the matter is unsettled would not.)
