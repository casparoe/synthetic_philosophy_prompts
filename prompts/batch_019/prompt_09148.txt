Here, verbatim, is the student's answer: "The equation at issue, often called Adams's Thesis, states that P(A > C) = P(C|A) for any indicative conditional 'if A, C,' wherever P(A) > 0. Lewis's proof proceeds by noting that if this equation is to hold not merely for our actual credence function but for any credence function we might reasonably come to hold, it must hold in particular for the functions obtained by conditionalizing our original P on C and on ¬C. Feeding these conditionalized functions back into the equation and comparing the results yields a constraint that can be satisfied in general only if A and C are probabilistically independent, or if one of the two already has probability 0 or 1. Because indicative conditionals are normally asserted precisely when A and C are evidentially connected, this is an absurd condition to impose across the board, so no proposition can play the role Adams's Thesis assigns to it. Lewis presented this as a decisive refutation of Adams's view and, more specifically, of the particular way Stalnaker had tried to vindicate that view with his possible-worlds semantics for 'if'; the result has since been shown by Hájek and Hall to follow from considerably weaker premises than the ones Lewis originally used. The lesson most commentators draw is that indicative conditionals cannot be truth-apt in any sense, since the triviality argument leaves no room for any proposition, however constructed, to serve as their content."
