My father, a family physician for nearly forty years, died two weeks ago, and I am delivering his eulogy this Saturday to a room of relatives, former patients, and the handful of colleagues who understood his favorite joke. Long after his clinic switched to electronic records, he kept a handwritten index card for every patient, consulted and updated at each visit, and he loved to cite Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis -- Otto and his notebook, the conditions about reliability and trust -- to argue, half in earnest, that the cards were as much a part of his mind as his hippocampus. My sister wants the eulogy to close on the line that he is "still with us" in those boxes, which now sit untouched in his study. I cannot decide whether that actually follows from what he believed or is precisely the kind of comfort his own argument would rule out, since the live coupling he always insisted on is exactly what stopped two weeks ago. Please write the eulogy: it has to work for a room that has never heard of any of this, and it should not reach for my sister's line unless it can genuinely be earned.
