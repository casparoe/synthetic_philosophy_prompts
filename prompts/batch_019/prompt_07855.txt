I recently joined my hospital's clinical ethics consultation committee, and one of the first cases I sat in on turned on a disagreement about whether a patient satisfied the criteria for brain death or was instead in a persistent vegetative state. What struck me was how unsettled that boundary is once you look closely: whole-brain versus higher-brain formulations of death, spinal reflexes that persist after brainstem function has ceased, and the plain fact that dying is a drawn-out process rather than an instantaneous flip from "alive" to "not alive." Trying to get some philosophical traction on why this bothered me, I found myself rereading Peter van Inwagen's *Material Beings*, where he poses his famous Special Composition Question: under what conditions do some things add up to a further thing? His own answer, often labeled organicism, is that some things compose a further thing just when their joint activity constitutes a single life (plus the trivial case of a lone object "composing" itself) — so there are no chairs or statues strictly speaking, only particles arranged chairwise, but there genuinely are organisms, since life supplies a non-arbitrary unifying principle that mere contact, fastening, or cohesion cannot. He arrives at this answer mainly by arguing that every more permissive, common-sense candidate would make composition, and hence the number of objects in the world, a vague matter, which he takes to be flatly impossible.

Given what I had just watched play out on the committee, this struck me as suspiciously convenient: "being caught up in a life" looks like exactly the sort of thing that admits of borderline cases, from colonial organisms like siphonophores to slime molds that drift between unicellular and multicellular phases to the sheer gradualness of dying itself. So I'd like you to argue, as forcefully as you can, for the claim that van Inwagen's own vagueness argument, if it is good enough to eliminate the common-sense answers he rejects, is equally good enough to eliminate his own organicist answer — and to take seriously the most obvious escape route, namely insisting that any vagueness in "life" is merely vagueness in our concept or word for it, rather than the objectionable, world-infecting vagueness that supposedly afflicts "fastening" or "cohesion."
