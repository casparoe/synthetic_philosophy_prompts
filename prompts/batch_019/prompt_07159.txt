My school offers a philosophy elective, and we just wrapped up a unit on modal metaphysics by reading some excerpts from David Lewis's On the Plurality of Worlds. The view he defends there, modal realism, is the thesis that every way a world could possibly have been isn't just a useful fiction — it's realized by some concrete world exactly as real as ours, merely cut off from us in space and time. A few of us kept arguing about the implications after class, and I've more or less talked myself into a conclusion that honestly unsettled me: if literally every possible sequence of events already plays out in full somewhere, then whatever I decide to do this weekend doesn't really add anything new to reality as a whole, since some world already contains a version of events where "I" make each of the other choices instead. One friend pushed this even further by comparing it to the many-worlds interpretation in quantum mechanics, where every measurement outcome supposedly occurs in some branch, so that no single choice is ever really the one that "happens."

What I can't square this with is the fact that Lewis himself, and the other philosophers who take his view seriously, don't seem to have concluded that deliberation is pointless — they kept doing ordinary philosophy, weighing reasons and defending positions as if it genuinely mattered which arguments they accepted. So, assuming my reasoning above is basically sound: why hasn't modal realism made any of its major defenders fatalistic about their own choices? And separately, is the comparison to quantum many-worlds actually doing real philosophical work here, or does it just sound similar to modal realism without being the same kind of claim?
