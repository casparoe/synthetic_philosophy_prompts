So this is going to sound like a weird tangent but bear with me. I'm a few years into a PhD in planetary dynamics (I run N-body sims of the outer solar system for a living, thrilling stuff) and last month I picked up a Newton biography purely for procrastination purposes, and I got completely derailed by the chapter on the General Scholium and the stuff around it. I knew vaguely that Newton was a heretic — anti-Trinitarian, obsessed with Daniel and Revelation, all that — but I hadn't realized how directly his theology fed into his physics. Apparently he thought the mutual gravitational tugging between Jupiter and Saturn (and other such perturbations) would gradually throw the solar system into disorder, and that God had to step in every so often to set things right again, and this wasn't just a throwaway remark — it connected to his whole thing about God's "dominion" needing to be an ongoing, active relationship with creation rather than God being some perfect being who built a self-running machine and walked away (which is basically the Leibniz/Cartesian picture he hated). Leibniz, of course, mocked him for this, saying it makes God out to be a lousy watchmaker who has to keep fixing his own watch.

Here's the part messing with my head, as someone who actually works on this stuff for a living: Laplace and Lagrange later came along with better perturbation theory and "solved" the wobble problem by showing its periodic rather than cumulative, so no divine mechanic required — but we now know, from Poincare's work on the three body problem onward, and from actual long run numerical integration (Laskar's results, the thing about Mercury having something like a 1% shot at going properly unstable over the next few billion years) that the solar system is genuinely chaotic in the long run, and that Laplace's "stability" proof was really only ever a statement about a lower order approximation. So in a weird roundabout way Newtons original instinct — that the system isn't perfectly self sustaining forever — wasn't crazy, even though literally everything about his proposed mechanism and fix was wrong.

I keep trying to imagine what Newton would actually do with this if you brought him up to speed on all of it. Would he take modern chaos theory as vindication and just relocate "God's job" to some other gap (initial conditions, whatever generates the unpredictability in the first place), would he see it as beside the point since his real theological claim was about ongoing dominion in general and was never really about this one specific gravitational glitch, or would learning that instability can arise from pure deterministic sensitivity to initial conditions actually undercut something deeper in his framework — since that kind of instability doesn't obviously call for any governing agent at all, just math? Given what we actually know about how Newton reasoned theologically (the General Scholium, his dislike of the "perfect self running machine" picture, his Arian emphasis on active sovereignty over passive first-cause deism), what do you think he'd land on, and is there a version of his argument that actually survives contact with chaos theory?
