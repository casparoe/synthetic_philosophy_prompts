I'm the token non-clinical member of the neuroethics working group at the academic rehabilitation hospital where I work, and I've spent enough years reading rationalist and philosophy-of-mind blogs and forums that I'm apparently now the person our chair turns to when a case gets philosophically weird. Over the past two years, two long-term patients on our disorders-of-consciousness unit — both diagnosed behaviorally as being in an unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, the term that has replaced "persistent vegetative state" — have tested positive on the fMRI paradigm that's become a kind of benchmark in the "cognitive-motor dissociation" literature: when asked, through headphones, to alternate between imagining playing tennis and imagining walking through the rooms of their house, both patients showed motor and parahippocampal cortical activity that switched on and off in step with the instructions, indistinguishable from healthy volunteers actually performing the task, though neither has ever produced a behavioral response of any kind. Neurology now wants standing guidance on what a positive finding like this should change: whether it obliges analgesia before any procedure classed as noxious, whether and how it gets disclosed to families, and what weight, if any, it should carry in conversations about continuing versus withdrawing life-sustaining treatment.

Because of my background, I was the one who reached for Chalmers's "meta-problem of consciousness" when we discussed this: his point that what can actually be explained in physical terms is not whether a system is conscious, but why systems like us produce the judgments, reports, and surrounding behavior that make consciousness look like a hard problem to begin with. I've argued to the group that our everyday confidence that other people are conscious was never based on direct access to their qualia either; it rests entirely on this kind of judgment-and-report-generating machinery, and that a patient with an intact cortex and a severed pathway from cortex to muscle should be treated the way we already treat a locked-in patient, not the way we treat someone whose cortex has actually stopped doing the relevant work. Two of our physicians think this proves too much: a single, narrow command-following signal, they say, is thin evidence that the whole judgment-generating apparatus survived intact rather than some dissociable fragment of it, and "the philosophy doesn't settle this, so don't let philosophy settle it" strikes them as the responsible position rather than an evasion. I've now argued both sides of this to myself often enough that I no longer trust my own read on it.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before our next meeting? It should recommend a specific, adoptable policy on the three questions above, name a concrete point at which the working group revisits that policy as the evidence develops, and take the "this is premature" objection seriously on its own terms rather than treating it as mere caution — while also being honest that, on a unit where findings like this keep arriving, declining to let the philosophy inform policy is itself a policy, with its own costs.
