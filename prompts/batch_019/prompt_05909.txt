I'm a volunteer TA for a friend's graduate seminar on formal epistemology (my day job is backend engineering, so I'm fine with the probability but I'm leaning on the group for the philosophical framing). I'm grading problem sets on anthropic reasoning and would like a second opinion on this one before I return it.

**Question set to the student:**

"Two cosmological theories, T-small and T-big, are considered equally likely a priori and are empirically indistinguishable short of a costly experiment physicists are about to run. T-small entails that exactly 10^12 observers will ever exist; T-big entails that exactly 10^24 observers will ever exist. A philosopher claims that, prior to the experiment, she can already conclude T-big is true with near certainty, purely from the fact that she exists as an observer.

(a) Reconstruct the philosopher's reasoning and identify which anthropic principle it relies on.
(b) Explain how a proponent of the rival anthropic principle would respond to her argument, and state whether that rival principle yields a different posterior for T-big.
(c) Bostrom uses this case to argue against one of the two principles. Which one, and why is the case supposed to be a problem specifically for that principle rather than for anthropic reasoning in general?
(d) This case is sometimes compared to the Doomsday Argument. Briefly say whether the two principles' verdicts on the Doomsday Argument run parallel to, or in tension with, their verdicts here."

**Student's answer:**

"(a) The philosopher is reasoning via the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), which says we should reason as if we are a random sample from the set of all *possible* observers, weighted by how many actual observers each hypothesis predicts. Since T-big predicts 10^12 times more observers than T-small, SIA updates the prior 1:1 odds to roughly 10^12:1 in favor of T-big, just from the fact that the philosopher exists at all — no data from the experiment is needed.

(b) A defender of the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) would respond that we should instead reason as if we are a random sample from the observers who actually exist within our reference class, in the single world that is actual. Since the calculation is now relative to one's own reference class rather than pooled across hypotheses, learning merely that one exists doesn't discriminate between T-small and T-big at all, so SSA leaves the 1:1 prior odds unchanged.

(c) Bostrom directs this case against SIA, since it lets pure armchair reasoning about population sizes pre-empt an empirical experiment, which is absurd — a philosopher shouldn't be able to out-reason physicists from her armchair. The case doesn't tell against SSA because SSA was constructed precisely to rule out this kind of population-weighted update, so the case targets SIA specifically rather than anthropic reasoning as such.

(d) In the Doomsday Argument, SSA is the principle that produces the unsettling update (shifting credence toward an earlier, sooner-ending human population, since we should expect to be typically located within the reference class of all humans who will ever live), while SIA cancels this shift out. So the two principles trade off: SSA avoids the presumptuous philosopher but bites the Doomsday bullet, while SIA avoids the Doomsday bullet but bites the presumptuous philosopher bullet."

Could you assign a grade out of 20 (with partial credit noted per part) and, for each of (a)–(d), give one or two sentences saying exactly what's correct and what — if anything — is mistaken or overstated? I'd rather have the reasoning spelled out than just a score, since I'll need to justify it if the student pushes back.
