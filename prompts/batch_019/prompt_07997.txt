For a seminar on Plato's political philosophy, we've spent the last few sessions working through Books I and II of the *Laws*, and one argument in particular has divided the class. It is strange that a treatise on legislation opens with a defense of drinking parties, especially given how far this cuts against the Plato we had all assumed we knew from the *Republic*, where appetite is something to be disciplined out of the guardians rather than deliberately provoked. In the *Laws*, though, the Athenian Stranger argues something closer to the opposite: since no drug exists that reliably induces fear in a subject on command, wine is proposed as the next best instrument available to a lawgiver, one that loosens inhibition rather than tightening it, and so exposes, cheaply and with little risk, whether a citizen's self-control (sōphrosynē) is real or only a front kept up by sobriety. Properly run, under sober elders whom he later terms the "officers of Dionysus," and hedged by strict age thresholds (no wine at all before eighteen, only moderate wine up to thirty, full participation withheld until forty, precisely because the old are said to need wine as a kind of medicine against the rigidity that age brings), the drinking party becomes for him both a test and a training ground for virtue. Megillus, the Spartan of the party, wants none of it: he opens the whole discussion by declaring Spartan law, which simply bans convivial drinking outright, the best law on pleasure any city has devised, and the Athenian only half-answers him by pointing out that courage tested solely against pain and fear, never against pleasure, is itself only half of virtue.

Half the seminar sided with Megillus and half with the Athenian, and I don't think either side actually landed a knockout blow on the other, which is what prompted this request. The Athenian's proposal seems to require an increasingly elaborate scaffold of safeguards just to stay safe, which suggests that even he does not fully trust the instrument he is recommending; and it is far from obvious that watching someone grow talkative and reckless after a few cups reveals anything stable about their character, rather than simply how they behave in one more contingent situation, no more diagnostic than the risky business dealings he explicitly rejects as too costly a test to run. Megillus's blunter solution, simply removing the substance from the city altogether, sidesteps all of that, even if it buys only the incomplete virtue the Athenian accuses him of settling for.

Could you write a dialogue that picks up directly where the two of them leave off, in which Megillus presses the Athenian specifically on (a) whether behavior under wine is really a reliable sign of settled character rather than noise, and (b) whether a regulatory scheme this elaborate is realistically safe to institutionalize, given that both the citizens being tested and the elders supervising them are drawn from the very same fallible population. Please keep it a genuine two-hander between Megillus and the Athenian Stranger (Clinias can stay in the background); neither should be allowed to simply concede or restate their opening position unchanged, and each should respond to the specific point the other has just raised, the way two people who have been arguing about this for years and still have not convinced each other would.
