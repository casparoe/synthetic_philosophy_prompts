For the past year or so I've been teaching myself the philosophy of personal identity, mostly by working through library copies of Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* and a few introductory books on Buddhist thought, since I never had the chance to study any of this formally. One claim keeps coming up in almost everything I read on the topic: that Parfit's Reductionist View of persons — the idea that a person's existence is nothing more than a brain and a body plus a series of interrelated physical and mental events, with no further fact of identity underneath all that — is essentially a modern, secular restatement of the Buddhist doctrine of anatta. Parfit seems to invite the comparison himself: he explicitly discusses the Buddha's teaching on non-self in the book, and describes his own shift to the Reductionist View in almost liberating terms, as though becoming a Reductionist had dissolved some kind of enclosure around him.

The more Buddhist material I actually read, though, the shakier that equivalence starts to look to me. The Abhidharma tradition doesn't just deny a self; it replaces the person with a very specific ontology of momentary, causally connected dharmas (as in the chariot simile from the Milindapañha, where "chariot" and "person" turn out to be nothing more than convenient labels for an assembly of parts), and it still treats those momentary dharmas themselves as ultimately real. Madhyamaka goes further and denies even that, insisting that the aggregates and the dharmas are just as empty of intrinsic essence as the self was supposed to be, to the point that its thinkers worry about reifying emptiness itself, which is presumably why they end up talking about an "emptiness of emptiness." None of this seems to map cleanly onto Parfit, who as far as I can tell isn't committed to momentariness, to any Buddhist-style dharma ontology, or to anything like emptiness of emptiness — he's only denying that there is a further fact of identity over and above ordinary physical and psychological continuity.

So rather than another side-by-side summary of both positions, I'd like you to actually argue for a specific claim: that the popular equation of Parfit's reductionism about persons with the Buddhist doctrine of anatta is mistaken, or at least considerably overstated, once you look past the shared rejection of a persisting self to what each view is actually committed to. Please give the strongest version of this argument you can, engaging directly with the specific points I raised above — the further-fact language, dharma theory and momentariness, and emptiness of emptiness — rather than resting on the vaguer observation that "Buddhism is more radical."
