So I'm meant to be writing my dissertation chapter on fitting-attitude accounts of value but I've fallen down a side-tunnel on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem instead, and I want someone to pressure-test an argument before I bring it to my advisor (this came up at reading group last week and I couldn't quite articulate it on the spot). Quick recap of the setup in case it helps to have it stated the way I'm using it: buck-passers say X is good iff X has properties that give us reason to favor X. The standard trouble case is the evil-demon scenario -- I think it's originally Rabinowicz and Ronnow-Rasmussen's, though I might be misremembering the source -- where a demon will torture you unless you come to desire some worthless saucer of mud. You now have a reason to desire the mud, but obviously not because the mud is good, so the buck-passing analysis threatens to spit out the wrong verdict unless "reason" in the analysis can somehow be restricted to the right kind.

There's the state-given/object-given move (say the demon's reason doesn't count because its grounded in facts about the state of desiring rather than the object desired), but I'm less interested in that one right now. What I actually want pressure-tested is the fancier "higher-order" fix, which I believe traces to Danielsson and Olson: the idea that the demon doesn't really give you reason to desire the mud, just reason to want to desire it, so the wrong-kind reason gets pushed up a level and never touches the attitude the buck-passing analysis is actually about. Please argue, as forcefully as you can, that this move fails because it's just a regress wearing a solution's clothes: for any level you push the corruption to, I can build a new demon (or a new case, doesn't have to be a demon) that attaches a wrong-kind reason to that level instead, and there's no principled place to call a halt. So the buck-passer either has to admit every level is potentially corruptible, which seems to just concede the original problem, or stipulate that some level is off-limits, which needs an argument they don't obviously have. Don't just assert the regress though -- take seriously the best reply on the buck-passer's behalf, something like: the hierarchy has to bottom out at a state (a brute desire, or something Frankfurt-style like an identification) that isn't itself the kind of thing reasons attach to, so there's nothing left to corrupt -- and tell me whether that stopping point is principled or just a stipulation with a fancier name.

If it actually strengthens things, feel free to bring in the thought (Jonathan Way's, I think, though again don't quote me) that this whole problem generalizes way past buck-passing, to basically any account that tries to explain a normative status via reasons for a response -- but only if it sharpens the regress point, I don't want a lit review, I want the single strongest version of the specific objection above.
