So I spent my career as a physicist, and since retiring I've been reading my way into philosophy of mind, mostly the predictive processing stuff (Clark's Surfing Uncertainty, some Friston on the free energy principle, that whole crowd). Everybody in that literature name-drops Helmholtz's "unconscious inference" as the ancestor of the idea, so being an old physicist myself I figured I'd trust him over the commentators and dug up Southall's old translation of the Physiological Optics. Now I'm stuck on this passage, from the bit called "Concerning the Perceptions in General," right on page 4:

"The psychic activities that lead us to infer that there in front of us at a certain place there is a certain object of a certain character, are generally not conscious activities, but unconscious ones. In their result they are equivalent to a conclusion, to the extent that the observed action on our senses enables us to form an idea as to the possible cause of this action; although, as a matter of fact, it is invariably simply the nervous stimulations that are perceived directly... But what seems to differentiate them from a conclusion, in the ordinary sense of that word, is that a conclusion is an act of conscious thought."

What I cant pin down is how much weight that last sentence is supposed to carry. Is Helmholtz just being cautious about the word "conclusion," while still meaning that something with real inferential structure (premises, a rule, an output) is happening in the nervous system, minus the awareness? Or is "conclusion" doing much less work for him, more like a loose metaphor for "the output resembles what reasoning would give you," without any actual inference going on underneath? It seems like the modern predictive-processing crowd, with their talk of the brain "doing" Bayesian inference, runs into the exact same fork, and I can't tell whether Helmholtz already saw that coming or whether his worry is about something else (conscious vs unconscious) that doesnt map onto it at all.
