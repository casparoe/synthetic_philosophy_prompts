"In Parfit's Hitchhiker, you're dying in the desert and a driver who can predict your future behaviour will only take you into town if she predicts you'll pay her $1000 once you reach a bank. Causal decision theory says that once you're safely in town, paying has no causal upshot - the ride already happened - so a CDT agent keeps the money and doesn't pay. But if the driver predicted this, she wouldn't have picked you up, so you'd have died in the desert. So it looks like it's better to be disposed to pay than to be a strict CDT-maximiser, even though paying, in the moment, isnt what CDT recommends. Parfit's point is that it can be rational (because it leads to better outcomes) to have a disposition that will make you do something irrational, by ordinary act-rationality standards, later on - that's the 'rational irrationality.' Evidential decision theory actually does better here than CDT: since paying is evidence that you're the type of person the driver would've predicted would pay, an EDT agent pays up, just like an EDT agent one-boxes in Newcomb's problem for analogous reasons. The general lesson here is close to what Gauthier calls 'resolute choice': you should stick to the plan you'd have chosen in advance instead of re-optimising at each moment, even when abandoning the plan looks locally better."
