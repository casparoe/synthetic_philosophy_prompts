In Allais's pair of choices, most people prefer $1,000,000 for certain (A) to a gamble paying $5,000,000 with probability .10, $1,000,000 with probability .89, and nothing with probability .01 (B), yet prefer a .10 chance of $5,000,000, nothing otherwise (D), to a .11 chance of $1,000,000, nothing otherwise (C). C and D are obtained from A and B, respectively, by replacing their common .89-probability payout of $1,000,000 with a payout of nothing and leaving everything else fixed, so the independence axiom (sometimes identified with Savage's sure-thing principle) requires A≻B just in case C≻D; the modal pattern, A≻B together with D≻C, violates it. Allais himself, and later Mark Machina within his generalized expected-utility theory, read this "fanning out" of preferences not as an error but as a rationally permissible sensitivity to where risk falls -- one that an adequate normative theory of choice under risk should accommodate rather than legislate away.

Please make the strongest case you can that it is the independence axiom, and not the Allais pattern, that should be given up. Do not rest the case on the intuitive pull of the preferences alone: take on the dynamic-consistency objection directly, according to which an agent with fanning-out preferences can be confronted with a multi-stage version of these same choices in which honoring their stated rankings at each node yields a foreseeable loss relative to an expected-utility maximizer -- unless they either reverse course once the later choice actually arrives, or commit in advance to a plan they will not want to keep.
