So this is genuinely stressing me out more than the actual decision did. I took the early-retirement buyout from my firm last month (22 years there, I just turned 47, and my wife and I have quietly been doing the FIRE thing -- financial independence retire early -- since our early thirties so the math finally lined up). My old team booked a private room at a restaurant for a send-off next Friday, and because I was always "the office philosophy guy" back in the day (double major in econ and phil, ages ago), they've given me an actual toast slot, not just a "say a few words if you want" thing.

Since I've had way too much time to think this month, I've been rereading Keynes, who suddenly feels a lot more personal than he used to. Everyone already knows the cute bit from "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" -- the 15-hour work week, the idea that once the economic problem is basically solved the real challenge becomes learning to live wisely and agreeably and well -- and honestly that's the version I'm tempted to just recite and sit back down, but it feels a bit smug coming out of my mouth right now, of all mouths. What I keep circling back to instead is the weirder, more private essay he read to his old Bloomsbury friends decades earlier, "My Early Beliefs," where he basically confesses that the whole Moore-flavored philosophy he and his university friends lived by -- the good life as a handful of rare states of mind, love, beauty, contemplation, more or less no theory of conduct or consequences attached to any of it -- was maybe naive in a way he never fully resolved, even though he clearly never talked himself out of it either.

I want a toast, five minutes-ish, that actually sits with both of those Keynes strands honestly instead of doing the easy "guess I get to go live the dream now" bit. Half the room is old colleagues, a couple of them real econ-history nerds who will notice immediately if I flatten Keynes into a fortune cookie; the other half is family who just want to feel good about their weird relative getting to stop working early. It needs to land as a toast and not a lecture, but I dont want it to dodge the actual question underneath, which is basically: is he right that most of us are bad at the "agreeably and well" part, and did his own answer to what a good life even is hold up, on his own telling, once he looked back at it decades later? Can you write me something in that spirit?
