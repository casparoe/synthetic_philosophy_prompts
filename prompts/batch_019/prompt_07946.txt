I've spent a long time reading LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and their various offshoots, and coherent extrapolated volition is one of those ideas from that world I keep returning to without ever quite settling what I think of it. The core move, as I understand it, is to avoid making a superintelligence's target some object-level ethical theory that the programmers have to get right on the first try; instead, the AI is supposed to act on whatever we would want if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, and had grown up farther together. Each person's volition gets extrapolated individually along those dimensions and then checked for where the extrapolated wishes actually agree, and the "coherent" part of the name is doing the work of licensing the AI to act confidently only where that convergence is real.

What's been nagging at me is that this looks structurally identical to the "full-information" or "ideal-advisor" theories of a person's good that metaethicists were already dismantling before CEV was written: the view that what's good for you is whatever your fully informed, fully rational counterpart would want for you. If I'm remembering the names right, that style of theory ran into a serious objection pressed by people like Connie Rosati and David Sobel: idealize someone enough to make them reliably wise, and you have usually idealized them into someone whose psychology has drifted so far from the original that it is unclear why the original person's good should be defined by — or why the original owes any deference to — what that hypothetical stranger wants. CEV's extra machinery of running this across an entire population and only acting on the overlap doesn't obviously dissolve that problem, and I suspect it might compound it, since the thing you are now being asked to defer to isn't even a hypothetical version of you, but a hypothetical consensus among idealized strangers.

I'd like you to argue for a specific verdict rather than survey the field: that CEV cannot escape the alienation/authority problem that already undermined naive full-information accounts of personal well-being, and that the coherence-across-persons step is not a fix for that problem but an independent, second way of losing the authority CEV needs in order to count as a legitimate target for a superintelligence's behavior. Take seriously the strongest reply available to a defender of CEV — that the idealization is meant only to strip away factual error and akrasia, not to install a new personality with new values — and explain concretely why that reply fails once you factor in how much room a clause like "grown up farther together" leaves for the extrapolation to drift.
