In a graduate seminar on Sidgwick's ethics that I am currently teaching, we spent a session this week on something that strikes me as a genuine puzzle rather than a mere biographical curiosity. The same philosopher who, in the Methods of Ethics, refused to admit any proposition as self-evident until it had survived the most exacting tests of clarity, coherence, and expert consensus also served, on and off for more than a decade, as founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, chairing its Census of Hallucinations and personally sitting with mediums such as Eusapia Palladino and Leonora Piper.

As several of his biographers stress, this was no idle curiosity: Sidgwick seems to have hoped that empirical evidence of the soul's survival of death might accomplish what pure argument could not, namely resolve his famous "dualism of practical reason" — the standoff between rational egoism and utilitarianism that left him unable to show that duty and self-interest must ultimately coincide. And yet, for all that he personally needed a positive result, he went to his grave conceding that decades of painstaking investigation had failed to deliver one.

This has stopped being a purely historical question for me, because I currently serve on a small interdisciplinary faculty committee — a few dozen colleagues drawn from philosophy, psychology, and physics — deliberating on whether to back a junior colleague's application for internal funding to run a large, preregistered replication of Daryl Bem's controversial precognition experiments. My own vote is not yet settled, and I notice I am torn between the two postures Sidgwick spent the better part of two decades trying to referee within his own Society: reflexive dismissal on one side, and the sort of performed open-mindedness, shading into credulity, that characterized the spiritualist members who eventually resigned from it in frustration at how skeptical his circle turned out to be. Please describe the most important implications of the particular way Sidgwick conducted his inquiry — not his ultimate, negative verdict on the phenomena themselves, but his manner of handling evidence he badly wanted to point in one direction — for how I ought to reason about my colleague's proposal and cast my vote.
