I've been rereading Yudkowsky's old "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities" post alongside the broader literature on money-pump and Dutch-book arguments, and I think I've assembled a version of the instrumental convergence thesis that is considerably stronger than the usual "plausible default tendency" framing you see in most AI safety writing. The argument runs like this: coherence theorems establish that any agent whose preferences cannot be represented by a single, stable utility function is exploitable via some sequence of trades, so any sufficiently capable agent that isn't systematically leaving resources on the table — whether due to competitive pressure, selection, or just its own aversion to dominated strategies — will end up behaving as an expected-utility maximizer relative to some consistent utility function. Separately, it seems almost definitional that for any non-trivial terminal goal (anything short of one that is already permanently and completely satisfied), continued existence, freedom of action, and control over resources are instrumentally useful, since a disabled or resource-starved agent cannot optimize for much of anything. Put the two together, and instrumental convergence stops looking like an inductive generalization about the particular AI systems we happen to be building and starts looking like a corollary of the coherence theorems themselves — something closer in kind to a proof than to an empirical trend with possible exceptions.

This came up in a long-running disagreement with a few people from my old LessWrong meetup group, none of whom could identify the specific step where the derivation actually fails, which has left me more rather than less confident in it. What I can't square is why, if the argument is really this tight, AI safety researchers who otherwise take power-seeking behavior very seriously keep hedging and describing instrumental convergence as a "default" or a "likely tendency" rather than presenting it with the same confidence they would give the Von Neumann-Morgenstern theorem itself. Is there a specific point in the chain where the reasoning only licenses a weaker, defeasible conclusion than the one I am drawing, or is the hedging mostly rhetorical caution about a result that is, in fact, about as solid as these things get?
