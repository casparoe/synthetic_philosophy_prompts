I'm a software engineer (mostly backend/ML infra) and a few of us at work run a little lunchtime reading group where we go through old AI safety papers. Last week we did Armstrong and Mindermann's "Occam's razor is insufficient to infer the preferences of irrational agents" (NeurIPS-ish, I want to say 2018, don't quote me), and it kinda broke my brain. The gist, as far as I understood it: if all you can observe is an agent's behavior, you cannot uniquely factor that behavior into "the reward function/preferences it's pursuing" plus "the planner that turns preferences into action" (which is where all the irrationality or bias would live), because for literally any observed behavior there's a degenerate planner-and-reward pair that reproduces it exactly, and no simplicity/Occam's-razor style prior can rule those degenerate pairs out in general, since their combined complexity isn't meaningfully higher then the "intended" pair's. So the split between preference and bias is underdetermined by behavioral data, no matter how much data you collect.

What I can't stop thinking about is that this seems like exactly the same problem that shows up in ethics under different names, just without anyone really pointing out it's the same problem. Like when Elster and Sen talk about people forming "adaptive preferences" to cope with bad circumstances, so there revealed choices stop tracking their real interests, or more generally whenever someone tries to sort a person's actual preferences into the ones that should count for something like preference-satisfaction views of welfare and the ones that should get thrown out as bias, prejudice, addiction, adaptive preference, whatever. Everyone kind of writes as if there's a real fact of the matter here, one that sufficiently careful psychology, or some idealization procedure like "what they'd want on reflection," could uncover in principle. I'm now wondering if the IRL-style result shows that's false even in the limit of complete information about someone's psychology, for structural reasons rather than just practical ones.

So here's what I'd like you to argue: that the Armstrong/Mindermann-style underdetermination generalizes fully to the moral case — that there's no way, even in principle, even given complete facts about a person's psychology and behavior, to draw a non-question-begging line between their "genuine" preferences and their "biases," and that any such line necessarily smuggles in ethical assumptions that aren't recoverable from the psychological facts alone. I'd like you to seriously engage with the obvious escape routes (e.g., "just idealize using reflective equilibrium or informed-desire theory to strip out the bias," or "biases are the situationally-triggered, unstable ones, and preferences are whatever's left stable across contexts") and explain concretely why they do or don't dodge the underdetermination, rather than just asserting the conclusion and moving on.
