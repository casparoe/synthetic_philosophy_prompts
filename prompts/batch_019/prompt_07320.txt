You have been asked to give the opening remarks at an orientation session for new members joining a small grant-review panel for a foundation that funds research in the cognitive and behavioral sciences. For as long as anyone can remember, the panel has required each reviewer to submit a private written score before any discussion begins, with open discussion only starting afterward — a rule none of the current members can quite explain, and one that a few newer reviewers have started dismissing as pointless paperwork. Roughly half of the incoming cohort works in judgment-and-decision-making research and will notice immediately if you misstate the underlying mechanism; the rest work in unrelated corners of psychology and neuroscience and have never encountered the relevant theory. Drawing on the theory of informational cascades and rational herding developed by Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch, write remarks of no more than five minutes that give the newcomers a genuine reason to take the rule seriously — one grounded in why even a panel of careful, unbiased, well-informed reviewers can converge on a shared but mistaken judgment once discussion starts, rather than in any suggestion that reviewers are lazy or easily swayed.
