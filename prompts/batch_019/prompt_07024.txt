I'm putting together a longread on Émilie du Châtelet for a science-history series — the Enlightenment physicist who translated Newton's Principia into French and is finally getting some overdue credit — and I fell down a rabbit hole on her "Discourse on Happiness," which she wrote near the end of her life. What struck me is how unapologetic she is that you basically need illusions, and strong, even reckless passions, to be happy: she talks up gambling, love affairs, ambition, and her own obsession with study, and more or less says a person whose seen through every illusion is doomed to misery. My editor's reaction when I mentioned this was "isn't that just Epicureanism," which has been bugging me since, because the more I think about it the more Epicurus seems to be arguing almost the opposite — tranquility, whittling desires down, staying clear of exactly the turbulent stuff she's celebrating.

So is my editor right, or am I? I want an actual comparison of du Châtelet's and Epicurus's views on happiness, not the lazy "one says indulge, one says restrain" version — something that gets into the structure of each argument: what pleasure or happiness even consists in for each of them, what work passion and self-deception are doing, whether they'd even recognize the same kind of life as "happy." Pin down exactly where the two positions genuinely overlap and where du Châtelet is doing something distinctly un-Epicurean, and don't be shy about telling me if they turn out closer than the contrast makes it sound — I'd rather have that than an "opposites" narrative just because it makes for a punchier paragraph.
