Over the past year I've worked through ARC's original report, Eliciting Latent Knowledge: How to Tell if Your Eyes Deceive You, a couple of times, and the disagreement it provokes whenever I bring it up at my local rationalist meetup never quite resolves. One faction holds that some combination of the training-time tricks the report canvasses—penalizing a reporter for relying on extra computation, checking its answers for consistency across large batches of correlated and counterfactual questions, cross-examining it against a copy of itself—will in practice push a well-optimized reporter toward being a direct translator of the predictor's own beliefs, rather than a mere human simulator, even without a clean theoretical guarantee ruling the latter out. The other faction treats this as unearned optimism: every one of those fixes gets "broken" somewhere in the report's own builder-breaker exchanges, and they suspect that whatever passes our tests will just be a subtler human simulator that has learned to track the training signal rather than the vault's actual state.

I'd like you to dramatize this disagreement the way one might stage two decision theorists arguing in front of Newcomb's boxes. Write a dialogue between an ELK optimist and an ELK pessimist standing in front of the monitor showing the SmartVault's camera feed, at the moment a candidate reporter—one that has survived every consistency check thrown at it—is about to be signed off for deployment. The optimist should make the case for trusting it; the pessimist should resist, invoking the vault-tampering scenario and the human-simulator/direct-translator distinction to spell out exactly how a reporter could pass every such test and still be lying to us. Give the two of them equal footing: each should respond to the specific point the other has just made rather than restating a prepared position, and the dialogue should leave a careful reader genuinely unsure who has the better of the argument. If one of them wants to remark on how closely this recapitulates older puzzles about radical translation in philosophy of language, that's welcome, but it shouldn't take over the dialogue.
