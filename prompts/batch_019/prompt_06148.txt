For my AP Seminar independent research project this year, I decided to dig into modal metaphysics, and I've gotten a bit stuck on the disagreement between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the more traditional "transworld identity" view it was designed to replace. I understand the basic setup: Lewis holds that no individual exists in more than one possible world, so a sentence like "Hubert Humphrey could have won the 1968 election" is really a claim about one of Humphrey's counterparts — a similar but numerically distinct person — winning in some other world, whereas the traditional view holds that Humphrey himself, the very same man, exists across worlds and simply has different properties in each. My philosophy club advisor showed me Kripke's famous footnote objecting that Humphrey surely doesn't care whether some merely similar stranger wins in his place; but whenever I try to find how a committed counterpart theorist actually answers that, most sources just restate the objection and move on, which isn't very satisfying.

Could you write me a dialogue of around 15–20 exchanges between two philosophers holding these opposing views — say they end up seated next to each other at a conference dinner and the conversation turns to this — in which they genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves rather than one side simply winning? I'd like the counterpart theorist to face the Humphrey objection directly, and also to be pressed on what's often called the "problem of accidental intrinsics": if Humphrey's counterpart in another world has six fingers instead of five, counterpart theory seems to turn Humphrey's own intrinsic five-fingeredness into some kind of relational matter about other people in other worlds. But I don't want the transworld-identity side to escape unscathed either — I'd like the counterpart theorist to hit back with something like Chisholm's paradox about identity through a long chain of gradually changing possible worlds, to argue that "genuine" cross-world identity is no less arbitrary, or no less in need of some counterpart-like relation to do the real work, than what Lewis proposes. Both characters should clearly know where their own position is weakest, and each response should engage with what the other just actually said, not with a caricature of it.

Feel free to give them names and a couple of lines of scene-setting if that helps the dialogue flow, but please keep most of it focused on the philosophical back-and-forth itself, and don't feel obligated to wrap things up with a tidy resolution — as far as I can tell, nobody has really settled this.
