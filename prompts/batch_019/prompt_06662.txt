As a philosophy PhD student with a side interest in philosophy of religion, I've been sitting in on a reading group this term that just spent a session on David Lewis's short essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" I went in expecting the usual modal-realism-and-counterfactuals Lewis, and was surprised to find him doing straightforward philosophy of religion, seemingly just because he found the puzzle interesting. As I read it, his argument looks close to fatal for the doctrine: punishment, unlike a monetary debt, doesn't seem to be the kind of thing that can be transferred to a willing innocent substitute. Nobody thinks a judge should release a convicted man because his brother has volunteered to serve the prison sentence in his place, or to sit in the electric chair instead of him; the offer looks less like an act of generosity than like a category error. Since penal substitutionary atonement is committed to exactly that structure, with Christ bearing the punishment sinners deserve so that they don't have to, it seems to me that Lewis has produced a fairly clean, general argument that the doctrine can't even be coherently stated, independent of whether Christianity is true.

A friend of mine in the religion department was unimpressed when I brought this up. She pointed out that some version of the objection goes back to the Socinians in the sixteenth century, and that contemporary analytic defenders of penal substitution, like William Lane Craig, are perfectly aware of it and keep writing papers defending the doctrine anyway. That makes me suspect I'm either misreading the structure of Lewis's argument or underestimating the resources available to its defenders, since it would be strange for serious philosophers to keep re-litigating a point that a short essay had already settled once and for all. Could you help me see what's actually going on here -- specifically, whether "punishment just can't be transferred, full stop" is really the argument Lewis is making, and what a defender of penal substitution who granted Lewis's premises might still be able to say in response?
