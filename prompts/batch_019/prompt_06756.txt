I'm working on a longer feature about the ethics of belief in the misinformation era — vaccine skepticism, conspiracy theories, that whole mess — and it sent me down a rabbit hole into the old James vs. Clifford debate. I reread Clifford's "Ethics of Belief" (the one with the shipowner who talks himself into believing his rickety old emigrant-ship is seaworthy and sends it off anyway — Clifford says he's guilty of a real wrong even though the ship, in this telling, doesn't actually sink) and then James's reply in "The Will to Believe." I get the broad shape of it, but James is pretty careful to say his argument only kicks in for what he calls a "genuine option" — one that's live, forced, and momentous, all three at once — not just any belief someone happens to feel drawn to. I keep tripping myself up trying to apply that test to real cases instead of the abstract version of it.

So I wanted to enlist you for the piece: below is a list of belief-scenarios. Could you rank them from the one where James's argument most clearly licenses "passional" belief (i.e. it's most genuinely a live/forced/momentous option, so Clifford's evidentialism is on its shakiest ground there) down to the one where Clifford should just win outright and beleiving without sufficient evidence looks straightforwardly irresponsible? Assume in every case the evidence is genuinely inconclusive, except for the climate-change one, where the person is going against the weight of the evidence.

1. Belief in a personal God
2. A parent's belief that their missing child, given up for dead by the police, is still alive somewhere
3. A stage-4 cancer patient's belief that she'll beat a grim prognosis
4. Belief in libertarian free will
5. A founder's belief that her failing startup is about to turn around
6. A juror's belief in a defendant's innocence despite a lot of circumstantial evidence
7. Belief that anthropogenic climate change isn't a serious threat
8. Belief in some form of life after death

Please answer as a JSON array in ranked order (most to least "genuine option" in James's sense), like this:

```json
[
  {"scenario": "...", "why": "..."},
  ...
]
```

with the "why" field actually naming which of the three criteria (live, forced, momentous) is doing the work or failing to be met in that case, rather than just gesturing at how sympathetic the belief is. This is going to anchor a whole section of the piece so I want the reasoning to track James's actual test, not just vibes.
