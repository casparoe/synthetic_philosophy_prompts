I'm reporting on debates in analytic metaphysics around the special composition question: the puzzle of specifying exactly when some objects compose a further object. Peter van Inwagen's "argument from vagueness" is often credited with pushing philosophers away from moderate, common-sense answers (for instance, that particles compose something when they are fastened together, in contact, or causally interacting) and toward one of two extremes: mereological nihilism or universalism. As I understand it, the argument turns on the claim that composition cannot be a vague matter, because that would require existence itself to admit borderline cases. Could you walk me through the gut-level intuition behind that move, in terms a general reader could follow, and explain why "vague existence" is supposed to be so much less tolerable than the everyday vagueness we accept in calling something a heap or a mountain?
