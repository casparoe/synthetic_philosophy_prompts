So in my Metaphysics of Causation seminar this week we got into causation by omission and I'm still kind of chewing on it. The example our professor used: a woman hires a gardener to water her plants while she's away, he just doesn't, and they die -- and the intuition is obviously that his not watering them caused their death. But then she pointed out that basically everyone else in the world also didn't water those plants that week: the neighbor, randos walking their dogs down the street, me, literally every person in Antarctica -- and none of THOSE omissions count as causing the plants to die, even though its exactly as true for all of us that if we'd watered them, they'd have lived. So there's this open question of what makes the gardener's not-watering special enough to be a real cause when everyone else's isn't, given that counterfactually they're all on a par.

From what I can piece together there are two camps on this. One camp (I think this traces back to Lewis) wants to say some omissions really are genuine causes, and then has to do extra work explaining why only some omissions get to count that way instead of all of them. The other camp bites the bullet and says omissions were never really causes at all -- that "his not watering them caused the plants to die" is smuggling in facts about duty and expectation (he was paid to do it, we weren't) and dressing them up as if they were about the causal structure of the world.

Me and my roommate, whos in the class too, argued about which camp is actually right after section and got no where, so now I want to see it played out properly. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers holding these two positions, where they actually work through the plant case and then pressure-test each other with a harder one -- like a doctor who deliberately withholds a medicine she knows would save her patient -- and see if "it's really just norms, not causation" still sounds plausible there? I don't want a strawman on either side; I want each of them responding to the other's actual best move, not just repeating themselves louder. If it helps, you can imagine them having this argument literally standing over the dying houseplant, though that part's optional.
