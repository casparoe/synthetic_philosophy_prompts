This past weekend a few of us went out for dinner after a training exercise, and I ended up in a two-hour argument with a close friend that neither of us really let go of — we were still texting about it the next morning. I volunteer with a small backcountry search-and-rescue team, and the dispute started over a scenario we sometimes use for training discussions: a storm rolls in on a mountain and two separate hiking parties get stranded on opposite ridgelines at the same time. The team has the resources to reach only one ridge before nightfall, and whoever isn't reached will almost certainly not survive exposure until morning. Suppose one ridge has a single hiker and the other has six, and that in every respect that would normally matter to a rescuer — health, age, how long each can hold out, difficulty of the terrain — the two situations are identical; the only difference is the number of people. My friend, who thinks in terms of expected value for a living, said the answer is obvious: you send the team to the six, because six lives outweigh one, and any protocol that didn't reflect that would be indefensible.

That's the part I couldn't accept, though I had trouble saying exactly why. It isn't that I think the lone hiker matters more, or that I have some competing calculation of my own — it's that treating the choice as simple addition, as though six people's survival can be pooled into one quantity that straightforwardly outweighs one person's survival, seemed to skip over the fact that each of these seven people stands to lose absolutely everything, individually, regardless of how many others also happen to be at risk elsewhere. At one point I suggested that the more honest response might be to give the lone hiker some kind of real chance, even something as crude as a coin flip, rather than treating him as automatically expendable because of a headcount. My friend thought this was a bad idea bordering on irresponsible: why deliberately pick the option with a worse expected outcome just to make a point about fairness to an abstraction?

Neither of us gave any ground, and I haven't been able to let it drop since. I'd like to know whether my instinct here tracks a real and defensible consideration, or whether my friend is simply correct and I'm just uncomfortable with cold arithmetic in a case where lives are on the line. Is the number of people on each side always the decisive factor in a choice like this, or is there a serious argument that it shouldn't automatically settle what a rescuer — or anyone in that position — owes to the people involved?
