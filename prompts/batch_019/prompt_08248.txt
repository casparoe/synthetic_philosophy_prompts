Here's the question (worth 20 points):

"The measurement problem is sometimes described as the tension between the deterministic, unitary evolution of the wavefunction under the Schrodinger equation and the single, definite outcomes we actually observe when we measure something. Explain how each of the following resolves (or dissolves) this tension: (a) the Copenhagen interpretation, (b) Everett's relative-state / many-worlds interpretation, and (c) the de Broglie-Bohm pilot-wave theory. Your answer should also say what each interpretation implies about whether the underlying physics is deterministic and whether it's local."

And here's the answer I'm stuck on:

"On the Copenhagen view, the wavefunction gives the probabilities of different measurement outcomes via the Born rule, but it isn't by itself a complete, observer-independent description of reality. When a measurement happens, the wavefunction collapses onto one outcome, and which outcome occurs is genuinely random rather than just unknown in advance -- this is why Copenhagen is usually classified as indeterministic. Because the theory only makes claims about what happens at measurement, it doesn't commit to particles having any definite trajectory or hidden property in between measurements.

Everett denies that collapse ever happens at all. Instead the universal wavefunction just keeps evolving unitarily forever, and 'measurement' is really just the measuring device (and the observer) becoming entangled with the system, so the total state now includes different versions of the observer, each recording a different outcome. Since the wavefunction never collapses, the theory is fully deterministic at the fundamental level, and the appearance of randomness is just what it's like to be one branch among many. Because literally every possible outcome happens in some branch, Everett doesn't need any separate story about where the Born-rule probabilities come from -- they just fall out of the branching structure automatically, which is a big part of why people say Everett is the cleanest resolution of the measurement problem.

Bohm's theory adds a 'pilot wave' that guides particles along definite trajectories at all times, so a particle always has a real position whether or not anyone is looking, and a measurement just reveals a pre-existing fact instead of creating one. This makes Bohmian mechanics fully deterministic, and because each particle's position is a genuine, well-defined property that belongs to that particle alone, it also counts as a local hidden-variable theory, unlike Copenhagen -- which is part of why some physicists like it, since it recovers a classical, common-sense picture of definite particles without giving up determinism."
