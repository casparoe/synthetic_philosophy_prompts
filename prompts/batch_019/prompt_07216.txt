I sit on the ethics board of a Christian relief organization whose code of conduct, following Augustine, holds that staff may never assert what they believe to be false in order to deceive another person — not even to save a life — though they may stay silent, refuse to answer, or redirect a conversation. Our teams currently work in a region where a religious minority is being hunted by militias conducting house-to-house searches, and staff increasingly face questioners who ask direct yes-or-no questions such as "Is anyone else in this building?" under conditions where silence itself functions as a confession, leaving an outright false denial as the only way left to protect someone. Several veteran staff have begun lying anyway and are asking the board to formally amend the code; other members insist that Augustine's grounds for condemning even life-saving lies were not an incidental feature of his view but followed from commitments — about truthfulness, trust, and the condition of the liar's own soul — that a carve-out would abandon rather than refine. Draft the memo I will bring to the board: recommend whether to retain, narrow, or abandon the no-lying rule for these encounters, engage Augustine's own reasons for his position rather than generic consequentialist objections to it, and give field staff guidance they could actually follow in the moment.
