I spent about thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before I retired, and lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole into philosophy of math (blame my grandson, he's doing a math PhD and keeps sending me stuff to read). The thing that's really been bugging me is the continuum hypothesis. I get the basic technical story - Gödel showed CH can't be disproved from the usual axioms (ZFC), then Cohen showed it can't be proved either, so its just independent of them. What I can't get straight in my head is what that actually MEANS. Does it mean CH simply has no truth value, full stop, kind of like asking whether the "true" geometry is Euclidean or not? Or does it mean there's still a definite fact out there (like Gödel himself apparently kept believing, even after Cohen's result) and we just haven't found the right axiom yet to uncover it? In my old field, if two models disagreed about some measurable quantity, I'd just assume nature had an answer whether or not our instruments were good enough to find it yet - so this idea of a well-posed-sounding math question just... not having an answer, independent of any axiom, really trips me up.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people who used to be close collaborators and now disagree hard on this: one who thinks of themself as a "universist" and insists CH has a determinate truth value in the one true universe of sets, we just need to find the right new axioms (large cardinals, forcing axioms, whatever) to reveal it; and one who's become a "multiverse" type and thinks that whole way of talking is a category mistake, since there are lots of equally legitimate set-theoretic universes and CH is just true in some and false in others, full stop. I don't want either one to be a pushover - I want them actually engaging with each other's best points, and maybe getting into the follow-up puzzle of why most of the fancy new large cardinal axioms seem to leave CH's status completely untouched either way. Set it wherever feels natural to you - a conference hallway, an old office with a chalkboard, wherever.
