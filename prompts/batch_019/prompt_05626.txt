Since retiring after three decades as a population geneticist, I've spent my free time reading moral philosophy, most recently on Ubuntu and other relational conceptions of personhood -- the idea, captured in the Nguni saying "umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu," that persons are constituted through their relationships rather than existing as individuals prior to them. I now sit on the ethics committee for a genomics biobank consortium that samples rural communities to study inherited disease risk. Current policy treats one signed consent form per participant as sufficient. Several committee members argue that where personhood is relational, an individual's signature obtained apart from the community's own deliberative process secures nothing the community would recognize as consent; others reply that formalizing "community consent" just hands veto power to elders who may not speak for women, youth, or dissenters. A further complication: the protocol also seeks broad, indefinite consent to bank samples for unspecified future research, and the committee is split on whether relational personhood supports that -- consent was never a one-off transaction between strangers to begin with -- or actually rules it out, since an ongoing relationship has to be renegotiated rather than banked once. Please draft the memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one of: approving the protocol as submitted, approving it with specific required changes, or tabling it pending a pilot community-engagement process, and it should engage the philosophical disagreement on its merits, being fair to the strongest version of whichever side you don't recommend.
