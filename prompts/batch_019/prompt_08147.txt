"For Schopenhauer, every art except music occupies itself with the Platonic Ideas, which he treats as the direct and adequate objectification of the Will at a given grade. Architecture, for instance, is essentially the aesthetic contemplation of the conflict between gravity and rigidity, the lowest grades of the will's objectivity in inorganic nature; sculpture and painting exhibit the Idea of the human and animal form; and tragedy, the highest of the arts that work through Ideas, exhibits the Idea of humanity itself in the collision of individual wills. In each case the artist grasps an Idea through will-less, disinterested contemplation and objectifies it in a medium, so that the beholder is likewise lifted for a moment out of ordinary willing into pure perception of the Idea.

Music, Schopenhauer insists, differs from the other arts in kind rather than degree: it does not copy the Ideas at all, but is a direct copy or objectification of the Will itself. This is why he can say that we might just as well call the world embodied music as embodied will, and why music, unlike painting or poetry, does not need the detour through a represented world of particular things in order to express the innermost nature of that world. Because it bypasses the Ideas altogether, music is a kind of universal language, immediately intelligible across cultures without the mediation of concepts, and Schopenhauer treats its effect on us as correspondingly deeper and more overwhelming than that of any art that still depends on representing particular objects.

He cashes this out through an analogy with the texture of a musical composition. The bass, moving slowly and confined to the intervals permitted by the strict rules of fundamental harmony, corresponds on his view to the self-conscious, rational will of human beings, since it alone proceeds according to settled law; the inner, complementary voices move more freely than the bass and correspond to the duller stirrings of the plant and animal kingdoms; and the melody, as the quickest and least constrained voice, wandering wherever it likes without a fixed course, is what Schopenhauer assigns to unorganized, inorganic nature. He takes this to be essentially the same insight behind Leibniz's remark that music is an unconscious exercise in arithmetic in which the mind does not know it is counting, a phrase he approvingly adopts as his own summary of music's essence, since on his view the numerical ratios between tones simply are the will's grades of objectification presented in an abstract, non-conceptual form."
