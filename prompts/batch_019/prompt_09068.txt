I've been down a Kierkegaard rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm sneaking onto the ideas desk about online pile-ons, and I keep getting stuck on this one episode from his life that I can't stop thinking about. Back in December 1845, Kierkegaard got annoyed that a gossipy Copenhagen satire sheet called The Corsair kept praising him while savaging every other writer in town, so he basically dared them, in print, to attack him to. They obliged way harder than he expected: caricatures of him as a hunchback with lopsided trouser legs, butcher boys and university students shouting "Søren!" and "either-or!" at him in the street, even his own tailor asking him to buy his pants elsewhere because the mockery was bad for business. He gave up his old habit of wondering Copenhagen chatting with strangers and pretty much never got it back. What came out of it a few months later was an essay called "The Present Age" (originally just the second half of a longer book, "Two Ages"), where he argues that "the public" isn't a real collective at all, just a kind of phantom or "monstrous abstraction" that only exists once a press is around to conjure it into being — and that the press basically functions as this phantom public's dog, set loose on whoever briefly stands out, not out of any real conviction, just for the amusement of "leveling" them back down to size, before getting bored and calling the dog off.

I mentioned all this to a couple of philosopher friends over drinks and one of them pointed out how eerily well it maps onto how online dogpiles work now, except our "Corsair" is a few thousand anonymous accounts instead of one gossip rag. Honestly it kind of got under my skin that a guy writing in 1846 basically called it. So here's what I'd love from you: a short story, maybe 1000-1500 words, that dramatizes this dynamic through one character's ordeal — someone who, a bit like Kierkegaard, half-invites their own leveling (by publicly resenting being ignored, or mocking the crowd's cowardly anonymity, or demanding finally to be taken seriously) and then gets far more attention than they bargained for. You don't need to namecheck Kierkegaard or set it in Denmark — modern, period, or something in between is fine by me — but I'd like the actual mechanics of the plot to track his specific claims rather than just gesturing at "cancel culture" in general: the public as a manufactured phantom rather than any real group of people, the press or platform as a dog that gets set loose and then called off once everyone's bored, and the leveling as something that happens to the person precisely because no single person is doing it or takes responsibility for it. Bonus points if the ending gestures at what Kierkegaard called "the single individual" as the only real way out, even if your character fumbles it.
