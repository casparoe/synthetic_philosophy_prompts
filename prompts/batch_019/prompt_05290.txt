The question was: "Harman and Doris argue that virtue ethics has an empirical problem, not just a conceptual one. (a) Explain the empirical evidence behind the situationist critique and what it's supposed to show about the kind of character virtue ethics needs. (b) Give one serious response a virtue ethicist could make to this critique, and say whether you think it actually works."

Here's the answer (I fixed a couple of spelling things but kept the content and structure exactly as written):

"The situationist critique says virtue ethics assumes people have stable character traits like honesty or courage that show up consistently across different situations, but the psychology doesn't back this up. Hartshorne and May's old studies on children's honesty found that a kid who cheated on a test was barely more likely than chance to also lie about a game score or steal a coin -- honesty in one context just doesn't predict honesty in another very well, and Mischel later put the typical cross-situational correlation at around 0.3, well below what you'd need to call something a robust trait. The point is made even more vividly by Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, where seminary students on their way to give a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan mostly walked right past a man slumped in a doorway because they'd been told they were running late. This is basically the fundamental attribution error: what these studies actually show is that the same person's behavior swings wildly with tiny situational cues like being in a hurry, instead of staying constant the way a real character trait would require -- which is exactly what you'd expect if 'character' is really just us over-projecting stable traits onto behavior that's actually driven by the situation.

A good response to this is Doris's own point that Aristotle never claimed most people are virtuous -- virtue is supposed to be rare and the product of a lot of habituation, so of course a random batch of undergrads or seminarians isn't going to show robust virtuous traits. The studies just confirm what Aristotle already conceded, that most of us are somewhere in the mediocre middle, not that virtue itself is empirically empty. I think this basically works, since it defuses the studies without denying any of the data -- the situationists showed that ORDINARY people lack robust traits, not that virtue is impossible, so the critique loses most of its force once you remember Aristotle was never talking about ordinary people to begin with."
