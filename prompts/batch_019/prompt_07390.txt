I've been chasing a feature for the last couple of months on long covid and ME/CFS patients, and something keeps nagging at me. Almost everyone I interview describes some version of the same experience: they bring symptoms to a doctor that dont fit a recognised diagnostic box, get told it's "probably anxiety" or "just deconditioning," and after enough rounds of that, several of them said they'd started doubting their own read on their own bodies before they even walked in the door. One woman literally said "I edit my symptoms down before I say them out loud now, so I sound more believable."

A friend doing a philosophy PhD pointed me toward Miranda Fricker's stuff on epistemic injustice - the distinction between testimonial injustice (getting discounted because of who you are) and hermeneutical injustice (not even having the shared concepts available to explain what's happening to you) - and mentioned there's a whole strand of philosophy-of-medicine people (I want to say Havi Carel and Ian James Kidd, though don't hold me to the names) who've applied this directly to chronic and contested illness. It maps onto what I'm hearing almost too well, which is exactly why it makes me nervous - I don't want to just bolt a trendy philosophy term onto the piece as decoration without it actually doing real work.

So here's what I'd like from you: talk me through what you think are the most important implications of the testimonial/hermeneutical injustice framework for two specific things - first, how I should actually conduct and write up interviews with patients whose conditions are medically contested, and second, the ways I myself, as the one choosing quotes, framing, and how much "expert" pushback to include, could end up reproducing a smaller version of the very same injustice. I already know the textbook definitions, so skip the primer and go straight for the less obvious, harder-to-argue-for points - including any places you think the framework is being stretched further than it should be.
