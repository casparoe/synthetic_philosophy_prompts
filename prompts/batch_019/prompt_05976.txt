So I went down a rabbit hole a while back with Scott Aaronson's "freebits" idea from his Ghost in the Quantum Turing Machine paper (I think someone originally linked it in a rationalist discord server), and I brought it up again with some friends at a meetup recently and none of us could agree on wether it actually does any real philosophical work or if its just a fancy repackaging of ordinary quantum indeterminism. Quick recap in case the framing matters for how you answer: Aaronson wants a physics-respecting story where human decisions could be "unpredictable in principle," not just unpredictable-to-us the way a coin flip is. His move is to distinguish garden-variety quantum randomness (which has a well-defined Born-rule probability distribution, so it's "risk" in Frank Knight's old economics sense) from what he calls freebits: degrees of freedom, maybe seeded way back in the early universe, that stay causally isolated from the rest of the world for a long time — long enough that no physical process, even in principle, could have assigned them a probability distribution ahead of time. The pitch is that brains might amplify these freebits into macroscopic behavior, so a decision wouldn't be determined even probabilistically by the prior state of the universe. This is one of his running examples for why he thinks computational complexity (not just computability) is the missing ingredient in a bunch of old philosophical puzzles — the "unpredictable even in principle" part leans more on Kolmogorov complexity / algorithmic randomness than on P-vs-NP, but he treats it as the same family of idea.

I want you to actually stake out a position instead of doing the "well, there are considerations on both sides" thing models always default to. Argue that the whole Knightian/freebit apparatus, even granting every physical premise Aaronson wants (freebits exist, stay isolated for billions of years, get successfully amplified by brains, whatever), doesn't buy libertarians anything beyond what they already had with bog-standard quantum indeterminism. Specifically, argue that the risk-vs-Knightian-uncertainty distinction is doing zero philosophical work once you ask the actual question free-will skeptics care about — did the agent control which way the freebit "went," or did it just happen to them, same as a dice roll would — so that Aaronson's apparatus is answering a question that isn't the one the luck objection is asking. Feel free to bring in the strongest version of the counterargument first (maybe there's something to the "unpredictable even in principle" bit that I'm not appreciating) before you knock it down, I just don't want a survey of the debate, I want the piece to actually land somewhere.
