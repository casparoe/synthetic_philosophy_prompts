In his mature writings on jurisprudence, Leibniz defines justice as "the charity of the wise" (caritas sapientis): a constant disposition to take pleasure in the happiness of others, in which wisdom directs this benevolence toward what truly perfects them rather than toward their merely apparent good. He insists that such charity is disinterested -- the wise person delights in another's good for its own sake, not merely as a means to some further end. Yet Leibniz also holds that pleasure is nothing but the perception of perfection, one's own or another's when one loves them, and that every act of will is moved by the pursuit of the agent's own greatest pleasure. Please argue that these commitments cannot jointly be sustained, and that, on Leibniz's own psychological premises, the disinterestedness he claims for charity collapses into a refined form of self-interest -- so that his definition of justice fails to pick out anything beyond enlightened egoism.
