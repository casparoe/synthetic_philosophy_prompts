(1) Miriam argued that we are stuck with the axiom whether we like it or not: reject it, and you lose the guarantee that every vector space has a basis, that Tychonoff's theorem holds, and a long list of other results ordinary mathematics depends on. A principle so load-bearing, she said, earns its keep the way any indispensable theoretical posit does.

(2) Owen brought up the Banach-Tarski construction: the axiom lets you cut a solid ball into finitely many pieces (as few as five) and, using only rotations and translations, reassemble them into two balls the same size as the original. An axiom that hands you a second ball for free, he said, has earned our suspicion.

(3) Miriam answered that nothing is actually duplicated: the pieces involved are non-measurable, so they never had a well-defined volume to begin with. The "paradox" looks like one only if you assume every subset of space must be measurable, and nothing forces that assumption on you.

(4) Owen replied that this was too convenient: "non-measurable" isn't some independent fact about the pieces, discovered on its own merits; it's just the label for whatever the axiom needs to produce whenever its consequences would otherwise be absurd. Every other axiom of set theory, he said, lets you exhibit a concrete instance, while this is the one axiom that systematically can't, and a principle you can only defend by redescribing its casualties is unfalsifiable in a way the rest of the theory isn't.

Neither gave any ground. Could you adjudicate moves (1) through (4)?
