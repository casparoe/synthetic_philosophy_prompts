Three weeks ago a close friend of mine left an abusive relationship and spent about two weeks on my couch while she sorted out a protective order. Her ex-partner came to my door twice asking whether I had seen her; both times I told him no, which was false, since I knew exactly where she was in the apartment. I haven't lost sleep over the choice itself, but it has collided in an odd way with a graduate seminar I'm taking this term on patristic and medieval moral philosophy, where we just worked through Augustine's two treatises on lying, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium. Going in, I expected something like the "never lie, except obviously in extreme cases" position I'd absorbed secondhand from discussions of Kant's murderer at the door; instead I found Augustine explicitly considering cases very close to my own — Rahab hiding the Israelite spies, the Hebrew midwives lying to Pharaoh to save the infants — and still refusing to grant the exception.

I've started sketching a term paper on this and would like comments before I go further. My working claim is that Augustine's absolutism isn't just one more application of a blanket "no lying, full stop" rule, but rests on something more specific: since speech has a God-given function of communicating what is actually in one's mind, deliberately asserting what you believe to be false corrupts the liar's own soul in a way that is largely independent of consequences, much as he treats the misuse of other natural faculties elsewhere in his ethics. That would explain why no benefit to others, even saving an innocent life, can offset a lie for him. But it also seems to strain against his own handling of Rahab and the midwives, where he insists God rewarded their mercy and not their lie, as though the lie were a discrete bad ingredient that can be peeled off an otherwise virtuous act and separately forgiven. If lying is really a corruption of the liar's character rather than primarily a wrong done to someone else, I don't see why it should come apart so cleanly from the rest of the action.

I'd value pushback on three things specifically: whether I actually have Augustine's position right, including where a lie like mine — told to protect someone from a violent ex, harming no one and helping someone — would land on the eightfold ranking of lies he works out across these treatises, and whether that ranking is meant to carry any real moral weight given that he still classifies all eight as sins; whether the tension I think I've spotted between the "corruption of the liar" reading and the Rahab episode is a genuine interpretive problem that others have raised, or whether there's a more charitable way of holding both commitments together that I'm missing; and, more generally, whether this looks like a well-focused topic for a fifteen-page seminar paper or whether I'm circling something better addressed from a different angle entirely.
