I'm a PhD student in economics doing my minor field in history of economic thought, and this term we spent a week on Berkeley's The Querist, which none of the philosophy people in my program had ever even mentioned to me -- over there he's apparently just the "esse est percipi" guy. What struck me is that the whole thing is written as a long list of rhetorical queries, and the throughline is that money isn't wealth at all but more like a counter or ticket for moving "power" around, and that its value is basically conventional/social rather than intrinsic -- he's arguing this against the bullionists of his day who wanted to identify a nation's wealth with how much gold and silver it's got sitting around. He also spends a bunch of queries pushing Ireland to set up a national bank that issues credit against future industry, partly because Ireland had a chronic coin shortage and partly because he clearly thinks a public, accountable institution should be steering credit toward productive work rather than leaving it to whatever specie happens to wash up on shore.

My advisor made an offhand joke that Berkeley would've either loved or hated Bitcoin, no middle ground, and now its stuck in my head and I kind of want to actually settle it instead of leaving it as a throwaway line. Could you work out, as carefully as you can, what Berkeley would plausibly say about Bitcoin and cryptocurrencies more generally, given the actual content of the Querist (and his immaterialism/nominalism too, if you think that's genuinely relevant rather than just a cute surface analogy)? I dont want the easy answer where he comes out a proto-crypto guy because money is "just a ticket with no intrinsic value anyway" -- I want you to also take seriously the parts of his view that cut the other way, like his moralizing about idleness and speculation, and his insistence that credit be administered by an accountable public body aimed at the common good rather than just circulating on its own with no one steering it. Basically I want a real, somewhat torn verdict, not a one-liner.
