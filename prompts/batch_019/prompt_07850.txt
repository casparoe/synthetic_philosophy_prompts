My dad died about ten days ago — throat and lung cancer that the VA finally admitted was probably from the burn pits, after two tours in Iraq (2004, then again during the surge in 07-08). I'm giving his eulogy this Saturday and I've gotten stuck on one specific thing. Dad had this line he'd fall back on any time Iraq came up at Thanksgiving or wherever: "I didn't start that war, I just did my job and looked after the guys next to me." He wasn't being defensive when he said it, he actually believed it, it's how he made his peace with the whole thing. After he passed I got into it with my cousin (who is extremely online) about whether that's a legitimate way to think about it or just a comforting story soldiers tell themselves, and it sent me down a whole rabbit hole reading about just war theory. Turns out it's an actual real disagreement among philosophers and not just internet people yelling past each other — one side basically says soldiers on opposite sides of a war have the same rights against each other no matter who's cause is just, so you genuinely can judge a soldier's conduct completely apart from judging the war itself. But then there's this other guy, I want to say McMahan, who argues that's basically a comforting myth: if the side you're fighting for is unjust then you are actually doing something wrong by fighting for it, full stop, even if you had no way of knowing better — the most you get is an excuse, not an actual justification. So now I don't feel like I can just use dad's line at face value without knowing whether I actually think it's true.

Here's what makes Saturday harder: two guys from his old platoon are flying in and they will absolutely want the "he served with honor, the politicians started that war, not him" version, and I don't want them to feel like his service is on trial. But my aunt (his sister) is also going to be there, and she protested the war back in 03, and she and dad didn't speak for almost two years after he re-upped for the second tour — they'd only just really patched it up before he got sick, and I don't want her sitting there feeling like I'm pretending it was all simple. Can you write the actual eulogy? I want it to take a real position on how to think about a soldier's decency separately from the justice of the war he was sent to fight, using whatever is actually true in that disagreement instead of dodging it with something vague like "he served his country" — but it still has to work as a real eulogy and not a philosophy lecture, it can't run more than about five or six minutes out loud, and it can't say one single thing about my dad that isn't true.
