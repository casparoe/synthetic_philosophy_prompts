I've been slowly working through Hobbes's minor mathematical writings for a reading group on early modern philosophy of science, and I keep getting stuck on a short but dense passage from the preface to his 1656 tract *Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematics* — the pamphlet he appended to the English translation of *De Corpore* specifically to go after John Wallis and Seth Ward, the two Savilian professors at Oxford. The passage reads:

"Of arts, some are demonstrable, others indemonstrable; and demonstrable are those the construction of the subject whereof is in the power of the artist himself, who, in his demonstration, does no more but deduce the consequences of his own operation. The reason whereof is this, that the science of every subject is derived from a precognition of the causes, generation, and construction of the same; and consequently where the causes are known, there is place for demonstration, but not where the causes are to seek for. Geometry therefore is demonstrable, for the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves; and civil philosophy is demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves. But because of natural bodies we know not the construction, but seek it from the effects, there lies no demonstration of what the causes be we seek for, but only of what they may be."

I can follow the surface claim well enough: Hobbes wants to ground the certainty of a science in whether we ourselves are the makers of its objects, and that's why geometry and "civil philosophy" (his term for the science of the commonwealth) both count as demonstrable while natural philosophy does not. What I'd like help with is the actual structure of the inference rather than the slogan: why, precisely, is authorship of a construction supposed to convert into knowledge of causes in a way that mere observation of effects never can? And why does the criterion sort disciplines the way it does here — it seems to say nothing about arithmetic or logic, and it lumps something as abstract as a geometrical figure together with something as historically contingent as an actual, existing commonwealth.

I'd also welcome help thinking through two things that look like tensions to me, though I'm not sure whether they're real problems in Hobbes's view or just my own confusion. First, later in the body of *De Corpore* Hobbes wants to ground geometry itself in a broadly kinematic account of figure-generation, treating a line or a circle as the path traced by a moving point or body — which seems to pull geometrical objects back toward the domain of body and motion that natural philosophy studies, rather than leaving them as self-contained artifacts of pure stipulation. Doesn't that sit uneasily with treating geometry's certainty as resting on construction alone, independent of any facts about bodies in motion? Second, this passage comes from the very same tract in which Hobbes was defending, against Wallis's already quite thorough refutations, his claim to have squared the circle. If geometrical certainty is supposed to rest on the transparency of our own constructions to ourselves, is there something philosophically diagnosable in the fact that this transparency failed Hobbes so conspicuously in exactly the domain where, on his own account, it should have been most secure — or is that just an unrelated biographical irony that shouldn't be read back into the epistemology?
