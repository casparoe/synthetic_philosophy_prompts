I am designing a unit for my early modern political philosophy seminar that pairs Hobbes's *Leviathan* with the political fragments scattered through Pascal's *Pensées*, and the more I reread the latter this week, the more redundant one of my planned sessions starts to seem. In the fragment traditionally headed "Justice, force," Pascal writes that justice without force is powerless while force without justice is tyrannical, and concludes that, unable to make what is just strong, we have instead made what is strong count as just ("n'ayant pu faire que ce qui est juste fût fort, on a fait que ce qui est fort fût juste"). Elsewhere among the same notes he adds that established laws are necessarily regarded as just without examination simply because they are established, and that the whole art of overturning a state consists in tracing its customs back to their origin so as to expose their want of justice. Put together, these fragments seem to reach exactly the conclusion Hobbes reaches in *Leviathan*: legitimacy just is whatever force has succeeded in stabilizing, and a sovereign's edicts count as just in virtue of nothing more than the sovereign's capacity to make them stick.

Given how closely the two positions line up, my inclination is to present Pascal's fragment to students as a more epigrammatic restatement of Hobbesian sovereignty theory. My plan is to set aside the theological scaffolding that surrounds it elsewhere in the *Pensées* — the material on concupiscence, the three orders, the wager — as biographical background that we can safely bracket once we have isolated Pascal's properly political argument from his religious apologetics. A colleague of mine who works on Jansenism insists that this does real violence to the text, and that the fragment cannot be lifted out of the larger apologetic project without changing what it actually claims. I confess, though, that I have trouble seeing what, philosophically, would be lost by treating it as free-standing. Could you help me anticipate the strongest version of her objection, so that I can either work it into the lecture or reassure myself that the comparison with Hobbes holds up without it?
