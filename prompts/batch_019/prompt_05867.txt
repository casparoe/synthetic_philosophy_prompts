I'm a software engineer, and a while back I went down a rabbit hole reading about reversible computing and Landauer's principle -- the result that erasing a single bit of information necessarily dissipates energy as heat -- which is what first got me curious about entropy and the direction of time more generally. Since then I've picked up enough popular physics to know the standard statistical-mechanical story: entropy tends to increase because high-entropy macrostates correspond to overwhelmingly more microstates than low-entropy ones, so a system's trajectory through phase space will, with near certainty, wander toward higher entropy. What I only recently learned is that this story runs into two classic problems -- Loschmidt's reversibility objection and Zermelo's recurrence objection -- and that the standard way of patching things up is to simply stipulate that the universe began in an extremely low-entropy state (what I gather philosophers of physics call the "Past Hypothesis"), something that apparently cannot be derived from the dynamical laws themselves and has to be added separately, by hand.

What I can't get an intuitive grip on is why this is treated as a genuinely hard problem rather than a shrug-worthy brute fact. If the microscopic laws are time-symmetric, why isn't "entropy just happened to be low a long time ago" a perfectly adequate answer, on par with any other contingent fact about initial conditions that physics doesn't further explain? I'd like to understand exactly what work Loschmidt's and Zermelo's objections are doing -- what they rule out that a naive statistical story would otherwise get away with -- and why so many physicists and philosophers find the Past Hypothesis move unsatisfying rather than simply true. Since I gather the two issues are connected, I'd also like to understand, at the same non-technical level, why leaning on this sort of statistical/typicality reasoning is exactly what generates the notorious "Boltzmann brain" problem: I've seen it invoked as an objection to certain cosmological models, but I don't have a real feel for why it would ever come out as more "typical," probabilistically speaking, for a single deluded brain to fluctuate into existence with false memories than for the ordinary low-entropy history we take ourselves to remember to have actually happened. Could you walk through how these three pieces fit together -- the reversibility and recurrence objections, the Past Hypothesis as a response to them, and the Boltzmann brain worry as a further consequence -- in plain language, without leaning on the statistical-mechanics formalism itself?
