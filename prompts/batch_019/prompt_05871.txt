I'm a philosophy professor working mainly on moral psychology -- weakness of will, self-control, akrasia, that whole literature -- and a couple of years ago a former student of mine, who now directs a small residential addiction treatment center, decided I should "put my money where my mouth is" and added me to the place's clinical ethics committee. It's been a genuinely useful corrective to how neatly these things get discussed in seminar.

Last year the committee approved a "self-binding directive" program, which is basically a Ulysses contract: patients who've just finished detox and are stable can sign a form stating that if they come back in active withdrawal within some window and demand to leave against medical advice, staff should hold them for up to 72 hours regardless of that in-the-moment demand. I'm actually the one who talked the clinical director into explaining the hyperbolic-discounting logic to patients in plain language in the handout -- why you might reasonably want to bind a future self you don't yet trust -- and I am now mildly regreting how well that pitch apparently worked.

Here's tonight's case. A patient signed one of these directives three weeks ago, relapsed as predicted, came back in voluntarily, and is now about fourteen hours into withdrawal. She's lucid, tracking the conversation fine, and is furious, demanding discharge right now, correctly pointing out that she meets none of the state's criteria for an involuntary hold. Half the committee wants to honor the directive as exactly the considered precommitment the whole program exists to protect; the other half thinks a person answering questions competently in the present just is the authority on what she wants, and that "the earlier self gets a veto" is paternalism wearing a philosophy costume. I have to get the committee's actual decision memo out before shift change at 8 -- it needs a clear recommendation, with a time frame, for what happens to her tonight, plus a line on what our standing policy on these directives should be going forward. Could you draft that memo -- an actual recommendation, not just a survey of the debate -- and give whichever side you don't land on a fair, serious run at their strongest argument?
