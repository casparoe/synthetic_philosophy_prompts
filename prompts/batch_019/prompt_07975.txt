Aumann's epistemic defense of backward induction treats common knowledge of rationality as a fact about entire strategies — about what each player would do at every node of the game tree, including nodes that mutual rationality guarantees will never be reached — so the conclusion survives the observation that fully rational players would never let a six-move centipede game get past its first move. Pettit and Sugden's "backward induction paradox," later pressed by Binmore, denies that this saves the argument: the second node can only be reached by a departure from the very rationality that common knowledge is supposed to underwrite there, so "common knowledge of rationality" by itself tells us nothing about what player 2 is entitled to believe, or do, upon actually watching player 1 pass. Whatever answer one gives — that it was a tremble, that it licenses further passing, or anything else — imports an assumption about belief revision that the theorem itself does not supply.

Write a dialogue between two game theorists holding these opposing positions, set at a whiteboard where a six-move centipede tree has just been sketched between them. Have the Pettit-Sugden-Binmore side press the Aumann side on the concrete question of what player 2 is entitled to believe after player 1 passes at move one, and have the Aumann side answer that challenge directly rather than retreating to a restatement of the theorem. Neither character should be allowed to talk past the other, and neither should concede more than their position actually requires.
