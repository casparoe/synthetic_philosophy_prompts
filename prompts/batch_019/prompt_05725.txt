And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The grandfather paradox imagines a time traveler, Tim, who goes back before his grandfather ever met his grandmother, planning to shoot him. If Tim succeeds, his father is never born, so Tim is never born, so he could never have traveled back to do the shooting in the first place - a straightforward contradiction. Lewis's response is that the sentence 'Tim can kill Grandfather' is ambiguous, not contradictory. Relative to one set of facts - Tim's marksmanship, his loaded rifle, his clear shot, nobody guarding Grandfather - Tim can kill him, in exactly the sense that any of us 'can' do something we have the ordinary ability and opportunity to do. Relative to a wider set of facts, though, including the fact that Grandfather did survive to father Tim's father, it's not compossible for Tim to succeed, so in that sense he can't. Whenever Tim tries, something perfectly mundane goes wrong: the gun jams, a bird startles him, he loses his nerve. Because reality has to remain self-consistent, Lewis holds that a law of temporal consistency is ultimately what guarantees that every attempt like Tim's will fail - essentially the same idea physicists later formalized as the Novikov self-consistency principle. And since the past can never be changed, it follows that time travelers can never affect it either: Tim is really just a bystander inserted into a story whose every detail was already fixed before he arrived. I find the solution convincing, since it explains why nothing paradoxical ever actually happens without appealing to anything supernatural - though it does make me wonder why Tim would keep trying if a law of nature is just going to cancel him out every time."
