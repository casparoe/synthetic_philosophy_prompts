I'm finishing a feature on selfish genetic elements for a general-interest science section, and I'd like a check on its central move before it goes to my editor. Biologists usually treat the organism as the natural unit on which selection acts, treating "gene selection" and "group selection" as just alternative bookkeeping for the same underlying process. My piece argues that a certain class of genetic outlaws complicates that picture. In house mice, a variant region of chromosome 17 called the t-haplotype sabotages sperm carrying the rival chromosome in heterozygous males, so that it is passed on to far more than the fair fifty percent ordinary meiosis would predict; the only thing holding its frequency in check is that it causes male sterility once it becomes too common. Fruit flies have an analogous system, Segregation Distorter, which disables sperm carrying the competing allele in much the same way. Biologists sometimes describe ordinary meiosis as creating a kind of "parliament of the genes": because every gene gets an equal, fair shot at the next generation, each one's interests end up aligned with the organism's as a whole, and elements like these are the rebels that found a way to cheat that arrangement. My draft then concludes that this shows gene-level selection is the more fundamental process, with organism-level selection merely a description of the stable equilibrium that results when genes can't get away with cheating, and group-level selection an even more fragile, derivative case built on top of that. Does that inference actually follow from the biology, or am I overreaching against biologists who would insist that organism- and group-level selection do real explanatory work of their own? I would also welcome a flag on anything above that is simply wrong.
