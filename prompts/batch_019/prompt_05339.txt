Okay, this is a weird case of my actual life colliding with my coursework, but here goes. I'm a 4th-year PhD student and I somehow ended up as my department's rep on our Graduate Student Association's health insurance advisory committee, which reviews proposed changes to our student health plan (the SHIP) every spring before they go to the university benefits office for sign-off. Completely seperately, I'm taking a bioethics seminar this term and we just spent two weeks on the treatment/enhancement distinction — Norman Daniels' species-typical-functioning stuff, the objections to it, Sandel's giftedness essay, all of that. I mentioned the committee case to my seminar prof half as a joke and she said it's basically a perfect illustration of the whole unit, which has not helped me feel less stressed about it.

Here's the actual situation: our plan currently covers fertility preservation (egg/sperm/embryo freezing) only when it's "medically indicated" — you're about to start chemo, or you've been diagnosed with something like primary ovarian insufficiency that's going to tank your fertility fast. A group of grad students has petitioned the committee to extend coverage to elective egg freezing done for purely "social"/age-related reasons, no diagnosis required, on the grounds that the PhD-then-postdoc-then-tenure-track pipeline pushes people into their late 30s before they have any job security, and by the time age-related decline is bad enough to actually get diagnosed as a problem it's often too late for freezing to work well anyway. Other members are pushing back hard: covering this raises premiums for everyone including students who'll never use it; age-related fertility decline is just normal species-typical aging, not a disease, so it's not obviously what a health plan is even for; and a couple people keep bringing up how Apple and Facebook got heavily criticized back in 2014 for offering this exact benefit, on the theory that it quietly pressures women to delay kids to fit a company's (or in our case, academia's) timeline instead of actually fixing that timeline.

Could you draft the short recommendation memo I'd actually bring to our next meeting? I need it to commit to one concrete option — keep the current exclusion, extend full coverage, or approve it with specific conditions/limits — instead of just listing considerations on both sides, and whatever it recommends, it needs to seriously grapple with the strongest version of the argument on the other side, not just wave at it. Something like a page, written the way an actual memo to the committee would read.
