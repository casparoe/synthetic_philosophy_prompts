I'm putting together the session on diachronic norms of belief for my graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I'd like to push the discussion past the point where most textbook treatments of van Fraassen's Reflection Principle stop. The class has already worked through the basic statement of the principle — that one's current credence in a proposition, conditional on being certain that one's credence in that same proposition at some later time will take a particular value, should itself equal that value — along with the standard diachronic Dutch Book argument van Fraassen offers on its behalf, modeled on the earlier arguments for probabilism and for conditionalization. What I want to dig into next is the well-known complication that this formulation, taken at face value, seems to commit an agent to deferring to a future credence even when she has every reason to expect that credence to be the product of something obviously non-rational: a manipulative neuroscientist, a memory-erasing drug, plain old senility. Van Fraassen was alert to this and, if I remember correctly, tried to head it off by restricting Reflection to cases in which the agent has no reason to expect a "violation of rationality" to intervene between the two times — a restriction he later revisited, I believe, through something like a Ulysses-and-the-Sirens framing, though I wouldn't swear to the exact source.

Could you walk me through why that restriction is more philosophically uncomfortable than the usual one-line gloss ("he just excludes those cases") lets on? What I keep getting stuck on is this: doesn't applying the restriction require an independent, non-Reflection-based account of what counts as a legitimate versus illegitimate change in credence — in which case the Dutch Book argument is no longer doing the foundational work it was introduced to do, since we would already need to know which future selves deserve deference before the principle could get any grip at all? I'd be interested in your own verdict on whether this is fatal to Reflection as a genuine diachronic coherence constraint on a par with the case for conditionalization, or whether there is a non-circular way of cashing out the restriction. And if you think it sheds light on the matter, I would welcome a few words on how this bears on the later literature connecting Reflection to prohibitions on epistemic akrasia — though that is very much a secondary interest of mine here.
