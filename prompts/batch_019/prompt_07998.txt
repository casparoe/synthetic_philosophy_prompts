Exam question: "Reconstruct the core logical structure of Gödel's ontological argument, in the version deriving from Dana Scott's notes. Your answer should explain: (a) what role the definitions of 'God-like' and 'essence' play in the argument; (b) how the axioms governing positive properties combine with these definitions to yield the conclusion that a God-like being exists of necessity; and (c) what the 'modal collapse' result shows, and why it is usually treated as a problem for Gödel's argument specifically rather than for modal logic as such. Close with a brief assessment of whether any proposed emendation to the argument succeeds in blocking the collapse while still delivering the argument's intended conclusion."

Student's answer: "Gödel's proof, at least in the version cleaned up by Scott, starts from a single primitive, the notion of a 'positive' property P, which Gödel glosses as positive in a moral-aesthetic sense, independent of the contingent way the world happens to be arranged. Two structural axioms govern P: exactly one of any property φ or its negation is positive, so that positivity behaves rather like an ultrafilter, and any property necessarily entailed by a positive property is itself positive. From these two axioms alone we can already show that a positive property cannot be self-undermining: if a property could never possibly be exemplified, it would (vacuously) necessarily entail its own negation, which by the entailment axiom would make the negation positive too, contradicting the dichotomy axiom — so every positive property is at least possibly exemplified. A further axiom stipulates that being God-like — having every positive property there is — is itself a positive property, so it follows that a God-like being is possible.

The definition of 'essence' is what lets the argument move from mere possibility to necessity: a property is an essence of an object just in case the object has it and it necessarily entails every other property the object has. Using the dichotomy axiom, Gödel shows that God-likeness is its own essence: anything that is God-like has, of necessity, all and only the properties that follow from its being God-like. The last axiom does two jobs at once: it tells us that positive properties are themselves necessarily positive, so that positivity is not a contingent matter, and it tells us that necessary existence is itself among the positive properties. Once God-likeness has been established as an essence, and necessary existence as positive, it follows that a God-like thing's existence, wherever it occurs at all, occurs of necessity; combined with the fact that a God-like being is possible, the distinctively S5 principle that whatever is possibly necessary is simply necessary lets Gödel conclude that a God-like being necessarily exists, and therefore exists.

The trouble, as Sobel showed in 1987, is that exactly this machinery is too powerful: essentially identical reasoning can be run for any true proposition whatsoever, by folding that proposition into an ad hoc essence, with the result that every truth turns out to be a necessary truth. That is a far stronger and less palatable conclusion than merely that God exists, and commentators generally locate the blame in the argument's own axioms about essence and necessary existence rather than in the S5 logic used for the final step, which is a widely used system that produces no such difficulty on its own. Robert Adams's response to this was to revise the definitions of 'essence' and 'God-like' so that merely having a property of necessity no longer automatically makes that property part of one's essence; on this repair, the intended theorem that a God-like being necessarily exists still goes through, but the derivation of collapse is blocked, because the trick of folding an arbitrary true proposition into an essence no longer works. If that repair succeeds, Gödel's argument survives Sobel's objection, though the older worries about whether 'positive' is a well-defined, non-question-begging notion in the first place remain exactly where they were."
