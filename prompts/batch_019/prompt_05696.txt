Here's the exchange, compressed to the six moves that actually mattered:

(1) Dan said the 5-and-10 problem isn't a cute edge case, it's a symptom of something structural: once an agent has to treat its own action as a fact about the world rather than something a Cartesian intervention can just set, a naive proof-search agent can derive a spuriously "true" conditional about the action it doesn't take (since the antecedent turns out false, the material conditional comes for free), and that vacuous proof can crown the worse option before the good proof is even found.

(2) Sofia said that's exactly why the chicken rule exists: make the agent take the disprivileged action if it ever proves in advance that it won't, and the pathological proof can't go through. So 5-and-10 is a bug in one family of decision procedures, not a fact about embedded reasoners generally.

(3) Dan replied that the chicken rule doesn't dissolve the tension, it just relocates it: it only works by keeping the agent's proof system too weak to settle its own action ahead of time, and Löb's theorem is exactly why you can't have a strong self-referential system that trusts its own (or a similarly built successor's) proofs without that kind of self-imposed weakness. So the Löbian obstacle to self-trust will dog any embedded agent whose self-model is powerful enough to be interesting.

(4) Sofia granted the theorem but said it only bites idealized provability-based agents, and no real embedded agent -- human, RL policy, or language model -- reasons by proving things about its own source code in a fixed formal system. Real agents carry graded, calibrated uncertainty about their own future outputs, so Löb's theorem is a fact about an architecture nobody actually builds, not a problem for embedded agency in general.

(5) Dan answered that probabilistic agents hit the same wall in a different spot: to act well around other agents (including future versions of itself) as complicated as itself, an embedded agent needs a prior with a "grain of truth" on the policy actually being run, and there's no single reasonable computable prior that puts positive weight on every computable policy, including its own. Self-reference just re-enters through the prior instead of the proof system.

(6) Sofia said that only holds if you insist on computable priors -- reflective oracles give the grain-of-truth problem a clean fix -- so at most this shows idealized self-reference needs a slightly exotic mathematical setting, not that theres a real philosophical wall for embedded agents.
