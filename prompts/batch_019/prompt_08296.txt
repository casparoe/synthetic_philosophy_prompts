Sometimes I find myself more confused by a paper's own second thoughts than by its main argument, and that's exactly where I've gotten stuck this week. My seminar on the metaphysics of probability paired Lewis's "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" with his later "Humean Supervenience Debugged," and while the original statement of the Principal Principle made sense to me, I'm having real trouble with the section where Lewis turns on his own formulation and diagnoses what he calls the "big bad bug." As I understand it, the trouble starts from the fact that, because Lewis is a Humean about chance, the chances holding at a time have to be fixed by the pattern of particular fact across all of history, past and future alike — which seems to leave room for a wildly atypical future course of events, compatible with but not required by the chances now in force, to end up making it false that those were ever the chances to begin with.

What I can't reconstruct on my own is exactly why this "undermining" possibility generates a real problem for the Principal Principle, as opposed to just being a strange but harmless fact about chance under Humean Supervenience. My study group spent a good hour arguing about whether the issue is that historical evidence turns out not to be admissible after all once undermining is possible, or whether it's something more fundamental about what a chance proposition is even allowed to mean once you've gone Humean. A friend who's further along in the reading mentioned that a rigidified "New Principle," associated with Hall and Thau, is supposed to dissolve the bug rather than just patch around it, but I don't yet see why merely rigidifying what the chance proposition refers to should matter to the argument at all.

Could you walk me through this in fairly intuitive terms, leaning as little as possible on formal notation: (a) how the undermining-futures argument is actually supposed to run against the original Principal Principle, (b) why Lewis regarded this as serious enough to call a bug rather than a minor wrinkle, and (c) how the rigidified fix is meant to block the argument? I'd also appreciate it if you could flag whether that fix is generally treated as fully successful in the literature, or whether something of the original worry is thought to linger even after the reformulation.
