Ok so this happened on a committee I'm stuck on and I cant stop thinking about it. I'm the elected grad rep on my university's Graduate Appeals Panel (five of us total, mix of faculty and grad students), and last week we heard the case of a PhD candidate who got dismissed from her program after a bad annual review. Per the bylaws, we're only supposed to overturn a dismissal if the panel finds BOTH (a) that the department violated its own review procedure in some specific way, and (b) that the violation was material, i.e. it could plausibly have changed the outcome. Seems like a totally sensible rule. Except here's what happened when we actually voted: three of the five of us (me included) thought (a) was true, the department skipped a required feedback step, and a different three thought (b) was true. Only one person, our chair, believes both. So if you poll each panelist individually on "do YOU personally vote to overturn," it comes out 4 to 1 against her. But if you instead tally each question separately and just mechanically apply the bylaw to whatever the majority said on each question, both (a) and (b) come out "yes" - which means overturn. Same five people, same underlying votes, opposite results depending on how you count them.

I got stuck drafting the panel's memo on how to resolve this particular appeal, and I also want to propose a default counting procedure for next time, since apparently some version of this exact split has happened before and everyone just improvised. Small wrinkle: our chair has obviously clocked which method gets her preferred outcome and keeps steering discussion toward it, which is exactly why I'd like something with real principled backing rather than a coin flip dressed up as reasoning. I'm in the philosophy program myself, though my own work is in aesthetics and nowhere near this, and a friend in my cohort swears this is a well-known paradox from voting theory or philosophy of law, but neither of us could reconstruct it past a vague memory over drinks. Could you draft that memo (due before we reconvene next Tuesday)? It needs to actually commit to a counting procedure instead of punting with "reasonable people disagree," say plainly what that means for this student, and be honest about the strongest case for the method it doesnt end up recommending, not a strawman of it.
