Ok, this has been living in my head for months, probably because I write novels for a living so it feels personal. Rousseaus whole case in the Letter to d'Alembert is that theatre's a moral con: it lets people feel pity for characters who don't exist so they never have to feel it, or act on it, for people who do, and -- contra d'Alembert's claim that theatre could teach Geneva virtue -- it actually corrodes the plain civic virtue the republic depends on. Then a few years later he turns around and writes Julie, a novel engineered from page one to make readers weep over people who also don't exist (apparently demand was so high some lending libraries rented it out by the hour). He tries to draw a line between the two cases somewhere in the preface material tacked onto the novel, but I've never bought it. Can you make the strongest possible case, using his own premises against him, that his reasons for exempting the sentimental novel -- Julie especially -- from his own theatre critique don't actually hold up? I don't want "he was a hypocrite," I want the precise seam where the argument breaks.
