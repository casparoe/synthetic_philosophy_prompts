I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and for a graduate seminar on self-knowledge I've drafted the note below on Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument from *Knowledge and Its Limits*. I'd like your comments before I bring it to my advisor next week.

Williamson's target is the luminosity thesis: that certain conditions are such that, whenever they obtain, one is thereby in a position to know that they obtain. He aims to show that no non-trivial condition is luminous in this sense, and, crucially, that this includes the condition of knowing itself, so that the KK principle (Kp → KKp) fails quite generally, not just in exotic cases.

The argument works by margin for error. Consider a morning during which the room warms so gradually that no two consecutive moments feel different to you, though the first moment is clearly cold and the last is clearly not. For your judgment "I feel cold" to count as knowledge at a given moment, it must be safe: not merely true, but such that the same judgment, formed the same way, could not easily have been false at the nearest moment indiscriminable from that one. Since consecutive moments are by construction indiscriminable, this means that if you know you feel cold at some moment, you must still feel at least somewhat cold at the next, on pain of the method being unsafe there. Now suppose feeling cold is luminous, so that feeling cold anywhere in the series suffices for knowing it there. Feeling cold at the first moment then gives you knowledge at the first moment; by margin for error that carries feeling-cold to the second moment; luminosity turns that into knowledge at the second moment; and so on, indiscriminable step by indiscriminable step, until you would have to feel at least somewhat cold at the last moment too, where you are by hypothesis warm. Contradiction: feeling cold is not luminous. Run the same reasoning on the condition of knowing rather than the condition of feeling, and knowing turns out not to be luminous either, which is just to say that the KK principle fails.

The natural place to resist this is phenomenal self-ascription. Williamson considers pain directly, since pain looks like a state with no gap between having it and believing one has it, and so no independent target for a margin of error to miss. His reply is that classifying a pain, as mild or severe, as this kind or that, still takes the same fallible discriminative judgment, so the sorites reasserts itself one level down. That reply seems to me to leave a narrower claim untouched: not "I am in this pain," which is comparative and graded and so still exposed, but the bare "I am in pain, of whatever sort," which involves no discrimination among degrees at all. If there is no relevant series of indiscriminable cases running through the bare ascription, because pain and its absence are not adjacent in the way degrees of pain are, perhaps it survives as an isolated bit of luminosity.

Even granting that, it does nothing for the KK principle itself, since knowing that p is not a phenomenal state in the relevant sense. I am left with a split verdict: bare phenomenal self-ascription possibly luminous, all iterated knowledge definitely not, and I cannot tell whether the split is principled or just where I happened to stop pushing.

I would like comments on three things especially: whether my reconstruction of the margin-for-error argument is faithful to Williamson, particularly the move from indiscriminability to the safety requirement, rather than a caricature of it; whether the bare-ascription/degree-ascription distinction is doing real philosophical work against his reply on pain, or merely relocates the same problem to a different predicate; and whether the resulting split verdict is a defensible resting point or an ad hoc stopping rule. I won't have library access again until next week, so please engage the reasoning directly rather than pointing me toward further reading.
