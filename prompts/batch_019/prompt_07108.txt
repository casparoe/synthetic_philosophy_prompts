Six months ago, age-related macular degeneration cost me most of my central vision, and I was subsequently diagnosed with Charles Bonnet syndrome. I now regularly hallucinate things that are not there: intricate patterns on bare walls, small figures crossing the room, once even a lattice of windows spreading across the ceiling; in the moment, these episodes look every bit as vivid and solid as anything I ever saw with healthy eyes. Reading about the condition led me to disjunctivism about perception: the view, opposed to what M.G.F. Martin calls the "Common Kind Assumption," that a veridical experience and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination need not share any common underlying mental state. My neurologist, however, describes these hallucinations as arising from spontaneous activity in the same visual-cortex pathways that process ordinary sight, which sounds like exactly the sort of causally matching hallucination that critics invoke against disjunctivism, since if the same proximate brain state does the causal work whether or not a real object is present, the external object seems to become explanatorily redundant even in cases of genuine, veridical seeing. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that a disjunctivist can grant everything my neurologist says and still deny that my hallucinations and my ordinary, healthy perceptions belong to a common fundamental kind of mental state, meeting this causal/explanatory challenge head-on rather than simply biting the bullet on it.
