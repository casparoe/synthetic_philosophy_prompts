So this happened in my metaphysics seminar's study group and I can't stop thinking about who actually won. We just finished a unit on material constitution, and for the last session we read that old Gibbard paper with the Lumpl and Goliath example -- the version where the sculptor presses the clay into statue-shape at the exact instant the lump itself comes into being, so you can't run the easy "the clay existed before the statue did" argument. Afterward a few of us stuck around to keep talking about it, and two people, Devon and Ana, ended up going back and forth for like twenty minutes while the rest of us just watched. I wrote down roughly how it went because neither of them would budge, and I want to know who actually had the better of it, move by move, not just who seemed to win the room (we've also got a paper due on this exact puzzle in two weeks, so I'd like to actually understand the moves and not just who was more stubborn).

Devon opened by saying Lumpl just is Goliath, full stop: same matter, same location, same causal history, so putting two objects on the pedestal is spooky double-counting. Ana said that can't be right, because Lumpl can survive being squashed into a ball and Goliath can't, and by Leibniz's Law anything true of one but not the other means they arent identical. Devon shot back that this is question-begging -- Ana's already assuming there's a "Goliath" that gets destroyed by squashing, but if there's really just one object, squashing it doesn't destroy anything, it just reshapes it, and calling that "destroying the statue" smuggles in the very distinctness that's supposed to be the conclusion. Ana answered that it's not an assumption, it's a conceptual truth that nothing survives being squashed flat and still counts as a statue, so the inference is valid, not circular. Devon conceded that much but pivoted to what our professor calls the grounding problem: fine, say they're distinct -- for the whole time Lumpl and Goliath coexist they're made of the exact same particles, arranged the exact same way, doing the exact same causal work, so what in the world makes it true that only one of those two "things" has the property can-survive-squashing? Positing a second object doesn't explain the asymmetry, it just names it. Ana said sortals like "statue" and "lump" just do carry different persistence conditions built in, same as plenty of ordinary count nouns, and that's not some extra spooky posit, it's just what it is to fall under one kind instead of another. Devon wasnt having it: by that logic his coffee mug is also "a pen holder," "a doorstop," and "a 200-gram mass," and nobody thinks he's got four overlapping objects on his desk each with its own modal profile just because those descriptions can come apart -- so "they're different sortals" can't be the whole answer, since it would generate coincident objects everywhere, not just for statues and clay.
