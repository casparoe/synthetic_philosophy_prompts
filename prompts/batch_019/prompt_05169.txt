I've been reading about the Gould–Lewontin critique of adaptationism, mostly because I kept running into echoes of it in discussions of "emergent abilities" in large language models, and I ended up writing some rough notes connecting the two. I don't have formal training in either philosophy or biology, so before I develop this further I'd like a sanity check on: (1) whether I've represented the original debate fairly to both sides, (2) whether the analogy to language models is doing real conceptual work or is just a superficial pun, and (3) anything that's unclear as writing. Here are the notes.

Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper, "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm," uses the mosaic-covered spandrels of the Basilica of San Marco as an image for a specific kind of evolutionary error. On their telling, a spandrel isn't designed to hold the mosaic it displays; it's the unavoidable geometric leftover of setting a dome on rounded arches, pressed into decorative service only after the fact. Their target was what they called the "Panglossian paradigm": the assumption that every trait of an organism is itself an adaptation, fine-tuned by selection for its current job, plus the habit of inventing a plausible story whenever one is needed to support that assumption. Some traits, they argued, are better understood as leftovers of other adaptations, or of developmental and physical constraints, that only later get pressed into a new function. Gould and Elisabeth Vrba later coined "exaptation" for that kind of secondary co-optation, so as not to keep overloading "adaptation" with both a historical sense and a current-use sense.

The obvious rejoinder, which I understand Dennett and others have pressed pretty hard over the years, is that this attacks a strawman: no careful adaptationist thinks every trait is optimized free of constraint, and "adaptationism" is better read as a research heuristic generating testable hypotheses than as a metaphysical doctrine about design. There's also, I gather (though I haven't chased down the original source, so this detail might be garbled), an architectural rejoinder: that the San Marco spaces are, strictly, pendentives rather than spandrels, and that spandrels elsewhere in architecture aren't always the forced consequence Gould and Lewontin needed them to be. If that's right, their own illustration is shakier than the paper lets on.

Here's the part I actually want comments on. The recent argument over "emergent abilities" in large language models feels like the same fight with the labels swapped. Wei et al.'s original claim was that certain capabilities — multi-step arithmetic, some forms of reasoning — appear only past a scale threshold, not something anyone trained the model to do directly, but a byproduct of optimizing next-token prediction at scale, later exapted by users through prompting into a general-purpose reasoning tool. Schaeffer et al.'s "mirage" paper plays Dennett's role: they argue the apparent discontinuity is largely an artifact of scoring these capabilities with discontinuous metrics like exact-match accuracy, and that the underlying improvement is smooth and predictable — no real spandrel, just a measurement choice that makes ordinary adaptation look like a leftover.

Where I think the analogy strains, though: natural selection is a population-level process across generations, built on heredity and differential reproduction, and I don't see an obvious equivalent of any of that in a single gradient-descent training run. So maybe "spandrel" and "exaptation" are just words I'm borrowing because they sound apt, not because the underlying causal structure actually lines up. I'd like to know whether that gap is fatal to the analogy, and whether there's a better concept from this literature — constraint, pleiotropy, "frozen accident," something else — that would map onto the language-model case more honestly than spandrel and exaptation do.
