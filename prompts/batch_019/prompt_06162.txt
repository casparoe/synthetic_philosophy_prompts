In a philosophy of mind reading group I attend, we spent a session on Clark and Chalmers's "extended mind" thesis, and the discussion of their Otto/Inga example turned into a real argument between two other participants that the rest of us mostly just watched unfold. In that example, Inga wants to see an exhibition and recalls, from ordinary biological memory, that the museum is on 53rd Street; Otto, who has Alzheimer's, consults a notebook he carries everywhere and finds the same address written there. Their claim is that Otto's notebook is not merely a tool he uses but is partly constitutive of his belief about where the museum is, on a par with Inga's memory.

(1) Ines defended this using the "parity principle": if a process would count as cognitive when it runs entirely inside the head, it shouldn't lose that status merely because it runs partly outside it, so treating Inga's neurons as belief-forming while denying the same status to Otto's notebook is just bio-chauvinism dressed up as metaphysics. (2) Grant answered that this move commits what Adams and Aizawa call the coupling-constitution fallacy: from the fact that something is reliably, even indispensably, causally coupled to a cognitive process, it doesn't follow that it is part of that process. His glasses, he pointed out, are coupled tightly to his vision, but no one thinks the lenses are part of his visual cortex, and a car's fuel is essential to its motion without being part of the engine. (3) Ines replied that this objection attacks a strawman, since the original argument never claims mere reliable coupling is sufficient for extension; it adds further conditions, namely that the resource be constantly available, automatically trusted rather than critically checked, and consciously endorsed by the agent at the time it was stored, and neither glasses nor gasoline satisfy those conditions, so the fallacy charge misses its actual target. (4) Grant conceded that this rules out the easy counterexamples, but argued the deeper problem survives one level down: even a notebook meeting every one of those further conditions is at most functionally similar to memory as a storage device, and that functional similarity cannot give its entries the intrinsic, "non-derived" content that a genuine belief requires, since the marks on the page mean what they do only because of prior linguistic convention and Otto's own past acts of interpretation. On his view, no amount of trusted, endorsed, constant coupling turns derived content into original content, so the notebook still is not part of Otto's mind, however indispensable it is to him.

I would like a verdict on this move by move rather than just an overall winner. Is Ines right that Grant's initial coupling-constitution objection fails to engage with the conditions Clark and Chalmers actually place on extension, or does some version of it survive against her more careful statement of the parity principle? Is Grant right that the non-derived-content point in his last move is a genuine, independent difficulty for the extended mind thesis, or is it really just importing a contestable theory of intentionality that begs the question from the outset? And is either of them overlooking a consideration that would settle the disagreement more cleanly than anything either actually said?
