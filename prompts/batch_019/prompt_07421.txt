A friend and I spent last night arguing about Karl Friston's free energy principle, circling around a historical detail neither of us could shake: the "free energy" the theory minimizes descends directly from the free energy Hermann von Helmholtz defined in thermodynamics, and Helmholtz was also the physiologist who first argued, on epistemological grounds, that perception is a species of unconscious inference -- the very idea predictive processing claims to formalize and generalize. My friend thought Helmholtz, as a physicist wedded to precisely defined quantities, would have resisted seeing his thermodynamic concept redeployed as a loose analogy for brains, action, and life itself; I thought his commitment to perception-as-inference, and to grounding psychology in physiology wherever possible, would make him receptive to the core idea even if he balked at extensions like active inference or free-energy minimization as the mark of life as such. We never settled it. Drawing on Helmholtz's actual empiricism about perception, his dispute with Hering's nativism, and his views on how physics, physiology, and psychology ought to relate, how do you think he would have reacted to the free energy principle -- and which parts would he have claimed as vindicating his own ideas, versus dismissed as overreach?
