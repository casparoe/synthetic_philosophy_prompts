So work just started this thing where part of our bonus pool gets donated to charity, and a few of us on the "giving committee" actually get to hash out where it goes (i got roped into this because i mentioned once that i'd read a GiveWell post, lol). At the last meeting two guys on the committee, Marcus and Dana, got into a whole back-and-forth that's been stuck in my head since.

1. Marcus said the entire cost-effectiveness spreadsheet is basically theater, because nobody actually knows what a donation does 50 or 100 years out -- saving a kid from malaria could just as easily produce a future dictator as a future Jonas Salk, so the "expected value" numbers are fake precision sitting on top of total ignorance.
2. Dana said ok, fine, but you can't run an actual decision that way, so the fix is to stick to interventions where the near-term benefit is clear and robust (bednets, deworming, whatever) and just treat the murky long-run stuff as a wash between the options.
3. Marcus said that doesn't actually fix anything, because the near-term interventions differ in how many extra people end up existing, and population size is exactly the kind of thing that flips the sign of the far future -- so "robust near-term evidence" doesn't get you out of the problem, it just hides it one level down.
4. Dana said if you take that seriously you can't act at all, since literally every option, including doing nothing, changes who eventually gets born, and a theory that recommends paralysis has gone wrong somewhere.

Neither of them budged and then the meeting just had to move on. I dont know enough philosophy to tell if one of them is actually winning this or if they're both quietly missing something more basic. Can you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step -- not just who comes out ahead overall?
