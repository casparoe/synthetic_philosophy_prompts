So my sister is getting married in like three weeks and asked me to give the maid of honor speech, which is terrifying enough on its own, but the timing is extra annoying because Im in an epistemology seminar this semester that just spent two whole weeks on contextualism vs. pragmatic encroachment, and now I literally cannot turn it off every time someone at a wedding thing says "how did you just know he was the one." We did DeRose's bank cases in class — the couple who are totally fine saying "I know the bank's open Saturday" when nothing's riding on it, but the second real money is on the line with the exact same evidence, "I know" suddenly feels too strong and they should probably go check first — and now that's basically all I hear whenever people treat "knowing" someone is right for you like it's obviously an easier claim than knowing your bank's hours.

Here's my actual problem: her fiancé is finishing a philosophy PhD, so a real chunk of the guest list is his cohort, people who will 100% notice if I'm leaning on the low-stakes/high-stakes distinction, or on the idea that "know" just means something stricter in a wedding toast than it does over coffee (the contextualist move — standards shift with the conversation), versus the idea that it's not the word that gets harder to apply but the actual knowing that gets harder, because so much more rides on being wrong (that's more the pragmatic encroachment line, closer to what Stanley argues against people like DeRose). But the other half of the room is our extended family, who will not know or care what any of that means and just want something warm and a little funny about two people they love.

Can you write me the toast — five minutes-ish, meant to be spoken out loud, not an essay? I want "how do you know" to actually be the backbone of the whole thing rather than a throwaway line: it should acknowledge that "I just knew" is the kind of claim that would sound way too easy in literally any other high-stakes situation, and it should earn the right to say it anyway by the end, instead of just asserting it. But it has to work as a genuinely moving speech about my sister first and a sly epistemology reference second — no defining contextualism or pragmatic encroachment out loud, no air quotes around "know." If the philosophy crowd gets what I'm doing, great, they'll get it; if my aunt doesn't, she should still be crying happy tears by the end regardless.
