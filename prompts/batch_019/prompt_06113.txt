Okay so this is kind of a weird one but I've been stuck on it for like a week. I got into Nick Bostrom's stuff after reading Superintelligence for a school project, and I found out he actually wrote his PhD thesis on this totally different topic called "anthropic reasoning" — like, how you're supposed to update your beliefs based on the fact that you exist at all. There's apparently two competing rules for this: the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), where you treat yourself as a random draw from everyone who actually exists in some "reference class," and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), where you ALSO have to factor in how many observers each hypothesis predicts in the first place, so theories where way more people/observers exist get boosted in probability just from you existing.

The classic objection to SIA is this "presumptuous philosopher" scenario: two cosmological theories are equally supported by all the evidence so far, except theory 2 says the universe contains like a trillion trillion times more observers than theory 1. Physicists want to run a expensive experiment to figure out which is true, but a philosopher barges in and says "don't bother, SIA already tells me theory 2 is basically certain, since I exist and I'm way more likely to exist if theory 2 is true." That's supposed to be a reductio of SIA because obviously you can't settle physics from your armchair like that. Every summary I can find just kind of ends there with "and that's why SIA is wrong," which feels like a bit of a cop-out to me.

Can you actually make the strongest possible case FOR the self-indication assumption — i.e. argue that the presumptuous philosopher is basically right to reason that way, or at least that this is a bullet worth biting? I'd rather you engage with the actual probability/decision theory here than just gesture at intuitions, and if it's relevant I'd love to hear how this connects to the doomsday argument / SSA's own weird bullets, so I can see the trade offs clearly.
