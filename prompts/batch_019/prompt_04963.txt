Rationalist blogs and forums like to invoke "Knightian uncertainty" for the sort of risk that a single sharp probability seems to misrepresent, but the formal-epistemology debate underneath that blog-post shorthand is older and considerably more precise. Proponents of imprecise probabilities—following Peter Walley's theory of upper and lower previsions, or Isaac Levi's earlier work on indeterminate credal states—argue that when evidence is sparse or ambiguous, as in Ellsberg's urn cases, representing your credence as a whole set of probability functions is more honest than forcing out a single number via something like the principle of indifference. But the move has costs: Adam Elga has argued that imprecise credences, once paired with any plausible rule for choosing among options that different distributions in the credal set rank differently, license sequences of individually defensible choices that jointly amount to turning down a sure gain, and there's a related "dilation" result (Seidenfeld and Wasserman) where updating an imprecise credal state on new evidence can leave it strictly less precise than before.

Write me an essay that takes this dispute seriously rather than just surveying it: lay out the strongest case for imprecision, then the sharpening arguments (Elga's sequential-choice problem and dilation), engage with at least one serious attempt to rescue imprecise credences from those objections, and end with an actual verdict on whether, and under what conditions, rational credences are allowed to be imprecise. Don't leave the question hanging.
