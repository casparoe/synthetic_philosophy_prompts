I write novels for a living (three out, a fourth that's overdue and driving me insane), and for the last few years I've also been one of five judges for a small first-novel prize that a regional arts council runs. It's not a big deal money-wise, but it comes with a cash award and a decent amount of local press, which genuinely helps an unknown writer's career get off the ground. This year one of the finalists is the sole title from a tiny indie press that everyone in town knows is about to fold. On our final judging call, another panelist argued hard that we should give the prize to that book, even though at least three of us privately agreed a different finalist was clearly the stronger novel - better sentences, better structure, more ambition, no contest really. Her reasoning was that if this book wins, the press probably limps on for another year or two and keeps publishing five or six other unknown writers who have nowhere else to go; if it loses, the press is done. When I pushed back she said something like "the fallout of our choice is part of what we're choosing, not some extra thing bolted on after the fact," so pretending otherwise isn't neutral, it's just a colder way of judging that happens to feel more objective.

I texted my partner about it (a lawyer, not a novelist, for what its worth) and she sided with the other judge completely, which didn't make me feel less crazy. I still can't shake the sense that she's quietly swapping out the question. "Should this press survive" and "is this the better book" feel like different questions to me, and once you let the first one leak into the second there's no obvious place to stop - do we also factor in which finalist needs the money more, or is more likeable, or already has a paperback deal lined up elsewhere? But when I actually said this out loud, all I got back was "okay, so what kind of reason would count then, if not one about what actually happens as a result" - and I didn't have a good answer.

So: is she right that weighing this stuff is just an honest, undodgeable part of judging, or is there a real, principled reason to keep "would the world be better if this book won" separate from "is this the better book" - and if so, what is it, exactly? I don't think this is just about book prizes, either; the same move seems to lurk behind blurbs, grades, performance reviews, basically anywhere someone's asked to certify that something is good while also knowing the certifying itself will do something in the world.
