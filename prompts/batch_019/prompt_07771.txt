Write a dialogue between a Humean about laws of nature -- someone who accepts a best-system analysis in the tradition of David Lewis, on which laws are simply the theorems of whichever true deductive system best balances simplicity and strength -- and an anti-Humean who takes lawhood to consist in a primitive relation of natural necessitation between universals, along the lines developed by D. M. Armstrong. Set the exchange at a whiteboard on which someone has written two true, exceptionless generalizations for the pair to argue over: "no sphere of pure gold has ever exceeded a mile in diameter" and "no sphere of pure U-235 has ever exceeded a mile in diameter." The anti-Humean should press the Humean to explain what, short of covertly helping themselves to some notion of necessity, entitles them to treat the second generalization as backed by a genuine law while dismissing the first as a mere accident of history. The Humean, in turn, should press the necessitation relation with some version of the inference problem: simply naming a relation "necessitation" does not yet explain why any regularity must follow from two universals standing in it. Each side should respond to the specific point the other has just made, concede ground where it is genuinely due, and neither should be allowed to win by default. Close the dialogue with each character stating, as precisely as they can, what -- if anything -- would actually change their mind.
