For nearly thirty years I worked as an experimental nuclear physicist, spending a good part of that time measuring decay constants and half-lives, and like most of my colleagues I fell into the habit of treating "probability" as a purely operational notion: the probability of decay within a given interval simply was the long-run relative frequency of decays recorded across a large population of nominally identical nuclei, or the frequency we would expect if the measurement could be repeated indefinitely. Since retiring I have been working through the philosophical literature on the interpretation of probability, and on the whole I have found the standard three-way division between frequentism, subjectivism (or Bayesianism), and the propensity interpretation illuminating. Subjectivism strikes me as obviously different in kind from the other two, since it locates probability in an agent's state of belief rather than in the world. The propensity interpretation, however, which I gather is due chiefly to Popper and was developed partly to make sense of single-case probabilities such as those arising in quantum mechanics, strikes me on reflection as nothing more than frequentism wearing a causal-sounding disguise: a "propensity," as far as I can tell, just is the disposition of a chance set-up — an unstable nucleus, a loaded die — to yield outcomes in a given frequency were the underlying trial repeated indefinitely, so that any statement about a propensity ought to be translatable without loss into a statement about a hypothetical long-run frequency.

I raised this recently with an old colleague from my research group, now also retired and auditing philosophy courses at the local university, who pointed out that Popper wanted propensities to attach even to single, unrepeatable trials — the decay of one particular nucleus in the next minute, say — rather than to repeatable set-ups as such, since frequentism has no way of assigning a probability that is not, at bottom, a frequency over some class of repetitions. Neither of us, however, could see why this counts as a substantive difference rather than a merely terminological one: that single nucleus is still just one instance of a repeatable type of physical system, so pinning the probability to the individual trial rather than to the reference class it belongs to looks to me like a difference in bookkeeping, not in what is actually being claimed about the physical world. Given this, I am at a loss as to why philosophers of probability continue to treat the propensity interpretation as a genuinely distinct third option, complete with its own dedicated literature and internal disputes, rather than as a mere terminological variant of hypothetical frequentism. Is there a principled distinction here that I am missing, or does the disagreement between propensity theorists and frequentists ultimately come down to little more than a difference in philosophical rhetoric?
