A friend of mine and I have been working through Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* for a small reading group, and last week's session on the "hinge" remarks that start around §341 -- where Wittgenstein suggests that propositions like "I have two hands" (his nod to Moore's proof of an external world) or "the earth has existed for a long time before my birth" function as hinges on which our ordinary practices of doubting and giving reasons turn, rather than as things we doubt or support with evidence -- ended in a two-hour argument that neither of us won.

My friend, who had just read Crispin Wright's "Wittgensteinian Certainties," defended an entitlement reading: we cannot have evidence for hinges, since any evidence-gathering already presupposes them, but we are nonetheless rationally entitled to accept them without evidence, because doing so is a precondition for any cognitive project -- including the skeptic's own reasoning -- getting off the ground at all. On this view, entitlement is a genuine, if non-evidential, positive epistemic status, so hinges are in good standing epistemically even though no argument could ever establish them. I had been reading Duncan Pritchard's *Epistemic Angst* and pushed the opposite line: that talk of "entitlement" quietly re-admits hinges into the space of reasons, when Wittgenstein's actual point -- especially the river-bed and riverbank imagery around §96-99 -- is that hinges are not warranted or unwarranted at all, any more than the rules of a game are true or false. On my reading they are arational commitments, closer to a standing practical orientation than to a belief with even a minimal rational pedigree, and "entitlement" is just warrant wearing a disguise. My friend's comeback was that this is a distinction without a difference: we both agree hinges are groundless yet not arbitrary, so "arational" versus "non-evidentially warranted" just sounds like the same view dressed up in different jargon. I don't think that's right, since only the non-epistemic reading can block the skeptic from reasonably asking what justifies our entitlement -- but I could not fully articulate why in the moment, and we left it unresolved.

Could you help us settle what we were actually disagreeing about? First, spell out, in substantive terms rather than merely terminological ones, what really divides an entitlement reading of hinge commitments from a non-epistemic reading, and say whether my friend has a point that the dispute collapses into semantics once you look closely enough. Second, and separately, give your own verdict on two further questions: which reading sits more comfortably with Wittgenstein's actual text, and which is the more defensible position if we set exegesis aside and simply ask what a contemporary epistemologist should say about the status of our hinge commitments. Please justify both verdicts rather than just asserting them.
