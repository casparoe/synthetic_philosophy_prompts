I went down a total rabbit hole a few weeks back reading about medieval Islamic philosophy (started with a random YouTube video about Averroes, somehow ended up on his rival Al-Ghazali instead) and I can't stop thinking about this one idea of his that's apparently called "occasionalism." From what I can piece together, his view is that fire doesn't actually have any power to burn cotton - when the cotton touches the flame and burns, the fire isn't the real cause of that, God is directly causing the burning right at that moment, and he just usually does it that way out of habit. So the fire and the burning are two things that reliably go together, not one thing making the other happen. Ghazali apparently needed this move to explain how miracles (like a prophet getting thrown in a fire and not burning) aren't logical contradictions, since fire never necessitated the burning to begin with. I also read that this ties into a bigger idea some of these theologians had where the whole world is basically made of instants that God re-creates from scratch each time, so nothing really "carries over" its powers from one moment to the next either.

Heres where I get stuck: this sounds suspiciously close to Hume's whole argument that we never actually observe necessity between cause and effect, only one thing following another, over and over. But Hume gets treated as a serious, hard headed philosopher for saying that, while Ghazali's version gets filed under "medieval religious apologetics." Is that actually fair, or is there a real difference between them? And either way, if literally nothing in the world has genuine causal power and its all just God repeating the same pattern on a moment by moment basis, I don't get how trusting ordinary stuff like "letting go of this cup will make it fall" or doing science at all is supposed to still make sense - apparently even Averroes thought this was such a disaster that he wrote an entire book responding to Ghazali just to defend the idea that causes really do necessitate their effects. So clearly smart people on both sides took this seriously, which makes me think I'm missing something.

Could you write me an essay that actually digs into whether Al-Ghazali's occasionalism holds up as a serious theory of causation, rather than just a theological escape hatch? I want the strongest arguments for it and the strongest arguments against it, weighed against each other properly (not a wishy-washy "some say X, some say Y" survey), and I want it to actually land on a real verdict at the end - something like "it's internally coherent but implausible because of Z" or "it's basically fine as long as you also accept W" is the kind of answer I'm after, not a non-committal shrug.
