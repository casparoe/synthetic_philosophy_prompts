A few years ago, some friends from my local logic reading group and I set up a small trust that funds an annual public lecture on the history of logic at our town library. We call it the Frege Lecture. I have no academic background in any of this -- I came to it through the reading group -- but I'm the trustee drafting the recommendation before we lock in this year's program, which goes to the printer in three weeks.

This year's invited speaker has sent her abstract, and it isn't about the Begriffsschrift or the sense/reference distinction at all. It's about Frege's 1924 diary, the notebook that Michael Dummett came across decades later and found so disturbing, in which Frege rails against parliamentary democracy and writes that Jews should be stripped of political rights and preferably expelled from Germany. Her argument is that this is not a shameful footnote separate from "the real philosophy" but continuous with it: the same impulse behind his lifelong campaign against psychologism and his insistence that logical truth is objective, fixed, and answerable to no vote also shows up in his politics. Two trustees think this is exactly the kind of talk the series exists to host; two others think it's speculative psychobiography dressed up as philosophy that will make our small series look like it's chasing controversy.

Write the recommendation memo I'll circulate before we finalize the program. Choose one course of action -- approve the talk, approve it contingent on specific changes (say what they'd need to be and by when), or decline it in favor of a different topic -- and give reasons the trustees on the losing side would recognize as genuinely engaging with their view rather than simply overruling it.
