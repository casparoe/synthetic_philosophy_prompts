I've spent a fair amount of time over the years reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums, and along the way I picked up the standard toolkit for anthropic reasoning: Bostrom's self-sampling assumption (SSA) and self-indication assumption (SIA), the Doomsday argument, the presumptuous philosopher, and so on. Recently I've been trying to properly understand the "Boltzmann brain problem" as it comes up in cosmological discussions of eternal inflation and de Sitter vacua, and there's a specific move in the argument that I can't quite pin down precisely for myself.

The setup, as I understand it, is this: if a cosmological model implies that some region of the universe persists forever in a thermal quasi-equilibrium, then over infinite time random fluctuations will spawn vastly more short-lived, isolated "Boltzmann brain" observers — complete with false memories of an orderly cosmic history — than the number of "ordinary" observers produced the normal way, via a low-entropy Big Bang followed by billions of years of structure formation. Sean Carroll, in his writing on entropy and time, doesn't treat the resulting conclusion ("this model implies you're almost certainly a Boltzmann brain") as merely a surprising Bayesian update to be swallowed; he calls the scenario "cognitively unstable" and treats it as a reductio of the model itself, something to be engineered away — say, by positing that our vacuum eventually decays — rather than accepted. I believe David Albert makes a related point in Time and Chance about why a bare statistical-fluctuation explanation of our universe's low entropy is untenable.

What I'd like help with is making that "cognitively unstable" charge precise. Why exactly does a theory that predicts Boltzmann brain domination undermine itself, in a way that's worse than just being a very improbable hypothesis one should shrug off — and how is this structurally different from a garden-variety skeptical scenario like the brain-in-a-vat, which we also can't rule out but don't treat as self-undermining in the same way? Separately, my understanding is that the diagnosis actually depends on your background anthropic framework: under something like SSA, ordinary evidence is supposed to straightforwardly disconfirm Boltzmann-brain-dominated theories, whereas under SIA (or similar observer-counting rules), that same evidence is sometimes said to instead confirm those theories, which just relocates the skeptical problem rather than resolving it. Could you unpack both points, ideally illustrating the SSA/SIA divergence with a concrete toy example rather than just restating the two principles in the abstract?
