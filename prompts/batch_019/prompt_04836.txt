I'm writing my IB Extended Essay on just war theory, and I've narrowed my research question to the dispute over the "moral equality of combatants." Traditionalists such as Michael Walzer hold that jus in bello permissions are independent of jus ad bellum: soldiers on both sides of a war are equally entitled to fight and equally legitimate targets, provided they respect discrimination and proportionality, regardless of which side's cause is actually just. Jeff McMahan's revisionist account denies this, arguing from the logic of individual self-defense that combatants who fight without a just cause are not morally justified in killing their opponents even when they observe the very same rules of engagement, since it is their side that is responsible for the unjustified threat -- though he grants that such combatants are often excused rather than justified, given the epistemic pressures of war.

Write an essay that weighs McMahan's argument from individual morality against the traditionalists' practical defense of combatant equality (chiefly, that soldiers are poorly placed to judge the justice of their own cause, and that abandoning formal equality would undermine compliance with the laws of war). Reach and defend a definite verdict: a qualified answer is fine -- for instance, endorsing McMahan's claims about individual responsibility while still defending Walzer's rules as the right standard for law and convention -- but simply concluding that "both sides have a point" is not.
