Over the past month I have been serving on an internal review committee at the engineering firm where I work, investigating a data-handling breach that turned out to have been caused, at least in part, by my older brother, who works two floors up in a different division. Before the committee's own inquiry reached him, he had already told me privately, as family, roughly what he had done; when it came time for me to report my findings to the rest of the committee, I disclosed only what I had independently verified through the audit logs and said nothing about that earlier conversation. Two colleagues on the committee later told me, not unkindly, that I had effectively covered for him; my brother's wife told me, rather less kindly, that I had already told the committee more than a brother owed him. I have been turning the whole episode over since, partly because I am also halfway through a continuing-education seminar on comparative ethics, where we had just covered the episode in Analects 13.18 in which the Duke of She praises a fellow villager — a son who turned his own father in for stealing a sheep — as a model of uprightness, only to have Confucius reply that genuine uprightness (zhi, 直) runs the other way: fathers conceal their sons' offenses, sons conceal their fathers', and integrity lies precisely in that mutual concealment.

I want to turn this into a short piece for the seminar's shared blog, and the thesis I keep drafting and redrafting is something like this: on Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont's "role ethics" reading of early Confucianism, uprightness is not a role-independent virtue that a father-son relationship can satisfy or violate from the outside; it is partly constituted by how well each party is living up to what that particular relation calls for, so treating a son's concealment of his father's wrongdoing as a lapse from some prior, impartial standard of honesty already begs the question against the framework. My worry, which I have not resolved, is that this makes the view unfalsifiable in more or less the way critics such as Liu Qingping have complained: if role-fidelity is what uprightness consists in, what could ever count as a family's role obligations going too far? I want to test the thesis against the harder case Mencius raises (7A35, if I have the numbering right), where his student Tao Ying asks what the sage-king Shun should do if his own father, Gusou, committed murder. Mencius has the minister Gao Yao arrest Gusou regardless of Shun's rank, and has Shun neither obstruct the arrest nor use royal power to pardon him, but instead abdicate the throne and flee with his father into hiding by the sea. That answer seems to preserve both impartial justice and role loyalty at once, by having Shun exit the conflicting role rather than adjudicate between its two demands — which is not an option my brother or I had. I want the essay to argue that this is where the role-ethics picture actually earns its keep, rather than where it quietly collapses into family-first partiality, but I keep suspecting I am being too generous to my own view.

Could you read the argument sketched above as though it were a full draft and comment on three things: first, whether I am representing Ames and Rosemont's position accurately enough that a specialist would not wince; second, whether the Mencius case really does the work I want it to do, or whether I am cherry-picking a convenient exception to a much less flattering pattern; and third, and most importantly, whether the committee story actually illuminates the philosophical issue or simply flatters my own decision by dressing it up in classical clothing. I would much rather the essay end up honest about where the analogy breaks down than have it read as self-serving.
