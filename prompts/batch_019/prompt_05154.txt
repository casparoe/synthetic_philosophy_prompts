Over the winter break my grandfather went into cardiac arrest and was resuscitated in the ICU; once he had stabilized, my aunt, who is very devout, gently but firmly resisted the idea that the defibrillator and the medication had done anything themselves. In her view, these were only the customary means through which God happens to act, and had He willed otherwise, the same shock at the same voltage would have accomplished nothing at all. I found this almost irritating at first, but it kept nagging at me, and when I mentioned it to a friend who is finishing a doctorate in Islamic philosophy, she told me I had reinvented, in miniature, Al-Ghazālī's position in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahafut al-Falasifa ("The Incoherence of the Philosophers"): his argument that there is no necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect, that the burning of cotton touched by fire is created directly by God at each instant, and that fire itself has no power to burn anything. On this view, what we call a law of nature is really just God's habitual custom, a pattern with no more logical necessity behind it than any other habit — which is exactly why a genuine miracle would involve no contradiction whatsoever: God simply declining, this once, to do what He usually does.

I'm now sitting in on a philosophy of religion seminar largely because of that conversation, and the final assignment allows a short story in place of a standard essay, provided it engages seriously with a position from the syllabus rather than merely name-dropping it. I would like to write mine on Ghazālī's occasionalism, but I want to avoid a piece where a character simply explains the doctrine aloud to someone else. Could you draft a short story of roughly 1000–1500 words in which a protagonist is driven, by some specific and fairly ordinary incident, to genuinely suspect that the objects around them have no causal powers of their own, and that the regularities they have always relied on are sustained moment to moment by something else's habit rather than by any necessity residing in things themselves? I would like the story to dwell in the resulting disorientation — what it would do to trust, prediction, and ordinary action — rather than settling comfortably into either straightforward faith or straightforward debunking by the end.
