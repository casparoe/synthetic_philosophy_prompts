During my career I ran a comparative-cognition lab that worked mainly on problem-solving and tool use in corvids and cephalopods, two lineages that people in my field like to cite as independent evolutionary experiments in complex intelligence. Since retiring, I have been working slowly through the history of moral philosophy, and Kant's brief but striking remarks on animals, in the Lectures on Ethics and again in the Doctrine of Virtue of the Metaphysics of Morals, have stayed with me longer than I expected. As I understand his position, Kant denies that we have any direct duties to animals at all, on the grounds that they lack self-consciousness and the capacity to legislate universal law for themselves, and so cannot be "ends in themselves" in the relevant sense; whatever wrongness there is in cruelty to animals is, on his view, wrongness toward ourselves, since habitual cruelty dulls a sympathetic disposition we need in order to treat other rational beings properly. Even his more generous-sounding remarks, such as the claim that an old horse or dog that has served faithfully deserves continued care, are explicitly filed under duties to oneself rather than duties owed to the animal.

What strikes me, coming at this from outside the discipline, is how much of that argument's plausibility depends on a picture of animal minds that the intervening two centuries of research have complicated rather than simply confirmed or refuted. My own work, and that of colleagues studying corvid tool manufacture, elephant and magpie mirror self-recognition, and great-ape future planning, repeatedly turned up behavioural flexibility that looks nothing like the fixed, instinct-driven mechanism I sometimes sense lurking behind Kant's examples, without ever amounting to the kind of self-legislating rational autonomy he treated as morally decisive. The 2021 UK government-commissioned review that led Parliament to extend statutory welfare protections to cephalopods and decapod crustaceans strikes me as a telling institutional symptom of the same tension: strong evidence of sentience, but nothing remotely resembling evidence of Kantian autonomy.

This is the question I would like your help thinking through. If Kant had been given a thorough briefing on this literature before writing the relevant sections of the Metaphysics of Morals, would his own premises have forced him to concede direct duties, or at least some intermediate moral status, to certain animals? Or is his position actually immune to this kind of evidence by construction, because it turns on an all-or-nothing capacity for self-given universal law rather than on any graded psychological trait that behavioural science could ever confirm or disconfirm? I would be grateful for your best reconstruction of how a philosophically consistent Kant would have had to respond, argued from the internal logic of his ethics rather than from how we might simply wish he had come out.
