In my formal epistemology seminar we recently covered Good's theorem: for a Bayesian agent facing a decision, the expected value of cost-free evidence is never negative, provided the agent conditionalizes on it and then chooses whatever act maximizes expected utility afterward. Two classmates of mine, Mara and Devon, got into a running argument about what this actually shows, and I'd like an impartial referee.

(1) Mara opened by claiming the theorem proves a rational agent should always accept a free, no-strings look at more evidence before deciding, since declining it can never do better in expectation than accepting.

(2) Devon objected with apparent counterexamples: people who turn down a free genetic test for a condition with no available treatment, or who ask a friend not to reveal how a film ends before they watch it, without thereby seeming irrational.

(3) Mara replied that these aren't real counterexamples. Once dread or spoiled suspense is part of the payoff, "learn the result" and "stay ignorant" are two different acts, each with its own built-in utility, rather than a case of freely inspecting evidence before choosing an otherwise fixed act — so the theorem was never about situations like that.

(4) Devon countered that this move only saves the theorem by making it unfalsifiable: whenever information seems to leave someone worse off, you can redescribe the case so that the harm was "really" part of the payoff of the act of learning, guaranteeing the theorem comes out true no matter what.

(5) Mara granted that she couldn't say in general, in advance, which real-life decisions have the clean structure the theorem needs, but maintained that this only shows applying the theorem takes judgment, not that it is empty or unfalsifiable.

Could you go through Mara and Devon's exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each of (1) through (5) rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important that you think both of them are overlooking about what Good's theorem does and doesn't license us to conclude about seeking out more information?
