okay so i've been down a rabbit hole about frequentist probability the last few weeks (started from an argument about poker odds, of all things) and somebody linked me this old 1878 essay by C.S. Peirce called "The Doctrine of Chances." most of it is him laying out the standard long-run idea, probability = the proportion of times a certain type of inference turns out true "in the long run," and he even argues every gambler and every insurance company is mathematically doomed to eventually go bust if they keep playing/insuring forever, since nobody actually gets an infinite run.

but near the end he does something i can't fully untangle. first he admits a real problem: if probability only means anything as a long run ratio, it "can have no meaning" for a one-off single case. then he gives this example:

"An individual inference must be either true or false, and can show no effect of probability; and, therefore, in reference to a single case considered in itself, probability can have no meaning. Yet if a man had to choose between drawing a card from a pack containing twenty-five red cards and a black one, or from a pack containing twenty-five black cards and a red one, and if the drawing of a red card were destined to transport him to eternal felicity, and that of a black one to consign him to everlasting woe, it would be folly to deny that he ought to prefer the pack containing the larger proportion of red cards, although, from the nature of the risk, it could not be repeated."

fine, that part makes sense to me, obviously you take the 25-red deck even on one draw. but then instead of just explaining why that's still rational, he goes somewhere i really don't follow (skipping a bit where he talks about gamblers and insurers going broke):

"But what, without death, would happen to every man, with death must happen to some man. At the same time, death makes the number of our risks, of our inferences, finite, and so makes their mean result uncertain. The very idea of probability and of reasoning rests on the assumption that this number is indefinitely great. We are thus landed in the same difficulty as before, and I can see but one solution of it. It seems to me that we are driven to this, that logicality inexorably requires that our interests shall not be limited. They must not stop at our own fate, but must embrace the whole community. This community, again, must not be limited, but must extend to all races of beings with whom we can come into immediate or mediate intellectual relation. It must reach, however vaguely, beyond this geological epoch, beyond all bounds. He who would not sacrifice his own soul to save the whole world, is, as it seems to me, illogical in all his inferences, collectively. Logic is rooted in the social principle."

i genuinely dont get the moves hes making. why does the fact that i'll die eventually bring back "the same difficulty as before" from the card game? and how does caring about some unlimited community actually fix anything mathematically, versus just being a nice sentiment he's tacked onto a math problem? is there a real logical argument in there or is he just hand waving an ethical conclusion into a spot where a technical one was needed? and separately, is this basically an early, clumsy version of what i've seen called "propensity" theories of probability that were invented later to handle single cases, or is that solving a totally different problem?
