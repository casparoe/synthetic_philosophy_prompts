A close friend of mine, who builds structural equation models for a living, and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about the nature of causation without either of us budging an inch. The argument started because I've been sitting in on a metaphysics reading group working through David Lewis's writings on causation; when I explained his counterfactual analysis to her afterward, she was fairly dismissive, arguing that the whole apparatus of possible worlds and similarity metrics has effectively been superseded by the interventionist framework she uses professionally, in which causal claims are cashed out via directed graphs, structural equations, and hypothetical interventions in the style of Woodward and Pearl. I thought this was too quick, and we ended up circling the same handful of examples for a good two hours without making real progress.

The example we kept returning to was the standard case of late preemption: Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle; Suzy throws first, or perhaps just throws harder, so her rock reaches the bottle first and shatters it, while Billy's rock arrives just after, striking nothing but air where the bottle used to be, even though it would certainly have shattered the bottle had Suzy not thrown at all. On Lewis's original 1973 analysis, the shattering doesn't counterfactually depend on Suzy's throw, even via a chain of intermediate events, since the bottle would have broken anyway thanks to Billy — the wrong verdict. Lewis went through two further revisions, the short-lived "quasi-dependence" proposal of 1986 and, later, an account of causation as a pattern of "influence," to try to recover the judgment that Suzy, not Billy, caused this particular shattering; even the final version seems to grant Billy's throw some influence, via its bearing on the timing and manner of the break, forcing Lewis to argue that this residual influence is too slight to count as full causation. My friend's position is that none of this trouble is necessary if you instead represent the scenario with a structural equation for the bottle's state and ask what happens under an intervention that sets Suzy's throw to "did not occur" while holding Billy's throw fixed at its actual value: you get the right answer immediately, and the same apparatus handles symmetric overdetermination just as cleanly (say, two arsonists who independently drop lit matches in a forest, either being sufficient on its own to start the resulting fire). I objected that Woodward himself has described the resulting view as a species of counterfactual theory rather than a genuine rival to one, and that since an "intervention" is explicitly defined as itself a causal notion, his account is openly non-reductive in a way Lewis's was never meant to be — so I doubt the interventionist has actually solved anything here, as opposed to relabeling the same underlying problem (which counterfactuals, or which variables held fixed, are allowed to count) in more convenient notation.

I'd like your help settling this properly, rather than just cataloguing both positions. Please compare Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation, including its successive revisions, with the interventionist/structural-equations approach associated with Woodward, Pearl, and their successors, using the preemption and overdetermination cases above as running examples throughout. In particular, I want to know whether interventionism is best understood as a genuinely distinct theory of what causation is, or as a more formally disciplined, restricted version of a counterfactual theory that inherits the same basic commitments and difficulties; whether Woodward's admitted circularity costs him something important that Lewis's original, fully reductive project was aiming for; and which framework, on balance, handles preemption and overdetermination with less ad hoc machinery. Please commit to an actual position rather than hedging, and tell me, on the basis of your reasoning, whether my friend or I had the better of the argument.
