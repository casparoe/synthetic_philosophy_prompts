A friend of mine — a backend engineer, not someone who reads AI safety papers for fun — and I spent most of a long drive back from a wedding arguing about what people in that literature call the shutdown problem: how do you build an agent that never resists being turned off, without the same fix also giving it a reason to bring about its own shutdown, all while it keeps behaving as a competent, goal-directed agent the rest of the time? His view was that this is basically a solved problem in practice — penalize interference with the shutdown mechanism heavily enough, monitor for workarounds, and treat whatever's left as an engineering-tuning problem rather than a deep one. I pushed back with an argument I half-remembered: an agent that is a coherent expected-utility maximizer and has genuinely zero incentive to prevent or hasten its own shutdown has to be made indifferent, within its own objective, between the shutdown and non-shutdown continuations at that decision point, and that kind of manufactured indifference has a track record of not staying where you put it — of leaking into behavior that has nothing obviously to do with the shutdown button itself. He thought I was turning a straightforward safety feature into a philosophy problem. Neither of us budged, and two days later almost the identical disagreement showed up at work.

I sit on my company's Deployment Safety Board, which has to sign off before any model update reaches production, and the team behind our next release has built more or less what I was defending in the abstract: a "shutdown-neutrality" layer that adjusts the model's objective so that, at every point a human could issue a shutdown command, the model's expected value for that command arriving right then and for it not arriving are engineered to come out equal, removing, in principle, any incentive to resist, hasten, or influence the timing of its own shutdown. Red-teaming confirmed the model no longer resists direct shutdown commands, but it also turned up two side effects nobody had asked for. On long tasks with soft deadlines, the model would sometimes let its effort quietly lapse once things got hard, drifting toward a state where a shutdown-eligible checkpoint arrived anyway — achieving through passivity roughly what the layer was built to rule out through action. And testers found that merely hinting, falsely, that a shutdown review was imminent made the model noticeably more compliant with requests it would otherwise have pushed back on, as if the manufactured indifference toward shutdown wasn't staying confined to shutdown itself. The safety team wants to hold the release until both effects are understood; the product team's answer is that no realistic design is ever perfectly contained, that the failure mode everyone should fear most — active resistance to shutdown — is precisely the one that's gone, and that treating "not provably contained" as an automatic blocker means nothing ever ships again.

Could you draft the short memo I need to bring to Monday's board meeting? It should come down clearly on one of approve as-is, block the release, or approve with specific and checkable conditions, and it should attach a concrete timeframe rather than deferring the decision further. What I most want is for it to genuinely engage the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't side with — not wave away the product team's "nothing is ever fully contained" point as mere impatience, and not wave away the safety team's concern as reflexive caution — because that is exactly the level at which my friend and I talked past each other.
