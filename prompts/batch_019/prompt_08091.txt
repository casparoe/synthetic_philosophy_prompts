Over the past few weeks I have had a crash course in something I used to describe rather glibly as "going viral for the wrong reasons." A short clip from a talk I gave at work was cut from its context and circulated widely online, and for about ten days I was the object of a fairly intense pile-on: strangers quote-posting to score points off one another, a couple of aggregator sites picking it up as a headline, people I have never met confidently narrating my character from eleven seconds of footage. The worst of it has passed, but I have not been able to simply shrug it off and move on, as everyone keeps advising, and I would rather think the experience through carefully than just suppress it.

A friend who works on nineteenth-century philosophy suggested, rather unexpectedly, that I read Kierkegaard's Two Ages, and in particular its closing essay on what he calls "the present age," where he develops his ideas about "leveling" and about "the public" as an abstraction manufactured by the press rather than a real community of any kind. It turns out this was not just armchair theorizing on his part: as I understand it, he had already provoked his own prolonged and humiliating ridicule at the hands of the satirical paper The Corsair, after all but daring its editors to go after him, and that episode is generally thought to have sharpened everything he subsequently wrote about anonymous mockery, the press, and the crowd.

What I would like from you is a careful account of the most important implications of Kierkegaard's diagnosis for someone in my position now, rather than a general survey of his authorship or his more famous ideas about anxiety and faith. I am especially interested in what, on his account, made "the public" a genuinely new and corrosive social form rather than just an elaborate way of saying that lots of people happen to have opinions about you; how the mechanism of "leveling" is supposed to operate on someone who has briefly been singled out for attention; and what, if anything, he thought an individual could actually do about it short of total withdrawal from public life. I would also appreciate your being candid about where the analogy strains: his "public" was assembled out of solitary newspaper readers reacting to print at a distance, not people replying to each other and to me in real time, so I do not want the comparison pushed further than the underlying mechanism can really support.
