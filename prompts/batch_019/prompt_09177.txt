Here's the question: "In the 17th discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazali argues that the connection between a 'cause' like fire and an 'effect' like the burning of cotton is not a necessary one. Reconstruct his argument. What does he think actually accounts for the regular patterns we observe in nature, if not necessary causation? Briefly compare his view to Hume's later account of causation."

And here's the student's answer, typed up exactly as turned in: "Al-Ghazali's famous example is fire burning cotton. The philosophers he's arguing against think fire necessarily causes the burning, but Ghazali points out we never actually observe the 'causing' itself, only that burning happens when fire touches cotton — so at most this shows the two events are conjoined, not that one produces the other by necessity. Since fire is just an inanimate object with no will, it can't really be an agent at all, so the true agent behind the burning has to be God, who creates the burning directly each time. Because there's no necessary link, there's nothing actually keeping the universe consistent from one moment to the next — its only ever an accident that fire burns cotton again next time instead of turning it into a bird or whatever, and that's exactly the loophole that allows for miracles, like Abraham not burning in the fire. This idea anticipates Hume almost exactly, and quite a few historians actually think Hume picked the argument up directly from Ghazali through Latin translations of Islamic philosophy that were floating around European universities at the time — Hume's idea of causation as mere 'constant conjunction' rather than necessary connection is basically Ghazali's position, just secularized."
