I'm several chapters into a novel set among the founding circle of the Society for Psychical Research, and one of my principal characters is modeled closely on Henry Sidgwick. I'd rather not write him as the stock figure of a "rigorous philosopher with an embarrassing hobby," so I want to know whether that's even a fair way to see him. Sidgwick spent decades investigating mediums and apparitions -- rigorously enough that his own circle eventually exposed some of the Society's most promising early cases, like the Creery sisters, as fraudulent -- even as he argued in his ethical work that ordinary practical reason harbors an unresolved conflict between the claims of self-interest and the claims of impartial duty, what he called the dualism of practical reason. Some of his biographers read the psychical research as a serious attempt to find empirical grounds for a moral world-order that could close that gap, on which view his skepticism toward individual mediums shows how seriously, not how half-heartedly, he took the project; others read the whole enterprise as a rationalization -- a need for the universe to underwrite morality, dressed up as disinterested inquiry -- that sits badly with the empiricism he practiced everywhere else in his philosophy. Write an essay that weighs these two readings against each other and defends a definite verdict, both on which one better fits the historical Sidgwick and on whether, by his own lights, the decades he spent on this research amounted to time well spent.
