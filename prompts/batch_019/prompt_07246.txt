In my school's philosophy elective, we recently started a unit on formal epistemology, and I've been reading beyond the syllabus about one debate in particular that I can't stop thinking about: the relationship between what philosophers call "higher-order evidence" and "epistemic akrasia." The case that hooked me goes roughly like this. Imagine a mathematician who has just checked, line by line, a moderately intricate proof, and is fully confident it is valid. She then receives excellent evidence that she was recently given a drug which, in a known percentage of people, subtly degrades logical reasoning without the affected person ever noticing anything is wrong -- they stay just as confident in their now-faulty reasoning as before. She has no introspective way of telling whether she got the real drug or the placebo, and when she looks back over the proof, it still seems airtight to her. It seems irrational for her to just shrug off this new information about her own reliability, yet it also seems irrational for her to abandon a belief that, by her own lights, the first-order evidence (the proof itself) clearly supports. If she keeps believing the proof is valid while also believing that this isn't the belief a fully rational appraisal of her total evidence would recommend, she is -- in the jargon -- "epistemically akratic."

From what I've read since, some philosophers hold to something like an enkratic principle (also sometimes called a non-akrasia constraint) on rational belief, on which this particular combination of beliefs is never permissible, so the mathematician really ought to lower her confidence even though she cannot locate any flaw in the proof itself. Others defend so-called "level-splitting" views, on which she can rationally go on believing the proof is valid at the first-order level even while conceding, one level up, that a fully rational assessment of her total evidence doesn't obviously support that confidence -- otherwise, the worry goes, our beliefs would be hostage to any story anyone chooses to tell us about our own unreliability, however flimsy or self-serving. When I brought this up with a friend from my debate team, she insisted you should always defer to the higher-order evidence, full stop; I'm honestly less sure, especially once I started noticing how the same structure seems to show up whenever a philosophical argument's conclusion strikes you as obviously wrong even though you cannot find the flawed step.

Could you write me an essay that lays out the strongest form of each side of this dispute and then actually commits to a verdict rather than staying neutral: is epistemic akrasia always irrational, or can it sometimes be the rational response to higher-order evidence? Whatever you conclude, please address the strongest objection to your own position before you close.
