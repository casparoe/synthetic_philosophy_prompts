I'm finishing a PhD in development economics, and I've been offered a short-term consulting contract with an NGO to help coordinate its emergency response after a major earthquake, in a country whose government has repeatedly been accused by aid researchers of diverting international assistance to entrench its own rule. The direct case for taking the job seems straightforward: the relief effort will save lives that would otherwise be lost, and my background is exactly what the logistics team needs. But a mentor who has spent two decades in the sector warns that large aid deployments to states like this one tend to free up the regime's own budget for repression and patronage, and that this effect could easily outweigh the immediate medical benefit over the following decade; neither she nor anyone else I've asked can tell me with any confidence which effect is larger, or even which way the net effect points. Every time I try to trace the reasoning through to a verdict, I turn up another downstream consideration that cuts the other way, and I never arrive anywhere stable. My instinct is that "you can't know everything, so just do the most good you can see how to do" is the sensible response, but it feels like it's dodging the puzzle rather than resolving it. Is there a genuine problem here beyond ordinary uncertainty, and if so, what would be a principled way to think about decisions where the considerations on each side seem to run equally deep and irresolvable?
