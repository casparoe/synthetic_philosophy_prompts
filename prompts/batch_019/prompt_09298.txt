Longtermist writers routinely justify heavy present-day spending on existential-risk reduction with an expected-value argument: even a tiny probability of safeguarding an astronomically large future is said to outweigh any merely large near-term good. For a piece I'm writing, I want to test that argument against a technical worry raised in the "infinite ethics" literature. Nick Bostrom argues that if the reachable universe contains an infinite quantity of value — a possibility some cosmological models leave open — standard aggregative, expected-value ethics collapses into what he calls "infinitarian paralysis": totals become undefined or invariant under anything we could do, so no intervention counts as making the world better or worse in expectation. Proposed fixes — discounting value by distance, ranking worlds by whose cumulative value eventually and permanently overtakes the other's, or restricting comparisons to local Pareto-improvements — each seem to sacrifice some feature, such as impartiality or completeness, that made the original argument compelling. Please lay out the most important implications of this problem for how I should weigh expected-value arguments for longtermism in my reporting: does infinite ethics show this style of reasoning to be close to unsalvageable once genuine infinities are in play, or merely in need of restriction that leaves its practical force largely intact?
