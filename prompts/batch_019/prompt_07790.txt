I sit on the internal safety and policy committee at the AI company I work for, and next week we owe the executive team a recommendation on a question that has turned out to be far more contentious than it first appeared. Three months ago, a group breached a contractor's systems and threatened to publish an early checkpoint of an unreleased model unless we met a set of demands; we let the deadline pass without paying and nothing came of it, but the episode prompted a proposal to formalize, and publish alongside our other public safety commitments, a blanket policy that the company will never negotiate with, pay, or otherwise accommodate anyone who tries to extract concessions from us through threats -- explicitly modeled on the standard government line against negotiating with hostage-takers. Most of the committee treats this as an easy call: a credible, well-publicized refusal to negotiate is exactly what removes the incentive for anyone to try this against us in the first place, so committing to it openly and irreversibly is the whole point of the policy.

One colleague objected in a way that stopped the room, and that I recognized immediately from years of reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum: she argued that adopting a rigid, public, hard-to-reverse commitment like this right now is a textbook instance of what people there call the "commitment races problem" -- the worry that, because the advantage in these situations normally goes to whoever locks in a strategy first, everyone is under pressure to commit early and irrevocably, before they have the wisdom or the information to know whether the commitment will actually serve them, and often before they can even foresee the cases it will end up covering. She doesn't think commitment is worthless; she agrees a credible refusal changes an attacker's incentives before the fact. Her point is that we would be fixing the rule today, in response to a low-stakes leak that never actually happened, and letting it silently govern much higher-stakes and structurally different situations later -- including future cases where the party issuing threats, or the party negotiating on our behalf, is itself a sophisticated AI system capable of reading our stated policy and adjusting its own strategy accordingly. Worse, she argued, publishing the commitment doesn't just bind us; it invites everyone else in a comparable position, human or artificial, to rush out their own hasty, symmetric counter-commitment before we do, which is precisely the dynamic by which these races leave every party worse off than if nobody had committed to anything in a hurry.

I'd like your help drafting the memo the committee will actually send. It should give one clear recommendation -- adopt the policy as proposed, reject it, or adopt some specific modified version, such as a narrower scope, an internal escalation and review step before the policy can be invoked, a scheduled-reconsideration clause, or separate treatment of human versus automated counterparties -- and it should defend that recommendation on the merits, engaging with my colleague's commitment-races worry seriously enough that she could actually respond to the argument, rather than recognizing it as a restatement of the deterrence case she already accepts.
