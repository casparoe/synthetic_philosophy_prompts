I work as an electrician and never had any occasion to study philosophy, but over the past year I have fallen down a fairly deep rabbit hole after my ten-year-old son was diagnosed with ADHD. Between the school pushing medication, relatives insisting he "just needs more discipline," and a stack of articles claiming his brain is simply "built for a different environment," I started digging into what it actually means to say a trait has a "function." That search eventually led me to a 1992 paper by a psychologist named Jerome Wakefield, and from there back to two older philosophy papers that I gather are the classic starting points for this whole debate: one from 1973 by Larry Wright, the other from 1975 by Robert Cummins. It turns out there is no single settled meaning of biological "function" at all, and the two leading candidates can quietly disagree about the very same trait.

Below is a short essay I have written, in my own words and without any formal training to fall back on, about why I think this split is doing real, if hidden, work in the popular "evolutionary mismatch" explanation of ADHD — the claim that it is not really a disorder at all, just an old adaptation stuck in the wrong environment. I would like your honest reaction to it, and I would rather you be blunt than encouraging.

Why "It's Just a Mismatch" Doesn't Settle Anything

When biologists or doctors say that a body part or a behavior has a "function," they usually seem to mean one of two quite different things, and the gap between them matters more than it looks.

The first idea, which comes from Wright, says that a trait's function is whatever effect explains why the trait is still around. The heart's function is to pump blood because ancestral hearts that pumped blood well were favored by natural selection over hearts that didn't, generation after generation, and that history is why we have hearts today. Call this the etiological account: it looks backward, to a history of selection, to decide what something is for.

The second idea, which comes from Cummins, does not look backward at all. On this view, a part's function is simply whatever causal contribution it makes to some capacity of the larger system you happen to be interested in explaining right now. The heart's function is to pump blood because that is the contribution it makes to the circulatory system's capacity to deliver oxygen, regardless of how the heart came to be there. Call this the causal-role account.

Ordinarily the two accounts agree, which is probably why most people never notice there are two of them. They stop agreeing exactly in the cases that matter most for questions about disorder, and I think the "mismatch" explanation of ADHD is a case where this disagreement is being papered over rather than resolved.

The mismatch story goes roughly like this: the restlessness, quick shifts of attention, and impatience with repetitive tasks that get diagnosed as ADHD today may have paid off in small-scale foraging environments, where constantly scanning for opportunity and threat was useful, and only look like a disorder now that childhood mostly means sitting still in rooms built for a different kind of mind. The conclusion usually drawn is that ADHD is not really a dysfunction, just an old adaptation in a new setting.

But look at what is actually being claimed. To say the trait "was adaptive" and is merely "mismatched" is to say that it is still doing exactly what it was selected to do; it simply no longer pays off the way it used to. That is a claim about etiological function, and on the etiological account, it is precisely why the trait would not count as a dysfunction: dysfunction there means failing to do what you were selected to do, not succeeding at it somewhere it no longer helps. Fair enough — but that only shows the trait is not a dysfunction in the etiological sense.

It says nothing about whether the same trait is a dysfunction in the causal-role sense, because that question was never asked. If the system we actually care about is not the ancestral foraging band but the life a modern child has to lead — sitting through school, keeping friends, managing frustration — then the relevant question is simply whether these attentional mechanisms are making the causal contribution needed to succeed at that life, and the foraging band has nothing to do with answering it. A trait can be exactly what natural selection built it to be and still fail, in the causal-role sense, to do what we currently need it to do.

So "it's just a mismatch" does not settle whether ADHD is a disorder. It settles the question only for someone who has already decided, without saying so, that the etiological account is the right one to use when defining disorder in the first place, and that is exactly the kind of quiet, unstated choice I would want to know I was making before I repeated it to my son's teachers.

Specifically, I would like to know: first, whether I have actually represented Wright's and Cummins' positions correctly, or flattened them into something a specialist would wince at; second, whether the argument in the essay's last two paragraphs is doing real work, or is a fairly obvious point that someone in this literature has already made, in which case I would like to know who; and third, whether there is an easy response available to the "mismatch" side that I have simply failed to consider.
