My aunt, a philosopher of religion who died last month at seventy-eight, spent her career defending a Leibnizian argument from contingency for a necessary being -- one grounded in the Principle of Sufficient Reason, and valuable to her precisely because, unlike the kalam argument, it does not require the universe to have had a beginning. She was equally firm that even a fully successful version of the argument establishes only a necessary being, not the personal God of any particular religion, and she would patiently correct relatives who claimed her work had 'proven God exists.' I am delivering her eulogy to a room divided between former colleagues who will notice any imprecision and family members hoping to hear that her life's work vindicated their faith. Write the eulogy. It should be warm and intelligible to the non-specialists, should never credit her with a claim she would have considered sloppy, and should still make clear why establishing the bare existence of a necessary being counted, for her, as a meaningful way to spend a life.
