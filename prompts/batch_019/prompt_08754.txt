In my school's philosophy elective, we recently read an excerpt from Galen Strawson's 1994 paper "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility" as part of a unit on free will. As I understood the "Basic Argument," Strawson claims that no one can ever be truly, ultimately responsible for their actions, because that would require being truly responsible for one's own character and motives, which would in turn require having chosen that character according to some prior set of values — values that would themselves have had to be freely chosen, and so on backward without end, so that no one could ever be a genuine causa sui, a cause of oneself. To me this reads as basically a more careful, technical restatement of the classic hard-determinism worry: we aren't responsible for our actions because they trace back to a causal chain that started long before we existed and that we never had any hand in choosing.

That same week, our AP Physics class covered quantum indeterminacy, and it struck me that this might actually rescue moral responsibility from Strawson's regress. If events like radioactive decay are genuinely uncaused rather than fixed by prior states, then perhaps the neural processes behind at least some of our choices are also genuinely open rather than being the inevitable output of a deterministic chain reaching back before our birth — in which case "the way we are" would not be entirely fixed by forces outside our control, and the argument would seem to lose its force. A couple of friends I mentioned this to thought it sounded like a reasonable way out, though our teacher just said the relationship between indeterminism and the argument was "not that simple" without elaborating further. Does introducing quantum-level indeterminism into the picture actually block the Basic Argument and restore genuine moral responsibility, or is there something about how the regress is supposed to work that this move fails to address?
