I spent about thirty-five years doing lattice QCD calculations before I retired, and lately I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole -- an old colleague dragged me into a little reading group at the library, and a couple months back we worked through Bostrom's original "are you living in a computer simulation" paper. I have to say the actual argument (the three-way split about whether civilizations ever reach a "posthuman" stage, whether posthumans bother running ancestor simulations, and what fraction of observers like us would end up simulated if they did) is a lot more careful than the version everyone argues about at dinner parties, which I found refreshing.

Anyway, this has now collided with my actual life. I sit on the small scientific advisory panel for a private trust that hands out modest grants -- fifteen to forty thousand dollars, no new instruments, just reanalysis of existing public data -- for "foundational but rigorous" physics questions. A proposal came in from a young theorist who wants to reanalyze public arrival-direction data from one of the big ultra-high-energy cosmic-ray observatories, looking for a faint directional anisotropy at the very highest energies. Her reasoning: if spacetime is discretized the way our old lattice calculations discretized it, that discreteness breaks continuous rotational symmetry down to whatever symmetry the lattice happens to have, and the only particles energetic enough to "feel" a lattice that fine are the rarest, highest-energy cosmic rays -- so instead of an isotropic sky you'd expect faint clumping along preferred axes. She pitches it explicitly as a test of whether we're in a simulation, not as a generic Lorentz-violation check, which is where the trouble starts.

The panels split about fifty-fifty on this and the email thread has gotten a little tense. Two members think it's a perfectly legitimate, dirt-cheap shot at real quantum-gravity phenomenology no matter how she's framed it, since discreteness-of-spacetime tests are a small but respectable field either way, and you don't get to reanalyze public data this cheaply very often. The other two think we'd be laundering an untestable philosophical thesis through a physics grant, and that it's actually confused on the merits besides, since Bostrom's trilemma is about the proportion of observers, not about any particular implementation, so it doesnt predict a lattice signature one way or the other -- and a positive anisotropy result would be just as well (more parsimoniously, even) explained by ordinary discreteness theories with no simulation talk needed at all. I lean toward the second group myself, though I'll admit my own lattice background makes me want to like anything that mentions one. I don't want to just impose that hunch on the vote, though. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before we decide next Tuesday? I need one concrete recommendation -- fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions -- with reasoning solid enough that the applicant could actually respond to it point by point, and it needs to take the "this is legitimate phenomenology" camp's view seriously rather than just waving it away as confused philosophy.
