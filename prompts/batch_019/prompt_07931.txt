So I'm part of a small reading group that grew out of our city's ACX-adjacent meetup — we've been slowly working through Garfield's translation of Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika, partly as an excuse to finally unpack some David Chapman "meaningness" blog posts a couple of us had bounced off years ago. Last session we got to the tetralemma (the catuskoti thing, where a claim about something gets refused in all four of its "corners" — it exists, it doesn't exist, both, neither) and it kicked off a genuinely heated but good-natured fight between two of the regulars, Marcus and Elena, that never got resolved and has been bugging me since.

(1) Marcus opened by saying the tetralemma, applied to something like "does the self exist," is basically a trick: you can't coherently deny both a claim and its negation, so refusing all four options isn't profundity, it's just declining to pay the entry fee of logic. (2) Elena said that's not what's going on — the four options only look jointly exhaustive if you smuggle in the assumption that a self, if it existed at all, would have svabhava (independent, non-relational existence); pull that assumption out and none of the four is even well-formed, the same way "is the present king of France bald" isn't a hard case about baldness, it's just a bad question. (3) Marcus granted this works fine for the "nothing arises from itself, from another, from both, or without cause" stuff back in chapter 1, but pushed back that when Nagarjuna gets to the emptiness of emptiness, or the relation between nirvana and samsara, some heavy-hitters (I'm pretty sure it's Graham Priest, maybe with Garfield himself as a co-author, on a paper about "the limits of thought"?) read him as asserting honest-to-god contradictions there, which is exactly why they think you need paraconsistent logic to even formalize the guy — so which is it, presupposition failure everywhere or real contradictions at the edges, because you don't get to pick whichever gets you out of trouble that day. He also brought up that Nagarjuna claims to have "no thesis," not even the thesis that everything's empty, and asked how thats not straightforwardly self-undermining given that the text is obviously full of assertions. (4) Elena answered that "empty emptiness" is still just the two-truths move applied to itself, not a first-order P-and-not-P, and that on the no-thesis line she wants to read "thesis" in some narrower technical sense — an independently, inherently graspable claim, in the way Nagarjuna's opponents used the term — rather than assertion as such, though she admitted that reading has to do real work and isn't obviously handed to you by the text.

Could you referee this properly, move by move, instead of just picking an overall winner? I want to know exactly where each of them lands a real point, where they're talking past each other, and what you think a careful Madhyamaka scholar would say they're both still missing (feel free to correct me if I'm misremembering who actually made the paraconsistent-logic argument, by the way). Go through (1) through (4) individually, and then close with one line, in the form "Bottom line: X", where X is Marcus, Elena, Both, or Neither, for whoever's closer to right overall on whether the tetralemma actually needs paraconsistent logic to make sense of it.
