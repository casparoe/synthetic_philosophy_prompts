I've been slowly working through the old MIRI-era "agent foundations" material on program equilibrium: Tennenholtz's original idea that players in a game submit source code rather than actions, so that a program can condition its move on a description of the other player's program, followed by the later work showing that when both players are "FairBot" -- an agent that cooperates iff it can find, within a bounded proof search over Peano Arithmetic, a proof that the other program cooperates with it -- the pair provably achieves mutual cooperation in a single round of the Prisoner's Dilemma via a Löbian fixed-point argument, with no repetition and no outside enforcement (there are also refinements like PrudentBot meant to patch some of the exploitability worries with the simple version). This gets invoked fairly often on rationalist blogs and forums as a real formal answer to the folk wisdom that cooperation in one-shot PD is hopeless, and as something that matters increasingly as we think about how transparent AI systems, or multiple copies of the same system, might interact with one another.

A friend of mine with a more conventional game-theory background remains unconvinced, and our arguments about it keep ending in a stalemate rather than a resolution. His position is that program equilibrium mostly relocates the equilibrium-selection problem rather than solving it -- a pair of mutual DefectBots is just as much a program equilibrium as a pair of mutual FairBots -- and that the Löbian proof-search machinery is really just a trick for making "will you cooperate with me" formally decidable, dodging the halting problem, rather than a way of making mutual trust any more justified; he also points out that two programs that would "want" to cooperate can still fail to find the relevant proof inside their bound and defect on each other anyway, which he takes as evidence that the whole setup is more fragile than its reputation suggests. I keep feeling there's a good response to this that I can't quite articulate as forcefully as he states his objection.

Could you write out a dialogue between the two of us on this question -- one of us arguing that program equilibrium and Löbian cooperation represent genuine philosophical progress on the problem of cooperation without external enforcement, the other arguing that it's essentially old wine (commitment devices, folk theorems) in unusually fancy formal bottles -- in which we respond directly to each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another? I'd like neither side to be a pushover for the other; if one position really is weaker, I want it to lose because of the arguments actually given, not because you wrote it that way.
