And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as they wrote it: "Fitch's paradox (Fitch 1963) starts from two things the anti-realist wants to hold onto at once: the knowability principle KP, that every truth is knowable — for all p, if p then possibly Kp — and non-omniscience NO, that some truth is never actually known — there's a p such that p and not-Kp. Instantiate NO with some true, unknown q, so q & ¬Kq is true, and apply KP to that whole conjunction: possibly, K(q & ¬Kq). But knowledge is factive, so if it's known that q and that nobody knows q, that's already contradictory — you'd have to know q while your very knowing makes "nobody knows q" false at the same time. So K(q & ¬Kq) can't hold in any possible world, which contradicts what KP just handed us, and by reductio NO is what has to go: KP by itself commits you to the omniscience thesis that every truth is actually known by someone.

This hits anti-realists harder than realists because a realist can just deny KP and move on with their life, but the anti-realist more or less needs something in the neighborhood of KP, since for someone like Dummett truth just is what can in principle be verified — rejecting KP isn't a free move for him the way it is for the realist. Edgington's fix keeps KP but, like Tennant's, restricts it so it no longer ranges over self-referential, Moore-paradoxical conjunctions like q & ¬Kq, which is exactly the kind of proposition that got us into trouble to begin with — so instantiating KP with the Fitch sentence is no longer licensed and the derivation never gets off the ground."
