At a check-up last month, my doctor used the ACC/AHA pooled cohort equations to put my ten-year risk of a cardiovascular event at 9%, then mentioned, almost in passing, that my family history of early heart disease counts as a "risk-enhancing factor" that the equation itself does not incorporate. Working out how much weight to give that bare 9% figure sent me back, for the first time since a graduate seminar on the philosophy of probability, to David Lewis's Principal Principle -- and specifically to the admissibility clause, which is supposed to say what further evidence I am entitled to bring to bear on top of a stated chance.

Write an essay addressing two connected questions: first, whether a population-derived estimate of this kind is even the sort of thing to which the Principal Principle applies, as opposed to a merely statistical or evidential probability that carries no presumption of deference; second, whether facts like my family history should count as "admissible" in Lewis's sense, so that they may legitimately move my credence away from 9%, or whether the admissibility clause is too thin (perhaps even circular) to settle the matter. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but do not leave the question open: defend a definite verdict about how someone in my position should treat the 9% figure.
