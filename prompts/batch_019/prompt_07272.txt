I've spent a while trying to work through the liar sentence ("this sentence is false") on my own, just from reading around rather than any formal training, and two rival fixes for it keep coming up. One, due to Kripke, lets the sentence fall into a genuine gap: inside the language it is simply "ungrounded," neither true nor false. The other, associated with Priest and other dialetheists, does the opposite: it says the sentence is both true and false at once, a "glut," and it relies on a logic where a contradiction doesn't force you to accept everything else as a result.

What I can't settle for myself is which cost is more bearable. The gap view seems to invite a "strengthened" liar - "this sentence is not true" - that looks true precisely because the theory calls the original not true, as though the paradox has simply resurfaced one level up rather than gone away. I suspect something similar could be built against the glut view too (a sentence insisting that it is false and not also true), though I can't tell how damaging that would really turn out to be for a dialetheist. Compare the two approaches specifically on how well each survives its own strengthened, "revenge" version of the liar, and say which one, if either, offers the more defensible way of living with the paradox, and why.
