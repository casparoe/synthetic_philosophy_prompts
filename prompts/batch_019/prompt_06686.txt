A friend of mine, who is finishing a law degree, and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about a hypothetical from her torts seminar, and the argument ended up spilling into a much longer disagreement about the metaphysics of causation that we never actually settled. The case was a lifeguard, under contract to watch one stretch of beach, who is absorbed in her phone and fails to notice a swimmer in trouble until it's too late; he drowns. Further down the same beach, an off-duty triathlete is sunbathing, notices nothing either, and could just as easily have swum out and saved him in time. We agreed immediately that the lifeguard is responsible in some sense that the triathlete is not, but we couldn't agree on whether that's a difference in what each of them caused, or only a moral difference laid on top of causal facts that are otherwise identical. I went home and wrote up roughly the following, and I'd like an outside read on it before I take it any further.

On a counterfactual theory of causation, both omissions look equally necessary for the drowning: had either the lifeguard or the triathlete acted, the swimmer would have lived. If that's what causation by omission comes down to, the theory seems forced to count both as causes, which is essentially the over-generating horn of the dilemma Sarah McGrath poses for any theory of omissive causation. The standard patch restricts the causes to whoever had a duty or could reasonably have been expected to act, but that just relocates the problem, since it makes whether X caused Y depend on a normative fact about what X owed to whom, which seems to get the explanatory order backwards: we usually want facts about causal contribution to help ground facts about responsibility, not the other way around. What I tried to argue is that McGrath doesn't fully credit a third option: swap the moralized "duty" criterion for a non-moral normality criterion of the sort Hitchcock and Knobe use to explain causal judgments more generally, so that the lifeguard's inaction counts as a deviation from an antecedently fixed default (she's stationed there to watch the water), while the triathlete's doesn't deviate from any default at all, and defaults can in principle be fixed by statistical or role-based regularities without themselves being moral facts. My friend's reply, which I couldn't fully answer, was that the "default" doing the work in the lifeguard case isn't a brute statistical regularity but is constituted by her contract, so the normality criterion just reintroduces the duty criterion under another name, which would vindicate both horns of McGrath's dilemma. That pushes things toward something closer to Helen Beebee's harder line that absences were never really causes at all, with talk of the lifeguard "causing" the death actually tracking blame rather than causal structure.

What I'd like from you is a genuinely critical read, not reassurance. Does the normality-based move actually survive my friend's objection, or is she right that it collapses back into the moralized horn as soon as you ask what fixes the relevant default? Have I been fair to Beebee, and to the alternative move that someone like Schaffer's contrastive account of causation might offer instead, or am I flattening real disagreements between the two? And is the lifeguard/triathlete case actually the sharpest way to run this, or is there a better-known case from the literature that would make the disagreement between the normality view and the eliminativist view show up more starkly? Please tell me where the argument is thin, not where it's already fine.
