So this is a bit of a random rabbit hole, but bear with me. I was reading about subjective probability for a Bayesian modelling thing at work and kept running into references to Frank Ramsey, this Cambridge guy who apparently invented a good chunk of modern decision theory, plus a whole field of combinatorics named after him ("Ramsey theory"), plus some foundational economics stuff, all before dying at 26 in 1930. So I went and poked around for what else he wrote, and it turns out shortly before he died he read a short reflective piece to some Cambridge society he was in that's basically his take on the meaning of life (I think its usually just referred to as "Epilogue" now). I don't have the actual text in front of me so I'm going off memory/summaries, but bare with me on the details.

The gist as I understood it: he says his "picture of the world" is drawn in perspective rather then to scale, like human beings take up the whole foreground and the stars get shrunk down to something trivial, and he's explicit that this isn't him coping or fooling himself — he just doesn't feel remotely humbled by how big and old the universe is, because "big" and "important" are simply different categories, there's no law saying the more space or time a thing occupies the more it matters. He does something similar with time: yeah the world will eventually die out, but that's so far off it barely has any "present value," kind of like a payment so far in the future that discounting it knocks its value down to basically nothing. And then there's a bit (fuzzier in my memory) where he compares himself, someone who finds all this thrilling, to an imagined reader who'd find it depressing, and argues neither reaction is more "correct" about the facts than the other — but his is still the better reaction to have, since it's more pleasant and better for actually getting on with things.

I keep flip-flopping on whether this is a genuinely good answer to the "universe is unimaginably huge and old and we're a speck, so none of this really matters" worry, or whether it's a slick move that just quietly changes the subject. Like sure, inside my own head I can choose to care about human-scale stuff and ignore the star-scale stuff, nobody's stopping me — but that just seems like a psychological fact about what Ramsey (or I) can manage to feel, not an actual rebuttal of the thought that, from some more external vantage point, none of the human-scale stuff really matters at all. Can you build the strongest possible argument that Ramsey's "perspective, not to scale" move doesn't actually work as a response to that worry — that it's smuggling in exactly the assumption that was in question rather than earning it? I want it to actually engage with the specific pieces of his argument I laid out (the perspective/scale metaphor, the time-discounting bit, and the thrilled-vs-depressed-neither-one-is-more-correct bit), not just another generic "the universe is big, meaning is subjective" essay, since I've already read a dozen of those and they're not what I'm stuck on.
