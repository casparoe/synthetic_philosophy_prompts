Ever since I retired after 35-odd years running a physical chemistry lab, I've gotten weirdly into philosophy, and lately I've been stuck on the "logical induction" framework (the one where probabilities for logical/mathematical sentences get built up through a market of computable trading strategies, rather than treating math as stuff you either know or don't yet). Here's what's bugging me: an old colleague of mine has been "morally certain" for years that the Collatz conjecture is true, purely because its been checked computationally for an enormous number of starting values without a single counterexample, even though there's still no proof. Classical Bayesian probability doesn't really leave room for that kind of confidence, since a logically omniscient reasoner is supposed to just assign every true mathematical statement probability 1 right out of the gate. What are the most important implications of the logical induction framework for how someone like my colleague should actually think about their confidence in a well-tested but unproven conjecture like that one, and is there a catch to it I should watch out for?
