In 1738, Daniel Bernoulli proposed to resolve the St. Petersburg paradox by supposing that rational agents evaluate gambles not by their expected monetary value but by their expected utility of wealth, where utility increases at a diminishing rate. Because this is still an expected-utility rule -- a probability-weighted average of utility across outcomes -- it satisfies what would later be formalized as the independence axiom: a rational agent's ranking of two options should depend only on the outcomes where they differ, not on outcomes the options have in common.

In 1953, Maurice Allais described a pair of choice problems in which most people's actual preferences violate exactly this axiom. Could Bernoulli's apparatus be rescued simply by choosing a different, more sharply diminishing utility function, or does the pattern resist any fix of that kind?

Now suppose Bernoulli had lived long enough to be confronted with Allais's results and with the debate they provoked about what such violations show. Based on the specific reasoning of his 1738 paper, rather than his general standing as an Enlightenment mathematician, how do you think he would have responded -- by treating the choices as errors to be corrected, by revising his own framework, or in some other way?
