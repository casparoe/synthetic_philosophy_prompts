I'm taking a philosophy elective this semester, and last week our unit was on free will and moral responsibility. In class, we discussed the classic Frankfurt-style case: a neuroscientist wants a man named Jones to perform a certain action, and has a device that would force him to do it if he showed any sign of choosing otherwise, but because Jones ends up deciding to do it entirely on his own, the device never actually needs to activate. My teacher used this to explain why some philosophers think a person can be morally responsible for what they do even if they technically could not have done otherwise, which the "principle of alternative possibilities" says should be required. That much made sense to me, but I did some further reading on my own afterward and found out that many philosophers were unsatisfied with this version of the case, since Jones still exhibits some "prior sign" right before the device would have kicked in, and he could presumably have shown a different sign, so a tiny flicker of alternative possibility seems to survive after all. This apparently pushed later philosophers (I believe Mele and Robb were among them) to construct newer versions in which the alternative pathway in the agent's brain is blocked, or simply never exists, from the very start, with no sign and no near-miss of any kind, so that there is nothing left to flicker.

I find this second, "no-flicker" version considerably more unsettling than the original chip-in-the-brain case, and it strikes me as good material for a short story, but I am having trouble figuring out how to dramatize it without it collapsing into a generic mind-control plot. Would you be willing to write me a short story of around 700 to 900 words, told from the point of view of someone who is admired for one difficult, principled choice they made years earlier, such as refusing to betray a friend or turning down an easy way out of serious trouble, who then discovers late in life that the alternative pathway in their brain had, in fact, been quietly closed off the whole time by an old experimental procedure, with no sign, no close call, and no instant at which things could genuinely have gone otherwise? The story does not need to resolve whether this person still deserves the credit they have received, but I would like it to end in a way that makes clear why this "no-flicker" version of the case is supposed to be so much harder to escape than the original.
