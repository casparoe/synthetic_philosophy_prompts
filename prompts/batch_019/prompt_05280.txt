I'm a junior on my high school math team. Each spring, three of us — our coach, another officer, and I — judge the club's "Constructive Proof Award" for the best solution to that year's existence problem (a Ramsey-type coloring problem this time). The rule, written two years ago, says winning entries must "actually construct the object, not just argue that one must exist," but nobody ever defined what that means. This year's strongest entry proves the object exists by assuming otherwise and deriving a contradiction, without ever saying what the object is. Our coach, who's been reading about intuitionism, argues that a proof which only rules out non-existence hasn't earned the prize, and that we can't settle this by invoking the law of excluded middle, since that's exactly what's in dispute. The other officer thinks this imports a fringe philosophical stance into ordinary competition math, where contradiction proofs have always counted as rigorous and complete. I need to write our decision memo before we announce the winner at next week's meeting: does the entry win, and, since this exact disagreement will come up again, what test should future judges use to tell a constructive proof from a non-constructive one? Argue for a specific answer, and take the position you reject seriously.
