I'm several drafts into a novel narrated in part by a woman who underwent a callosotomy in her late twenties to control severe epilepsy, and I want the passages that deal with the unity — or disunity — of her consciousness afterward to carry real philosophical weight, rather than lean on the handful of demonstrations that have hardened into cliché: the hand that buttons a shirt while the other undoes it, the object flashed to her blind field that she can point to but never name. A reader in my workshop called this out just last month — he'd already met both bits, once in a documentary and once in someone else's novel, and to him they read as furniture rather than as evidence that anything genuinely strange is happening to her. I've since gone back to Nagel's old essay on brain bisection, and I understand well enough why he declined to simply say she is "one person" or "two" — but I'd like the novel itself to sit inside that unresolved question rather than resolve it for the reader in a paragraph of exposition.

So: could you give me one well-specified example — an actual experimental finding from the split-brain literature, or an honestly flagged composite if nothing real is clean enough on its own — that a reader who already knows the standard demonstrations wouldn't have met before, and that cuts against both extremes at once? It should give real trouble to a view like Puccetti's, on which there are simply two separate subjects sharing her skull, but just as much trouble to the more conservative view that ordinary personal unity survives the surgery essentially intact. Describe the setup and the result with enough concrete detail that I could stage it as a scene rather than state it as a fact, and then tell me, briefly, which existing position in that literature it puts the most pressure on and why — Puccetti's, Nagel's own agnosticism, Bayne's suggestion that unity itself might switch on and off with context, or something else entirely if you think it fits better.
