I have no formal background in philosophy, but I've spent a lot of spare time working through decision theory puzzles on my own, and the one I keep coming back to is "Death in Damascus" — the version decision theorists usually trace back to Gibbard and Harper's 1978 paper, not the older folk tale it's based on. Death is a perfect predictor who will turn up wherever you actually end up tomorrow, so whichever city you choose, that's where Death will be. What bothers me isn't just that you seem doomed either way; it's that no single choice, and seemingly no way of randomizing between choices either, keeps looking like the right choice once you're confident you'll actually make it. That's the "ratifiability" problem, and it seems to bite every decision rule I try, not only the obvious suspect, causal decision theory.

I'd like your judgment on how well different decision-theoretic approaches actually cope with that specific unraveling in this exact case, rather than with predictor puzzles in general. Please rank the following from best to worst at yielding a stable, well-motivated recommendation:

- Standard causal decision theory, using probabilities for Death's location that don't get updated by your own impending choice
- Evidential decision theory
- Either of the above, plus the requirement that the chosen act must still look best once you're sure you'll take it
- Treating the decision as fixing the output of whatever abstract procedure Death is predicting, rather than just the physical act itself
- Committing in advance to a policy and refusing to reconsider once the moment of choice arrives
- Denying that anything could really be a perfect predictor of a deliberating agent in the first place

Please answer as a JSON object with keys "S", "A", "B", and "C" (S = genuinely resolves the unraveling, C = fails badly or just changes the subject), each mapping to an array of the approaches placed in that tier, plus a "notes" key mapping each tier letter to a one-sentence justification.
