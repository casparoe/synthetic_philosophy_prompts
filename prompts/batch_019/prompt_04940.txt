I've been working through the early history of computability theory for a dissertation chapter on the epistemology of the Church–Turing thesis, and I keep running into something that seems to undercut the standard line that the thesis is unprovable in principle. By 1936–37, Church's λ-definability, Turing's machine-computability, the Gödel–Herbrand–Kleene notion of general recursiveness, and Post's independent formulation had all been shown to pick out exactly the same class of number-theoretic functions, despite arising from strikingly different technical starting points. That convergence is a genuine mathematical theorem, not a conjecture, so I'm puzzled by my advisor's insistence that the thesis itself remains merely "extremely well supported" rather than proved. Her usual reply is that "effective calculability" is an informal notion that no amount of convergence among formal systems can mathematically pin down. But that feels like relabelling the problem rather than identifying any real gap, especially once four independently motivated analyses of the same intuitive idea agree with one another. Doesn't that agreement amount to a proof of the thesis, in the way that several independent derivations of a claim jointly establish it as a theorem? I would appreciate help seeing what, precisely, a proof would still be missing at that point.
