I'm a novelist working on a contemporary retelling of the old fable behind "Appointment in Samarra" — the servant who flees to Samarra to dodge Death, not knowing Death already expects to find him there. As research, I've been auditing a decision theory seminar, and one week the discussion moved to the version of the story that decision theorists call "Death in Damascus," which grew out of Allan Gibbard and William Harper's work on causal decision theory: a man encounters Death in the Damascus market and, being a good Bayesian, treats Death as a near-perfect predictor who will already be stationed in whichever city the man ends up in that night, Damascus or Aleppo. Death wants to match the man's location and the man wants to avoid it, so working out the expected utility of staying seems to recommend fleeing, and working out the expected utility of fleeing seems to recommend staying — the recommendation flips depending on which act you provisionally suppose you're going to perform, so that no fixed, "ratifiable" choice appears to exist at all.

Two people in the seminar, Naomi and Felix, spent a good twenty minutes on this, and I've been unable to stop thinking about who had the better of it. (1) Naomi opened by saying this simply shows causal decision theory doesn't work: a theory that recommends fleeing whenever you suppose you'll stay, and staying whenever you suppose you'll flee, hasn't told the man what to do, and a decision theory that outputs no decision has failed at its one job. (2) Felix answered that this is too quick: the theory does output something, namely that no pure act is ratifiable and the man should randomize, exactly as game theorists are untroubled by games whose only equilibrium is mixed — a theory that says "flip a coin" has told you what to do. (3) Naomi replied that the game-theory analogy smuggles in something Death doesn't have: in a mixed equilibrium the other party is responding to your mixed strategy, whereas Death has already predicted and positioned himself before any coin lands, so the coin doesn't cause Death to be anywhere — it only describes the man's own ignorance, and dressing that up as "ratifiable" borrows the vocabulary of equilibrium without the causal structure that earns it. (4) Felix conceded the causal structure is different, but said Naomi's own complaint only had force because ratifiability is supposed to track stability, and the mixed act has exactly that: once the man is randomizing fifty-fifty, his credence about Death's location is also fifty-fifty, so staying, fleeing, and the coin flip all carry identical expected utility, leaving no pure deviation he'd wish he'd made instead — and if that isn't stability, Felix wasn't sure what ratifiability was ever supposed to mean, even in the ordinary cases where both of them agree it works. (5) Naomi answered that this was exactly the sleight of hand: an ordinary ratifiable act is supposed to come out uniquely best given itself, whereas all Felix had shown was a three-way tie, and calling a tie "stability" quietly redefines success as merely not regretting your choice, rather than having had a good reason for it. (6) Felix's last word was that Naomi still hadn't offered anything better: an evidential decision theorist doesn't obviously do better here either, since with a predictor this good, conditionalizing on your own act makes staying and fleeing come out equally likely to get you killed, so evidential decision theory's answer is just to shrug and call it a wash — which raises the question of whether "broken" was ever the right word for what's happening to causal decision theory, as opposed to just "hard."

Since I'd like to get the philosophy right before I let a character in my novel reason this way, could you go through the exchange move by move — for each of the six numbered points, say who had the better of it and why — rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be grateful if you'd flag anything you think both Naomi and Felix are overlooking, since a novel gets to cheat a little and let a third character notice what the arguing pair missed.
