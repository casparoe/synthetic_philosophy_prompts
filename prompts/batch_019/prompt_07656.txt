John Stuart Mill's ethics contains two strands that sit uneasily together. In *Utilitarianism*, he argues that the qualitative ranking of pleasures cannot be entrusted to the untutored preferences of the many, but must be settled by competent judges: those whose faculties are cultivated enough to have experienced, and fairly weighed, both the higher and the lower pleasures. Yet in *On Liberty*, he insists that the only legitimate ground for overriding a competent adult's own judgment about their own good is the prevention of harm to others, and treats even benevolent paternalism as itself a kind of injury. Eliezer Yudkowsky's proposal of Coherent Extrapolated Volition would define a person's good not by what they currently want, but by what they would want if they knew more, reasoned better, and had become more the person they wished to be — an idealization they have neither undergone nor consented to have carried out on their behalf. Arguing strictly from Mill's own texts and commitments, what do you take to be his most consistent verdict on Coherent Extrapolated Volition: an extension of his own standard of competent judgment, or a paternalism that his harm principle was designed to forbid? Defend a single answer, and show how it resolves, rather than merely restates, the tension between these two strands of his thought.
