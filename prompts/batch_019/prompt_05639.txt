My aunt Miriam, a philosopher of religion who died last month at seventy-eight, spent much of her career on one question: whether penal substitution — the doctrine that Christ was punished in our place — actually holds together. Her best-known paper takes up David Lewis's old puzzle that a debt can be paid by anyone, but punishment, on the ordinary retributive picture, is supposed to fall on the guilty party as such, so handing it to an innocent substitute looks less like satisfying justice than compounding the wrong. She argued that the popular version of the doctrine doesn't survive that objection, used to joke that she'd haunt whoever preached it at her funeral anyway, and died a believer regardless, without ever deciding, even to her own satisfaction, what could honestly be said instead. As the novelist in the family, I've been asked to give the eulogy. Half the people in the pews will be colleagues who know her work and will wince at the pious version; the rest just want to hear that she's home because Jesus paid for her sins. Write it so the second group gets that comfort and the first gets nothing to wince at, without ducking the problem by staying vague about the cross or pretending she settled it herself. About five minutes, please.
