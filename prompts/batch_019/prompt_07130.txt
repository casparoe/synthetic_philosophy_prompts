I've spent the past several weeks reporting a feature on "prolonged grief disorder," the diagnosis the American Psychiatric Association folded into the DSM-5-TR in March 2022 as the newest addition to the manual, covering grief that remains acute and impairing a year or more after a death. The clinicians I've interviewed mostly treat it as a straightforward clinical entity, but the philosophy of medicine I've been reading alongside it makes the diagnosis look far less settled. Christopher Boorse's naturalism holds that whether something counts as a disease is, in principle, a value-free biological fact: some part or process is functioning at a statistically subnormal level for one's age- and sex-matched reference class, where "normal" is fixed by that part's species-typical contribution to survival and reproduction. Normativists working in the line of Lennart Nordenfelt and Georges Canguilhem deny this can ever be fully value-free; for Nordenfelt, health is roughly the ability, under standard circumstances, to achieve one's vital goals, which makes disease partly a matter of harm and human purposes rather than statistics alone.

What's needling me is a specific wrinkle in the PGD criteria: alongside the yearning, disbelief, and functional impairment, the diagnosis explicitly requires that the bereaved person's reaction be out of step with what their own cultural, religious, and social context would lead a clinician to expect. That looks like normativism smuggled directly into a manual that otherwise presents itself as biologically neutral, and I'd like help working out exactly how much weight that observation should carry.

Could you write me a dialogue of roughly 700 to 1,000 words between a naturalist about disease in the mold of Boorse and a normativist in the mold of Nordenfelt or Canguilhem, arguing specifically over whether prolonged grief disorder is a genuine disease? Imagine the two of them still going at it after a grand-rounds talk on the new diagnosis has emptied out, and set it down as a transcript with speaker labels, no narrator, and no tidy resolution at the end. I'd like the naturalist to cash out "dysfunction" here as some attachment- or grief-processing mechanism failing to complete its species-typical course, and to be pressed on two fronts in return: first, why a supposedly value-free biological kind would need a cultural-expectations clause written into its own diagnostic criteria, and second, the reference-class worry that if an entire cohort's grief failed to resolve — survivors of a war, or of a particularly devastating pandemic wave, say — the naturalist's own apparatus would seem to make that the new statistical norm for the group, and therefore not a disease after all. In turn, I'd like the normativist pressed on a case that cuts the other way: someone whose indefinitely prolonged mourning is fully expected and honored by their community, yet who is, by every clinical measure, unable to work, sleep, or sustain other relationships — does the normativist really have to withhold the diagnosis simply because the community endorses the practice, and if not, hasn't some community-independent notion of impaired functioning crept back into the theory? I'd like both figures to be equally sharp, actually responding to each other's last move rather than restating their opening position, and I don't want either side to come out clearly ahead by the end.
