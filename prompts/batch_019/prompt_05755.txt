I run a small interview podcast about technology and society out of my apartment — nothing fancy, just me and a decent microphone — and I don't have any academic background in philosophy; everything I know about this comes from a couple of years of reading on my own, kicked off by noticing how oddly evasive chatbots get whenever you ask them something politically loaded, always laying out "several perspectives" and declining to land anywhere. Chasing down why that design choice might actually be defensible led me to the work of a philosopher I've booked for next month's episode. Her position, roughly, is that a general-purpose AI assistant should be aligned the way she thinks a legitimate liberal state treats its citizens: it shouldn't encode its own developers' values, and it shouldn't just average everyone's stated preferences into a global consensus either. Instead it should stay neutral among the different reasonable comprehensive moral, religious, and political doctrines its users hold, appealing only to grounds that people who disagree about the good life could still accept. When I described this to a couple of friends over dinner, their instinctive reaction was that it sounds like a clever way of dodging the actual question, and I suspect she's used to hearing that.

I can already find her stating and motivating the view in her published essays, so I don't want interview questions that just invite her to restate it. Could you help me draft eight or nine questions that press on the harder parts underneath it instead? I'd like at least one question on each of the following: the gap between a system merely intending to be neutral and its actually landing neutrally on the world, given that any particular set of guardrails will foreseeably help some worldviews spread faster than others; the fact that some of the very doctrines she wants the assistant to be neutral among explicitly deny that rival doctrines deserve tolerance, which makes "just be neutral" look like it's already siding with one party to that disagreement; whether a framework built for citizens who share a single political culture even transfers to a product used by people across dozens of countries with no such shared culture; and who gets to decide which doctrines count as "reasonable" enough to be included in the neutrality in the first place, since that gatekeeping looks like exactly the sort of value judgment neutrality was supposed to avoid making. For each question, please add a short, pointed follow-up keyed to the answer or deflection she's most likely to reach for.
