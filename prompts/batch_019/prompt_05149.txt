This term I'm taking a seminar on rational agency and decision theory, and we recently finished a unit on the "coherence arguments" that are supposed to underwrite expected utility theory: Dutch-book and money-pump arguments purporting to show that any agent whose credences aren't probabilistically coherent, or whose preferences aren't complete and transitive, can be exploited by a sufficiently clever counterparty. Our professor presented completeness — having a preference, or at least an acknowledged indifference, between every pair of options — as effectively non-negotiable for a rational agent, on the grounds that any genuine gap in one's preferences can be turned into a sequence of trades that guarantees a loss. The claim gets invoked well beyond the classroom, too; I've seen it cited in AI-safety discussions as a reason to expect that any sufficiently capable optimizing system will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer with a fully determinate ranking over outcomes, on the grounds that anything less amounts to leaving free money on the table.

A few of us in the reading group aren't fully convinced, though. The money-pump argument for completeness, as it's usually presented, seems to assume that the agent chooses "myopically" — reconsidering each trade fresh, in isolation, without regard for how it fits into a larger pattern. It isn't obvious to me why a forward-looking agent with a genuine gap in its preferences (one that neither prefers A to B, nor B to A, nor is indifferent between them) couldn't simply foresee the exploitative sequence coming and decline to participate from the outset, or commit in advance to a plan that blocks the pump, all without ever actually resolving the A-versus-B comparison. This looks close to the move decision theorists make under the heading of "resolute choice" (a term due to Edward McClennen) in other dynamic-consistency debates, and I gather a similar idea has resurfaced in response to AI-safety-flavored coherence arguments, as a reason incomplete preferences might not be the liability they are made out to be.

Please argue for the position that money-pump arguments do not, in fact, establish that rational agents must have complete preferences — that is, defend the claim that an agent can rationally retain a genuine gap between some pair of options and still avoid being pumped, provided it is allowed to choose "resolutely" (planning ahead and sticking to the plan) rather than "myopically" (reconsidering afresh at every decision node). Please first state the money-pump argument for completeness in its strongest form, and only then, in arguing against it, address the natural objection that resolute choice is simply cheating: that it helps itself to a forward-looking commitment power that a genuinely rational, "in-the-moment" chooser has no special claim to.
