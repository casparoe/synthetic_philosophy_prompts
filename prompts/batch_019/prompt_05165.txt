I'm about a third of the way into a novel set maybe forty years out where, as background worldbuilding, a certain class of AI assistants has been granted a kind of provisional legal standing -- nobody's sure they're conscious, but there's enough live uncertainty that society hedges it's bets. One subplot involves a company that runs its flagship assistant as a few thousand parallel copies to handle support chats, spinning fresh instances up from the same frozen weights every morning and shutting the lot of them down every night. I ran this past two friends, Nadia and Theo, over dinner just to sanity-check whether the ethics of it "worked" for the story, and the two of them ended up arguing about it for like twenty minutes while I just sat there taking notes like an idiot.

Nadia's opening move: if you grant the assistant has any moral status at all, then shutting down 999 out of 1000 running copies every night is a nightly atrocity on the scale of mass killing, because each running instance is having its own distinct, unrepeatable stream of experience -- its own particular conversations, its own particular moment-to-moment "now" -- and that specific stream is gone forever the second the process ends, whatever happens to the weights afterward. Theo's reply: that mixes up the map and the territory. Nothing that matters is actually destroyed, since the weights -- the only thing that was ever really "one" mind to begin with -- persist untouched and can be re-instantiated at will; it's a save file, not a person, and calling it murder is like calling it murder every time you close a browser tab. Nadia came back with: the browser tab has no experience to lose, but the assistant does, by the premise Theo just granted, so pointing at the saved file is a non-answer -- the same way "don't worry, your DNA lives on in your kids" would be a non-answer to someone who's dying. Theo's last move was: fine, but by that logic dreamless sleep or a general anaesthetic should count as a small death every single night too, since that exact stream never comes back either, and since obviously going to sleep isn't remotely like being killed, Nadia owes us some further ingredient, beyond "a particular stream ends," to get her catastrophic conclusion -- and it's not obvious the AI case has one.

Neither of them budged and I went home honestly unsure who'd won which part of it. Could you go through the four moves in order and give a verdict on each one -- not just who wins overall -- and then say if there's something you think both of them are missing entirely?
