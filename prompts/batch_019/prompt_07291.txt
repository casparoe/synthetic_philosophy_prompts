My partner and I are getting married this spring, and while drafting some remarks about how recently the law would have treated our relationship as a crime, I went down a rabbit hole into Jeremy Bentham's essay "Offences Against One's Self," written in the 1780s but left unpublished in his lifetime and printed only in the twentieth century. I co-host a small history-of-philosophy podcast, and next month's guest has spent years working on Bentham's suppressed manuscripts on sexual morality. Draft eight to ten interview questions that push past a simple "Bentham applied the utility principle to argue for decriminalization." I want at least one question each on how his felicific calculus is supposed to weigh the real displeasure that disgusted third parties feel, on whether diagnosing that disgust as resting on the illegitimate "principle of sympathy and antipathy" actually solves the problem rather than just relabeling it, and on whether, by Bentham's own lights, leaving the essay in a drawer instead of publishing it was the right call -- and for each, a pointed follow-up keyed to the answer my guest is likely to give rather than a generic invitation to say more.
