I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to a decade now, and along the way I've picked up a fair amount of their self-management toolkit: trigger-action plans, Ulysses contracts, Beeminder-style commitment devices, and the like. I'm currently trying to apply that toolkit to a genuinely hard decision: whether to leave a stable, well-paid job to spend a year on an independent research project I care about, something I've talked myself out of twice already. Because I don't trust the version of me who will still be sitting in that job six months from now - anxious about rent, freshly flattered by a promotion, or otherwise compromised - I've been trying to write myself a binding rule in advance, while I'm relatively calm and clear-headed, that my future self would simply have to obey.

The trouble is that every time I try to specify the rule precisely enough to close off every loophole, I hit what feels like the same wall. To make the rule airtight, I'd have to anticipate every way a more anxious, more invested version of me might reinterpret its key terms in bad faith ("well, this offer isn't really as stable as it looks, so it doesn't count"), which means simulating that future self's rationalizations in advance, using the very brain that generates rationalizations in the first place - so I never get far enough outside the system to check my own work. There's a stranger wrinkle, too: if I could predict with real confidence what future-me will do, the rule would be beside the point, since the outcome would already be settled; but the entire reason for writing the rule is to change what future-me does, which means my "prediction" of my own behavior and my present attempt to engineer it aren't actually independent of each other. I raised this at a rationalist meetup recently and got answers ranging from "just trust yourself more" to "that's exactly why you need a friend or a contract instead of a self-policed rule," but neither response seemed to engage with the structure of the problem so much as route around it.

What I'd like is to understand this properly rather than collect more productivity tips. Is there a principled way to think about the limits on how much a person - or any agent, for that matter - can bind or verify its own future conduct entirely from the inside, given that it can never fully step outside itself to audit the rule? Is this a recognized dead end with some real theory behind it, or am I just dressing up ordinary self-doubt in fancier language?
