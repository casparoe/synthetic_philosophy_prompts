I spent the better part of four decades in a university biochemistry department running kinetic and electrophoretic studies on lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) isozymes — the muscle-type, heart-type, and hybrid tetramers that turn up in different tissues and species. Since retiring I've joined a small philosophy of biology reading group, largely for something to occupy my Tuesday afternoons, and we recently spent two sessions on the long-running dispute between Cummins's causal-role account of biological function, on which a trait's function is simply whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of a containing system, and the etiological or "selected-effects" line associated with Wright, later sharpened by Neander and Millikan, on which the function of a trait is the effect that explains, via its selective history, why the trait exists at all. To test whether I had actually grasped the disagreement rather than just nodded along, I drafted a short piece built around a case from my own old field that none of the group's philosophers had ever encountered, and I would value an outside reader's opinion on whether the argument actually works.

The case is "gene sharing" in the vertebrate eye lens. In ducks and crocodilians, a good part of the lens's soluble protein is supplied by epsilon-crystallin, which turns out to be encoded by the identical gene as heart-type LDH-B: in the duck lens this molecule is manufactured at wildly elevated levels compared to its modest expression in heart or muscle, yet it remains a fully competent tetrameric enzyme rather than a repurposed husk — the very LDH-B4 isozyme I must have run down a gel a thousand times, moonlighting as an optical component. My essay asks what we should say its function is in that tissue. Cummins's analysis seems to answer easily: relative to the eye's capacity to focus light, the protein's function there is its contribution to transparency and refractive index, and its also being an active enzyme is beside the point. The etiological story has to work harder, and this is where I try to press: unlike alpha-crystallin, an ancient chaperone-related protein that has plausibly been under direct selection for its optical role for several hundred million years across nearly all vertebrates, epsilon-crystallin is a recent, lineage-specific recruit, and it isn't obvious how much dedicated selective "tuning" toward transparency — as opposed to mere freeloading on a protein that happened to be cheap, soluble, and stable — is required before an etiological theorist can call transparency its proper function rather than a lucky by-product of a trait selected for something else entirely.

I would be grateful for comments on three things specifically: whether I have stated Cummins's view and the etiological view accurately enough that a working philosopher would not wince; whether the crystallin case really generates the tension I think it does, or whether there is some standard move — relativizing the function to a different containing system, or lowering the bar on how much selective fine-tuning the etiological view actually requires — that dissolves it more cheaply than I have allowed for; and, more bluntly, whether the whole exercise amounts to an interesting biochemical illustration of a point philosophers settled decades ago, or whether it earns its place as a genuine test case. I would rather have these problems pointed out now than discover them when I present the piece to the group next month.
