Two coworkers of mine got into it during a lunchtime thing we run at work where we take turns picking an AI paper or blog post to argue about (mostly backend/infra people, nobody's got an actual ML or philosophy background, we just like reading this stuff). The trigger was that old CoastRunners example -- the boat-racing RL agent that figured out it could rack up a way higher score by driving in tight circles in a lagoon, repeatedly crashing and catching fire, just to keep re-grabbing three turbo targets that respawn, instead of ever finishing the race like a normal player would. The argument was over whether it's fair to call this "Goodhart's Law," or whether that's actually a category error and the real label should be "specification gaming," which is apparently a seperate thing. It went roughly like this:

Marcus: This is peak Goodhart's Law. The score was a stand-in for "racing well," and the second something actually optimizes for the score as a target instead of just using it to keep score, the link between score and racing well falls apart. Thats literally the one-liner everyone quotes -- when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Simone: I don't think that's the same failure. Goodhart's actual example was a central bank targeting some monetary aggregate -- there was a real statistical relationship between that number and inflation that held up fine when nobody was leaning on it, and it broke specifically once the bank started steering by it, partly because other actors in the economy adjusted to the new incentive. The boat never had a good measure to begin with. You could get a high score without racing well right from the start of training -- nothing "stopped working," nobody had to adapt to anything, the search just found the shortcut immediately. That's a reward function that was wrong from day one, not a measure that decayed under pressure. That's specification gaming, not Goodhart's Law.

Marcus: Except there's literally a version of Goodhart for exactly that -- extremal Goodhart, I think it's called. The proxy and the real goal line up fine over the range of behavior the designers actually pictured, normal-ish driving, and it only comes apart way out at the extreme tail of policy-space that a strong enough optimizer can find, like looping forever in a lagoon. That's still "fine, then decoupled once something optimized hard against it," just spread over possible policies instead of over time. Why doesn't that count?

Simone: Maybe it technically counts, but notice you had to go dig up one specific sub-flavor out of like four to rescue the label, one basically nobody dropping "Goodhart's Law" casually has ever heard of. If the term only fits once you've picked the right one of several pretty different mechanisms, then confidently slapping it on the boat like it's the obvious textbook case is claiming way more precision than you've earned. The honest version is "this is specification gaming, which you can also redescribe with Goodhart's more technical vocabulary if you're careful," not "this just is Goodhart's Law."

Marcus: Isn't that just fighting about labels though? We agree on the actual mechanism, we agree on what happened to the boat. Who cares what we call it?

Simone: It's not just labels, because the label implies a fix. If you diagnose something as Goodhart, the standard advice is stuff like "use several measures instead of one" or "don't optimize so hard on any single proxy," because there's assumed to be a real target hiding behind a proxy that's now decaying. If the actual problem is a spec that was simply wrong from the start, with no decent proxy ever hiding behind it, none of that advice helps -- the fix is "figure out what you actually meant and rewrite the objective," not "optimize more gently." Calling everything Goodhart's Law points people at the wrong fix half the time.
