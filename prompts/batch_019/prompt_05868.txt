Last month a friend and I had a long and, frankly, quite heated argument that started over something trivial: a hiking trip we cancelled because the forecast gave only a fifteen percent chance of rain, and it then rained on us the entire day. He had recently been auditing a philosophy of science seminar and used the occasion to argue that the forecast had never really been a meaningful statement in the first place. His case rested on what he called the reference class problem, which he traced back to Hans Reichenbach's classic work on the frequency theory of probability. Any single day, he pointed out, can be assigned to indefinitely many different reference classes—all days with a similar pressure system, all days in that month and region historically, all days on which the forecasting model has produced a similar output, and so on—and each of these classes yields a different historical frequency of rain, with nothing in the mathematics itself telling us which class is the right one to use. Since the forecaster's choice of reference class is, on his view, just a convention, the resulting percentage doesn't actually describe anything about that particular day at all.

I didn't have a good rebuttal at the time, and I've more or less come around to his view since. It seems to generalize unsettlingly well: a doctor telling a specific patient they have a seventy percent chance of responding to a treatment, or a pollster giving a candidate a sixty percent chance of winning an election, looks like it rests on exactly the same unprincipled choice of reference class. So I would like to know how far this really goes: should I now treat any single-case probability I encounter—meteorological, medical, or electoral—as, in effect, a dressed-up guess with no real content behind the number, given that the reference class used to generate it could always have been drawn differently?
