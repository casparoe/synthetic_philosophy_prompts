so i've been going down a rabbit hole on al-ghazali's occasionalism, specifically that cotton-and-fire example from the Incoherence of the Philosophers (the "17th discussion" i think its called). basic point i get: fire touching cotton and the cotton burning arent necessarily connected, God just creates the burning right alongside the touching out of habit, and its at least conceivable he could not do that. fine so far, kind of a theological cousin of hume's problem with induction.

two things about it dont sit right with me though, and i think they actually pull in opposite directions. first: if literally every event, including stuff i do with my own body, gets created directly by God moment to moment with zero real causal power in the things themselves, how is there any room left for ME being responsible for what i do? the standard ashari answer seems to be "kasb" (acquisition) — God creates the act, i just "acquire" it — which sounds suspiciously like relabeling the problem rather than solving it. does kasb actually have philosophical teeth, or is it a dodge dressed up in arabic?

second: i've also seen it argued (marmura maybe? he's the guy who translated the edition i read) that ghazali's actual argument in the cotton/fire bit is way more modest than the famous version everyone repeats — that he's only claiming reason alone cant PROVE the connection is necessary, not that there's definitely zero causal power in the fire. that's a pretty different claim from "God does literally everything and created things have no causal oomph of their own."

so which is the "real" ghazali — full occasionalism where secondary causes are just an illusion of habit, or the weaker "you philosophers havent demonstrated necessity" claim — and does kasb make more sense under one of those readings than the other? id love an explanation that actually walks through the structure of the argument instead of just handing me the cotton-fire soundbite again.
