I'm several chapters into a novel set in early sixteenth-century Navadvipa, in the circle of scholars that Vasudeva Sarvabhauma built after he reportedly memorized the entire Nyaya curriculum under Pakshadhara Mishra in Mithila and carried it home to found his own school there. My protagonist is an invented young Naiyayika training in that circle alongside Sarvabhauma's real and far more celebrated student, Raghunatha Shiromani, who by this point is already unsettling his elders with the reinterpretations of Gangesa's logic that will become his Didhiti. I want to give my protagonist a set-piece debate (a vada) with a Mimamsaka pandit visiting from Kashi, and a friend from graduate school who now teaches Sanskrit warned me against letting the scene collapse into two generic sages trading proverbs at each other — so I've been reading around the pramanas, the recognized sources of knowledge, to make sure the disagreement has real teeth.

Here is my working assumption, and I would like you to stress-test it before I build a scene on top of it. As far as I can tell, the question of what counts as a source of knowledge in the first place was settled early and stayed settled across schools: the four pramanas that Gautama lays out at the start of the Nyaya Sutras — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — are common ground that Naiyayikas and Mimamsakas alike simply inherited, going on to build their more distinctive doctrines about the self, ritual injunction, or the Veda's authority on top of that shared foundation. If that's right, the epistemology in my debate scene is really just throat-clearing, and the genuine disagreement — the part with some dramatic heat to it — has to live elsewhere, in metaphysics or theology, rather than in how the two men think knowledge itself gets generated and certified. Is that a defensible way to stage the scene, or would I be flattening a disagreement that was still very much alive at the level of the pramanas themselves?
