Ok so we've been doing the problem of old evidence in my Bayesian epistemology seminar for like two weeks now and I still feel kinda shaky on it, so I wanted to get an outside take before the paper's due. Basic setup as I understand it: if E is old evidence, meaning its already known so P(E) = 1 for us, then conditionalizing on E literally cannot change your credence in H, since P(H|E) = P(H) whenever P(E) = 1. But that seems totally wrong given how science actually works -- e.g. everyone treats the fact that Mercury's perihelion precession (measured decades before 1915) came out exactly right once Einstein worked out GR as huge confirmation of GR, even though the "evidence" wasn't new at all. So the naive Bayesian story of confirmation seems to break down on exactly the cases that are supposed to be its bread and butter.

There are two fixes for this that keep showing up in our readings and I want you to actually compare them instead of just listing them side by side. Fix #1 (I think this one's Garber, and maybe Jeffrey too?) says you shouldn't conditionalize on E at all, but on the fact that H entails E, since real agents aren't logically omniscient and discovering that entailment is itself a genuine, temporally located learning event. Fix #2 is the counterfactual move -- you measure how much E confirms H by asking what your credence in H would be if you were learning E for the first time, stripped of the fact that you already know it (honestly not sure who to credit for this one, it shows up under different names in different readings). Can you lay out what each of these is actually doing to the Bayesian machinery to make room for old evidence, say whether they're secretly the same trick wearing different clothes, and then give me an actual verdict on which one (if either) really rescues Bayesian confirmation theory here rather than just shifting the bump to a different part of the rug? I dont want a "both have their merits" non-answer, I want you to actually pick a side and defend it.
