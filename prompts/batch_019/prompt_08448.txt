I'm working on a feature about whether we owe wild animals anything beyond being left alone, and I want to pressure-test the two positions that keep recurring in that literature before I write about them. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers who have just watched a fox kill a rabbit at the edge of a nature reserve. One defends the "welfare biology" view that the sheer scale of suffering from predation, starvation, disease, and the fact that most individuals of many wild species die within days of being born gives us at least a provisional duty to research interventions in nature, rather than treating the status quo as morally neutral. The other defends a Leopold-style land ethic, on which what has value is the integrity and stability of the ecosystem rather than the welfare of its individual members, so that predation and early death are ordinary features of a healthy system rather than tragedies calling for correction. Have each press the other on the least comfortable consequence of their own view — for the welfare biologist, that consistency seems to demand large-scale management of ecological processes whose effects we can barely predict; for the land ethicist, that indifference to individual suffering looks hard to justify the moment you imagine applying the same standard to humans — rather than letting either retreat to a slogan.
