I'm taking a seminar this semester on the semantics of conditionals, and last week's session centered on the classic puzzle of why indicative and counterfactual conditionals can diverge in truth value even when they look like minimal variants of one another. The standard illustration is the Oswald/Kennedy pair: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" (which nearly everyone accepts) versus "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" (which nearly everyone rejects), despite the two sentences appearing to share the same antecedent and consequent. Afterward, two classmates of mine, Jonah and Sasha, kept arguing about it over coffee, and I mostly just listened, since I could see something to each side and didn't want to nudge the discussion either way.

(1) Jonah opened by saying the pair shows that "if" is ambiguous between two genuinely distinct conditional operators, an indicative one and a counterfactual one, since nothing else could explain how two conditionals sharing an antecedent and consequent diverge in truth value; if "if" had a single meaning, he said, changing the mood alone shouldn't flip the verdict. (2) Sasha answered that this proves too much, because the two conditionals were never being evaluated against the same set of worlds to begin with: on her view, both are handled by one mechanism along the lines of a Ramsey test, where you provisionally add the antecedent and see what follows, but the indicative version is restricted to worlds compatible with what conversational participants already accept (so it inherits everyone's knowledge that Kennedy is dead), while the counterfactual version ranges over worlds merely similar to actuality prior to the antecedent's falsity, with no such restriction. So the divergence is a difference in contextual input, not in the lexical meaning of "if" -- about as informative as noting that "tall" picks out different heights for a building and a toddler. (3) Jonah replied that the analogy undersells the problem: "tall" varies continuously along one dimension, whereas indicative and counterfactual conditionals seem to differ in kind rather than degree -- most tellingly, he said, the indicative conditional doesn't even look like it expresses a stable proposition, since Adams' Thesis holds that its assertability tracks the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, rather than the probability of whatever proposition it's supposed to express, while the counterfactual plainly gets bivalent truth conditions from a Lewis-style similarity ranking of possible worlds. (4) Sasha pushed back that Adams' Thesis is a fact about assertability conditions, not a proof that indicatives lack truth conditions altogether: Stalnaker's own selection-function semantics -- which picks out the closest antecedent-world relative to context -- assigns truth conditions to indicative conditionals too, constrained by the conversational context set, and still recovers most of what Adams noticed, so Jonah, she said, was treating one contested position in an open debate as though it had already settled the matter.

Could you go through Jonah and Sasha's exchange move by move and adjudicate it -- not just hand down an overall winner, but say specifically where each of the four numbered moves gets something right and where it overreaches? I would also be curious whether there is an important consideration neither of them raised that bears on who ultimately has the stronger position.
