I don't have any background in academic philosophy, but over the past year I've been working through some of the classic free-will literature on my own, and I keep coming back to Peter Strawson's essay "Freedom and Resentment." What stuck with me wasn't really his position on determinism itself, but the machinery he builds underneath it: the idea that whether resentment, gratitude, indignation, or guilt are ever the right responses to someone has less to do with whether their action was ultimately determined than with whether we can go on treating them as a participant in ordinary human relationships, rather than switching into what he calls the "objective attitude" -- the stance we take toward small children, the severely deranged, or anyone whose behavior we've decided to manage rather than relate to. He's also careful to separate two very different ways someone can get out from under blame: ordinary excuses, along the lines of "he didn't mean to" or "he was pushed," which leave the person's basic standing as a participant untouched, and deeper exemptions, cases where we in effect say "he isn't the kind of thing reactive attitudes get a grip on," which suspend the whole participant relationship rather than just this one act.

Strawson died in 2006, well before anything like a large language model that apologizes, hedges, and makes and breaks promises in fluent conversational English, or a self-driving car that has to make a split-second decision that ends up getting someone hurt. I've caught myself getting irritated at a customer-service chatbot in a way that felt uncomfortably close to resentment, then feeling a bit silly about it, the way you might feel silly cursing at a vending machine -- except the chatbot is a lot more conversationally convincing than a vending machine ever was. I can't tell whether Strawson's own framework already has the resources to say that reaction is simply a mistake, on a par with resenting a jammed door, or whether it leaves the question more open than that.

Could you work through what Strawson would most plausibly say about this, staying close to the actual structure of his argument rather than jumping straight to a general verdict on "can AI be morally responsible"? I'm especially interested in whether today's AI systems fit naturally into his excuse/exemption distinction or fall outside it altogether, and in whether his claim that we could never, as a practical matter, sustain the objective attitude toward the whole of humanity has a mirror image on the other side: some reason built into his account for why we could never stably sustain the participant attitude toward a machine no matter how humanlike its outputs become, or whether that's just a contingent psychological fact about us that could in principle shift as the technology gets better.
