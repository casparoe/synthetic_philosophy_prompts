Me and my friend got into a whole argument last night about whether he even has the standing to call me a hypocrite over something (long story, not important). It sent me down a rabbit hole reading about the philosophy of "standing to blame," and I noticed that a lot of the writing on this kind of mushes together two different reasons someone might loose their standing to blame you: (1) they're a hypocrite -- guilty of the same thing themselves without ever owning up to it -- versus (2) they were actually involved in or complicit in you doing the wrong thing in the first place (like they set up the conditions, or egged you on, or failed you in some way that contributed to it). Intuitively these feel like separate complaints -- a hypocrite doesn't have to be complicit, and someone complicit doesn't have to be a hypocrite -- but almost every real-life example I can come up with has both going on at once, which makes it hard to tell if they're really doing separate philosophical work or if one just reduces to the other.

Could you help me pull these apart with actual examples? I'm picturing something like two versions of basically the same scenario: one where the blamer is complicit in the wrongdoing but has never claimed to live by the standard they're now invoking (so no hypocrisy, just involvement), and another twist on it where the blamer IS a huge hypocrite about the standard but had literally zero causal role in the other person's wrongdoing (so no involvement, just hypocrisy). If you can construct a pair like that -- or explain why it's actually harder to do than it sounds -- I'd love to have it in my back pocket for round two with my friend.
