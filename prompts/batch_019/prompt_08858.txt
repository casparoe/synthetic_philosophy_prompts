A friend of mine, Naomi, gave a novel five stars and a glowing one-line blurb on Goodreads without having read it: she had only sat in on her book club's discussion, where two other members described the plot in detail and raved about the ending. When this came up at dinner, another friend, Callum, objected. (1) He said that what she'd done wasn't quite dishonest, but it wasn't a real review either, since you cannot come to know that a novel is beautifully written secondhand: its beauty isn't a hidden fact someone can simply hand you, but something that exists only in the actual experience of reading the book. (2) Naomi replied that this can't be right in general, since we accept plenty of secondhand evaluative claims without personally verifying them: nobody thinks you must re-derive a proof yourself before believing a mathematician who assures you a theorem is true, and nobody thinks it dishonest to buy a bottle of wine because it received a 95-point score from a critic you've never met. (3) Callum answered that those cases are different in kind, not just in degree: a proof carries its certainty with it regardless of who checks it, and even a wine score is anchored to a shared, trainable scale, whereas "my book club loved the ending" only reports how three particular people happened to react, and readers' reactions vary too much for that report to settle anything about the book itself. (4) Naomi's comeback was that this only shows aesthetic testimony is less reliable than mathematical or expert testimony, not that it belongs to some different category altogether: eyewitness accounts of a car crash are unreliable and vary between witnesses too, yet nobody concludes that it's impossible in principle to know what happened to the car without having seen the crash yourself.
