Condorcet's most famous result is the jury theorem: majority voting is a great truth-tracker as long as voters are independent and each one is a little better than a coin flip. But he's also the guy who discovered that majority voting over multiple options can cycle even when every individual voter is perfectly rational (his paradox). Theres a related puzzle he never got to see though: the discursive dilemma (aka the doctrinal paradox), where a court votes issue-by-issue on the premises of a case and gets one verdict, then votes directly on the overall conclusion and gets the opposite verdict, even though no single judge holds an inconsistent set of beliefs. If you explained this to him, what do you think Condorcet would say — would he treat it as basically a cousin of his own paradox, which voting procedure (premise-by-premise vs. straight up-or-down on the conclusion) would his own commitments push him toward, and would he try to dissolve it with more probability calculus like he did with the jury theorem, or admit it's a structural issue that math can't fix?
