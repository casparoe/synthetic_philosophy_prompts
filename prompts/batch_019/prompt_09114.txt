A friend and I spent most of a long car ride arguing about Nozick's entitlement theory without reaching any agreement, and the exchange has been nagging at me since. It reminded me of a sharper version of the same dispute that I watched unfold a few months ago between two people in a political philosophy reading group I used to attend, Elena and Marcus, over the Wilt Chamberlain example from Anarchy, State, and Utopia. I jotted down the gist of it afterward, since neither of them conceded a point and I still can't tell who got the better of the exchange. Roughly, it went like this:

(1) Elena argued that the Chamberlain example is close to decisive against any patterned or end-state theory of justice, including Rawls's. Start from any distribution D1 you're willing to call just. Let a million fans each freely hand over an extra 25 cents to watch Chamberlain play, so that he ends up with $250,000 more than anyone else in the resulting distribution, D2. Since every individual transfer was voluntary and wronged no one, D2 must be just too, even though it's unequal — so no pattern can be maintained except by continually interfering with people's free choices, and that interference is itself unjust.

(2) Marcus replied that the argument only goes through if you assume people hold their D1 shares as full liberal property, free to dispose of as they like with no further strings attached — which is exactly what a patterned theorist denies. On a Rawlsian view, what's just is the basic structure as a whole, including a standing scheme of taxation and transfer; nobody is entitled, prior to that scheme, to a "pre-tax" share that a later tax then violates. D1 was never a freestanding entitlement to begin with, so its being just tells you nothing about whether an untaxed D2 would be.

(3) Elena answered that this just relocates the interference rather than removing it. Whether the state takes its cut after the fact or has written the rule into the system in advance, it is still helping itself to part of what fans and Chamberlain agreed to between themselves — and predictability alone can't be what legitimizes a taking, since a rule announced in advance that confiscated everything above subsistence would obviously still be objectionable. This, she said, is exactly the intuition behind Nozick's comparison of taxing income from labor to forcing someone to work.

(4) Marcus answered that the forced-labor comparison proves too much: any legal background condition that helps define what counts as a "voluntary" transaction in the first place — contract law, safety regulation, whatever — would then also count as forced labor, which is absurd. He added that Nozick still owes an account of why D1 counts as just to begin with, and that once you factor in how unjust most real-world original acquisition actually was, the entitlement theory's own principle of rectification might license about as much redistribution as the patterned theories it's supposed to refute.

Neither budged, and I've gone back and forth myself on who's actually ahead at each stage. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
