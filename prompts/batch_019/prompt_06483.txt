I'm reporting a feature on how economists and philosophers end up meaning different things by the word "rational," and the best material I've collected for it so far came out of an argument I only half-expected to have to referee. After a game theory seminar I was sitting in on for the piece, two people I had separately interviewed that same week -- Elena, an experimental economist, and Jonah, a philosopher who works on decision theory -- fell into an increasingly pointed disagreement, over drinks, about the centipede game: the two-player game in which a pot of money grows each round for as long as both players keep "passing"; either player can end things at any point by "taking," which gives the taker more than half of whatever is in the pot at that moment. The textbook backward-induction argument concludes that the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium is for the very first player to take immediately, even though both would do far better by passing for a while -- a mismatch between theory and behavior that has fed a long-running dispute among game theorists and philosophers sometimes called the backward-induction paradox. I wrote the exchange up from memory as soon as I got home, because I wanted to think it through properly, and I'd like your help doing that.

Here is roughly how it went. (1) Elena opened by arguing that the large experimental literature showing most subjects keep passing for several rounds is really just a catalogue of mistakes: backward induction shows that taking immediately is the only rational move, so subjects who don't are simply reasoning badly. (2) Jonah objected that this gets the logic backwards: the whole backward-induction chain only tells a player what to do at a given node on the assumption that every player, at every future node, will reason the same way, but reaching any node beyond the very first already falsifies that assumption for everyone still in the game -- so the theory, by its own lights, has nothing determinate to say about what a rational player should believe or do once that has happened. (3) Elena replied that this confuses explaining a node with acting from it: subgame perfection just requires playing optimally from wherever you find yourself, regardless of the history that got you there, and that a well-known epistemic theorem of Aumann's about games of this kind shows exactly this -- common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome, with no residual puzzle once the epistemics are set up correctly. (4) Jonah countered that Aumann's result earns its conclusion by definition: if "rational" and "knowledge" are built so that a player cannot even coherently entertain the possibility of an opponent deviating, then of course no crisis arises when a deviation occurs, but that dissolves the puzzle by stipulation instead of answering it, which is close to Binmore's published objection to Aumann. (5) At that point Elena granted that the epistemic foundations might be shakier than she had implied, but held that the behavioral prediction survives regardless: subjects who keep passing are not refuting backward induction, they are just revealing that they care about something other than maximizing their own payoff, and that is a fact about preferences, not about reasoning. (6) Jonah's closing point was that this concession quietly gives away the opening claim: if taking immediately is uniquely rational only relative to an assumption about preferences that Elena now admits is false for most people, she is no longer entitled to call the subjects who pass irrational -- at most she can say they want something other than money, which nobody had disputed in the first place.

Could you go through the exchange move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner? For each of the six numbered points, tell me who has the better of the argument -- Elena, Jonah, both, or neither -- and why. Then close with your own view on whether there is a way of resolving the underlying puzzle about backward induction that neither of them reached during the conversation.
