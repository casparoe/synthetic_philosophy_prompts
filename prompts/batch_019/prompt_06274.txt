Here is the student's answer:

(a) The whole-brain standard, which is what U.S. law actually uses, defines death as the irreversible loss of function of the entire brain, including the brain stem, whereas the higher-brain standard holds that death occurs once a person irreversibly loses the capacity for consciousness, even if brainstem-mediated functions like breathing continue on their own. Veatch defended the higher-brain view because he thought what matters morally is the loss of personhood, not the loss of biological integration. The difference is clearest in a case like Terri Schiavo's: by 2005, when the fight over her feeding tube reached its peak, she had satisfied the whole-brain criteria, since her diagnosis of fifteen years in a persistent vegetative state meant her higher cortical function was permanently gone. She could not be declared legally dead, however, because her brainstem was still driving her breathing, which is exactly the gap the higher-brain standard is meant to close.

(b) Shewmon's cases are meant to undermine the "integrationist" rationale for the whole-brain standard, associated with Bernat's foundational work on the definition of death, which holds that the brain is what integrates the body's separate organs and systems into a single functioning organism, so that once the brain is gone the body should fall apart into a mere aggregate of parts rather than continue functioning as a whole. Shewmon pointed out that some whole-brain-dead bodies, maintained on ventilators, went on to heal wounds, resist infection, and even grow for months or years, which looks like exactly the kind of integrated functioning the rationale says shouldn't be possible once the brain is gone. The President's Council on Bioethics took this seriously enough that its 2008 white paper gave up defending the original integrationist story and fell back on the higher-brain view instead, redefining what is lost at brain death as the capacity for conscious engagement with the world rather than bodily integration as such. That let the Council go on calling Shewmon's chronically-maintained patients dead without having to explain their continued integration.
