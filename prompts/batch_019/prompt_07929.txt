For my philosophy of probability seminar (cross-listed with the stats department), we've been going through the standard trio of interpretations of probability — frequentism, subjectivism, and propensity theories — and this week's discussion section basically turned into a two-person cage match between Priya and Marcus over the driest possible example: what a weather forecaster means when she says "there's a 70% chance of rain tomorrow." I wrote down roughly how it went because our TA's response was just "eh, you're both kind of right," which honestly didnt tell me anything.

Marcus opened by saying the 70% can't really be about tomorrow specifically, since tomorrow will either rain or not — there's no fact of the matter about "70%-rain" for one unique day. What the forecaster must actually mean, if she means anything precise, is that on about 70% of days that looked atmospherically like this one, it rained: a frequency claim about a class of days, not a claim about tomorrow itself. Priya shot back that tomorrow belongs to a ton of different classes at once — days with this pressure pattern, days in this month, days after a dry spell, "the only tomorrow there will ever be" — and frequentism has no non-arbitrary way to pick which class is the right one, even though the 70% is supposed to be a fact about tomorrow specifically. What the forecaster's really reporting, she said, is a rational degree of belief, and it's a good one exactly to the extent it comes from a reliable, well-calibrated process; the historical frequencies are just evidence feeding that belief, not a hidden definition of what the belief secretly means. Marcus admitted the reference-class point stings, but said subjectivism buys its way out too cheap: if probability is just whatever degree of belief someone happens to hold, two equally careful forecasters could assign 70% and 40% to the same tomorrow and neither would be technically wrong, which seems crazy — there's got to be some actual fact about how likely rain is that one of them is tracking better. And that, he said, is just an objective single-case propensity by another name, sitting in the atmosphere whether or not anyone ever tabulates a record of similar days.

Priya replied that "propensity" doesn't rescue him, it just renames the mystery: propensity-talk earns its keep for something like a radioactive atom's decay chance, where there's a clean repeatable setup behind it, but nobody can define, measure, or individuate "the propensity of this exact, unrepeatable atmosphere to produce rain" without secretly falling back on looking at a bunch of similar setups and their frequencies — so the propensity move smuggles frequentism back in right where it was supposed to save him from it. Subjectivism, she said, never needed a hidden objective single-case fact to exist at all, just credences that are coherent and answerable to evidence.
