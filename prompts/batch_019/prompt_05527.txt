I spent almost 35 years running an inorganic chemistry lab before I retired, and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group a friend of mine organizes out of the local library - a bunch of retirees, nobody with any formal training, just enthusiastic amateurs working through the great books one at a time. We're currently on Pascal's Pensees (the old Trotter translation, for what its worth), and this week we landed on a fragment in the section he titles "Justice and the Reason of Effects" - numbered 298 in our copy, though apparently the numbering is completely different in other editions, which strikes me as a very unscientific way to catalogue a book. Anyway, here's the bit that's been stuck in my head since Tuesday:

"Justice, might. It is just that what is just should be followed, it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

Half the group shrugged this off as bog-standard "might makes right" cynicism and moved on to the next fragment, but that last line keeps nagging at me - "unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just" doesn't read like Pascal approving of the arrangement so much as him wincing at it through clenched teeth. What really confuses me is the section heading. I've got a half-memory (could easily be wrong, my copy's index is useless) of Pascal doing a three-step version of this same move somewhere else in the book, with nobility or custom instead of justice - ordinary people believe in the thing for a naive reason, a "half-clever" sort sees through the naivety and gets cynical about it, and then a third, genuinely wise type ends up back at something close to the ordinary people's position, but for entirely different reasons than the ordinary people have. Is that the "reason of effects" this section title is refering to, and if so, where does the justice/might fragment sit on that ladder - is it stuck at the disillusioned middle stage, or is Pascal already gesturing at the third, wiser turn without quite spelling it out? And is his Jansenism actually doing any load-bearing work here (fallen human nature being incapable of real justice without grace, or whatever) - or could any garden-variety cynic about power have written this exact paragraph without believing a word of Pascal's theology?
