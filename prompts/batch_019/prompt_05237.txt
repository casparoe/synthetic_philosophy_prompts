I'm preparing a session on definitions of art for my aesthetics seminar, and before we turn to the institutional and historical theories, I want students to sit with Kant's account of genius as a foil. I keep getting tangled in one move he makes, though, and would like help thinking it through. The passage is from the Critique of Judgement, §§46–47 (trans. J. H. Bernard, 1892):

"Genius is the talent (or natural gift) which gives the rule to Art. Since talent, as the innate productive faculty of the artist, belongs itself to Nature, we may express the matter thus: Genius is the innate mental disposition (ingenium) through which Nature gives the rule to Art.

[...] For every art presupposes rules by means of which in the first instance a product, if it is to be called artistic, is represented as possible. But the concept of beautiful art does not permit the judgement upon the beauty of a product to be derived from any rule, which has a concept as its determining ground, and therefore has at its basis a concept of the way in which the product is possible. Therefore, beautiful art cannot itself devise the rule according to which it can bring about its product. But since at the same time a product can never be called Art without some precedent rule, Nature in the subject must (by the harmony of its faculties) give the rule to Art; i.e. beautiful Art is only possible as a product of Genius.

We thus see (1) that genius is a talent for producing that for which no definite rule can be given; it is not a mere aptitude for what can be learnt by a rule. [...] (3) It cannot describe or indicate scientifically how it brings about its products, but it gives the rule just as nature does.

[...] Thus we can readily learn all that Newton has set forth in his immortal work on the Principles of Natural Philosophy, however great a head was required to discover it; but we cannot learn to write spirited poetry, however express may be the precepts of the art and however excellent its models. The reason is that Newton could make all his steps, from the first elements of geometry to his own great and profound discoveries, intuitively plain and definite as regards consequence, not only to himself but to every one else. But a Homer or a Wieland cannot show how his Ideas, so rich in fancy and yet so full of thought, come together in his head, simply because he does not know and therefore cannot teach others."

Two things about this puzzle me. First, Kant seems to place two demands on the "rule" that genius supposedly gives to art, and they look to be in tension: fine art must proceed from a rule, on pain of not counting as art at all, yet that rule cannot be determinate or statable, on pain of collapsing into mere science or teachable craft — which is exactly what the Newton/Homer contrast is meant to show. Is this a genuine equivocation on "rule," or is there one coherent notion doing both jobs? Second, reading the passage strictly on its own terms rather than through a later Romantic gloss: does Kant's criterion of unteachability commit him to denying, in principle, that anything produced by an explicitly rule-following process — including a statistical model trained to imitate patterns in a corpus of prior works — could ever count as fine art? Or would that be overreading a point Kant means only to distinguish the pedagogy of poetry from that of geometry?
