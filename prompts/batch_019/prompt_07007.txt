Since retiring from a career in observational astronomy, I have been auditing an undergraduate History and Philosophy of Science seminar, and the instructor -- an old colleague -- has asked me to help mark a batch of short take-home responses. I would rather form an independent view before comparing notes with him, so I would value your read on the following.

The question set to students was: "In the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615), Galileo argues that apparent conflicts between the Copernican system and scripture should be resolved by reinterpreting the relevant scriptural passages rather than by rejecting the astronomical evidence. Reconstruct the main line of argument he gives for this conclusion, and explain precisely how his position differs from the one Cardinal Bellarmine set out in his own 1615 letter to Foscarini." (20 points.)

Here is one student's answer:

"Galileo's argument rests on the premise that Scripture and the book of nature have the same author, so, rightly understood, they cannot truly contradict one another. Because natural facts do not change to suit us, while the wording of Scripture is frequently adapted to the capacities of ordinary and unlettered people, he concludes that whenever the two seem to clash it is the scriptural passage that stands in need of reinterpretation, not the astronomical claim. In support of this he leans heavily on Augustine's De Genesi ad Litteram, which cautions Christians against rashly tying articles of faith to particular claims about the natural world, since a later demonstration to the contrary would only expose Scripture itself to mockery. Galileo takes this Augustinian caution to entail that, on questions of natural fact with no bearing on salvation, biblical exegesis carries no special authority, and that once a physical claim is adequately supported by observation and reasoning, passages such as Joshua's command that the sun stand still must be read figuratively rather than literally.

Bellarmine's letter to Foscarini agrees with this much: he too concedes that if the earth's motion were ever really demonstrated, theologians would have to go back and reinterpret the relevant passages. Where the two men differ, on close inspection, is mostly a matter of how much evidence is enough, rather than a disagreement in principle. Bellarmine wants the Copernican system treated, in the meantime, as a convenient mathematical device for saving the appearances, to be asserted as physically true only once a strict demonstration is available; Galileo, who did not yet have such a demonstration either, is really asking for the bar to be lowered rather than removed, arguing that the accumulated telescopic and physical evidence already meets Bellarmine's own standard of proof, so that Scripture should be reinterpreted now rather than at some future date. On this reading, the dispute between the two men is fundamentally evidential, rather than being, as it is sometimes presented, a disagreement about whether theology has any business pronouncing on questions of natural fact in the first place. The letter itself circulated only in manuscript for some time before eventually being condemned in its own right alongside Foscarini's book in the Index decree of 1616."
