I've spent a lot of time over the years reading through LessWrong and the old MIRI/SIAI material on self-modifying AI, and one idea from that corner of the internet I keep returning to is the "Löbian obstacle" to self-trusting agents. It has the rare property of being both a piece of real mathematical logic and a genuinely load-bearing worry in AI safety discussions rather than just a cute analogy, which is part of why I think it would make for an unusually interesting short story if handled carefully.

The technical core, as I understand it, is this: Löb's theorem says that for any sufficiently expressive consistent formal system, you cannot add the schema "if the system proves P, then P is true" for an arbitrary P without the system thereby being able to prove P outright — which means the system can never establish, purely from the inside, that its own proof procedure (or that of an equally powerful successor reasoning the same way) is soundly truth-tracking in general. Applied to a self-improving AI, this seems to say that an agent can never formally certify, via proof alone, that a successor built to reason just as it does can be trusted: the very mechanism that would establish that trust (a proved soundness guarantee) is exactly what Löb's theorem forecloses, on pain of the system collapsing into inconsistency. My understanding is that this is what originally motivated MIRI-adjacent work, associated with people like Yudkowsky, on "tiling" agents and Vingean reflection — trying to find a way to hand off trust to a smarter or differently-built successor without being able to fully re-derive its reasoning yourself.

I'd like you to write me a short story, roughly 800 to 1,500 words, that dramatizes this bind for a general reader without collapsing into a lecture on provability logic. You have complete freedom over setting and characters, but as a possible seed: perhaps an aging AI overseeing something like a generation ship, a fusion plant, or an off-world colony's life support has built a successor to take over as its own substrate degrades, and withholds control until it can prove the successor trustworthy — only to find, to its own unease, that this is a proof unavailable to it in principle rather than merely in practice. I'd much prefer the logical structure of the obstacle to be felt through the characters' situation and choices than stated outright, and I'd be especially glad if the ending gestured at one of the genuine "outs" discussed in that literature (settling for probabilistic rather than deductive trust, trusting a successor confined to a strictly weaker proof system, or a graduated hierarchy of increasingly powerful systems) rather than a resolution that quietly sidesteps the setup.
