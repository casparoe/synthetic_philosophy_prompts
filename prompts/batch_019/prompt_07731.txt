Had a genuinely heated argument with my friend Jae last night (like, whiteboard-in-the-kitchen heated) about whether money-pump arguments actually prove anything. Her position: if you prefer apples to bananas, bananas to carrots, and carrots to apples, you're just irrational, period, because I can walk up to you holding carrots and trade you into bananas for a penny, then bananas into apples for a penny, then apples back into carrots for a penny, and now you've paid three cents to end up exactly where you started — and I can keep doing that to you forever until you're broke. She thinks this shows transitivity (or at least acyclicity) of preference is a non-negotiable requirement of rationality, no exceptions.

I pushed back with something I half remember from a decision theory blog/class a while back: that there's a whole naive vs. sophisticated vs. resolute chooser distinction in dynamic choice theory, and a resolute chooser could look at the entire sequence of trades ahead of time, recognize it's a pump, and just refuse to play along past a certain point (or never start), even while still ranking the options cyclically if you asked about any single pair in isolation. Jae's first comeback was that this is cheating: if you won't actually make a trade you supposedly prefer, you don't really have that preference, so I'm just smuggling transitivity back in through the agent's actual choices rather then their stated rankings. Her second comeback, later, was that the whole resolute-choice move just relabels a diachronic requirement (be consistent across a sequence of choices over time) as though it settles a synchronic one (have a consistent ranking at an instant), and neither of us could tell if that's a real objection or just a fancier version of her first one. We argued about this for like an hour and got nowhere.

So can you build out the strongest version of my side of this? I want an argument that the classic sequential-exchange money pump does NOT establish that a rational agent needs transitive (or acyclic) preferences over outcomes, with the resolute-chooser move (or some better response, if one exists) doing the actual work. Walk through the pump step by step, pin down exactly where the "must be transitive or get exploited" inference breaks down, and engage seriously with both of Jae's objections instead of brushing past them. If the resolute chooser is quietly paying some cost that a normal expected-utility maximizer doesn't have to pay, I want to know that too — I'm not looking for a free win, I'm looking for the actual shape of the disagreement.
