I'm working on a piece about why instrumental music seems to move us so much more directly than the other arts, and I've been reading Schopenhauer's discussion of music in The World as Will and Representation. Near the end of Book Three, in section 52, after arguing that music, unlike the other arts, isn't a copy of the Platonic Ideas at all but a direct copy of "the will itself," he tries to cash this out with a more specific structural claim:

"In the whole of the ripieno parts that make up the harmony, between the bass and the leading voice that sings the melody, I recognize the whole series of the gradations by which the will objectifies itself. Those nearer the bass are the lowest of these grades: the still inorganic mass of unformed nature, moving slowly and in wide intervals, on which everything else rests and out of which everything else develops. The middle voices answer to the plant and animal kingdoms. Only in the high, singing, principal voice, leading the whole and moving with unrestrained freedom in one unbroken, significant train of thought from beginning to end, do I recognize the highest grade of the will's objectification: the intellectual life and endeavor of man."

I can follow the broader argument that music, for Schopenhauer, stands closer to reality than painting or poetry do. What I can't work out is what this four-part-harmony analogy is actually supposed to establish. Is he making a real, discoverable claim about a correspondence between musical structure and the hierarchy of nature, the kind of claim that could turn out to be right or wrong, or is it more of an illustrative metaphor not meant to bear much philosophical weight? And if it's the former, what would even count as evidence against it?
