Something that happened in my grad seminar on virtue ethics last week has been bugging me enough that I want a second opinion. We'd just finished the situationism unit -- Milgram, the bystander studies, Doris and Harman using all of it against virtue ethics -- and two of my students, Owen and Fatima, ended up arguing about it for something like forty minutes after everyone else left for the pub. I wasn't taking notes, obviously, but I remember the shape of it well enough, and honestly, neither of them is dumb -- I cant tell you who's more right.

(1) Owen opened with the hard situationist line: if whether a seminary student stops to help someone slumped in a doorway is predicted almost entirely by whether he's running late -- and barely at all by his stated religious motivations, or even by the fact that he's literally on his way to give a talk on the Good Samaritan parable -- and if finding a stray dime in a payphone coin return makes you way more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers a minute later, then whatever's driving "helping behavior" here isn't anything we should dignify with the word "compassion," it's closer to noise. So the cross-situationally stable, compassionate-person-shaped character that virtue ethics needs as its unit of analysis just doesn't show up in the data for ordinary people. (2) Fatima said this proves less than Owen thinks, because Aristotle never claimed random undergrads or seminarians *have* that kind of stable character to begin with -- full virtue, on his view, is a rare achievement of the phronimos after years of habituation, and most people are merely continent, incontinent, or just average, so finding that random subjects get pushed around by whether they're in a hurry is exactly what he'd predict. It would only be bad news if people independently known to be unusually honest or kind turned out to be just as hurry-sensitive as everyone else. (3) Owen replied that this move saves virtue ethics only by making it unfalsifiable: any time behavior tracks the situational variable instead of the supposed trait, you get to say "well, they weren't virtuous," and the only evidence for who counts as virtuous is... their behavior. That's not a theory anymore, it's a filter for explaining away counterexamples after the fact. (4) Fatima pushed back that it isn't unfalsifiable even in principle -- you could take people independently and repeatedly nominated by others as unusually honest, then check whether their behavior is any more cross-situationally stable than average, which is basically the spirit of what the old Hartshorne and May honesty studies were trying to get at. And she pointed out that the correlations those studies found, which Harman leans on so heavily, weren't zero -- something like .3, if she's remembering right -- so turning "modest but real consistency" into "no such thing as character" is itself overselling what the numbers show. (5) Owen granted the correlation isn't literally zero, but said .3 is nowhere near what ordinary talk of "an honest person" is supposed to buy you, and that they were both dodging the harder point anyway: being more likely to help right after a dime cheers you up, or less likely when you're in a rush, isn't the absence of character, it's a pretty direct description of what most people's character actually is -- mood-driven and hurry-sensitive -- which might mean the studies don't refute virtue ethics so much as force it to admit that almost nobody starts out with a virtuous character, and that the "aspiration" part of the theory is carrying alot more weight than it usually gets credit for.

They more or less left it at a draw and cornered me in my office the next day wanting a ruling, which I dodged. So now I'm passing the buck to you instead. Can you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each, whether it actually lands, whether it's talking past the other person's point, or whether it's right but for the wrong reason? And is there some synthesis view hiding underneath all this that neither Owen nor Fatima quite managed to state -- if you had to lean one way overall, which way, and why?
