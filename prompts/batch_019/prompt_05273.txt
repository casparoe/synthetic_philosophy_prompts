I sit on the three-person craft-and-conduct committee of an invitation-only workshop for novelists I've belonged to for a decade. Across three novels-in-progress by the same member, every desirable love interest is thin, white, and conventionally attractive, while characters who are fat, disabled, or people of color stay comic or minor; several members now want the pattern itself discussed, not each book taken in isolation. The author says her characters' desires are simply hers, that nobody owes attraction to any body, and that calling this a "pattern" merely polices her taste as craft notes; she cites the standard reply in online dating debates, that no one owes anyone attraction, as proof the objection fails. Others answer that a pattern spanning an entire body of work is a public claim about who counts as desirable, not a private taste, and is exactly what craft critique is for. Our guidelines commit facilitators to feedback on "the manuscript on the page," not the author's psychology or politics, without saying where that line falls. Please draft the committee's short written guidance for Thursday's session: a concrete recommendation, a brief standing policy for future cases like it, and a genuine answer to the dating-preference analogy rather than a restatement of "craft, not politics."
