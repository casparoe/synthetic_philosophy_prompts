Over the past year I've been working through the history of utilitarianism on my own, mostly in the evenings, without any formal background in philosophy beyond what I've picked up from books and the odd recorded lecture. Partly because of that, I recently started attending a small non-credit evening class at my local community college where we go through primary texts together, and the instructor has us take turns grading each other's short written responses before she adds her own mark, on the theory that this forces us to read more carefully than we would if we were just nodding along. This week I've been handed a classmate's answer on Bentham's unpublished writing on sexual morality, a topic I only knew existed because I'd come across a stray reference to it while reading about the origins of utilitarian ethics, and I'd like a second opinion on my grading before I hand it in, since I don't really have the training to trust my own judgment here.

The question set was: "Bentham devoted a substantial unpublished manuscript, usually referred to as 'Offences Against One's Self,' to arguing that the law had no principled basis for punishing the 'unnatural' consensual sexual acts of his day. Drawing on the account of the principle of utility, and of the principles Bentham treats as rivals to it, that he sets out in the early chapters of An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, reconstruct the argument by which he reaches this conclusion. Your answer should explain (a) what Bentham thinks is really doing the work in the ordinary condemnation of such acts, (b) how he answers the objection that such acts are harmful because they are non-procreative, and (c) how his view of the nature of pleasure bears on whether the pleasure such acts produce can be weighed in the calculus on the same footing as any other pleasure."

My classmate's answer, copied out exactly as submitted, was: "Bentham's target is what he calls, in the second chapter of the Introduction, the principle of sympathy and antipathy: the habit of treating one's own approval or disapproval of an act as though it were self-evidently a reason to praise or punish it, without asking whether the act actually makes anyone worse off. He thinks this is exactly what is happening when the law punishes consenting adults for 'unnatural' acts: no one can point to a victim, so the whole weight of the prohibition rests on the disgust of onlookers, dressed up as morality or as the law of nature. Because Bentham insists that a mere preference or distaste can only be turned into law if it is backed by some further, independent showing of harm, and disgust supplies no such showing on its own, it cannot count as a genuine reason within the calculus.

On the charge that such acts are harmful because they cannot produce children, Bentham's reply is essentially a consistency argument: intercourse with a partner who is pregnant, infertile, or past childbearing age is just as 'unproductive,' and yet was never punished with anything like the same severity, so procreation cannot really be the state's actual concern here, whatever is officially claimed.

Finally, because Bentham, following the principle of asceticism that he also describes in that chapter, ranks the pleasures of the intellect above those of the body, he can allow that the bodily pleasure at stake in these acts is a comparatively minor good without having to concede that it is no good at all: a lower-ranked pleasure denied is still a pleasure denied, and a real cost to be entered on the other side of the ledger from whatever benefit the law is meant to secure. Once that cost is entered against a punishment that was, historically, death, the law comes out badly wrong by its own professed standard of utility."
