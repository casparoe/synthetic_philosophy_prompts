Ok, this has been stuck in my head since last week. I was interviewing two people for a piece on those consumer DNA health-risk calculators, and afterward they got into an arguement about it instead of giving me anything I could use.

Owen's report had bumped up his heart-attack risk because he'd entered that his dad had one at 58; he figures that's obviously right, since a plain age-and-sex baseline throws out something true and specific about him. Farah, who's a biostatistician, said fine, but why stop at his dad's heart attack and not his zip code, job stress, cholesterol too — slice it finely enough and your "reference group" is four guys, and what comes out the other end is just noise wearing a lab coat. Owen said that cant be right in general, since doctors narrow things exactly like this all the time and it works — a BRCA test beats a generic breast-cancer baseline, and nobody calls that fake precision. Farah answered that "works" is doing all the question-begging here — you only know a family history counts as real signal because someone tested it on a big group first, and that group's boundaries (which patients, which country) were just as arbitrary as anything he's proposing; her own "safe" national baseline is no more principled at its edges, she admitted, but it's actually been checked against outcomes and his four-guy group hasn't, so "equally arbitrary" isn't "equally justified."

Nobody won, dinner just moved on. Can you go through this move by move and say who's actually right at each step — not just who wins overall — and what both of them are missing?
