I'm researching a piece on Newcomb's Problem — the puzzle where a near-infallible predictor places a million dollars in an opaque box only if it foresees you taking that box alone, and you must decide whether to take just that box or take it together with a second, visible box holding a further thousand dollars — and a footnote has been nagging at me. David Lewis, one of the architects of causal decision theory (which recommends two-boxing), named his own metaphysics "Humean supervenience" in explicit homage to Hume's denial of necessary connections between distinct events. Yet causal decision theory's case for two-boxing depends on treating "causing the money to be there" as different in kind from "being correlated with the money being there" — a distinction that looks precarious if causation really is nothing but constant conjunction, as Hume argued. There's a second complication: Hume's essay "Of Miracles" holds that no testimony should move us unless its falsehood would be stranger than the event it reports, which bears on whether he'd even grant the premise of a predictor who is right almost every time. Taking Hume's actual arguments about causation and testimony seriously, rather than his reputation as a generic empiricist, would he one-box, two-box, or reject the dilemma altogether — and why?
