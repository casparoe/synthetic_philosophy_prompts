I spent thirty-odd years doing fieldwork in population genetics before I retired, and these days, partly because a philosophy of science course I audited at the community college got its hooks in me, I sit on our county school board's curriculum advisory commitee. We're up for a vote next month on revisions to the high school biology standards, and a parents' group submitted a formal comment that's been bugging me more than I expected. They're not creationists exactly, and they're not asking for equal time for intelligent design or anything like that. What they want is a sentence added to the standards saying, roughly, that students should be made aware the mechanism of natural selection has been questioned as unfalsifiable by philosophers of science, Karl Popper by name. A couple of the other committee members want to just laugh this off, but I went and did some reading, and it turns out Popper really did say something along these lines at one point -- called it close to a tautology, more of a metaphysical framework than a testable theory, if I'm getting the gist right -- and then, later in life, seems to have walked at least part of that back in some lecture tied to Darwin College. I dont fully trust my own grip on the details of what exactly he retracted versus what he still stood by, and I'd bet nobody else on the committee does either.

Either way I have to bring a written recommendation to the meeting, and I don't want it to just say "Popper was wrong, evolution is settled, next item," because that dodges the actual point being raised, and a couple of these parents are genuinely sharp and will notice a dodge when they see one. Could you draft that memo for me? It needs to land on a specific recommendation about the wording -- leave the standard as is, reject the addition outright, or amend it some other way -- but the reasoning has to actually engage with what was real and defensible in the Popper objection versus what doesn't hold up, rather than just asserting the conclusion and hoping that's enough.
