I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student, and in my current seminar on self-knowledge we spent last week's session on Chapter 4 of Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits -- the anti-luminosity argument and its bearing on the KK principle. I followed the formal skeleton well enough: stipulate a long, finely-grained series of instants across which a subject's felt temperature shifts smoothly from clearly-cold to clearly-not-cold; invoke a margin-for-error premise to the effect that knowing you're cold at one instant requires that you still be cold at the next (since adjacent instants are, by construction, indiscriminable); then run the induction through to the endpoint, where it's stipulated that the subject isn't cold, to get a contradiction. What I didn't come away with is a real gut-level sense of why this is more than a sorites paradox dressed up in epistemological language. One person in my cohort pushed exactly this line -- that the whole argument just trades on the vagueness of "feels cold," and that you could run the same trick on any predicate with borderline cases -- and while our professor was adamant that this misses what's distinctive about Williamson's argument, we ran out of time before she could really spell out the disanalogy.

Could you help me get a firmer intuitive handle on two things? First, why is the margin-for-error/safety premise doing genuine independent epistemological work, such that the argument would still go through even for a perfectly precise (non-vague) condition -- i.e., why isn't this just a sorites argument that happens to be dressed up in talk of "positions to know"? Second, I'd like to understand more precisely how the general anti-luminosity conclusion is supposed to transfer onto the specific case of "knowing that P," so that we end up with a genuine counterexample to the KK principle (rather than a weaker and less surprising claim that introspection is somewhat unreliable). This matters for a side project of mine relating the KK principle to the positive-introspection axiom in epistemic logic, so I'd especially appreciate it if you could flag any place where you think a committed KK-defender has the best shot at resisting the argument.
