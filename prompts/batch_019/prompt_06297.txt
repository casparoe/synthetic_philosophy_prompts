I'm working on a novel featuring a contemporary Daoist scholar who is invited to a cryonics conference to comment on the practice of freezing the newly dead in the hope of eventual biological revival, and I want her remarks to draw on Zhuangzi with some real precision rather than resorting to generic "embrace impermanence" sentiment. He left quite specific material bearing on exactly this tension: at his wife's death, he stops mourning once he reasons that her dying is simply another turn in the same cycle as the four seasons; in the parable of Master Lai, someone who insists on remaining human forever is likened to a piece of metal on the forge that cries out, insisting it be cast into nothing but the great sword Moye, which the smith would take as a very bad omen; and on his own deathbed he refuses an elaborate funeral, telling his disciples that heaven and earth will serve perfectly well as his coffin. Reasoning specifically from these three episodes rather than from loose talk of Daoist detachment, how do you think Zhuangzi would actually have judged cryonic preservation: one more turn of the great transformation, to be accepted without complaint, or precisely the anxious insistence on a fixed shape that his own parable treats as a bad omen? Give me two or three tight paragraphs I could plausibly adapt into her remarks, and don't smooth over the places where his philosophy seems to pull in both directions.
