I've started building an agent framework in which a controller is only allowed to deploy an upgraded successor once it has produced, within a fixed formal system, a proof that the successor will only ever execute actions it has itself verified as safe. Looking into why this kind of scheme is considered fundamentally hard to formalize, I kept running into the "Löbian obstacle" from the tiling-agents literature: the claim that Löb's theorem -- if a theory proves "Provable(P) → P" for some statement P, it already proves P outright -- makes the whole certification scheme collapse before it gets off the ground. I can follow the syntax of the proof, but I want the gut-level mechanism, not a restatement that "Gödel says machines can't verify themselves." Concretely: why does insisting on a general guarantee that "whatever my successor proves is true" force the controller to already possess a proof of the target statement, rather than merely licensing it to defer to the successor's future proof? And why doesn't the obvious patch -- having the successor reason in a strictly weaker formal system than the controller's -- make the obstacle disappear? An intuitive walkthrough of exactly where the self-reference bites would be more useful to me than the formal derivation itself.
