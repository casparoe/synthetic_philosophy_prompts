I'm a novelist, and I sit on the jury for a national prize in literary nonfiction. This year's shortlist has split us down the middle. One finalist is a memoir describing the author's cardiac arrest on the operating table, during which she says she left her body and afterward reported, accurately and in specific detail, a corridor conversation among staff who assumed she was unconscious -- an episode her publisher promotes not as devotional flourish but as verified, medically inexplicable fact. Half the jury wants to judge the book like any other memoir, on craft and sincerity alone; the other half, noting that our rules require entrants to offer "a truthful account of the author's experience," wants to apply Hume's test from "Of Miracles" directly: no such report should be believed unless it would be more extraordinary for the testimony to be mistaken or embellished than for the experience to have actually happened as described. A third juror calls this a category error, since Hume was telling us what to believe, not what shelf to put a book on, and worries we are dressing up a literary or theological judgment as epistemology. Draft the jury's short decision memo ahead of Friday's final vote: recommend that the book remain an eligible nonfiction finalist, be recategorized or annotated, or be ruled ineligible, and give reasoning the author could actually answer, including a fair statement of the strongest case against whatever you recommend.
