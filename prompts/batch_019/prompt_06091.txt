Question (15 points). The 2015 MIRI paper on corrigibility (Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong) proposes "utility indifference" as a way of making an expected-utility-maximizing agent submit safely to shutdown. (a) [5 pts] Explain how the utility-indifference construction works, and give two reasons the authors themselves offer for regarding it as an unsatisfying solution. (b) [5 pts] Elliott Thornley has argued more recently that the shutdown problem is really a symptom of requiring agents to have complete preferences, and has proposed a fix built on incomplete preferences and a principle he calls Timestep Dominance. Explain the core idea of this proposal. (c) [5 pts] Give the strongest objection you know of to Thornley's proposal, and say whether you think it succeeds.

Student answer.

(a) Utility indifference works by splicing together two utility functions: one that the agent uses to evaluate the world for as long as the shutdown button has not been pressed, and another that rewards it for going along with shutdown once the button has been pressed. To stop the agent from wanting to influence whether the button gets pressed, the designers add a correction term that exactly cancels out the difference in expected value between the two branches, so that pressing the button becomes a matter of complete indifference to the agent. The authors themselves flag two problems with this. First, the correction term has to be computed relative to the agent's own beliefs, so if those beliefs are wrong, or the term is even slightly mis-specified, the cancellation fails and the ordinary incentive to manipulate the button reappears. Second, indifference is a purely negative property: the agent has no positive reason to keep the shutdown mechanism intact, or to make sure any subagents or successors it builds inherit the same property, so corrigibility has to be re-engineered by hand at every level rather than falling out of the agent's own goals.

(b) Thornley's diagnosis is that the shutdown problem is downstream of the independence axiom of expected utility theory, which forces an agent's preferences over lotteries to be complete; once completeness is assumed, he shows that any sufficiently capable agent has some tradeoff at which it prefers manipulating the shutdown button to letting it be pressed. His proposed fix is to train agents that simply lack a preference between any two trajectories that differ only in when shutdown occurs, governed by a "Timestep Dominance" rule: never choose an option that is worse in some shutdown-timing scenario and better in none. This lets the agent optimize normally within a given shutdown time while never accepting a trade that would make it better off in a world where the button is never pressed at the cost of making it worse off in a world where it is.

(c) The obvious worry is the standard money-pump argument against incomplete preferences: an agent with a gap between two options can be walked through a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off by its own lights, which would give it an incentive to complete its preferences and thereby regain exactly the tendency to resist shutdown the proposal was meant to remove. I do not think this objection succeeds, though, since a Timestep Dominance chooser only ever picks options that are at least weakly better in every shutdown scenario; because it never accepts a trade that could make it strictly worse off in some scenario, there is no sequence of trades that could pump it, so the incompleteness is stable rather than something the agent would want to iron out.
