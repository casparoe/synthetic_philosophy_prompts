I've spent a good part of this year reading about Galileo on my own time, mostly because the standard "lone rational scientist versus superstitious church" story never quite satisfied me, and I wanted to know what he actually argued rather than the simplified version most of us grew up with. Once I worked through his 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, I realized his position was far more careful than I'd expected: he wasn't claiming scripture is mistaken, only that its purpose is to teach salvation rather than astronomy, that scripture's human interpreters can err even though its divine author cannot, and that a passage should only be reread non-literally once some claim about nature has been strictly and necessarily demonstrated, since mere probability isn't enough to justify reinterpreting the text. He even warned that rushing to defend a literal reading against a genuine demonstration would eventually embarrass the faith in the eyes of people who understand the natural world.

That distinction has stayed with me because of an ongoing disagreement with a relative of mine, a committed Young Earth Creationist who holds that the universe is roughly six thousand years old and that radiometric dating, geology, and cosmology must be mistaken wherever they conflict with a literal reading of Genesis. It struck me that this is structurally the same dispute Galileo was in, except the two sides have essentially swapped places: he was the one insisting his own science had crossed the threshold of "necessary demonstration" while the Church wasn't yet convinced, whereas today it's mainstream science that has that kind of evidence in abundance, and my relative is the one holding the line on a literal text against it.

So I'd like your help thinking through what Galileo himself, reasoning from his own stated principles rather than his popular reputation as a science-versus-religion martyr, would actually say to someone like my relative. Would he treat modern evolutionary biology and cosmology as having met his bar of "necessary demonstration," obligating a non-literal reading of Genesis in the same way he thought Copernican astronomy obligated one for passages like the sun standing still for Joshua? Or is there something else in his actual position, such as his deference to the Church's final authority over doctrine, the caution he adopted only after his own trial, or some distinction he might draw between physical claims and historical or moral ones in scripture, that would make him a less reliable ally to my side of this argument than I initially assumed?
