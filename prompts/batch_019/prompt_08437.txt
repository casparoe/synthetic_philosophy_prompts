My mother died this past spring, and while going through her books I found her copy of Gödel's published correspondence, worn open to the letters he wrote his own mother about the afterlife. There Gödel argues, in terms indebted to Leibniz's rationalism, that because the world is rationally constructed, it would be absurd for a being's intellectual and moral development to be permanently cut off by death just as that development becomes possible -- so an afterlife must exist to complete it. It struck me as a cousin of Kant's postulate of the immortality of the soul in the Critique of Practical Reason, where an infinite progress toward holiness is required to satisfy the demands of the moral law. Please compare the two arguments in structural terms, focusing on this question: is Gödel's premise about the "rational construction" of the world simply the kind of theoretical claim about the soul and the cosmos that Kant's first Critique -- through the Paralogisms and the Antinomies -- set out to show reason cannot legitimately make, even though Kant's own postulate in the second Critique reaches a similar conclusion by an explicitly practical route? In short, is Gödel helping himself to exactly the premises Kant's critical philosophy was designed to deny him?
