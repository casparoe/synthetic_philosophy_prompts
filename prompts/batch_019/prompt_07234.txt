Different ways of formalizing 'random' give different answers. (a) Describe two of the classical constructions and the probability each one gives. (b) Explain E.T. And here's the student's answer, typed up exactly as turned in:

"(a) In the random-endpoints method you pick two points uniformly on the circumference and join them; this gives P = 1/3. In the random-radius method you pick a random direction and then a uniform point along that radius, drawing the chord perpendicular to it there; this gives P = 1/2.

(b) Jaynes pointed out that nothing in the problem statement pins down the circle's size, location, or orientation, so a reasonable solution shouldn't change if we translate, rotate, or rescale the whole setup. Once you require the distribution to be invariant under rotations like that, it turns out the random-endpoints construction is the one that survives, so Jaynes's answer comes out to 1/3 -- the same number you get from the naive method.

(c) I'd say this basically kills the paradox rather than just relocating it. Bertrand's puzzle makes it look like 'probability' is arbitrary or subjective, but Jaynes shows there's a principled way to break the tie once you state your invariance assumptions explicitly, and the fact that his argument lands right back on the classical random-endpoints number seems like good evidence he's actually settled it, not just pushed the arbitrariness somewhere else."
