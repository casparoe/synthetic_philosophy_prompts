So me and a friend got into a two-hour argument last night about Penrose's claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorem proves human minds can't just be computers -- he thinks its basically airtight that we can "see" truths no formal system could prove about itself, and that this licenses looking for actual noncomputable physics in the brain. Weirdly this is now relevant to my week: I sit on my university's small internal exploratory-research grant committee, and one of this cycle's proposals is from a physicist wanting seed money to hunt for quantum coherence effects in neuronal microtubules, basically an Orch-OR (orchestrated objective reduction) style project, whose entire justification section leans on the Lucas-Penrose argument to explain why this deserves funding over ordinary computational neuroscience. About half the committee wants to reject it outright as dressed-up pseudoscience; the other half says that's exactly the kind of closed-minded gatekeeping the fund exists to counteract. Can you draft the memo I'll bring to Friday's meeting -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- that actually engages whether the Gödelian argument is strong enough to earn the proposal's premise a serious hearing, rather than either a pure physics risk-assessment or a one-line "Gödel doesn't say that"? Whichever way you come down, give the losing side's strongest version a real hearing, not the pop-science strawman.
