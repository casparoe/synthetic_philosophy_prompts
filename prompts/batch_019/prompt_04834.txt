In my metaethics seminar we've been working through the wrong kind of reasons (WKR) problem for fitting-attitude analyses of value. The stock illustration is the demon who will torture you unless you admire him: you clearly have a reason to admire him, but it is the wrong kind of reason, since it says nothing about whether he is admirable. The usual diagnosis, following Parfit, is that such a reason is state-given (grounded in facts about the state of admiring) rather than object-given (grounded in facts about the object itself), and that only object-given reasons are of the right kind. I'd like you to put pressure on that diagnosis by constructing an example — not a variant of the demon case, and not a Pascal's-Wager-style case about belief — in which an agent has a reason to adopt some other fitting attitude (fear, contempt, gratitude, remorse, or the like) that is naturally object-given in Parfit's sense, yet still strikes us, on reflection, as the wrong kind of reason for that attitude. Then explain what your example shows about whether the object-given/state-given distinction can really do the work it is supposed to do in solving the WKR problem.
