Then explain the 'targetless higher-order representation' problem, and say whether you think it's a bigger problem for actualist HOT theories (like Rosenthal's) or dispositionalist HOT theories (like Carruthers'), and why."

The student's answer:

"Both HOT and HOP theories say a mental state becomes conscious in virtue of being targeted by an appropriate higher-order state. They differ in what kind of state does the targeting: for Rosenthal it's a thought, a roughly assertoric, non-inferential belief-like state to the effect that one is currently in the first-order state. For Lycan the higher-order state is modeled on perception rather than cognition: an 'inner sense' system, an idea he inherits from Armstrong, that scans first-order states the way outer perception scans the world.

The targetless representation problem points out that since HOT theory identifies phenomenal consciousness with the occurrence of a suitable higher-order state, nothing stops that state from misfiring and representing a first-order state, like a pain, that isn't actually happening. The theory then seems committed to saying the subject is conscious of a pain that doesn't exist.

I think this is actually worse for actualist theories like Rosenthal's, since actualism needs an actual, free-standing higher-order thought whose content can simply fail to latch onto anything real. That's presumably why Rosenthal's own more recent work has shifted toward the dispositionalist picture usually associated with Carruthers: if a state just needs to be disposed to produce a higher-order thought rather than requiring an actual one, there's no longer a free-floating representation that can be empty, so the objection loses its grip. If dispositionalism can defuse the objection that easily, it suggests the explanatory gap for HOT theories was always a problem about representational content, not really about phenomenality as such."
