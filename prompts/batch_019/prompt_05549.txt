I have been reporting a magazine feature on the psychology of moral motivation, and one example keeps resurfacing in conversations with the philosophers I have interviewed: Huckleberry Finn's decision, in Twain's novel, to help Jim escape rather than turn him in. Huck does the right thing, but he does so while explicitly believing that he is doing something wicked and will be damned for it; what moves him is affection and loyalty toward Jim, not any thought that helping him is required or right. One philosopher I spoke with said that this case, together with Kant's older example of the shopkeeper who deals honestly with his customers only because honesty happens to be good for business, sharpens a question about moral worth that turns out to be harder than it looks: must an act be motivated, at least in part, by the agent's recognition that it is right in order to count as a genuine moral achievement, or can it be just as worthy when the agent is moved by nothing more than direct concern for the people or values involved? She mentioned that the first demand has been accused, since Michael Smith, of a kind of moral fetishism — caring about one's own rightness rather than about the person in front of one — while philosophers sympathetic to Huck, such as Nomy Arpaly, want to locate his moral worth in his responsiveness to Jim's humanity rather than in any correct moral belief he holds about the case.

For a sidebar of about a page, I would like to dramatize this disagreement rather than summarize it myself. Could you write a dialogue between two contemporary moral philosophers with opposing, carefully worked-out views on the question: one who maintains that some recognition of the act's rightness has to be doing real motivational work, though she need not insist that duty be the agent's only or uppermost motive on every occasion, and one who holds, in a view associated with Julia Markovits, that what matters is only whether the agent's motivating reasons coincide with the reasons that in fact make the act right. I would like each to press the other on hard cases rather than simply restate her own position: the first should have to say exactly what Huck is missing, morally speaking, and the second should have to say something about cases where an agent's motivating reason and the act's right-making reason line up only by luck, so that the agent seems to deserve no more credit than someone who happens to dial the right phone number. Please let the disagreement run to a genuine impasse rather than tying it up neatly at the end.
