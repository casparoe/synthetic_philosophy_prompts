I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but a recurring situation on my team has left me wanting to think something through more carefully than I can manage on my own. We have a running joke that nothing we ship is truly "correct" until it has survived a few weeks in production, since most of us have shipped a change that passed every unit test, integration test, and staging soak test, only to fail for some subset of real users under conditions nobody had anticipated.

When I described this to a friend of mine who is finishing a philosophy PhD, she said it was a nice small-scale illustration of an old, and by her telling still not fully settled, dispute between Charles Peirce and William James over what "truth" actually amounts to. Apparently Peirce, who originally coined the term "pragmatism," became so unhappy with how James (and F. C. S. Schiller) had popularized it that he relabeled his own position "pragmaticism" in 1905, insisting that truth has to be cashed out as the opinion an idealized, indefinitely extended community of inquirers would eventually converge on, rather than whatever presently helps a given person cope with their experience, which is closer to how James is usually read. I spent part of a weekend reading a couple of overviews of this disagreement and can restate both positions in rough outline, but I don't yet have a good feel for what substantively follows from taking one side over the other, as opposed to its being mostly a difference in emphasis or rhetoric.

Could you describe the most important implications of the Peirce/James split over the nature of truth for how I should think about "correctness" in exactly the kind of episode described above? Concretely: would Peirce want to say that the change which worked reliably in every test we had, but later failed in production, was therefore never really true to begin with (merely believed to be true on insufficient evidence), or would he instead say something like "true so far as tested," with truth itself admitting of degrees tied to how much convergence a small, decidedly finite team can approximate? And how would James's account describe that same episode differently, given that by his lights the change genuinely did "work," at least for a while? I would also value your view on whether this distinction has real practical teeth for someone doing iterative, empirical work like mine, or whether it mostly dissolves once you are careful about what "works" is supposed to mean in the first place.
