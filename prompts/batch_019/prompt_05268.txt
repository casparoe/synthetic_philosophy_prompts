I host a small philosophy podcast on the history of political thought, and next week's guest is a scholar whose new book defends a coherent reconstruction of Aquinas's scattered remarks on tyrannicide: roughly, that a ruler who seized power with no legitimate title at all may licitly be killed by a private person acting for the common good when there is no possibility of appeal to a higher authority, whereas a ruler who came to power legitimately and only later degenerates into tyranny may be removed solely through the action of whatever public body first instituted him, never through private violence. A friend and I argued about this at length over dinner last night; he thinks the title/exercise distinction is little more than a convenient device that lets Aquinas condone killing usurpers in the abstract while insulating actually-existing Christian kings from the same logic, since the prudential worries Aquinas raises in "De Regno" -- that assassins are usually moved by ambition rather than virtue, that killing one tyrant tends to produce a more vicious successor, that legitimizing the practice endangers good rulers along with bad ones -- apply with exactly the same force to a usurper as to a hereditary tyrant. I didn't have a good answer ready, and afterward it also struck me that I'm not sure how much of the reconstruction survives once you factor in that the later books of "De Regno" were completed by Ptolemy of Lucca after Aquinas's death, and that Ptolemy's own sympathies run considerably more republican than anything in the earlier, securely authentic chapters.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press on this specific cluster of tensions rather than simply inviting my guest to restate her reconstruction? Please also include a question about how much interpretive weight the reconstruction places on material that is not securely Aquinas's own. For each question, add a brief follow-up keyed to the response she is most likely to give, so the exchange doesn't stall out at her first line of defense.
