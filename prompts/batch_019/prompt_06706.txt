I am a retired physicist who has spent the years since leaving the lab reading early modern philosophy of science, and I now host a small discussion group of former colleagues who have taken up the same interest in retirement. For our next meeting we are trying something different: one member has agreed to assume the persona of Thomas Hobbes for the evening and defend, in character, his claim that geometry alone qualifies as a fully demonstrable science, since we ourselves construct its figures, whereas natural philosophy must remain merely conjectural. Draft eight to ten interview questions that do not let him simply restate that thesis: I want at least one that forces him to reconcile it with his own repeated, publicly refuted attempts to square the circle in his long-running quarrel with Wallis, one that presses him on why grounding lines and figures in the motion of bodies is any more secure a foundation than the algebraic methods he scorned, and one that tests whether his 'geometrical' construction of the commonwealth in Leviathan really earns the name, given that the passions it is built from belong to nature rather than to our own stipulation -- and for each, a follow-up tuned to the evasion he is most likely to attempt.
