I've been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty for a novel I'm plotting out (long story short — my protagonist is a translator who slowly comes unglued, and I want her unraveling to have some actual philosophical spine instead of just vague "reality is breaking down" vibes), and I keep getting snagged on one idea in there. He talks about "hinge" propositions — the stuff we don't so much know as stand on, like a riverbed that the water of our other beliefs just flows over, and which can supposedly shift over time even though from inside it looks totally fixed and self-evident. Then theres this other bit, almost thrown off as an aside, where he suggests that someone who genuinely, experientially doubted one of those hinge things — not "is the external world real" in the abstract seminar-room way, but something small, like their own name, or that the thing in front of them is a hand (Moore's whole "here is a hand" proof, basically) — wouldn't come off as a bold skeptic. They'd just look insane to everyone around them.

That's the part I want dramatized rather than explained to me. Could you write a short story (a page or two, doesn't need to be long) where a character's grip on one very mundane hinge certainty — pick something small and specific, not the big "does the external world exist" stuff — genuinely starts giving way, and everyone around them reacts to it as illness or breakdown rather than as a philosophical position, since that's sort of the whole point Wittgenstein's making. Please don't make it a "turns out it's a simulation" story, I've read that one a thousand times — I want the doubt to stay ambiguous, maybe never resolved, the way an actual hinge coming loose would probably feel from the inside, not like a twist ending. And if you could tack on a couple sentences afterward about which specific moves from On Certainty you were leaning on, that'd be great, just so I know I'm not misreading the guy before I go steal his ideas for my book.
