I work as a backend engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but a theory of computation course I took years ago left me with a lasting interest in Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and I've recently been reading about the Lucas–Penrose argument: the claim that these theorems show human minds cannot be equivalent to Turing machines, or to any single fixed, consistent formal system. The core move is elegant — for any formal system F proposed as a model of my reasoning, there is a Gödel sentence G(F) that is true but unprovable within F, and Lucas contends that I, unlike F, can simply see that G(F) is true, so I cannot be identical to F. Since this is supposed to hold for whichever F is proposed, I supposedly cannot be any formal system at all.

What I find more interesting than the argument itself is the standard rebuttal, which turns on the second incompleteness theorem rather than the first. As I understand it (I believe this line traces back to Putnam, though I may be misremembering), seeing that G(F) is true is not free: on the usual proof, it follows from knowing that F is consistent, and the second theorem says that a consistent F can never prove its own consistency. So when I assert "G(F) is true," I am helping myself to a belief in my own consistency that I have no more entitlement to than a machine would. And if I am allowed to simply assume my own consistency as an unproved article of faith, a machine can be handed that very same assumption as an extra axiom, and it will then derive G(F) as a theorem too. There is also a related complication, which I associate with Benacerraf, about whether I can ever be sure which formal system is supposed to be "me" in the first place — without that, it is unclear I am even entitled to run the diagonal argument on myself.

I would like a joke built out of this specific tangle: one whose punchline genuinely turns on the gap between "seeing that your Gödel sentence is true" and "being entitled to believe you are consistent," rather than a generic "robots can't really think" bit. It should be funny, or at least land as clever, for someone who actually knows the second incompleteness theorem and the Lucas–Penrose literature, and it should more or less fall flat for someone who only knows the one-line pop-science version of Gödel's theorems. A short, sharp joke is preferable to a long shaggy-dog story.
