so I've been nerd-sniped this week by the continuum hypothesis. I think I get the basic shape of it: Cantor conjectured there's no set who's size sits strictly between the integers and the reals, then Godel showed CH can't be disproved from the usual ZFC axioms and Cohen later showed it cant be proved either, so it's just "independent" of the axioms. Cool, mystery solved, right?

Except then I went further down the rabbit hole and found out plenty of actual set theorists do NOT treat this as case closed. Apparently guys like Woodin spent decades building all this machinery (omega-logic, something called the "Ultimate L" program) specifically to try to find the real answer to CH, as if it's a fact of the matter we just haven't uncovered yet -- kind of like how Godel himself apparently believed CH was flat-out false and that we just hadn't found the right axioms yet. But then there's apparently a totally different camp (Hamkins is a name that keeps coming up) who think that whole project is confused, that CH is just true in some models of set theory and false in others and there's no further "real" universe out there to be asking about.

As a non-philosopher this messes with my head, because it feels like it should be a technical math question but it's actually a fight about what mathematical truth even is. Can you write me an essay that lays out the strongest case for each side -- call them the "there's a fact of the matter, we just haven't found it" camp and the "it's genuinely indeterminate" camp -- and then actually commits to a verdict instead of hedging? Don't just say "reasonable people disagree" -- if you had to bet, is CH true, false, or is "neither" actually the most defensible answer, and why?
