I've been down a rabbit hole reading about "coherence arguments" in AI safety -- the claim that any sufficiently advanced agent will end up acting like an expected utility maximizer, because if it doesn't, it's exploitable via a money pump. The part that really got me stuck is the move where people try to rescue incomplete preferences from this. You know the setup: if an agent has no preference either way between X and Y, you bring in a slightly sweetened X+ (strictly better than X by a cent, so a clear preference exists there) and walk the agent around the loop Y -> X -> X+ -> Y, taking a small fee each lap, since at no individual step does it supposedly do anything it disprefers -- and yet it ends up strictly poorer then where it started. The standard comeback is that an agent can dodge the whole thing just by being "resolute": pick a plan up front and refuse any trade that would walk you back off it, even one that looks locally fine in isolation, so the loop never actually closes.

I tried writing up why I don't think that comeback is free, and I'd like your take before I turn what's currently just a note in my phone into an actual post. My worry is that resoluteness doesn't get rid of the coherence requirement, it just relocates it up a level: the agent now needs a stable ranking over entire plans decided in advance (otherwise its choice of which plan to be resolute about is itself arbitrary, and pumpable), and it has to sometimes turn down a trade that looks locally fine purely because of a past commitment -- which seems to give up the "always do whats best given what's in front of you" picture of rationality that made money pumps compelling to begin with. I mentioned the gist of this to a friend who did some decision theory in grad school, and she said it sounded like a repackaging of an old sophisticated-vs-resolute-choice debate (she metioned a name like McClennen, tied to dynamic consistency and the Allais paradox) that apparently predates any of this AI stuff by decades -- I'd never run into it and haven't been able to track it down properly.

So, two things: does the "relocates rather than dissolves" argument actually hold up, or is there an obvious standard reply I'm missing here -- like maybe plan-level coherence is cheaper to buy than outcome-level coherence for some reason, or doesn't have to be transitive in the same way? And separately, is my version of the money-pump-for-incompleteness setup even right, or am I garbling the formal version somewhere -- I've only ever picked this up secondhand from blog posts and forum threads, not an actual course.
