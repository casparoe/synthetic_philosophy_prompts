(1) Theo opened by saying the paper basically wins: biologists have a habit of assuming a trait exists because selection built it for its current job, and unless you can rule out drift, pleiotropy, or plain structural constraint you've got no business telling that story - that's the Panglossian error Voltaire was mocking, and the field needed the wake-up call.

(2) Nora said that's too generous to the paper: nobody serious (Maynard Smith, Dawkins) ever claimed every trait is a perfect optimum for its current use, they build constraint and pleiotropy into their models too, so G&L are knocking down a "hyperadaptationist" strawman that nobody actually holds, and the paper's punch comes from the vivid cathedral example more than from catching real biologists in an actual error.

(3) Theo replied that even granting the extreme version is a strawman, the everyday practice wasn't: journals were (and still are) full of papers that propose a function after the fact and never test it against non-adaptive alternatives, so the methodological complaint holds against the field's habits even if nobody endorsed the doctrine in print.

(4) Nora then argued that the "just-so story" charge actually cuts both ways: calling something a spandrel takes just as much speculative reconstruction as calling it an adaptation, and Gould's own later idea of exaptation - a byproduct getting co-opted for a new use, like the San Marco spandrels literally getting painted with images of the evangelists - shows "spandrel" and "adaptation" aren't even stable, mutually exclusive categories, so there's no principled reason to default to the non-adaptive story either.

(5) Theo's response was that this doesn't rescue the adaptationist default: if both kinds of explanation need real evidence, G&L were only asking biologists to drop adaptation as the default assumption absent that evidence, not to ban adaptive stories outright, and Nora's point doesn't actually touch that.
