I'm several chapters into a novel set in Cambridge in the last years of the 1920s, built around the final months of Frank Ramsey's life, and I've arranged a recorded interview — forty-five minutes, no more — with a philosopher who has published on Ramsey's ethics and on his unpublished papers, to help me get one particular scene right rather than merely plausible. In it, the dying Ramsey talks in hospital with his brother Michael, already the more conventionally devout of the two and already on the path that would eventually make him Archbishop of Canterbury, about the fragment on the meaning of life that Frank left among his papers: the one where he refuses to be humbled by the vastness of the universe and insists that his picture of the world is drawn deliberately in human perspective, foreground and all. I don't want the exchange to read as a set piece in which one brother is obviously right, and I'm aware that I don't yet know where the argument's real pressure points are, as distinct from where I merely assume them to be.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions for this scholar? I'd like at least one that asks whether Ramsey's refusal to feel small is a genuine argument against cosmic-insignificance worries or just his own temperament dressed up as one; one that ties the fragment to his more technical commitments — his deflationary account of truth, his subjectivism about probability, the same impatience with Wittgensteinian mysticism that produced his line about not being able to whistle what we can't say — so as to test whether the cheerfulness is actually earned by the rest of his philosophy or merely asserted alongside it; and one that takes seriously the standard objection that changing the scale of the picture doesn't answer the metaphysical worry so much as change the subject, and asks how a sympathetic reader ought to respond. For each of these, please add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — in her own writing she tends to call Ramsey's cheerfulness "constitutional," which I suspect is a way of not quite arguing for it, and I'd like the questions to make that move harder for her to fall back on. I'd also like one question about whether anything in the historical record gives me license to write Michael as genuinely tempted by his brother's view rather than merely tolerant of it, and one on how to let the scene carry the irony of a man dying at twenty-six without tipping into either triumphalism or bathos.
