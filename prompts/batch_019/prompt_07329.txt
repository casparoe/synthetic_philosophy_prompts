For an independent project in my philosophy elective, I've been working through Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, where he argues that death is "nothing to us," since so long as we exist death has not yet arrived, and once it arrives we no longer exist to be troubled by it. My teacher paired this with Thomas Nagel's essay "Death," which defends the common-sense view that dying really is a misfortune for the person who dies, on the grounds that it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have gone on to enjoy, even though there's no particular moment at which they actually undergo that loss. I followed the basic shape of both arguments, but I got stuck on what seems to be the sharpest form of the Epicurean challenge, which different sources I found call the "existence condition" or "existence requirement": the idea that something can be bad for a subject only while that subject exists, so that, since there's no subject left after death to bear the deprivation, it's unclear how death could be bad for the person who died rather than merely bad "from the outside," for the people who survive them. To complicate things further, I also came across Lucretius's so-called symmetry argument, which points out that none of us is troubled by the eternity of nonexistence before our birth, and asks why the eternity of nonexistence after our death should be treated any differently, if mere nonexistence is supposedly what does the harm.

Could you write me a dialogue between an Epicurean and a Nagel-style deprivation theorist who are evenly matched and genuinely engage with each other's strongest points rather than just repeating their own side? I especially want the deprivation theorist to be pressed hard on both the existence condition and the Lucretian symmetry argument, instead of getting to wave them away with one quick line, and I want the Epicurean to have to reckon honestly with whatever the best replies to those two challenges turn out to be. It doesn't need to end with either side winning outright — I'd rather see where the actual pressure points in the debate are than get a falsely tidy resolution, especially since it sounds like professional philosophers are still divided on this themselves.
