Two months ago I moved into a newly created "head of strategy" role at a mid-sized online marketplace, and one of the first things I inherited from my predecessor was a half-finished proposal to run an internal prediction market: several dozen engineers, analysts, and product managers would trade small-stakes contracts on questions like "Will the new seller-onboarding flow ship by Q3?" or "Will seller churn exceed 8% next quarter?", with a subsidized automated market maker keeping the contracts liquid enough to trade. Our data science lead is enthusiastic about the idea and points to the internal markets that companies like Google and Hewlett-Packard have famously run for exactly this purpose. Our chief economist, who joined us from academia and still writes memos like a journal referee, is far more skeptical: she circulated a memo effectively reconstructing the no-trade theorem, arguing that if it is common knowledge that everyone in the market is rational, risk-averse, and trading only on private information rather than on any liquidity or hedging need, then a colleague's mere willingness to take the other side of your trade should already update your beliefs enough to erase whatever edge you thought you had — so genuinely informative trading should collapse to nothing, and whatever volume we do see would really just be noise, overconfidence, or people playing games with points. The data science lead's rebuttal is that this is exactly why real-world markets subsidize trading and tolerate noise traders in the first place, and that assuming common knowledge of rationality among a few dozen stressed colleagues was never realistic to begin with.

I turn out to be the deciding vote on the three-person steering group — the data science lead, the chief economist, and me — that must decide, within the next two weeks, whether to launch the pilot as designed, shelve it, or send it back for a specific redesign. Could you draft the memo I will bring into that meeting? I would like it to stake out a clear position — proceed, don't proceed, or proceed only with named changes to the market's design — attach a rough timeline to whichever recommendation you make, and, above all, engage with the chief economist's argument on its own terms rather than just gesturing at "well, markets seem to work in practice elsewhere." I think she has put her finger on something real about what this pilot can and cannot be expected to do epistemically, even though I suspect her conclusion is too strong given how similar markets have actually performed at other companies. Please write it so a colleague with no economics background could follow the reasoning without assistance.
