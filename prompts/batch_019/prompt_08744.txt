Hey, I've been chewing on Parfit's teleporter thought experiment ever since a dumb argument at work about fork() derailed into a philosophy tangent, and I sketched out an analogy I want a sanity check on before I embarrass myself and post it somewhere.

Quick recap of the puzzle in case its not fresh in your head: the teleporter scans you, destroys the original, and reconstructs an exact copy elsewhere, and Parfit asks whether that's survival or death. The sharpest version is the one where the machine glitches and doesn't destroy the original, so for a few days theres a "you" on Earth and a fully separate "you" on Mars, both equally continuous with the person who stepped into the machine, and neither one gets to claim they're the "real" one in any way the other can't equally claim.

My analogy: this is just fork(), but slow. Right up until the call, there's one process, one memory image, one call stack, zero ambiguity about identity. Then it splits into a parent and a child that are, for an instant, running identical code over identical (copy-on-write) memory, and they only start diverging once each one branches on the return value and takes its own path. Nobody asks "but which one is really the original process" in some anxious way — we just say there used to be one process and now there's two, full stop, and that's a totally satisfying thing to say, nothing feels unresolved. My tentative take: this is basically Parfit's own conclusion — identity isn't what matters, only the continuity relation is, and that relation doesn't have to be one-to-one — except stated somewhere it feels obvious instead of spooky, because we already think about processes that way.

Where I get nervous: is the entire reason we don't panic about fork() just that nobody thinks a process has interests or experiences to begin with, meaning I've quietly assumed personal identity is "just" information/computation and then acted surprised when the computation framing makes the puzzle dissolve?

Genuinely curious what you think: does the fork() analogy actually track Parfit's argument, or does it break down somewhere I'm not seeing? And is this take actually fresh, or is it the kind of move that's already been made (and shot down) better elsewhere?
