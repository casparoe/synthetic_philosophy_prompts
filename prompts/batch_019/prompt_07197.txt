Two people in a public-policy reading group I'm part of got into it last week over discounting the future, and I can't decide who came out ahead.

(1) Dana said any "pure" time preference — discounting someone's wellbeing just because it happens later, 2150 instead of now — is flatly indefensible. A harm doesn't hurt less because of the date on the calendar.

(2) Owen said sure, but set that pure rate to zero and, combined with ordinary economic growth, the standard math says our comparatively poor generation should be saving and sacrificing like crazy for the sake of a much richer future one. That's basically the same complaint people had about that famous climate report with the near-zero discount rate — it reads like a reductio.

(3) Dana said that's not really a problem with pure time preference at all, it's Owen smuggling in a totally different term from the same equation — the one about how much an extra dollar (or unit of wellbeing) is worth to someone, which shrinks as people get richer. You can hold the pure rate at zero and still dodge the absurd conclusion, since "future people will be richer" does that work by itself.

(4) Owen said fine, but now shes just dialing that other term up or down until the model tells her what she already believed, and he doesn't see why that's more principled than just admitting time itself can matter a little. Nobody thinks it's irrational to care more about your own future than a stranger's, after all, and that's also, at bottom, partiality justified by nothing more than "it's mine."

Neither budged.
