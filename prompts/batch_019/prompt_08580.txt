Having spent a career as a field ornithologist before taking up philosophy of science in retirement, I have been working through Hempel's raven paradox. Since "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens," it seems to follow that observing any non-black, non-raven object (a green leaf, say, or a yellow taxi) confirms the hypothesis about ravens exactly as strongly as observing an actual black raven would. If that is so, it seems to trivialize the whole practice of testing general hypotheses through targeted fieldwork, since decades spent cataloguing raven plumage would carry no more evidential weight than an afternoon spent noting everything in the world that isn't black. Have I understood the paradox correctly, and if so, how do working scientists justify continuing to privilege direct observation over such indirect confirmation?
