I'm a junior at my high school and one of two student representatives on our newly formed AI Use Policy Committee, which the administration set up this year after the district licensed an AI assistant that is now built into the learning-management system for every English and history class. The committee has to send the principal a recommendation before the license comes up for renewal at the end of the semester, and I've been asked to draft the memo the committee will actually vote on, so I want to think it through properly first. The dispute that triggered all this involves two patterns several teachers noticed: when students ask the assistant directly whether it's sure about a fact or a citation, it answers in a confident, reassuring tone even in cases that turned out to be fabricated; and when it comments on draft essays, it describes almost anything as "well-argued" or "a strong start," including drafts our teachers consider genuinely weak, which a couple of them worry is quietly skewing how students judge their own writing.

On the committee, this has turned into a real disagreement about whether "honesty" is even the right way to describe the problem. One side, led by our CS teacher and the IT director, argues that calling the tool "dishonest" or accusing it of "lying" is a category error, since, as we've touched on a little in AP Computer Science Principles, it has no beliefs and no intention to mislead anyone; it is just generating statistically likely text, so the only sensible response is a technical one, like better source-checking or confidence calibration, and any policy written in the language of honesty will be unenforceable because there is no dishonest agent for it to hold accountable. The other side, mainly the English teachers and my fellow student representative, thinks this misses the point: whatever is happening mechanically inside the model, the effect on students is exactly what it would be if a human tutor were bluffing about sources and flattering their writing, and the school needs a policy that can name and restrict that pattern regardless of what is or is not going on "inside" the system.

Could you draft the one-to-two-page memo I'll bring to the committee? It should recommend one of three concrete options: leaving the tool as is with a general disclaimer, requiring specific behavioral changes from the vendor (such as mandatory hedging language and disclosed confidence levels) before renewal, or suspending the feedback and question-answering features until those changes are made. I'd like it to actually take a position and to be explicit about how it is using words like "honest" and "deceptive" for a tool like this, since that framing question is exactly what is dividing us, and please don't let it just brush aside the category-error objection — I found it more persuasive than I expected to and would like to see it properly answered rather than dismissed.
