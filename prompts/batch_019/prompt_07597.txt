For the last few months, my friend Owen has been agonizing over whether to give up meat. It isn't that he's learned any new facts about farming; his trouble is that he genuinely doesn't know which overall view of right and wrong is the correct one, and the different views he takes seriously don't agree on how bad the habit is: one treats animals as barely counting for anything, another treats harming them as being on a par with harming a person, and a couple of others fall somewhere in between. At dinner a couple of weekends ago he walked us through, quite methodically, how he planned to decide despite not knowing which view is true, and a friend of ours, Renata, spent the rest of the evening taking the plan apart piece by piece. Neither of them has any background in this kind of thing -- Owen works in logistics, Renata's in marketing -- but I've found myself turning their argument over ever since without being able to say who came out ahead, and I'd like a more careful pair of eyes on the exchange itself, not on whether Owen should actually give up the habit.

(1) Owen's opening position was that you should treat this like any other decision made under uncertainty: put a rough probability on each rival view being the correct one, work out how bad or good giving up meat would be under that view, multiply and add it all up, and go with whichever option comes out ahead once everything is combined. (2) Renata objected that this doesn't really work, because the views aren't using the same ruler to begin with -- one view might treat eating meat as a minor demerit, a few points out of a hundred, while another doesn't deal in degrees at all and simply forbids it outright, so multiplying a probability by a "badness score" and adding the results across views assumes you can line up their scales, and nothing guarantees that you can. (3) Owen's fix was to forget the actual scores and just have each view rank the two options, the way voters rank candidates, weight each view's ranking by how likely he thinks it is to be true, and let whichever option comes out ahead win -- that way he's only ever using how likely a view is and which way it leans, never how much it thinks is at stake. (4) Renata replied that this trades one problem for a worse one: a view that finds the habit mildly distasteful and a view that finds it monstrous now both just contribute one vote against it, with no way for the view that treats the stakes as enormous to count for more than the one that barely cares, which seems to throw out exactly the information that made the disagreement worth having in the first place. (5) Owen then suggested dropping the whole idea of splitting the difference and just acting on whichever single view he actually finds most convincing, since he has to believe something, and it seems strange to let views he doesn't even think are true push his decision around. (6) Renata's last word was that this brings back the same recklessness from the other direction: if he's only, say, 55 percent confident in the view that says the habit barely matters and 45 percent confident in the view that says it's a serious wrong, throwing out that 45 percent purely because it isn't his top pick looks just as careless as treating every view as equally important did.
