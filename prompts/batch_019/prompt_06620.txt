Among Jeremy Bentham's unpublished manuscripts on sexual morality — drafted mainly in the 1780s and not assembled in full until the 2014 Collected Works volume *Of Sexual Irregularities, and Other Writings on Sexual Morality* — is a sustained utilitarian argument, usually cited under the heading "Offences Against One's Self," that consensual same-sex acts between adults should not be criminally punished. Bentham's case rests on the claim that such acts produce pleasure and harm no one, that the horror they provoke reflects mere "antipathy" and ascetic prejudice rather than any calculation of pain and pleasure, and he even compares condemning an unusual sexual taste to condemning an unusual taste in food. Yet in these same writings he continues to describe such acts as "irregularities" — departures from the natural or proper use of the relevant faculties — rather than placing them on an equal footing with the tastes he treats as wholly unproblematic.

Write an essay assessing whether Bentham can consistently hold both positions. Is calling a harmless pleasure "irregular" a merely descriptive, statistical judgment that a thoroughgoing hedonistic utilitarian is free to make without inconsistency, or does the language of natural versus unnatural use quietly reimport a teleological standard that Bentham's own attacks on natural law and on the "principle of asceticism" should have foreclosed? Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but do not end on a non-committal note: defend a definite verdict on whether Bentham's position is coherent by his own lights.
