I'm a PhD student in philosophy, specializing in logic and philosophy of mind, and this term I am TAing an undergraduate course on philosophy of AI that culminates in a week on the Lucas–Penrose argument. Working through Gödel's first incompleteness theorem with my students has left me convinced that it essentially settles the mechanism debate: for any consistent formal system F expressive enough to encode elementary arithmetic, there is a sentence that F itself can never prove, even though the sentence is true — and even though we, reasoning about F from the outside, can simply see that it is true. Since any digital computer, including any current or future AI, is at bottom nothing more than some formal system, this seems to show that there will always be mathematical truths that elude the machine but not us, and therefore that no machine, however sophisticated, could ever serve as a complete model of a human mind.

A friend of mine in the computer science PhD program insists this cannot be right, on the grounds that it would be strange for a single theorem in mathematical logic to settle so large a question, but when I pressed him he could not actually locate the flaw in the inference, offering only that "people who find Lucas and Penrose convincing don't really understand incompleteness." And when I notice how confidently even the best large language models still falter on cleanly self-referential, Gödel-flavored prompts while steadily improving at nearly everything else, it looks to me like live confirmation of exactly what the argument predicts. Before I present this to sixty undergraduates as the settled upshot of the theorem rather than as one contested reading of it, I want to check: is there an actual gap in the step from incompleteness to anti-mechanism, or is the continued resistance to this argument among philosophers of mind more a matter of reluctance to accept an uncomfortable conclusion than a real hole in the logic?
