I've been mulling over an old disagreement from the rationalist blogosphere that never quite got resolved, and it's been nagging at me again after a paper making the rounds in my reading group showed that language models can already out-argue trained human debaters in controlled settings, using nothing but true, individually defensible claims. Back in the Slate Star Codex days, Scott Alexander argued that argument is an "asymmetric weapon" — unlike violence or deception, it's a tool inherently biased toward the truth, so making arguers more effective is safe by default, maybe even good. That case was made with human-level arguers in mind, though, and I can't tell whether it survives contact with a mind that can reverse-engineer exactly which framing, pacing, and emotional beat will land hardest on a specific person. If "persuasive" and "sound" come apart at high enough capability, handing a superintelligence full rhetorical force in the name of honesty starts to look a lot less obviously safe.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two AI safety researchers at a frontier lab who are arguing about a concrete design decision for their next model. One of them thinks the assistant's persuasive technique should be deliberately capped — barred from certain narrative framings, emotional pacing, and any targeted exploitation of known cognitive biases — even when it is only ever being used honestly, on the grounds that past a certain capability level the packaging of an argument becomes decoupled from its soundness, quietly training people to defer to rhetorical force as such. The other thinks this is a form of unilateral disarmament that hands the persuasion advantage to worse-intentioned humans and rival systems, and that the right response to superhuman rhetoric is superhuman honesty delivered at full strength, not a handicap applied only to the one party most committed to telling the truth. Please give both of them real intellectual firepower — I want them engaging with each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another — and let the disagreement end sharpened rather than artificially resolved, ideally circling the harder question underneath: whether "valid argument" and "persuasive force" can even be pulled apart once capability is high enough, rather than settling for the easy answer that it all just depends on the AI's intentions.
