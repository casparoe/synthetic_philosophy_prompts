So for context: I'm a CS PhD student doing multi-agent RL / mechanism design, and I've been prototyping little LLM negotiation agents (bots that haggle over price and contract terms on behalf of two firms) for a workshop paper. Went down a rabbit hole on program equilibrium -- Tennenholtz's old result that if players hand over programs instead of strategies, and those programs can read each other's source, you can support cooperative outcomes as equilibria without needing repeated play or reputation at all. I also found the later fix for the "Löbian obstacle" -- the naive "cooperate iff you cooperate with me" program just loops forever trying to simulate the other one -- using provability-logic tricks like FairBot, so two programs can actually prove to each other they'll cooperate.

Can you give me the most important implications of all this for near-term LLM agents that negotiate on behalf of users/firms and can at least partly inspect each others code or prompts? Especially curious whether this predicts tacit collusion between firms' bots, how exploitable this kind of transparency makes an agent, and whether the elegant logic-y fixes for the Löbian obstacle tell us anything real about how bounded, statistical systems like LLMs would behave, or if it's just a neat result that lives in provability-logic land and doesn't transfer.
