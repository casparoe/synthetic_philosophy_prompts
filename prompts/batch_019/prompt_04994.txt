A while back I read about a brush with death that the philosopher A. J. Ayer had in 1988. He was in hospital being treated for pneumonia when he choked on a piece of smoked salmon and went into cardiac arrest, and was clinically dead for a few minutes before being revived. He later wrote a short piece about it (I believe it was called something like "What I Saw When I Was Dead"), describing some strange, vivid imagery, and afterward he was widely quoted as saying the experience had slightly weakened his conviction that his own death would be the end of him, though he still hoped it would be. I find that remark fascinating, since Ayer had spent much of his career arguing, via the verification principle, that talk of a soul surviving the body isn't just false but strictly meaningless, on the grounds that no observation could ever confirm or disconfirm it.

I have no formal background in philosophy, but I've been trying to work out on my own whether his brush with death really puts pressure on that view, and my tentative conclusion is that it can't — for a structural reason, not just a factual one. Anyone who survives to tell of a near-death experience, by definition, never underwent the one thing Ayer's argument was actually about: a death nobody returns from. However vivid what Ayer saw was, it can't be evidence about a case it was never an instance of. On this reading, when he said the experience had "slightly weakened" his conviction, he was reporting a change in his own nerve, not a change in the evidential situation his principle was built to track.

What I can't settle for myself is whether this objection misses the point of his own theory. Ayer's verification principle, at least in its "weak" form, only requires that some possible observation be relevant to a claim's truth or falsity, not that the person making the claim be the one who could ever make that observation. So the principle seems designed to leave room for third-party evidence (communications through mediums, and the like, which some of Ayer's contemporaries took seriously as relevant data) rather than to turn on first-person reports at all. If that's right, my structural objection may be true of near-death experiences specifically without telling us anything about whether Ayer's dismissal of survival claims as meaningless was ever correctly reasoned to begin with.

Could you look this over and tell me whether the reasoning actually holds together, whether I'm applying "weak verifiability" correctly, and whether there's a sharper way of stating what, if anything, an experience like Ayer's could ever show?
