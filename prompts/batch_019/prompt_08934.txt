My closest friend from graduate school, Clara, is getting married next month, and she has asked me to give the toast. I'm a novelist, not a philosopher; back then I lived two floors above her department and spent most evenings half-following her arguments over cheap department wine. Clara wrote her dissertation defending the coherence of Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds against the standard objections, chief among them the Lisbon earthquake, and roughly half the guests will be philosophers who sat on her committee or spent years trying to refute her. The other half are her family and her fiancé Theo's side, who have heard her introduced as "the one who defends Leibniz" for a decade without much sense of what that means, beyond a running joke that she is contractually obliged to insist everything is, secretly, for the best. The two of them met, characteristically, through a small chain of catastrophes — a cancelled flight, a sublet that fell through, a job that didn't pan out — that everyone at this wedding has already heard rehearsed as "it was meant to be."

I don't want to give that toast; Clara would correct me from her seat, and rightly. Could you write me a five-minute toast instead, one that uses Leibniz's actual argument rather than the Panglossian cartoon of it: the idea that God does not select the best world by maximizing every good separately, but by choosing the most harmonious compossible whole, so that the small disasters preceding this wedding are not "meant to be" in any crude providential sense, but might simply be inseparable, within this one compossible series of things, from the good that came after them — a stranger and more interesting claim than "it all worked out." If there is a natural place for the distinction between God's antecedent and consequent will, or for the principle of sufficient reason that supposedly forces the choice among worlds in the first place, I would like them handled precisely enough that the philosophers do not wince, but folded into something warm enough that Theo's grandmother does not feel she has wandered into a seminar. One joke that plays differently for both halves of the room, without excluding either, would be ideal, and I would like the toast to end as a blessing on the two of them rather than as a defense of a three-hundred-year-old thesis.
