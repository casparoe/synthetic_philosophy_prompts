I'm a physics PhD student (precision spectroscopy on quasar absorption lines is basically my day job) and a few of us started an informal reading group this term that wandered into philosophy of science, mostly out of guilt about not knowing our own field's foundational commitments. A couple weeks back we did the Humean vs anti-Humean fight over laws of nature: Lewis's best-system account, where a law is just whichever regularity earns a spot in the simplest, strongest axiomatization of the entire mosaic of events -- past, present, and future, all of it -- versus the Armstrong/Dretske/Tooley picture where laws are a real relation of necessitation between universals that governs events from outside the pattern rather than just summarizing it (there's also Maudlin's primitivist option, where lawhood is just brute and unanalyzable, which we sort of punted on). What's been bugging me since, and which we didnt really settle, is that on the Humean view the future gets a vote: if the best system has to fit literally everything that ever happens, a "law" like conservation of energy, or some constant being constant, could turn out never to have been a law at all, if something weird enough happens far enough downstream. Nothing gets violated, we'd just have been wrong the whole time about what pattern we were actually in.

Could you write me a short story out of that, something like 1000-1500 words? Picture a scientist -- metrologist, cosmologist, whoever, pick whatever setting earns the idea, doesn't have to be my exact subfield -- who gets data suggesting a "constant" they've built their career on (fine-structure constant, big G, whatever) isn't holding after all, and has to sit with what that means. What I really want is for the Humean and anti-Humean reactions to feel different in kind, not just degree: a committed necessitarian should find a "changing law" almost unintelligible, since a real governing relation isn't the kind of thing that just changes, while a Humean should be unsettled in a totally different and honestly worse way -- nothing was ever broken, we just misdescribed the pattern, and there was never a guarantee against a future correction. That contrast can live in one character's own head or in an argument between two people, whatever works dramatically. Just please don't let it cheat its way out with "it was a measurement error" or aliens or a simulation twist -- I want the unease to come from the metaphysics itself, not a plot device. Tone-wise go wherever you think earns it, quiet and literary or colder and more clinical, first or third person, doesn't matter to me, I just don't want the philosophy sitting there as decoration.
