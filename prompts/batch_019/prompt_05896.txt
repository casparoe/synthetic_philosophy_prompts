Me and my friend Elena got into a whole thing last week after a physics-for-non-physicists lecture on entanglement, and honestly neither of us really won. The lecturer went through Bell's theorem - that no theory with local hidden variables can reproduce the correlations quantum mechanics predicts between entangled particles - and then the actual experiments (Aspect in the early 80s, then the "loophole-free" versions around 2015) that confirm QM and rule those theories out. Elena's take was that Einstein, being the empiricist he obviously was, would've just accepted the data and conceded nature is nonlocal, "spooky action" and all. I said that's letting him off way too easy - his whole later career (EPR, the letters to Born where he complains about "spukhafte Fernwirkung," his lukewarm reaction even to Bohm's hidden-variable revival in the 50s) reads like someone who'd rather keep hunting for a loophole than give up locality and a mind-independent world with definite properties.

We went back and forth on which of the "loopholes" still on the table today he'd have actually found tolerable: biting the bullet on Bohmian-style explicit nonlocal hidden variables (which he reportedly wasn't too impressed by even before Bell came along), going superdeterminist and giving up the idea that measurement settings are independent of the hidden variables, some flavor of many-worlds where locality survives but definiteness doesn't, or just insisting QM is incomplete in some way nobody's pinned down yet. Problem is neither of us actually knows Einsteins philosophical commitments (seperability, realism, determinism) well enough to say which of these he'd have found acceptable versus just as bad as nonlocality. Could you actually work through this properly, based on what he really argued rather than the cartoon "Einstein just didn't like quantum mechanics" version? Specifically: which escape route, if any, would he most plausibly have taken, which would he have rejected outright and why, and is there any honest sense in which he ends up looking right once you factor in Bell's theorem and everything that came after it?
