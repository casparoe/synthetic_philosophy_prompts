I sit on my police department's training and standards committee. Last night I argued for hours with a friend, a psychology postdoc, over whether our "Character and Integrity" curriculum for newly promoted sergeants is defensible at all. She points to the classic situationist findings, especially Milgram's obedience studies and Darley and Batson's "Good Samaritan" study, where whether a subject was in a hurry predicted stopping to help far better than anything else about them, including whether they'd just been primed by thinking about the parable itself, and argues that stable character traits are largely fictions, so our money would do more good hardening the situations officers face than cultivating virtues in people who don't reliably have them. I answered that Aristotelian virtue was never supposed to mean behaving identically across circumstances — practical wisdom is precisely the skill of reading a situation correctly — and that her other pillar, Zimbardo's prison study, has since been undermined by revelations that the guards were coached. Neither of us budged. The committee votes Friday on renewing the curriculum's contract or reallocating its budget to a peer-intervention, situational-design program instead. Draft the memo I will circulate beforehand: recommend one path, or a specified mix, with a concrete timeline, and engage honestly with the strongest evidence on whichever side you don't favor.
