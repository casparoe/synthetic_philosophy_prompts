I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently reporting a feature on the modest revival of interest in sortition and randomized election mechanisms. One proposal that keeps coming up in my interviews is "lottery voting": instead of awarding a race to whichever candidate collects the most votes, the winner is determined by drawing a single ballot at random from all those submitted. Several of the reform advocates I've spoken with present this as a clean escape from the strategic-voting problems that plague ranked-choice and other conventional systems, since a ballot only ever matters in the event that it happens to be the one drawn, which supposedly gives every voter a dominant incentive to just name their sincere favorite.

Before I write this up, though, I went back to brush up on the underlying theory, and I came away thinking this selling point doesn't survive contact with it. As I understand the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, it proves, without exception, that once there are three or more candidates, every voting rule whatsoever — deterministic or randomized — is either a straightforward dictatorship or manipulable, in the sense that some voter in some situation can gain by misrepresenting their true preferences. If that reading is right, then dressing a voting rule up as a lottery doesn't change anything at the level that matters mathematically, and I'm inclined to write that the lottery-voting advocates' central claim to strategy-proofness doesn't really hold up.

Is that a fair way to characterize what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem actually establishes, or am I misreading its scope in a way that would make it unfair to print?
