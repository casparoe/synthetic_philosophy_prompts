Gödel's modal ontological argument for God's existence is well known in outline, but the story of what happened to it after his death is not. Gödel never published the proof himself: he showed a version of it to the logician Dana Scott in 1970, and it did not circulate widely until after he died in 1978. Soon afterward, Jordan Howard Sobel showed that Gödel's own axioms entail an unwelcome "modal collapse" — if the proof is sound, every truth turns out to be necessarily true, and genuine contingency disappears from the world. Other logicians, notably C. Anthony Anderson, tried to rescue the argument's conclusion by weakening the axiom responsible for the collapse. Drawing on what we know of Gödel's broader philosophical commitments — his mathematical Platonism, his deep debt to Leibniz's rationalism, and his conviction that the world is fundamentally rational and ordered for a purpose — how do you think Gödel himself would have responded to the discovery of modal collapse in his own proof? Would he have regarded it as fatal to the argument, favored a revision along Anderson's lines, or denied that the collapse is really the embarrassment his critics suppose it to be?
