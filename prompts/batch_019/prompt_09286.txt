A friend of mine — we were officemates in grad school and still can't resist re-litigating old coursework — was over for dinner last night, and the conversation turned into a two-hour argument about how to grade a problem-set answer from the graduate seminar on fairness in machine learning that I'm TAing this term. We ended up proposing quite different point totals and neither of us could talk the other out of their position, so rather than split the difference arbitrarily, I'd like an independent, careful read from someone with no stake in the disagreement.

The question, worth 10 points, read as follows:

"In 2016, ProPublica's investigation of Northpointe's COMPAS recidivism-risk tool concluded that it was racially biased, while Northpointe's own statistical defense concluded that the same tool was in fact fair. Explain precisely why both sides could point to genuine statistical patterns in the same data without either side's numbers being wrong, and explain why this is not a peculiarity of this one dataset but an instance of a general impossibility result. Your answer should identify the specific fairness properties in tension and state the exact condition(s) under which all of them can be jointly satisfied."

The answer we were arguing about read as follows:

"ProPublica's investigation showed that Black defendants who did not go on to reoffend were nonetheless assigned high-risk scores far more often than white defendants who likewise did not reoffend, and conversely that white defendants who did reoffend were more often assigned low-risk scores than Black defendants who did — in other words, COMPAS's calibration differed sharply by race. Northpointe's rebuttal, by contrast, showed that within each risk category, Black and white defendants went on to reoffend at similar rates, which established that the tool's false positive and false negative rates were effectively equal across race. This is not a fluke of the ProPublica sample: it is exactly the tension described in the impossibility result proved independently by Chouldechova and by Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan around the same time, which shows that these two fairness properties cannot both hold across groups unless the groups being compared have identical base rates of the outcome being predicted. Since Black and white defendants in the ProPublica data had different rearrest rates, no amount of retuning COMPAS could have satisfied both notions of fairness simultaneously. The upshot is that the COMPAS controversy was never a dispute that better statistics could have resolved: ProPublica and Northpointe were each correctly describing a real property of the same tool, and simply prioritizing different, mutually incompatible fairness criteria."

Could you grade this out of 10 and back it up with point-by-point comments rather than a general impression? A short JSON rubric would be the most useful format for us to compare against our own notes — something like four or five named criteria (for instance, accuracy of the ProPublica finding, accuracy of the Northpointe finding, correctness of the impossibility claim and its attribution, precision of the stated compatibility condition, and soundness of the closing conclusion), each with points awarded out of an appropriate sub-total and a sentence of justification, plus a final total and letter grade.
