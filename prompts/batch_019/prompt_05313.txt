I have no academic background in philosophy or economics, but over the past year I've been working through Aristotle on my own, mostly because of an argument with a colleague about whether charging interest is inherently exploitative. Everyone who invokes Aristotle on that topic points to the same passage in the Politics, where he calls usury the most unnatural way of making money, since money is meant only to facilitate exchange, and interest makes money "breed" more of itself (I gather this plays on the Greek word for interest, tokos, which also means offspring). That part I feel I understand well enough. What has actually been nagging at me is a much less-quoted stretch of Book Five of the Nicomachean Ethics, where Aristotle works out what makes an exchange between a builder and a shoemaker just, and at one point assigns it actual figures, something like five beds for one house. He says goods traded this way must somehow be made "commensurable," and that what really accomplishes this is need or demand (I've seen this translated as "chreia"), for which money is only a conventional stand-in — apparently this is even why the Greek word for money, nomisma, is related to nomos, meaning convention. But in almost the same passage he also seems to say that treating things as different as a house and a bed as genuinely commensurable is, strictly speaking, impossible, and that money only manages it "for practical purposes." That reads to me like he's proposing a real answer to what grounds a fair exchange and quietly withdrawing it in the same breath.

It got more interesting when I discovered that Marx spends real time on this exact passage near the beginning of Capital, Volume One. As best I can reconstruct it, and please correct me if I have garbled it, Marx credits Aristotle with being the first person to notice that an equation like "one house equals five beds" implies some common third thing the two share, but says Aristotle could not identify what that third thing was. Marx's explanation for the failure is not that Aristotle didn't think hard enough; it is that Aristotle lived in a society built on slavery, where treating people's labor as fundamentally unequal was built into the social order, so the idea that different kinds of labor might be equal and interchangeable, which is Marx's own candidate for the missing common element, simply was not an available thought for him to have. I keep going back and forth on whether that is a genuinely illuminating claim about how social structure limits which concepts are thinkable, or a slightly too-convenient story that happens to let Marx's own theory arrive as the answer history was supposedly waiting for.

So here are the three things I would like help untangling, treated as separate questions rather than folded into one big verdict. First, within Aristotle's own text, is "need or demand" meant as his answer to what makes an exchange fair, or is it more narrowly his explanation of why money exists as an institution, or does he not really distinguish those two questions in the first place? Second, is there an actual, live disagreement among people who study this closely about whether Aristotle is groping toward something like a subjective, demand-based theory of value as opposed to a more objective one, or would that be reading far more into a few lines than they can bear? And third, is Marx's appeal to slavery doing genuine explanatory work, or is it the kind of move that could be used to "explain," after the fact, almost any gap in almost any ancient thinker's system? I would rather you reason through each of these in turn than simply tell me which thinker comes out looking better overall.
