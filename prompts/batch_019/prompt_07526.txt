(b) Explain why philosophers of action found this result significant — what exactly does it put pressure on? Here's the answer, more or less verbatim:

"Knobe's vignette involves the chairman of a company who is presented with a new program that will increase profits but will also have a side effect on the environment. In one version the side effect is harm to the environment, in the other it's a benefit. Either way, the chairman says he doesn't care at all about the environment, he just wants to make as much profit as possible, and he starts the program. Knobe found a striking asymmetry: about 82% of subjects said the chairman had intentionally harmed the environment, but only around 23% said he had intentionally helped it, even though his beliefs (he knew the side effect would occur) and desires (indifference to it) were exactly the same across both versions.

This mattered to philosophers of action because the going assumption was that whether something counts as intentional should supervene on the agent's psychological states alone — his beliefs and desires — so two scenarios that match on beliefs and desires ought to get the same verdict. Knobe's results broke that assumption, and Knobe's own diagnosis is that this is a case of moral judgment contaminating an otherwise neutral factual judgment: disapproval of the harm leaks backward into subjects' assessment of the chairman's state of mind, making them see an intention that isn't really there. On this reading the asymmetry is a kind of mistake — exactly the sort of thing a good theory of intentional action should correct for rather than accommodate.

An alternative comes from Alicke's culpable control model, developed to explain blame attribution generally: once someone strikes us as blameworthy, we inflate our estimate of their control, foresight, and intent to fit that judgment, and this has nothing specifically to do with the semantics of 'intentionally.' But I don't think this is really a rival explanation of Knobe's effect, because the asymmetry is tied to that particular word: if you ask people instead whether the chairman 'decided' to harm or help the environment, or whether he was 'in favor of' the outcome, the asymmetry disappears, which suggests the effect is a fact about how 'intentionally' works in English rather than a general product of blame. A mechanism as general as Alicke's would predict the asymmetry everywhere, which isn't what we find."
