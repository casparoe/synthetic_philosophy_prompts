Here's the question:
The "boundary problem" in democratic theory asks how the boundaries of the demos -- the group entitled to participate in a given collective decision -- can themselves be legitimately and non-circularly determined.
(a) Briefly explain why Frederick Whelan thought this problem can't be solved by appeal to democratic procedures themselves.
(b) Compare the "all-affected interests" principle and the "all-subjected to coercion" principle as responses to the boundary problem, and give one case where they'd yield different verdicts about who belongs in the demos.
(c) Does either principle fully escape the circularity Whelan identified? Explain.

And here's the student's answer:
(a) Whelan's point was that democracy is a procedure for making decisions once you already know who the decision-makers are, but it can't tell you who those decision-makers should be in the first place -- settling that democratically would require an already-constituted electorate to vote on it, which just pushes the question back a step. So for Whelan, the composition of the demos has to be fixed by something outside democratic theory entirely, like history or geography.
(b) Goodin's all-affected interests principle says anyone whose interests are impacted by a decision should get a say in it, while Abizadeh's all-subjected principle says anyone coerced by a decision should get a say. In practice these two principles will usually pick out the same group of people, since being coerced by a law is generally what it means for your interests to be affected by it. One case where they'd actually disagree is climate change: a small island state that will be flooded by rising seas is clearly affected by the emissions policies of big industrial states, so the affected-interests principle includes its citizens in the demos for those policies, even though those citizens aren't coerced by the industrial states' domestic law in any direct way -- so the coercion principle would tend to leave them out.
(c) Goodin's principle escapes the circularity, because it grounds membership in an objective fact -- whether your interests are causally affected -- rather than a political decision about who counts, so there's no regress the way there is for Whelan's problem. Abizadeh's coercion principle doesn't fully escape it though, since you still need a decision about what counts as "coercion" before you know who's included.
