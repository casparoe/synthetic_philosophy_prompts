So last week I got into this whole arguement with a coworker, Jae, over lunch that's honestly been living in my head ever since. We work on the subscription side of things at my company, and our churn model spits out stuff like "this customer has a 24% chance of cancelling in the next 30 days," and someone on the growth team was using that score to decide whether to email this one specific customer a retention discount. Jae's whole point, standing there looking at the dashboard with me, was that the 24% is basically a fake number once you apply it to one person, because depending on which slice of past customers you compare them to (same plan tier, same signup cohort, same usage pattern, all of those at once) you'd get a totally different percentage each time, and theres no non-arbitrary way to say which slice is "the right one" for THIS customer. Taken far enough, he said, the customer is a group of exactly one and the whole idea of a frequency just breaks down. I pushed back that this can't be right, since the model is validated and calibrated in aggregate and literally every company in this space acts on individual scores like this, so clearly the number has to mean something.

Later that night I looked it up and it turns out Jae and I were just badly reenacting an actual decades-old debate in philosophy of probability called the "reference class problem" (there's a wiki page and everything, I felt kind of dumb for thinking we'd stumbled onto something new). I tried asking a chatbot to just tell me who's right and it gave me this mushy "both sides have a point" summary that didn't commit to anything or show what the actual disagreement even looks like in practice. What I'd really want instead is a proper dialogue, like a transcript, between two people holding real, well-argued versions of me and Jae's positions, hashing it out over something concrete like the churn-score example (or whatever case makes the problem sharpest). I want them to actually engage with each other's strongest objections instead of either one being a pushover, and ideally it shouldn't end with one side just steamrolling the other, since from what I can tell this is still a genuinely live disagreement among people who think about probability for a living.
