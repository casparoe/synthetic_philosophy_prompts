I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of promising this term, and I've been asked to help prepare questions for next week's department colloquium speaker. She defends a Scanlonian 'assurance' account of promising, built around something like his Principle F: what makes breaking a promise wrong is not that it disappoints the promisee's reliance, but that the promisor has intentionally created a mutually recognized assurance of performance and then failed to follow through, absent justification or the promisee's release. Please draft seven or eight questions that go beyond a restatement of the view. Include at least one built on the classic 'dying friend' promise, where no future reliance or ongoing assurance is even possible, to see what is left to ground the obligation; one asking whether grounding the wrong in a general norm against exploiting expectations collapses promising into a special case of anti-manipulation morality, losing whatever is distinctive about the performative act itself; and one using a promisee's power to release a promisor with no change in the promisee's actual expectations, to ask whether it is the promisee's will, rather than the state of assurance, doing the real normative work. Give each a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.
