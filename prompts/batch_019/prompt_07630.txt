So I'm a retired marine biologist — spent something like 35 years studying colonial invertebrates, siphonophores mostly, the group that includes the Portugese man o' war — and now that I'm retired I finally have time for all the philosophy I always felt guilty about skipping. A few months back I joined a little online reading group that's slowly working through the "classics," and we've just started on Leibniz's Monadology. I got stuck almost immediately, on sections 1 through 3 (this is the Latta translation, or close to it — found it on one of those public-domain philosophy sites):

"1. The Monad, of which we shall here speak, is nothing but a simple substance, which enters into compounds; simple, that is to say, without parts.
2. And there must be simple substances, since there are compounds; for a compound is nothing but a collection or aggregatum of simple substances.
3. Now where there are no parts, there can be neither extension, nor form, nor divisibility. These monads are the real atoms of nature and, in a word, the elements of things."

It amused me that he calls monads "the real atoms of nature" — not what a chemist would mean by that, obviously, but a funny echo anyway. What's actually tripping me up is the bit in section 2, where a compound is said to be "nothing but" a heap of simple substances. Taken at face value, doesn't that mean that, on Leibniz's view, there's strictly speaking no such thing as a rock, or a table, or even a whole animal — that these are just words we use for pluralities of monads, with no unity of their own except maybe in our own heads? And yet much later in the book he talks about a living thing being held together by a single "dominant" monad, in a way that seems meant to make an organism a genuinely unified thing and not just a heap of matter that happens to sit next to itself. So which is it: are organisms real composite things and rocks aren't, or is he saying that strictly speaking nothing is real except the monads themselves, full stop, and all the organism talk is just a looser, more forgivable way of speaking? I spent my whole career on borderline cases like the man o' war, which is really a colony of specialized little animals behaving as one, so the question of when a bunch of separate things add up to one further thing has been in my wheelhouse for decades — I just never expected to run into it in a 300-year-old book about "simple substances" that supposedly have no parts at all. I'd love help figuring out exactly what Leibniz is committed to here, and whether he (or people who've worked on him since) ever settled on a principled line between the two kinds of case.
