I am several chapters into a novel set in a Massachusetts village in the late seventeenth century. My protagonist, Patience, hides her childhood friend, Verity, when men come to arrest Verity for witchcraft. The complication I am trying to get right is this: Patience never stops believing, in the abstract, that witches are real and that most of the accusations already made in the village were probably justified. She simply cannot believe it of Verity in particular, and it is that private, unprincipled attachment, rather than any dawning skepticism about the trials themselves, that moves her to act. My writing group has split over whether this makes her act less admirable than it would be if she acted from a considered conviction that the accusations were unjust in general. One member, recalling a college ethics course, mentioned that philosophers argue over exactly this kind of case using Huckleberry Finn's decision not to turn Jim in: Huck never talks himself out of believing that helping a runaway slave is a sin, and he helps Jim anyway because he cannot bring himself to do otherwise to Jim in particular. She said there is a real debate about whether an act like that has full moral worth, and about what must be true of an agent's reasons for her to deserve credit for doing the right thing rather than merely having done it.

I would like an essay on moral worth and acting for the right reasons that takes this debate seriously rather than flattening it into "motives matter" against "only outcomes matter." Please reconstruct, as strongly as you can, at least three positions: first, that moral worth requires the agent's motivating reasons to coincide with whatever reasons actually make the act right, even if she never recognizes them as such; second, that this is too demanding, and that mere non-culpable responsiveness to the right-making features of a situation is enough by itself, whatever the agent believes about the act's rightness; and third, that both of these are mistaken, and that worth still requires acting from a settled commitment to doing what is right or what duty demands. For this third position, also give your own answer to the standard charge against it: that it makes the good person sound like someone in love with her own righteousness rather than with the person in front of her. Do not leave the three positions in equilibrium: argue for the one you take to be closest to correct, and tell me plainly what your verdict implies about whether Patience deserves moral credit for hiding Verity.
