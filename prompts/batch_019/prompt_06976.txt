Hey - I write a small science-and-ideas newsletter on the side, and I'm drafting an issue about the psychology of blame that leans heavily on the Knobe effect (the "chairman" experiments from experimental philosophy). I actually first heard about this from a philosopher I interviewed for a totally unrelated story a while back, and I've been chewing on it since, but my actual background is cog-sci reporting, not philosophy, so I'm worried I've smoothed over some real disagreements in the field just to make the piece punchier. Before I file it, could you read the paragraph below and tell me honestly where its shaky?

"Back in 2003 the philosopher Joshua Knobe ran a deceptively simple experiment that's become a classic of 'experimental philosophy.' People read about a company chairman who's told a new policy will boost profits but hurt the environment as a side effect; he says he doesnt care about the environment, only the profit, and goes ahead anyway. Nearly everyone who reads this says he harmed the environment intentionally. Flip the story so the same policy helps the environment instead, keep the chairman's 'I don't care, I just want the profit' line exactly the same, and now almost nobody says he helped it intentionally. The only thing that changed is whether the side effect was good or bad, and that alone is enough to flip our verdict about what was going on in his head. Knobe's own reading of this, which has basically become the standard one, is that our everyday concept of 'intentional action' was never the neutral, purely psychological thing philosophers assumed - it has a moral evaluation built right into it. That has consequences well outside the seminar room: if jurors lean on this same folk concept when they decide whether a defendant acted 'intentionally,' then how much they already blame the defendant for the outcome could quietly be doing the work of a factual finding that's supposed to be settled independently of blame."

A few specific things I'd love pushback on: (1) am I overselling Knobe's own "it's baked into the concept" story as the settled consensus, when I have this nagging memory that there are rival explanations floating around out there (something to do with statistical norms, something to do with a "deep self," maybe a culpable-control angle) that I'm just quietly not mentioning; (2) is the jump to jurors and mens rea an actual live idea people in the field take seriously, or does it just sound like a journalist reaching for a dramatic closing line; and (3), separate from accuracy, is this even a fresh angle at this point or is the chairman/environment case so overused that anyone who's taken one cog-sci class will roll their eyes reading it. Be blunt rather than nice, typos and all - I wrote this at like 11pm.
