Random topic but this has actually been on my mind a lot lately: I adopted a senior rescue dog a couple months ago (shes about 11, sweet old girl, and part of why I got her is that I've been wanting to be more thoughtful about how I treat animals generally) and it's sent me down a whole rabbit hole on animal ethics. Over dinner last week my friend who did philosophy in undergrad was walking me through why Kant thought we don't have direct duties to animals, and the way she explained it, his reasoning was basically the same as Descartes': animals are essentially organic machines, stimulus-response systems with no real inner feeling, so there's technically nothing there to wrong. On her account, that's exactly why Kant could still say cruelty to animals is bad, but only "indirectly" bad, i.e. because it might make us callous toward other people, not because the animal itself has any stake in the matter.

That struck me as a pretty big problem, though? We now know from neuroscience, and honestly just from watching any dog, that animals clearly feel pain and have something like emotions. So if Kant's whole indirect-duty argument was resting on the empirical premise that animals are basically unfeeling automatons, and that premise turned out to be false, shouldn't the argument just collapse? Is "we only have indirect duties to animals" a position serious philosophers still actually defend today, or is it basically a relic that only made sense back when people had Descartes-style assumptions about animal minds? Trying to figure out how much weight to put on this before I start feeling guilty about things like what dog food I buy her.
