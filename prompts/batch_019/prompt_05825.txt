I've been following the discourse around ARC's report on Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK) since it first went up on the Alignment Forum, and a disagreement about it flared up at a reading group I attend that I still haven't managed to resolve in my own head. Everyone in the group takes the standard SmartVault case as given: a Predictor network, trained to protect a diamond, ends up with an accurate internal model of whether the diamond is really still in the vault, and the goal is to train a separate "Reporter" to answer questions about the vault honestly, using only human labels generated by watching a camera feed the Predictor could in principle learn to fool.

Two people in the group, Nadia and Owen, went back and forth on whether a particular fix rescues the project:

(1) Nadia argued that we should penalize the Reporter according to how much of the Predictor's internal computation its answers depend on, since faithfully reading off a single latent "is the diamond there" variable should be far cheaper than simulating an entire human's judgment from raw sensor data -- so a complexity or speed penalty should favor the direct translator over the human simulator.

(2) Owen objected that this doesn't obviously work: if the Predictor is capable enough to plan actions that fool a human watching the feed, it must already be computing something like "what would this footage lead a human to conclude" somewhere inside itself, just to select good deceptive actions -- so a human-simulator Reporter can piggyback on machinery the network builds anyway, while the direct translator may need genuinely new circuitry to bridge the Predictor's own idiosyncratic internal ontology to human concepts. If anything, he said, this could make the human simulator the cheaper of the two.

(3) Nadia responded that we could route around this by cross-examining the Reporter with batteries of logically related counterfactual questions instead of penalizing raw compute -- a human simulator misled by doctored footage should eventually contradict itself once probed cleverly enough across enough correlated queries, in a way a genuine direct translator would not.

(4) Owen replied that this only raises the bar rather than closing the argument: a sufficiently high-fidelity simulation of a careful, logically consistent human reasoner will satisfy any fixed battery of consistency checks we write down in advance, because consistency is a property of the simulated reasoning process, not of whether the picture it's reasoning about is true -- so there is no way to specify, in advance, a behavioral test that every direct translator passes and every possible human simulator fails.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know the literature well enough to referee it myself. Could you work through moves (1) through (4) individually and say whether each is correct, partly correct, or mistaken, given how the ELK problem is actually set up? Close with a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>}, scoring each move from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), and then tell me what you think Nadia's and Owen's positions, taken together, are still missing.
