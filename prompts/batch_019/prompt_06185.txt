So I went down a rabbit hole this weekend reading about Bayesian decision theory and hit this thing called Good's theorem -- I.J. Good's proof that for a rational Bayesian, the expected value of getting a free look at more evidence before you decide is never negative. Basically you can never expect to loose out by looking, as long as you go on to act optimally on whatever you see. Sounds airtight, but it bugs me, cause theres a whole pile of "strategic ignorance" stuff that sounds like the opposite: people who don't want their genetic test results even though it wouldn't change anything they'd actually do, negotiators who'd rather not know their own walk-away number, a manager who avoids reading the file so they can honestly say they didn't know.

Write me a dialogue between two equally sharp Bayesians hashing this out. One is defending the theorem and thinks each of those examples either isn't actually free/costless, or quietly brings in another agent who reacts to what you now know, or isn't really about which action you'd take at all -- so none of them are real counterexamples. The other thinks that once you carve out all those exceptions there's basically nothing left of the theorem that applies to real decisions. Have them work through at least two of the examples in actual detail, pinning down exactly which assumption is supposedly being violated in each, instead of just trading slogans back and forth. Neither one gets to win by the other just giving up.
