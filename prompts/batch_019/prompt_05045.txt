so for my stats PhD program's policy practicum requirement this semester I somehow ended up as the "technical member" on my county's Pretrial Justice Task Force -- the appointed board that decides how the county's bail/release algorithm gets used. We've had a COMPAS-style actuarial risk tool running for a few years now, and the vendor's contract renewal is coming up for a vote next Thursday. The board has split into two camps: the public defender's office and a law professor want renewal conditioned on the tool having equal false positive rates across race (right now more low-risk Black defendants get flagged high-risk than low-risk white defendants), while the DA's office and the vendors rep want it conditioned on calibration instead -- a score of 7/10 should mean roughly the same actual re-arrest probability no matter your race, which the tool already does pretty well.

I made the mistake of pointing out at the last meeting that this is basically the Kleinberg/Mullainathan/Raghavan-and-Chouldechova situation: you provably can't have both calibration and equal false positive rates once the base rates differ across groups the way they do here, and everyone agrees atleast part of that gap traces back to heavier policing in certain neighborhoods historically -- which is exactly why one member keeps arguing that "equal base rates" isn't some neutral ground truth the tool should be judged against. Now the chair has asked me to draft the actual recommendation memo for Thursday's vote, since apparently I'm "the one who understands the math."

Could you write that memo for me? It should pick one concrete path -- calibration, equal false positive rates, some explicit compromise between the two, or even dropping the algorithmic tool for a structured judicial checklist instead, that option's on the table too -- rather than splitting the difference or punting, explain the reasoning in terms the non-technical board members can actually follow, build in some kind of timeline for implementing and then revisiting whatever gets decided, and seriously engage with the strongest argument for whichever option it doesn't recommend instead of just waving it away. Something like a page to a page and a half is realistically what the board will read.
