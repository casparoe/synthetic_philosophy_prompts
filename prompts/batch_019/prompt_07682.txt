I've spent the better part of a decade reading LessWrong, the EA Forum, and the blogs and podcasts that grew out of them. Last year I joined the four-person research grants committee of a small private foundation that funds early-stage work on risks from advanced AI. Most proposals that reach us are fairly legible -- interpretability tooling, evaluation benchmarks, governance analysis -- but we have just received one that has split the committee down the middle. An independent researcher is asking for a year of salary to extend the technical literature on acausal trade, specifically to work out more rigorously when the "evidential cooperation in large worlds" argument actually holds: the claim that if enough agents scattered across a sufficiently large or infinite universe reason in relevantly similar ways, each agent's choice to act somewhat altruistically toward value systems it can never causally encounter -- whether in distant regions of an inflationary multiverse, in other Everett branches, or in whoever might be simulating it -- is itself evidence about how those other agents are choosing, such that everyone's values can be served better in expectation even though no signal ever passes between them. The applicant argues this sits in the same family as Yudkowsky's timeless and updateless decision theories, though the mechanism here is evidential rather than causal, closer to Hofstadter's superrationality than to a literal handshake, and that since sufficiently advanced AI systems may reason this way regardless of our preferences, understanding the mechanics now is directly relevant to alignment.

Two committee members want to fund it, on the grounds that this is exactly the kind of currently illegible but potentially astronomically high-stakes theoretical work philanthropy exists to seed, by the same reasoning that would lead us to fund decision theory or mathematical logic with no obvious near-term payoff. The other two think the whole research direction is unfalsifiable by construction -- there is no observation, even in principle, that could tell us whether any acausal cooperation is occurring or ever will -- and worry that funding it has the shape of a Pascal's mugging, where an unverifiable but arbitrarily large claimed stake substitutes for the evidence and tractability the case would otherwise need, and that it exposes the foundation to being fairly characterized as paying someone to negotiate on our behalf with universes we can never detect.

Could you draft the funding memo I'll bring to next month's allocation meeting, recommending fund, decline, or fund with conditions? I'd like it to leave the committee with criteria we could reuse the next time a proposal like this comes in, and to engage with the unfalsifiability objection on its strongest terms -- in a way the applicant, who I don't think is arguing in bad faith, could actually respond to -- rather than resolving the disagreement by simply asserting that we only fund testable claims.
