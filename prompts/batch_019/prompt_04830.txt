I'm working on a piece about how historians of economic thought are re-litigating the canon's relationship to empire, and Adam Smith is proving the hardest case to pin down. In *The Wealth of Nations* he devotes a long chapter to demolishing the economic case for colonies, arguing that the monopoly of colonial trade enriches a narrow class of merchants at the expense of the nation as a whole, and he condemns the violence and injustice Europeans inflicted on the peoples of the Americas and the East Indies. Yet in the book's closing pages, confronting Britain's crisis with its American colonies, he weighs two alternatives — binding them more closely to Britain through proportional representation in Parliament, or voluntarily relinquishing them — without ever quite deciding between them. Some historians of political thought read Smith's economic analysis as amounting to a genuinely principled case against empire as such; others argue that his four-stage theory of historical development (hunting, herding, farming, and commerce) and his willingness to entertain a more integrated imperial union show that he wanted to rescue and rationalize a "liberal" empire rather than dismantle it. Write an essay that lays out this controversy and engages seriously with the strongest versions of both readings. Don't hedge or split the difference — defend a clear conclusion about which reading better fits Smith's actual arguments.
