hey so i've been down a rabbit hole about vague predicates lately (started with an argument at work over how to handle boundary cases in a fraud-scoring rules engine, long story) and i found this old Bertrand Russell essay from 1923 called "Vagueness". most of it i can follow fine but this bit is messing with my head:

Let us consider the various ways in which common words are vague, and let us being with such a word as "red". It is perfectly obvious, since colours form a continuum, that there are shades of colour concerning which we shall be in doubt whether to call them red or not, not because we are ignorant of the meaning of the word "red", but because it is a word the extent of whose application is essentially doubtful. This, of course, is the answer to the old puzzle about the man who went bald. It is supposed that at first he was not bald, that he lost his hairs one by one, and that in the end he was bald; therefore, it is argued, there must have been one hair the loss of which converted him into a bald man. This, of course, is absurd. Baldness is a vague conception; some men are certainly bald, some are certainly not bald, while between them there are men of whom it is not true to say they must be either be bald or not bald. The law of excluded middle is true when precise symbols are employed, but it is not true when symbols are vague, as, in fact, all symbols are.

(that "being" instead of "begin" is genuinely in the original, not a typo i added, so don't worry about that part)

what i can't square is that last sentence with the stuff i've since read about epistemicism vs supervaluationism, which are apparently the two big modern rival answers to this exact bald-man puzzle. epistemicists supposedly say "he's bald or he's not" is just true, full stop, there's a real precise hair-count cutoff, we just can't ever know where it is. supervaluationists supposedly also want "he's bald or he's not" to come out true no matter what (true on every way of making "bald" precise), even though neither "he's bald" nor "he's not bald" is individually true. but russell here just flat out says the law of excluded middle fails for vague words, which doesn't sound like either camp to me. is he defending some third position that both modern camps would reject, or is there a charitable reading where he's basically already anticipating one of them and im just tripping over the old-fashioned prose? can you unpack exactly what claim russell is committed to here and how it actually lines up against epistemicism and supervaluationism?
