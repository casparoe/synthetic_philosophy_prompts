In my graduate seminar on the structure of epistemic justification this term, we spent two sessions on the "bootstrapping problem," which Jonathan Vogel raised against process reliabilism and which Stewart Cohen then generalized into what he called the "problem of easy knowledge." The stock illustration is Vogel's driver, Roxanne, whose gas gauge happens to be reliable but who has never checked it against any independent source. Each time she consults it, she forms two beliefs: that the gauge reads, say, "full," and that the tank is in fact full. After enough such pairings, ordinary enumerative induction seems to license the further belief that the gauge reliably indicates the tank's contents, even though nothing in the procedure ever puts her in touch with the tank except through the very instrument whose reliability is at issue. Cohen's contribution was to show that this is no special embarrassment for reliabilism: any view that allows "basic knowledge" -- knowledge or justified belief acquired from a source without antecedently knowing that source to be reliable, a category that also covers internalist foundationalism and James Pryor's dogmatism about perceptual justification -- seems to license the same suspiciously cheap route to higher-level knowledge of that source's reliability.

My students ended up in three camps, and before I settle on how to present the material next year, I would like your help thinking the dispute through properly. One camp wants to follow Nozick and Dretske in imposing a sensitivity or relevant-alternatives condition on knowledge, on the thought that Roxanne's higher-order belief that her gauge is reliable fails to track the truth (or fails to rule out the relevant alternative that it is not reliable) even though each step leading up to it looks individually impeccable -- and this camp is willing to swallow the well-known costs of that move elsewhere in the theory, including a restricted closure principle. A second camp prefers something closer to Cohen's own apparatus, which borrows a version of Ernest Sosa's animal/reflective knowledge distinction and restricts the way basic knowledge can combine with self-knowledge to generate inductive support, so as to explain which instances of bootstrapping are illegitimate without abandoning basic knowledge altogether. A third camp, taking its cue from Jonathan Weisberg's argument that the very same structure arises for an ordinary Bayesian agent conditionalizing on a run of reports from a source, thinks the whole dispute has been misdescribed as a problem specifically for reliabilism or dogmatism, when it is really an unavoidable feature of how evidential support accumulates for any epistemic agent whatsoever.

Write an essay that reconstructs the strongest form of the bootstrapping argument, engages seriously with at least two of these three responses, and defends your own considered verdict on the following question: does the possibility of bootstrapping show that there is something specifically defective about reliabilism and dogmatism, giving us reason to prefer theories on which justified belief in a source's reliability must be secured independently before that source can yield knowledge -- or is bootstrapping instead a cost that attaches to "basic," non-inferentially-grounded knowledge as such, in which case it is no special mark against any one theory of justification? I would rather you commit to a real position, even a heavily qualified one, than conclude that both sides simply have a point.
