After forty years directing a laboratory in renal physiology, I retired three years ago and, largely at the urging of a philosopher friend from my graduate-school days, have been working my way through some of the classic problems in early modern epistemology. Molyneux's problem has held my attention for several weeks now: the question, put to Locke by his friend William Molyneux and later discussed in the Essay, of whether a man born blind, who can already distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, would be able to tell which was which by sight alone the instant he was made to see, before ever touching them. Locke and Molyneux were both confident the answer was no. Leibniz, in the New Essays, is usually reported as taking the opposite side, but on closer reading his "yes" strikes me as considerably more hedged than that reputation suggests: if I have understood him rightly, he thought the newly sighted man could work out the correct answer by reasoning about the underlying geometry, not that he would simply see it at a glance -- which raises the possibility that Leibniz and Locke were less flatly opposed than they are usually made out to be, since they may have been answering different questions about different capacities.

What draws me back into this as a former experimentalist is that this is one of the few philosophical thought experiments to have since received something like an empirical test, in case studies of people who gained sight in mid-life after a lifetime of blindness -- Richard Gregory's well-known study of a patient known as S.B. in the 1960s, and more recent work associated with sight-restoration programs for the congenitally blind. As I recall, such patients cannot reliably match a seen shape to a felt one in the first days after the operation, though most improve substantially within a week or two of visual experience. On the face of it, this looks like a fairly clean vindication of Locke, and yet the philosophers I've read treat it as nothing of the sort.

I would like your help understanding why. Specifically, could you (a) explain how the gap between "recognizing at a glance" and "working it out by reasoning" bears on interpreting a learning curve measured in days rather than a single moment, and (b) set out the further complication I gather has been raised by philosophers in the "enactive" or sensorimotor tradition of perception, who apparently deny that these patients are simply failing to connect two ready-made concepts -- on their view, the newly sighted have not yet acquired the embodied perceptual skill that seeing a shape even consists in, so the case studies may be answering a rather different question than the one Molyneux and Locke intended to pose. I am especially curious whether there is any observation, even in principle, that could tell the "missing association" story apart from the "missing skill" story, or whether the two are too entangled ever to be teased apart empirically.
