Frederick Douglass and William Lloyd Garrison agreed, through the 1840s, that the United States Constitution was irredeemably compromised by slavery—Garrison famously calling it "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell." By his 1860 Glasgow address, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?," Douglass had broken with Garrison and reversed this verdict, arguing that a legal document's meaning must be fixed by its own words and stated ends—justice, the general welfare, "the blessings of liberty"—rather than by the framers' well-attested intentions or by subsequent government practice, and that because the Constitution never once uses the word "slavery," this silence should be read in the document's favor.

Please argue against Douglass's later position. Defend the claim that the Constitution's operative clauses—the three-fifths clause, the twenty-year moratorium on abolishing the international slave trade, and the fugitive-from-labor clause—fixed the document's meaning through their concrete legal effect, regardless of its silence about slavery by name or the nobility of its stated purposes, so that Garrison's harsher verdict was in fact the more defensible one. Your argument should engage concretely with at least two of these three clauses and with Douglass's own reasoning about them, rather than resting on the general moral case against slavery.
