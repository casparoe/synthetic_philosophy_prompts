I'm researching a feature on the long afterlife of Hume's problem of induction, and I want to frame part of it around an imagined confrontation between Hume and Nelson Goodman's "grue" paradox. As a refresher: Goodman defines an object as grue if it is green and has been examined before some future time t, or blue and has not been examined before t; every emerald anyone has ever observed is equally good evidence for "all emeralds are green" and for "all emeralds are grue," yet the two claims disagree about emeralds first examined after t. Goodman's point is that Hume's problem — justifying the leap from observed to unobserved cases — isn't the whole story, since before we can project any regularity at all we need a non-circular way to rule in predicates like green and rule out gerrymandered ones like grue, and no purely logical criterion seems to supply that.

How do you think Hume himself, arguing strictly from his own account of induction as custom and habit built out of the felt resemblance between impressions, would respond to Goodman? Lay out the response you think he would actually give: would his psychology already privilege green-type predicates as the natural units of association, giving him a way to sidestep the New Riddle that a purely formal theory of confirmation lacks, or does his framework leave him with no principled way to exclude grue, so that the puzzle undercuts his account just as much as anyone else's? Commit to one answer and defend it on Hume's own terms, rather than cataloguing the range of things he might conceivably have said.
