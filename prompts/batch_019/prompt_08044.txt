In my seminar on decision theory and multi-agent systems, we recently worked through Tennenholtz's program-equilibrium framework and its refinement via provability logic: the result that two 'FairBot'-style programs, each cooperating in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma only when it can prove that the other cooperates with it, end up mutually cooperating as a consequence of Löb's theorem, and that a further refinement like PrudentBot preserves this outcome while no longer naively cooperating with unconditional cooperators. I can't quite decide how much philosophical weight this result can bear, so I'd like to see the disagreement played out directly. Could you write a short dialogue between two decision theorists — one a committed proponent of the provability-logic approach to rational agency, the other a more traditional game theorist — who are advising their respective clients on which program to submit in a one-shot program-equilibrium Prisoner's Dilemma? The first should argue that the Löbian construction is a genuine, non-question-begging vindication of rational cooperation, showing that transparency plus correct reasoning can do work that used to require repetition or external enforcement. The second should argue that this is an artifact of the formalism rather than a philosophical achievement: mutual defection remains an equally valid program equilibrium, exploitative programs against naive opponents are still available, and the whole construction presupposes exactly the kind of shared, trusted proof system and mutual provability that no bounded real-world agent can guarantee — so that 'Löb's theorem made them cooperate' describes a mechanism without justifying the prior choice to run FairBot rather than something else. Please have the two characters engage with each other's actual arguments, conceding points where warranted, rather than delivering parallel monologues, and don't let either position come off as the obviously weaker one.
