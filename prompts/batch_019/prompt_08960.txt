My housemate and I are both taking a seminar on formal epistemology this term, and last night the assigned reading on Dutch book arguments turned into a genuine argument between us that we never managed to resolve. She defended the classical line: if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie can offer you a set of bets that you would each accept individually, by your own lights, but that are jointly guaranteed to cost you money no matter how the world turns out -- and she takes this, on its own, to show that incoherent credences are irrational in a full-blooded epistemic sense, not merely inconvenient. I pushed back with the depragmatizing worry associated with Christensen: that sure-loss vulnerability shows only that an agent is exploitable by a bookie who is simply stipulated to already know her betting quotients and to be able to force a whole package of bets on her at once, which looks like an artifact of a rigged decision situation rather than a genuine mark against her epistemic standing. She answered that the converse Dutch book theorem blocks this escape, since it shows coherence is necessary, and not merely sufficient, for avoiding exploitability, so the vulnerability really does track something about the credences themselves. I tried gesturing at Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument as a way of getting a non-pragmatic version of the same conclusion, but neither of us could settle whether it actually avoids smuggling in betting-like assumptions about what is being traded off against what, or whether it just relocates the problem one level up.

Since we ended up talking in circles for the better part of an hour, I would like to see the dispute conducted properly by two people who can actually track every move. Could you write a dialogue between a philosopher who holds that the classical Dutch book argument is, by itself, sufficient to establish probabilistic coherence as a requirement of epistemic rationality, and a philosopher who presses the depragmatizing challenge against it? I would like the second speaker to explain concretely why exploitability by a bookie is only a practical failing rather than an epistemic one, and the first speaker to give a substantive answer rather than simply invoking the converse Dutch book theorem as though that settled the matter. If the second speaker wants to bring in something like Joyce's accuracy-based argument as an alternative route to probabilism, I would like them to be pressed on whether it genuinely stands free of the pragmatic argument or covertly depends on similar assumptions. Please write it as a real exchange in which each side responds to what the other has just said, rather than as two monologues in sequence, and keep it reasonably compact.
