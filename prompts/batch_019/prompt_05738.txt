Ok so I've been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs for something like a decade now (LW, the diaspora blogs, ACX, that whole cluster), and "hinge propositions" is one of those phrases that gets thrown around alot whenever someone wants to gesture at the idea of beliefs so basic you can't actually update on them, they're more like the frame the updating happens in. I'd always just absorbed the concept secondhand through blog posts though, so a couple weeks ago I finally sat down and read the actual source, Wittgenstein's On Certainty (the notes he was scribbling in roughly the last year and a half of his life, published posthumously). Most of it clicks fine on a first pass, but there's a tension between an early passage and the much more famous "hinge" passage later on that I can't hold together in my head, and my reading group didn't really resolve it either, so figured I'd bring it here instead.

Early on he writes:

§95. The propositions describing this world-picture might be part of a kind of mythology. And their role is like that of rules of a game; and the game can be learned purely practically, without learning any explicit rules.
§96. It might be imagined that some propositions, of the form of empirical propositions, were hardened and functioned as channels for such empirical propositions as were not hardened but fluid; and that this relation altered with time, in that fluid propositions hardened, and hard ones became fluid.
§97. The mythology may change back into a state of flux, the river-bed of thoughts may shift. But I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself; though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other.
§98. But if someone were to say "So logic too is an empirical science" he would be wrong. Yet this is right: the same proposition may get treated at one time as something to test by experience, at another as a rule of testing.
§99. And the bank of that river consists partly of hard rock, subject to no alteration or only to an imperceptible one, partly of sand, which now in one place now in another gets washed away, or deposited.

Then much later, in the bit everyone actually quotes:

§341. That is to say, the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn.
§342. That is to say, it belongs to the logic of our scientific investigations that certain things are in deed not doubted.
§343. But it isn't that the situation is like this: We just can't investigate everything, and for that reason we are forced to rest content with assumption. If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put.

My problem is that 343 seems to require that hinges literally cannot move - that's the whole point of the metaphor, a hinge that swings isn't doing its job as a hinge. But 96-99 is explicitly describing hardened propositions turning fluid and fluid ones hardening, the riverbed itself shifting over time. So which is it? Does "exempt from doubt" mean something like "permanently outside the space of things this practice can revise" (in which case the river-bed passage is describing a genuinely different, weaker phenomenon - ordinary belief change, not hinge-change), or is Wittgenstein actually fine with hinges themselves being temporary and revisable, in which case I don't get why he'd phrase 343 so absolutely - "the hinges must stay put" sure sounds stronger than "well, for now at least."

There's a related thing some of us in the reading group kept getting stuck on: people round these parts like to gloss hinge propositions as basically an "ur-prior" - the fixed thing sitting outside the whole updating process and so never itself getting Bayesian-updated, along the lines of what shows up in the updateless-decision-theory stuff on LW. But if hinges can harden and soften the way 96-99 describes, that sounds less like an ur-prior (which by construction doesn't change) and more like just a prior with unusually high inertia. Is that mapping onto Bayesian priors defensible, or does it quietly assume hinges are truth-apt propositions that get assigned credences at all, which I have a feeling Wittgenstein wants to deny? And when a riverbed genuinely does shift - when a hinge stops being a hinge - does he give us anything like an account of that transition being rational or evidence-responsive, or is the whole point that from inside the game you never see it coming, more like a Kuhnian paradigm shift? This feels adjacent to the regress problem Yudkowsky pokes at in "Where Recursive Justification Hits Bottom" - I'm curious whether you think Wittgenstein's hinge/riverbed picture is actually a rival answer to that regress, or more of a refusal to accept that the question needs one. Would love a close reading of what's actually going on across these two passages rather than the blog-post-summary version I already know.
