I'm working through the 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange between H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller for a jurisprudence seminar, and a suggestion a classmate raised in discussion section about the "grudge informer" case has been nagging at me ever since. As Hart and Fuller present the case, a woman denounces her husband to the authorities under Nazi-era statutes after he criticizes Hitler while home on leave; he is condemned but narrowly avoids execution, and once the war is over she is prosecuted for having unlawfully deprived him of his liberty. Her defense turns on the claim that his sentence had been lawfully imposed, since the court that convicted him was simply applying statutes that were in force at the time. Rather than follow the German courts in holding, in the spirit of Radbruch's formula, that those wartime statutes were never truly valid law because of their iniquity, Hart recommends a different route: concede that the statutes were law and that the wife may have acted within it, and then, if punishing her still seems necessary, do so openly through a retroactive criminal statute. He maintains that this is the more honest option, and that it does less damage to the rule-of-law value of legal certainty than Fuller's strategy of treating sufficiently iniquitous enactments as failing to count as law at all — a strategy he suggests licenses courts to override valid law whenever it strikes them as intolerable.

My classmate's suggestion, which I would like you to develop into a full argument, is that this certainty-based charge actually cuts the other way. Please construct the strongest case you can for the conclusion that it is Hart's own solution, rather than Fuller's, that does more damage to legal certainty in this case, focusing specifically on the rule-of-law value of non-retroactivity. The thought to develop is that Fuller's account, if accepted, implies that convicting the wife involves no retroactive lawmaking at all, since on his view the wartime statute never qualified as law in the first place, whereas Hart's own preferred fix requires an admittedly retroactive statute. Please engage directly with Hart's charge that Fuller's route is the more dangerous and destabilizing one, rather than resting content with the general observation that positivism and natural law simply disagree about the relation between law and morality.
