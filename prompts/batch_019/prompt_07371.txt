I'm a novelist, and I sit on the education advisory board of my city's museum of social and economic history, an institution that models its exhibits on Otto Neurath's old Vienna project of teaching statistics to ordinary visitors through standardized pictograms rather than prose. For its new permanent exhibit on how the regional workforce and its wages have shifted since 1980, the contracted design studio wants to break with strict Isotype practice: scaling individual icons up or down to show change instead of repeating a fixed-size icon (ten identical worker-figures rather than five), and cutting the small print on sample sizes, revisions, and contested figures that now runs beneath each panel, so that visitors can read a panel "at a glance." The museum's historian objects that this drops exactly the discipline Neurath insisted on — constant units, multiplied, with the harder evidentiary work left to accompanying text — and that the result will look cleaner while becoming quietly less honest. The board meets in three weeks to approve or send back the design, and I've been asked to write the opinion we'll discuss: please draft that memo, recommending (a) the studio's version, (b) strict Isotype conventions with full caveats, or (c) a specific, documented compromise, with reasoning that takes seriously the studio's strongest point — that Neurath himself cared about pictures actually being looked at, not only about their being technically honest.
