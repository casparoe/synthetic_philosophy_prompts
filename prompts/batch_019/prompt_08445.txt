I host a small podcast, born out of years spent reading rationalist blogs and forums, and my listeners expect real argumentative pressure rather than a restatement of the view. Next week's guest is a Thomist scholar who defends a "unified reading" of Aquinas on tyrannicide: she argues that the caution in De Regimine Principum against private individuals moving against a tyrant, and the claim in the Summa's treatment of sedition (II-II, q. 42) that a tyrant's own rule is what truly deserves the name "seditious," are two applications of one principle rather than an unresolved tension. Her stock response when pressed is that Aquinas was "a careful lawyer, not a revolutionary." Draft eight to ten questions that make that line progressively harder to fall back on. I want at least one question on why remedying what is essentially a principal-agent failure -- a ruler diverting the common good to his own -- should be reserved for "public authority" rather than any private citizen able to act, given that Aquinas holds that the tyrant has already forfeited his title to rule; one on whether his warnings about factional chaos and a worse successor make his opposition instrumental rather than terminal, such that a cleanly executed tyrannicide would stop being objectionable; and one on whether his failure to sharply distinguish a usurper who never held legitimate title from a rightful ruler turned tyrannical undercuts the unified reading. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close with a question about whether Juan de Mariana, who later invoked Aquinas to license private tyrannicide, was extending his logic or breaking with it.
