I'm working on a piece about the fine-tuning argument — the claim that the precise values of constants such as the cosmological constant and the strong nuclear force are so improbably well-suited to life that chance alone cannot be a satisfying explanation, leaving design and the multiverse as the main rivals. In researching the argument's philosophical pedigree, I returned to Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, where Philo raises several distinct objections to Cleanthes' design argument: that the analogy to human artifacts licenses at most a finite, flawed, or composite deity (perhaps a committee of minor gods, like laborers collaborating on a house); that apparent order might arise through something closer to generation or vegetation than to the contrivance of a mind; and, in what is often called his Epicurean hypothesis, that matter left to chance over infinite time would eventually and unsurprisingly settle into a stable arrangement. My question: the contemporary fine-tuning argument isn't really Cleanthes' argument revisited — it's usually run as a probabilistic, likelihood-style inference from the constants themselves, not as an analogy to watches or houses. If Hume could be briefed on the modern version in that form, which of Philo's objections would still do real work against it, which would need substantial retooling, and does the Epicurean hypothesis effectively anticipate today's multiverse reply, or is the parallel weaker than it looks? Please work through the objections one at a time rather than offering only an overall verdict on how Hume would have voted, and feel free to flag if treating Philo's arguments as simply Hume's own is itself questionable.
