I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist -- spent about thirty-five years studying how electrons move through semiconductors before I hung up my lab coat. These days it's mostly gardening and, lately, reading a lot of philosophy on my own, nothing formal, just whatever grabs me off the library shelf. Something's been bugging me though that feels like it belongs more to the new hobby than to medicine, even though it started as a medical thing. About two years ago I had an ICD put in -- that's the little device wired into your heart that watches for dangerous rhythms (ventricular tachycardia, in my case) and, the instant one starts, paces or shocks the heart back to normal before it can actually kill you. Its fired twice since then; both times I felt a jolt, sat down for a minute, and was completely fine. My cardiologist is adamant that the underlying electrical fault in my heart tissue hasn't gone away and isn't going to -- the device just steps in and intercepts it, everytime, without exception.

Here's my actual problem: I've started seeing someone new, and when she asked about my health I heard myself say "oh, my heart's fine now," which felt like a stretch even as I said it, but "I have a life-threatening heart condition" felt just as wrong, since, thanks to the gadget, it hasn't actually threatened my life even once -- and if the thing keeps doing its job, it never will. There's some kind of definitional puzzle buried in here that would've driven me up the wall back in the lab, but I can't pin it down on my own. Which of the two things I told her is actually true? Or is the real issue that "do you have a dangerous heart condition" just doesn't have a clean yes-or-no answer once you've built a machine whose entire purpose is making sure the danger part never happens?
