I'm giving a toast at the wedding of two close friends from my LessWrong-adjacent meetup group, and I'd like help thinking it through properly rather than just reaching for the easy Bayesian pun. The bride is finishing a philosophy PhD defending imprecise probabilism — the view that a rational agent's credal state is sometimes best represented by a whole set of probability functions rather than one sharp number, because forcing a single figure out of genuinely ambiguous evidence overstates what you actually know. She has made an actual point of principle out of this for as long as we've known her: ask her the odds of rain on an uncertain forecast and she'll hand you a range and a digression on the Ellsberg paradox rather than split the difference; recite Cromwell's rule at her — never assign credence 0 or 1 to anything short of a logical truth or falsehood — and she will agree with real feeling. Roughly half the room will be meetup regulars listening for a joke that actually engages with this, something that only fully lands if you know what dilation or E-admissibility are, and the other half will be relatives and her fiancé's coworkers, who have affectionately given up trying to follow any of it.

What I want help thinking through is the structural problem sitting underneath the joke, not just the joke itself. A wedding vow asks for something that sounds like a maximally sharp, unconditional commitment — "I do," no interval, no clause held open for new evidence — directed at a question (is this the right choice, will it last) that is about as evidentially murky as anything in a life, which is exactly the kind of case where her own view says a sharp answer is dishonest. I don't want the toast to just tease her for "finally rounding to a point estimate" on her wedding day, both because that's the easy laugh and because it gets her actual position backwards; I suspect there's a real and rather moving distinction available between committing to someone unconditionally and being certain about them, between resolve and credence, and I'd like the toast to earn that distinction rather than just assert it as a punchline. Could you write me a five-minute toast, built around one solid joke that works for both halves of the room, that takes her epistemic commitments seriously enough to use them to say something true about marriage?
