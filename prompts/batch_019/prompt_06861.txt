Ok so I've been prepping a seminar on Enlightenment "social mathematics" and I got into an argument about this with a colleague from the econ department over lunch, and now I can't let it go, so let me put it to you instead. Condorcet spent a good chunk of his career trying to put political decision-making on a rigorous probabilistic footing - the jury theorem is the famous bit, showing that if you've got a group of voters who are each indepedently more likely than not to pick the correct answer, simple majority rule converges on the correct answer with near-certainty as the group gets larger, and does the opposite (converges on the wrong answer) once the voters are worse then a coin flip. He really did think a well-designed collective procedure, with enough independent minds thrown at it, could reliably outperform any individual judge or philosopher-king, and that this was basically a discoverable, calculable fact about the world rather than just a political hope.

Now jump forward to Robin Hanson's futarchy proposal: have the public (or their elected reps) vote on some measurable definition of national welfare, then use conditional prediction markets - real speculators betting real money - to figure out which policy would actually maximize that metric, and auto-enact whatever the market says is best. Fans of the idea basically pitch it as a market-powered upgrade of exactly the kind of epistemic aggregation Condorcet was after, swapping "independent, equally competent voters" for "independent, equally motivated traders."

So here's what I want from you: if you resurrected Condorcet and walked him through futarchy in detail, do you think he'd recognize it as the natural heir to his own project, or would he be troubled by it? I'm especially curious what he'd make of the ways real prediction markets violate the specific assumptions his own theorem depends on - correlated errors and information cascades, wildly unequal stakes and information between traders, manipulation by a wealthy few, that sort of thing. Don't just tell me "he'd love it" or "he'd hate it," I want you to actually work through where his mathematical assumptions and his broader Enlightenment political commitments would pull in different directions.
