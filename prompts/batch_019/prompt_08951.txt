My friend and I just spent an evening arguing about whether Aumann's agreement theorem tells us anything about disagreement among real people, and we got stuck on a point that has nothing to do with the usual worries about common priors. Aumann's model represents knowledge as a partition of the state space, and it is a short exercise to show that any partition-based knowledge operator automatically satisfies positive introspection, the 'KK principle': if an agent knows P, they know that they know P. But Williamson's margin-for-error argument against luminosity is meant precisely to show that KK fails for the fallible kind of knowledge that creatures like us actually have. My friend thinks this undermines any claim that the theorem describes rational human disagreement, quite apart from the common-prior assumption; I suspect it only shows that 'knowledge' in Aumann's technical sense was never meant to track the ordinary, Williamsonian notion, so the apparent tension is merely verbal. Could you adjudicate: explain why partition models validate KK as a matter of construction, sketch how Williamson's argument is meant to defeat it, and then say whether the clash strikes you as philosophically substantive or just a case of two different things sharing the same name?
