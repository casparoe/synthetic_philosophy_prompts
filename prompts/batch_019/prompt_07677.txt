I'm a retired physicist, and since retiring I've fallen a fair way down the effective-altruism/rationalist rabbit hole online. That's how I ran into this idea called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL): if the universe (or multiverse) is vast or infinite, theres a huge population of agents out there we're causally cut off from forever, but who reason enough like we do that our choosing to act cooperatively -- giving some weight to values other than our own -- is evidence that similarly-minded agents elsewhere do the same, so it pays off in expectation even with zero causal influence on any of them. Someone on a forum told me this is basically just Hofstadter's old "superrationality" (two logically identical agents in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma cooperating because each knows the other reasons exactly the same way), just scaled up to cosmic size.

That struck me as too tidy an equivalence, though I couldn't say exactly why. Could you compare the two arguments properly: where does ECL genuinely run on the same logic as superrationality, and where does it need extra, shakier machinery -- correlation instead of identity between agents, evidential over causal decision theory, some story about how common "cooperative" reasoners even are out there -- that Hofstadter's version never needed? And once you drop the clean "two identical minds" setup, does the cooperative conclusion still survive, or does something load-bearing get lost?
