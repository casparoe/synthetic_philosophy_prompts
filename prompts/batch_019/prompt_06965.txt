And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"Aumann's theorem, from his short 1976 paper 'Agreeing to Disagree,' considers two agents who share a common prior probability distribution P over a state space Ω. Each agent i has a private information partition Pi: at the true state, agent i learns only which cell of Pi it falls into, and forms a posterior for some event A by conditionalizing P on that cell. Common knowledge of an event E means more than both agents knowing E -- it means both know it, both know that both know it, both know that both know that both know it, and so on forever. Aumann's result: if it's common knowledge at the true state that agent 1's posterior for A is q1 and agent 2's is q2, then q1 = q2.

The proof looks at the 'meet' of the two partitions -- the finest partition that's a common coarsening of both -- and takes the cell F of the meet containing the true state. Since q1 and q2 are common knowledge, F has to sit entirely inside the event 'agent 1's posterior is q1' and entirely inside 'agent 2's posterior is q2.' Because F is a union of whole cells of agent 1's partition, and each of those cells has posterior q1 for A, the shared prior gives P(A|F) = q1; running the same argument through agent 2's partition gives P(A|F) = q2. So q1 = q2. (A close cousin, the no-trade theorem of Milgrom and Stokey, uses the same setup to show such agents can't find a mutually agreeable speculative bet either.)

Two assumptions are doing the work behind the popular gloss. The common-prior assumption is really just a formality here -- it fixes a shared sample space for both agents to reason over, so it isn't carrying any real rationality content of its own. The actual heavy lifting is the common-knowledge condition, and Aumann actually showed that this is exactly what happens whenever two honest people just talk to each other: if they keep alternately announcing their current posterior for A back and forth, the exchange has to settle into agreement after finitely many rounds, since every new announcement is itself evidence the other person has to update on. So two economists who have genuinely and repeatedly hashed out a forecast, holding nothing back, are guaranteed by the theorem to end up naming the same number, whatever that number turns out to be. Continued, sincere disagreement after real discussion is therefore a fairly strong sign that somebody isn't updating the way an idealized Bayesian would."
