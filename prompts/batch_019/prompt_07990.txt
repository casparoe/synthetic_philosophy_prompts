G.E.M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to protest Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree, turns on a specific application of the doctrine of double effect: she argues that Truman intended the deaths of Japanese civilians as the very mechanism by which Japan would be forced to surrender, rather than merely foreseeing those deaths as a side effect of an otherwise legitimate act of war, and that this is precisely what makes the bombings murder rather than a permissible, if terrible, act of war.

Regular readers of these debates will already know the standard consequentialist rebuttal to Anscombe — that no act can be absolutely forbidden regardless of the lives at stake — and I'd like to set that aside. I'm more interested in a narrower problem internal to the doctrine of double effect itself: the so-called "closeness" or redescription problem, on which almost any foreseen death can be redescribed as the unintended side effect of some more narrowly specified goal (on such a redescription, Truman merely intended to shock Japan's government into surrendering, or to destroy war production, and merely foresaw that civilians would die as a result).

Please argue that there is nonetheless a principled, non-question-begging way of individuating Truman's intention that blocks this redescription move and preserves Anscombe's verdict — one that doesn't simply smuggle the desired conclusion into the description of the act by fiat.
