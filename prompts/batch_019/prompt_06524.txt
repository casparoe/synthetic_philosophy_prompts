I spent my whole career running a small neuroethology lab, mostly recording from single neurons in the toad and frog visual system - the classic "bug detector" retinal cells, and the tectal units that fire happily for a stimulus moving lengthwise like a worm but go quiet (or trigger avoidance) if you rotate the same stimulus 90 degrees, the "antiworm" configuration, very much in the Ewert tradition. Since retiring I've finally had time to read philosophy properly, and a friend from my old university's philosophy department steered me toward teleosemantics - the project of naturalizing mental content by grounding it in biological function and selection history (Millikan originally, and more recently Karen Neander's book, if I'm remembering the names right). It's the first bit of philosophy of mind I've come across that actually seemed to be about my kind of data instead of armchair thought experiments, which is probably why its also the first bit that's kept me up at night.

What's bugging me (sorry) is the disjunction problem. My tectal neurons never fired only for worms - they fired for worms, for a dangled bit of black paper jiggled the right way, and I'm fairly sure I've read somewhere that bullfrogs will even snap at BB pellets flicked past them on a thread. So when I wrote, paper after paper for thirty years, that a given cell "detects prey" or "encodes worm-direction," I was helping myself to exactly the kind of determinate content that teleosemantics is supposed to earn for the animal, and I don't think the theory obviously delivers "worm" rather than just "small-dark-elongated-thing-moving-lengthwise-or-whatever-else-has-ever-triggered-a-snap." This isn't just academic for me either - a former student of mine is finishing a manuscript right now and we're genuinely arguing over whether the abstract should keep the word "detects" or get hedged down to "responds preferentially to."

So could you lay out the most important implications of teleosemantics, and specifically of the disjunction problem, for whether scientists like us are actually entitled to that stronger representational language? I'm not after a textbook summary of who-said-what, I want to know what's really at stake in picking one horn of the problem over the other, and whether there's a version of teleosemantics that gets us out of the disjunction cleanly rather than just by definitional fiat.
