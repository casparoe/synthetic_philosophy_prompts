I'm drafting a scene in my novel in which two former graduate-school friends, now facing each other across a trade, meet the night before a large position settles. Elena is the seller, a derivatives-desk veteran who still reasons like the theorist she trained to be; Theo is the buyer, who runs a small fund and has just taken the other side of her position. I want the scene to dramatize the real puzzle behind no-trade theorems: if the two of them are rational, each knows the other is rational, and their priors are common, then the very fact that Theo wants to buy what Elena wants to sell should, on Aumann-style reasoning about common knowledge, be evidence to each of them that someone has made a mistake -- so the deal ought to unravel before it's signed. Write their exchange so that Elena tries to talk Theo out of the trade using that logic as rigorously as she can, and Theo tries to talk her into going through with it by invoking one of the genuine reasons economists give for why rational trade persists anyway -- a real difference in priors, a hedging or liquidity need unconnected to private information, or a noise-trader-style account -- rather than simply insisting markets are irrational. Have each respond to the specific point the other has just made rather than restate their own position, give neither the easier half of the argument, and end with the trade's fate still genuinely in doubt.
