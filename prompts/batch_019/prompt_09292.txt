I'm part of my school's philosophy club, and a few weeks ago we read about Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles — the claim that there cannot be two distinct things that share literally all of the same properties — followed by Max Black's thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, which is supposed to show the principle is false. During discussion, two other members, Maya and Owen, ended up going back and forth on it for most of the meeting, and our club leader had to move on to the next topic before it really got resolved, so it's been stuck in my head ever since.

Maya opened by saying the thought experiment settles the matter: the two spheres match in every intrinsic way — same size, mass, composition, temperature, and so on — and since there are still two of them, indiscernibility clearly doesn't force identity. Owen objected that the spheres aren't fully indiscernible after all, since each one is a mile away from a sphere, a relation neither bears to itself, so there is at least one true thing about them that isn't just a matter of their intrinsic makeup. Maya answered that this doesn't actually help him, since both spheres satisfy "is a mile from a sphere" in exactly the same way — nothing in that description tells you which sphere is which, so using it to distinguish them already assumes you can tell them apart, which is the very thing in dispute. Owen then tried a narrower version of the same move: he said he didn't need the relation to single either sphere out individually, only to be a relation that nothing bears to itself; since the two spheres do bear that relation to one another, there must be two things, without needing any qualitative difference between them at all. Maya replied that this is circular in a subtler way: to describe the spheres as bearing the relation "to one another," rather than one sphere bearing some relation to itself, you already have to assume two separate things are there to do the relating, so Owen's move doesn't establish their distinctness independently — it just restates it in fancier language. Owen wasn't convinced, and neither was I.

Could you go through Maya and Owen's exchange move by move and say who comes out ahead at each stage, including whether Owen's last move is really as circular as Maya claims? I'd also like to know whether, once all of this is taken into account, Black's scenario actually succeeds in refuting the identity of indiscernibles, and whether there's a way of stating the principle that survives it that neither Maya nor Owen landed on.
