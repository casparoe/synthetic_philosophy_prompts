The question I gave him: "Explain the Frege–Geach problem as a challenge to moral expressivism. Your answer should (a) say what expressivism claims about a sentence like 'Stealing is wrong,' (b) use an example to explain why embedding that kind of sentence inside something like a conditional creates a problem, and (c) briefly describe one way expressivists have tried to answer the problem."

Danny's answer, word for word:

"Expressivists think that when someone says 'Stealing is wrong,' they're not describing a fact about the world the way 'Stealing is illegal in Texas' does. Instead they're expressing a negative attitude toward stealing, something like disapproval -- the classic gloss is that it's closer to 'Boo, stealing!' than to a real description. This is opposed to cognitivist or descriptivist views, where moral sentences report beliefs that are true or false.

The problem is that this account runs into trouble as soon as you embed the sentence somewhere it isn't actually being asserted. Take this argument: 'If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong. Stealing is wrong. Therefore, getting your little brother to steal is wrong.' In the first premise, 'stealing is wrong' is just sitting inside the antecedent of a conditional, so nobody is actually expressing disapproval there, the speaker might not even think stealing is wrong, they're just weighing a possibility. But in the second premise, taken on its own, the same words are expressing disapproval outright. So the phrase seems to mean two different things across the two premises, and once the middle term shifts meaning like that, the argument commits a kind of equivocation, which technically makes it invalid for anyone who takes expressivism seriously, even though obviously everyone, expressivist or not, treats this as perfectly ordinary, good reasoning. This general point about content staying fixed independent of assertoric force actually goes back to Frege himself, who first used it to argue that moral sentences can't just be glorified expressions of feeling; Geach's role later on was mainly to popularize the point with an example close to the one above.

One response, associated with Blackburn's quasi-realism, is to build up a whole 'logic of attitudes,' where combinations of pro- and con-attitudes can stand in relations of consistency and inconsistency that mirror ordinary logical relations, so we don't need moral sentences to state facts in order to explain why arguments like this work."
