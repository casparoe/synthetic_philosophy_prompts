I'm about two-thirds into a historical novel set among a circle of Black abolitionists in upstate New York in the early 1850s, and I've hit a scene I keep putting off. My protagonist, Aaron, is recording clerk for an invented body — the "Ontario County Convention of Colored Citizens" — and the meeting I'm dramatizing falls right after the real 1851 split between Douglass and Garrison, when Douglass announced he'd come around to Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner's view that the Constitution, properly read, isn't actually a pro-slavery document, against Garrison's older line (the "covenant with death and an agreement with hell" line) that decent abolitionists shouldn't vote, hold office, or recognize the Union at all. I've been trying to get the actual arguments right instead of just gesturing at them — I read Douglass's 1860 Glasgow speech and some of Spooner's book on the subject — but most secondary sources just say "Douglass decided the Constitution was secretly antislavery" and move on, which isn't much use to me. My writing group keeps telling me to just skip the scene, but it's the hinge of the whole book's second act, so I can't.

What I want is the actual memo/resolution Aaron drafts for the convention to vote on the night before delegates leave for the state meeting — the kind of document that gets read aloud and then torn apart line by line. It should land clearly on one side (whether the county convention endorses forming or backing a political party and contesting elections, on the theory that the Constitution is with them if correctly construed, versus sticking with Garrisonian moral suasion, disunion, and refusing the ballot), lay out the real interpretive case for that side — the text-versus-secret-intentions argument, the fact that the pro-slavery clauses never actually use the word "slave," the preamble, all of it — and then take on the strongest version of the other side's objection honestly, not as a strawman. I genuinely don't know which way I want Aaron to come out yet, so feel free to make that call yourself as long as its well argued either way. Doesn't need to be long, just needs to feel like something a serious, worried thirty-two-year-old actually wrote in 1852, not a Wikipedia summary with old-timey spelling.
