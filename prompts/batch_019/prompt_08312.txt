"Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz was a nun in colonial Mexico in the late 1600s who was known as one of the greatest minds of her time, even though she was never allowed to attend university just because she was a woman. She wrote the Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz in 1691 as a reply to Sor Filotea, an older nun who had gotten worried that Sor Juana was spending too much time on secular subjects like science, poetry, and philosophy instead of focusing on religious devotion, and who wrote to gently push her to give it up. Sor Juana uses her reply to defend herself, and more broadly to argue that women in general should be allowed to study.

One argument is autobiographical. She tells the story of wanting to disguise herself as a boy so she could go to the university in Mexico City, and she describes how as a little girl she'd give herself a time limit to learn something and cut off her hair if she didn't make it, because she didn't think a head should be "dressed" in hair while it was empty of knowledge. This is meant to show that her love of learning was natural since childhood, not some kind of rebellious phase.

She also argues that ignorance is actually more dangerous than knowledge is, and that keeping women uneducated doesn't protect their virtue, it threatens it, since women who can't read or reason well are more likely to misinterpret scripture or get misled by bad teachers. To support this she brings up a long list of learned women from the Bible and from ancient history, to prove that women have always been capable of real scholarship, not just recently.

The whole controversy actually started inside this same letter: a big part of it is Sor Juana's point by point takedown of a forty year old sermon by the Jesuit Antonio Vieira about which of Christ's "finezas" was the greatest, and it was that takedown that scandalized the Bishop and is what got her in trouble to begin with.

Overall the Respuesta is usually read today as one of the earliest defenses of women's intellectual equality in the Americas, even though Sor Juana frames basically everything in religious terms instead of in terms of rights, since that was the only language available to her that wouldn't get her in even more trouble."
