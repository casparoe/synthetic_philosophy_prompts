After a forty-year career in experimental physics, I have only in the last couple of years begun reading seriously in philosophy, and one puzzle I keep returning to is G. E. Moore's observation that a sentence like "It's raining, but I don't believe it" is absurd to assert, even though it is perfectly consistent and could easily be true. Please compare this Moorean absurdity with the "transparency" account of self-knowledge that grew out of a remark by Gareth Evans and was developed at length by Richard Moran, on which we typically come to know our own beliefs not by introspecting some inner state but by looking outward, at the very facts the belief is about. How much of the irrationality of Moore's paradox can transparency actually explain, and where does the account come under the greatest strain — for instance, in cases of self-deception or a divided mind, where belief seems to come apart from whatever the outward-facing evidence would support? I would like a considered verdict rather than merely a survey of the positions on offer.
