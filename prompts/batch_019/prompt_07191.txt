Over the past few weeks, my graduate seminar on moral psychology has worked through the experimental philosophy literature on the "Knobe effect" (also known as the side-effect effect): the finding, first reported by Joshua Knobe in 2003 using his now-famous chairman-and-the-environment vignette, that ordinary speakers are far more willing to describe a foreseen side effect as brought about intentionally when it is harmful than when it is helpful, even though the agent's stated attitude and beliefs about the two outcomes are held fixed across conditions. What has struck my students more than the phenomenon itself is how many mutually incompatible explanations of it remain in circulation more than two decades on, pitched at quite different theoretical levels, and not always obviously answerable to the same body of evidence.

For the seminar's closing session, each student has to defend an ordering of six leading accounts, judged by how well they accommodate the full range of data now on the table: not just the original vignettes, but also the sensitivity of the effect to question order and wording documented by Nichols and Ulatowski, its extension to side effects that violate merely conventional or statistical norms rather than moral ones, and the various studies designed to pit the accounts directly against one another. The six accounts are: (i) Knobe's own view, on which evaluative considerations partly constitute competent use of the concept of intentional action; (ii) Adams and Steadman's proposal that the asymmetry is a pragmatic effect of conversational implicature rather than a difference in literal truth-conditions; (iii) Machery's trade-off hypothesis; (iv) Sripada's Deep Self Concordance model; (v) Alicke's culpable-control, blame-driven account; and (vi) Uttich and Lombrozo's norm-theoretic account, on which the relevant notion of a violated norm need not be moral at all.
