Three weeks ago my father suffered a cardiac arrest and has been unresponsive ever since: no reliable eye tracking, no reaction to his name, nothing that counts, by the hospital's own standards, as a sign of awareness. Last week the neurology team ran a test in which they asked him, through headphones, to spend several minutes imagining he was squeezing his right hand, then several minutes imagining he was wiggling his toes. The resulting EEG showed two distinct patterns of activity, each one matching the pattern seen in healthy volunteers actually performing the corresponding movement. The neurologist used a term I had to write down to remember — "cognitive motor dissociation" — and was careful to say only that his brain is still able to take in an instruction and do something different depending on which instruction it was; she would not say whether this means he is aware of doing it, or aware of anything at all. My aunt, who spent thirty years as an ICU nurse, thinks that caution is empty: if his brain can tell two instructions apart and act correctly on each one, she says, that already is understanding, and asking whether "someone is in there" over and above that is a question with no real content. I cannot decide whether she has cut through a piece of needless philosophizing or missed a real distinction that the test simply cannot settle either way. Practically, it matters to me which of them is right, since it bears on whether I should go on talking to him for his sake or only for mine. Who has the better of this argument?
