Anyway, I ended up arguing about this with my roommate last night because it turns out to be extremely relevant to something happening on the Learning Support Advisory Committee, which I sit on as the student rep.

Here's the situation: the committee has to decide by next Tuesday whether to renew, expand, or cut funding (about $80k/year) for our college's Supplemental Instruction (SI) program, where upperclassmen run extra study sessions attached to specific gateway courses. The provost's office gave us the pass-rate data, and depending on how you slice it, it tells opposite stories. Overall, across the whole student body, kids who went to SI passed their course at about 77% (347/450) vs about 86% (475/550) for non-attendees -- so on paper SI attendees do worse, and two committee members want to kill the program over this. But broken down by course it flips: in Calc I, SI attendees passed at 75% (300/400) vs 65% (65/100) for non-attendees, and in Intro Psych, SI attendees passed at 94% (47/50) vs about 91% (410/450) for non-attendees -- SI attendees do better in both courses individually. The reversal happens because SI attendance is way more concentrated in Calc I (which has a much lower baseline pass rate for everybody, SI or not) than in Psych. Our committee chair pointed this out and wants to expand SI, but the member who wants to cut it says breaking the data down by course just lets us keep slicing it until we land on whatever story we already wanted, and that the trustees are only ever gonna ask about the topline number anyway.

Could you draft that memo for me: a clear recommendation with a timeline for how we'd check it's actually working, and reasoning solid enough that it'd definately survive being read out loud to the one committee member who thinks this is all statistical sleight of hand?
