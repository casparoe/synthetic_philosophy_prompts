I'm a philosophy PhD student currently taking a graduate seminar cross-listed between philosophy of religion and the philosophy of punishment, and this week's set text was David Lewis's short, characteristically dry paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", in which he tries to reconstruct the Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in sinners' place using the ordinary tools of analytic philosophy rather than theology. As I understood the strategy, Lewis leans on an analogy with debt: a debt is the sort of thing a third party can simply discharge on your behalf, because all that matters is that the books balance, not whose hands do the balancing. He then presses on whether punishment can be modeled the same way, and worries that the ordinary retributivist picture — on which it is the wrongdoer specifically, and not just anyone, who must suffer, because desert attaches to persons rather than to some impersonal ledger — makes substitution look incoherent in a way that debt-transfer never does.

What's been nagging at a few of us in the cohort since the seminar is a generalization worry that seems to follow from taking the debt model seriously enough to actually rescue substitution: if punishment can be discharged by a willing innocent just as a debt can be discharged by a third party, that appears to license punishing a willing innocent stand-in in perfectly ordinary criminal cases too — exactly the kind of vicarious punishment that even non-theological retributivists overwhelmingly want to rule out. My advisor's offhand reaction was that anyone who accepts penal substitution is thereby committed, full stop, to a broadly consequentialist rather than retributivist theory of punishment; several of us thought that was too quick, since there might be some principled way of restricting the debt model that blocks the generalization without giving up on retributivism entirely.

Could you write an essay, pitched at other philosophy graduate students, that works through this as carefully as possible? Please lay out the strongest version of the dilemma as it bears on Lewis's argument and on penal substitution more generally, weigh the main ways one might try to block the generalization to ordinary criminal cases (e.g., by distinguishing kinds or purposes of punishment, or by restricting the debt model to some special feature of the theological case) against the reasons for doubting that any such restriction is principled rather than ad hoc, and then actually commit to a verdict — either that some specific version of the debt model renders penal substitution coherent without also licensing whipping-boy punishment generally, or that the doctrine comes out confused even under its most charitable philosophical reconstruction. Please don't just leave it at "there are considerations on both sides"; I'd like a real answer, defended.
