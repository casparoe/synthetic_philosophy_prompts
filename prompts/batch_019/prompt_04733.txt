I'm TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and before I enter grades I'd like an independent assessment of one student's answer to the take-home exam.

The question was:

"Frankfurt's Jones/Black case is often taken to refute the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP). (a) State PAP and reconstruct Frankfurt's counterexample. [6 points] (b) Explain the 'dilemma defense' of PAP associated with Robert Kane and Carl Ginet, distinguishing the two horns of the dilemma and what each is meant to establish. [8 points] (c) Explain why John Martin Fischer thinks the dilemma defense does not succeed, and how his own semi-compatibilism handles Frankfurt cases instead. [6 points]"

Here is the student's answer, in full:

(a) PAP claims that an agent is morally responsible for having performed an action only if she could have done otherwise. Frankfurt's case is designed to show this is false. Black wants Jones to perform some action A and can monitor whatever prior sign in Jones reliably indicates what Jones is about to decide. If the sign indicates Jones is about to decide against A, Black's device intervenes and causes Jones to do A regardless. If the sign indicates Jones is about to decide in favor of A on his own, the device does nothing, and Jones goes on to do A for his own reasons, entirely untouched by Black. Suppose this second scenario occurs: Jones does A on his own, and everyone, including defenders of PAP, is inclined to hold him responsible for it. Yet he could not have done otherwise, since had he wavered, Black would have stepped in. So, Frankfurt concludes, moral responsibility does not require the availability of alternative possibilities.

(b) Kane and Ginet's dilemma defense grants that a case like this can be told but denies that it refutes PAP. Everything turns on the status of the prior sign that Black's device monitors. Suppose first that the sign is not causally sufficient for Jones's subsequent decision, so that there is a genuine indeterministic gap between the sign's appearance and what Jones goes on to decide. Then, even once the sign appears, there remains some real chance that Jones would have decided otherwise, so a "flicker of freedom" survives: some alternative possibility is still available to Jones, just a thinner one than PAP's defenders originally had in mind, and PAP is not actually violated. Suppose instead that the sign is causally sufficient for the decision, so that determinism holds locally between sign and decision. Kane and Ginet argue that in this version the case begs the question against the compatibilist: it simply assumes, without independent argument, that a decision deterministically produced by the prior sign can still be free and responsible, which is exactly the point in dispute. Either way, Frankfurt has not managed to describe a single scenario that both closes off every alternative possibility and remains neutral on whether moral responsibility is compatible with determinism.

(c) Fischer's response is that the dilemma defense doesn't really save the friend of PAP, because even granting a residual flicker on the indeterministic horn, that flicker is produced by involuntary neural signs rather than anything Jones does or exercises, so it looks like the wrong sort of thing to ground a responsibility judgment on. His diagnosis is that we should therefore locate the relevant freedom directly in this residual flicker: since some thin alternative possibility persists whenever we press the indeterministic horn, his semi-compatibilism retains PAP in a suitably weakened, flicker-based form while still allowing that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism. On this view, an agent has "guidance control" just in case her actual sequence of reasoning is suitably responsive to reasons and is accompanied by at least this minimal flicker; full "regulative control," meaning robust, freely exercisable alternatives, is not required.
