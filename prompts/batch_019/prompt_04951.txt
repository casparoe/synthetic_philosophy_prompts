I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, mostly dull stuff about how electrons misbehave in solids), and since I retired three years ago I've gone a bit down the philosophy rabbit hole, starting with the logical positivists because a bunch of those guys started out as physicists like me. Which is how I ended up on Moritz Schlick -- the guy the Vienna Circle basically formed around, the "metaphysics is meaningless" crowd -- except then I found this odd, almost tender essay of his called "On the Meaning of Life," which reads nothing like what you'd expect from that gang. His actual argument is that if an act only gets its meaning by serving some further purpose, and that purpose serves a further purpose again, you never actually land anywhere -- it's purposes all the way up and nothing ever cashes out as meaningful in itself. The one place he says meaning shows up unconditionally is in play: activity done for no reason beyond itself, which kids do naturally and which most adults quietly lose the knack for once "real life" (career, duty, and so on) takes over.

So here's my actual problem. My old collaborator Renate is retiring after forty one years running her lab, and I've been asked to give the toast at her retirment dinner next month. About half the room is scientists who worked with her at some point and would absolutely catch a Schlick reference done properly; the other half is her kids, grandkids and neighbors who've never opened a philosophy book in their life. The thing is, I can't just do the standard retirement toast about how she can finally stop working and go enjoy herself, because Renate would rightly object to that -- she's said for years that the research itself, the actual doing of it, was never some chore she suffered through for the payoff of tenure or grants, it was the fun part, full stop. So could you write me a toast, maybe five or six minutes read aloud, that actually uses Schlick's purpose-vs-play distinction as the backbone -- one that rewards the people in the room who'll recognize what's going on, but still lands as warm and clear for the people who won't, and that doesn't quietly undercut its own point by making her retirement sound like just one more deferred goal (relax now so you'll be healthier later, and so on) rather than simply more of the same thing she already had.
