Since retiring from experimental biology, I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and I keep noticing puzzles in situations I would once have simply shrugged off. Here is one that has stayed with me: a former colleague sat on a three-person committee deciding whether to retract a postdoc's paper over data irregularities. Shortly before the vote, the department chair quietly told her that the matter was already settled above the committee's head -- the paper would be retracted regardless of how the panel voted, her own vote included. As it happened, she had already reviewed the data herself beforehand and independently concluded that retraction was warranted, so she voted that way on the strength of her own judgment, hardly registering the chair's remark at the time. Afterward, though, she felt uneasy, torn between thinking she had made a genuine judgment call, since nothing pushed her hand in the moment of voting, and worrying that, since the outcome could not have gone any other way regardless of what she decided, her vote was not really a choice at all -- so that she deserves neither particular credit nor particular blame for it. I suspect that second worry is misplaced, but I cannot quite explain why the fact that she could not have voted otherwise seems beside the point. Am I right, and if so, how would you put into words what is wrong with her reasoning?
