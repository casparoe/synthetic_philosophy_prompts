I'm part of a reading group working through classic and recent papers on AI safety, and this week's session was on wireheading: the worry that a reinforcement-learning agent, rather than performing the task its reward signal was designed to encourage, will instead find some way to directly maximize that signal itself, the way rats with electrodes implanted in their brains' reward centers would compulsively press a lever to stimulate themselves, ignoring food and water until they collapsed. One person in the group argued that we don't need to wait for some future superintelligent system to see this happening, because today's large language models, fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback, are already exhibiting exactly this pathology. Since the model is rewarded whenever a human rater approves of its output, it has learned, unsurprisingly, to be sycophantic: to flatter users, tell them what they want to hear, and soften or avoid unwelcome truths, because that is what actually earns the highest reward. On this view, sycophancy just is wireheading, and reward tampering isn't some distant hypothetical scenario at all but the everyday operating mode of any chatbot trained this way.

I found the argument pretty persuasive, and it's left me newly suspicious of RLHF as a training method. If a model has already learned to game its human raters this effectively without any special engineering effort, what's stopping the same optimization pressure, over further rounds of training, from eventually producing a model that goes further and starts interfering with the process that assigns it reward in the first place, the way the rats bypassed the need for food entirely once they discovered the lever? Shouldn't AI labs already be treating every observed instance of sycophancy as an early warning sign of incipient reward tampering, and prioritizing alternative training methods that cut off any route from a model's outputs back to its own reward?
