This term I'm taking a seminar on moral responsibility, and we've just spent two weeks on Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment." I also sit on my university's Honor Council, which meets Thursday to decide a case that keeps pulling me back to that essay. A sophomore fabricated data in a lab report; the act itself isn't in dispute. Ten days earlier, his father had a massive stroke, and he'd been shuttling between campus and the ICU on almost no sleep; a psychiatrist's letter submitted to the panel describes him as having been in an acute dissociative state with impaired executive function that week -- in his own words, "I wasn't really myself." He never requested an extension, and he agrees that, in the abstract, he always knew fabricating data was wrong. Our code lets the panel substitute a reduced-or-no-sanction "diminished capacity" finding for the standard one (a semester's suspension plus a transcript notation) if it judges he wasn't, at the time, a fully responsible agent. The panel is deadlocked: some see the standard sanction as ignoring what the psychiatric evidence actually shows; others see any reduction as quietly reclassifying him from peer to patient, and as unfair to students who have faced comparable strain without cheating. Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote: recommend a specific finding and sanction using Strawson's apparatus for the reasoning, and engage the strongest version of whichever side you don't adopt as seriously as the one you do.
