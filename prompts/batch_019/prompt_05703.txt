So I fell down a rabbit hole starting from the St. Petersburg paradox and ended up on this weirder cousin called the Pasadena paradox (Nover and Hájek, if that name means anything to you). Setup: flip a fair coin til it first comes up heads, on flip n, and you get paid (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n / n. Multiply each payout by its probability (1/2)^n and the n's cancel nicely, so the expected value is just 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + 1/5 - ..., the alternating harmonic series. That converges to ln(2) if you add it up in the order I just wrote it, but its only conditionally convergent, not absolutely (the harmonic series itself blows up), so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem you can reshuffle those exact same terms to sum to literally any number you want, or to +infinity, or to nothing at all. And theres no privileged "correct" order to sum over a bunch of simultaneous possible outcomes the way there would be for events happening one after another in time. So unlike St. Petersburg, where the expected value is at least a well-defined infinity, here it's genuinely undefined.

Can you invent a brand new gamble (not the coin flip one above) with that exact same disease built in -- perfectly well-defined individual outcomes and probabilities, but a total expected value that's only conditionally convergent -- and frame it as something a person could semi-plausibly be offered, rather than an obviously abstract math object? Show me the actual series so I can see exactly where the conditional-not-absolute convergence sneaks in.
