After thirty-seven years as a field geologist with a state geological survey, I retired four years ago and have since been filling the resulting gap in my life with a rather belated education in philosophy, worked through slowly with a small reading group that meets once a month at our local library. We are presently making our way through some epistemology, which is perhaps why a disagreement I had this week in an entirely unrelated setting has stuck with me more than it probably deserves. The group won't meet again for another three weeks, so you'll have to serve as a stand-in in the meantime.

Since retiring, I have volunteered every third Saturday at our regional natural history museum's identification desk, where members of the public bring in odd stones they suspect might be meteorites. The great majority, in practice, turn out to be terrestrial oddities — slag, hematite, chunks of magnetite — which the veterans on the desk call, half-affectionately, "meteor-wrongs." I usually staff this desk alongside another retired scientist, a former metallurgist named Hendricks, whose relevant expertise is if anything slightly greater than my own. Two Saturdays ago, a farmer brought in a heavy, pitted, blackish stone he had turned up while ploughing. Hendricks and I went over it together, out loud, step by step: a magnet test, a check of the surface for fusion crust and the thumbprint-like dimples called regmaglypts, a small polished window to look for the rounded grains and metal flecks you'd expect in an ordinary chondrite, and a rough density comparison against a reference specimen. A thin section under the microscope, or better, an isotope test, would presumably settle things eventually, but the university's queue for that runs several months, and the museum wants a provisional verdict logged today. Neither of us withheld anything from the other, yet we finished the identical procedure in different places: Hendricks came away close to convinced it is an ordinary stony meteorite, while I remained fairly convinced it is an iron-rich terrestrial nodule. If pressed, I'd have put my own confidence at something like seventy percent terrestrial, and I'd guess Hendricks was at least that confident the other way. By any external measure we are equally qualified to make this call — we've staffed this desk together for six years, and I cannot point to any real asymmetry in our training — except for a nagging private impression that I have been right slightly more often than he has in our handful of earlier disagreements, an impression I cannot fully trust, since I am hardly a disinterested judge of my own history.

So here is my question, which I suspect is really about something more general than rocks: given that Hendricks and I have gone through exactly the same evidence and the same reasoning and still arrived in different places, is it simply irrational for me to go on holding my view with much confidence? Should intellectual honesty push me most of the way toward his position, on the grounds that I have no independent reason to think myself the more reliable judge in this particular instance? Or does my private sense of a better track record entitle me to keep more of my original confidence, even though I cannot cash that sense out in any way that doesn't ultimately just come down to my own say-so? I would be glad to hear how you would work through a case like this — both for the rock itself, which is presumably still sitting in a drawer in the museum office, and for what it might suggest more generally about how much ground one ought to give to someone who disagrees with you despite being, by every external measure, just as competent and just as informed as you are.
