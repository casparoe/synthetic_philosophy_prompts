I spent thirty-four years as a comparative physiologist, most of them studying the cardiovascular adaptations that let diving mammals remain submerged for extraordinary lengths of time, before retiring six years ago. With more free time than I knew what to do with, I started working through philosophy of biology at a friend's suggestion, and I now belong to a small monthly reading group made up mostly of other retired scientists. Twice a year we invite an outside speaker for a proper Q&A rather than simply discussing papers among ourselves, and I have been asked to lead the questioning this time. I confess to some amusement at discovering that my own organ of study is this literature's favourite example: nearly everyone who writes on biological function, Wright and Cummins included, reaches for the heart's pumping blood as the paradigm case.

Our guest this term is a philosopher of biology who has built her reputation defending the etiological, or "selected-effects," theory of function -- in the tradition running from Wright through Neander and Millikan -- against causal-role rivals descending from Cummins. On her view, what a trait is for is fixed by whatever effect explains its having been selected for in evolutionary history, not by whatever it presently contributes to the organism. She argues the position with real skill, but in her published exchanges with causal-role theorists I have noticed a recurring escape route: whenever a hard case comes up, she tends to say the two accounts are simply answering different questions and so are not genuinely in competition at all. I would like the session to press on that move rather than let her retreat into it whenever convenient.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me, each paired with a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give? I want at least one full exchange on each of the following: how the selected-effects theory can assign any function at all to a brand-new trait or one-off structure with no selective history behind it (the sort of case people press using Swampman-style thought experiments); whether the revised "modern history" version of the theory genuinely solves the problem of traits co-opted for new uses over time, or merely relocates the arbitrariness in deciding how far back the relevant selective episode lies; and whether her theory's tidy handling of malfunction is a real explanatory advantage over causal-role accounts or simply builds the answer in by definition. I would also like one exchange built around a concrete case in which field biologists must settle on a single function for a trait in order to design or interpret an actual experiment, chosen so that etiological and causal-role considerations point to different answers -- I want to see whether the "different questions" deflection survives having to give a first-order verdict under real research pressure. Please close the sequence with a question that asks her directly what she would accept as evidence against her view, or what it would take for her to abandon the pluralist line altogether.
