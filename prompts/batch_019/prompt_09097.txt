I spent thirty-five years as a condensed matter physicist before I retired, and these last couple of years I've fallen hard into philosophy, mostly epistemology, since its the part that still feels the most like doing physics to me. A friend from my old reading group, who actually has a philosophy degree unlike me, pointed me at something called the problem of logical omniscience a few weeks back, and it hasn't left me alone since. As I understand it: if you model belief or knowledge the standard way, as a set of possible worlds compatible with what someone believes, you end up saying that person automatically believes every logical truth and every consequence of anything they already believe, all at once and for free. That struck me as exactly like assuming a frictionless plane and then forgetting to ever put the friction back in, except here the idealization doesn't just simplify the mind, it describes a completely different kind of mind than any of us actually have. I keep coming back to a homemade example: if you know the rules of chess, you "know," in some sense, whether a given position is mate in five, since it's derivable from the rules alone, but obviously nobody knows that without sitting down and calculating it out.

From some further reading (forgive me if Im garbling the attributions, I'm still new at this) there seem to be two quite different repair jobs on offer, and they don't just disagree on details, they disagree about where the actual mistake is. One side, which I think traces back to Hintikka's own later work plus Rantala's so-called impossible worlds models, keeps the believer as one single, unified state, but widens the space of worlds so that some of them are logically impossible -- worlds where the rules of chess hold but that particular mate-in-five doesn't -- so you can believe the rules without believing every forced consequence of them. The other side, which I've seen called "fragmentation" and think traces back to Stalnaker, keeps the worlds perfectly sane and logical, but denies that a believer is one unified state at all: we're each supposedly running several loosely connected compartments of belief that never get fully pooled together, which is meant to explain how you can believe something in one context and its opposite in another without ever feeling the contradiction.

Could you write me a dialogue between a proponent of each camp, hashing this out over the chess case, or over whatever sharper test case you can come up with? I don't want two monologues -- I want them actually responding to each other, each pressing the other on what their own fix costs elsewhere: does fragmentation just relocate the omniscience problem inside every little compartment instead of solving it, and does letting in impossible worlds make belief so cheap and unconstrained that it stops explaining anything at all. End it with them agreeing, even if only grudgingly, on what kind of case would actually settle which repair is closer to being right.
