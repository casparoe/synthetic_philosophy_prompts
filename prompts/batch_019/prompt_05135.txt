I am several drafts into a novel whose protagonist is built around a single, hard-won virtue: a physical courage forged over decades, the sort of fixed disposition Aristotle would call a matter of settled character rather than a passing mood. A friend finishing her doctorate in psychology insists this kind of characterization is empirically naive. She points me to Milgram's obedience studies, Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment, and Walter Mischel's finding that behavioral consistency across situations rarely yields a correlation above roughly 0.3, as proof that "character" this robust is largely an illusion we project onto people whose conduct is really being driven by whatever situation they happen to be in. If the psychological evidence truly shows that stable traits of this kind don't exist in real people, should I abandon my premise and let my protagonist's courage fail unpredictably from scene to scene, so the novel stays honest about how people actually behave?
