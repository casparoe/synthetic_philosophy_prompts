My sister is getting married next month, and she's asked me—the family's philosophy student—to give a toast at the reception. She and her fiancé actually met as undergraduates in an ethics class, during the week on Kant's Groundwork, and according to family legend their first real conversation was an argument about the shopkeeper example: whether it's bleak or oddly admirable that Kant denies moral credit to a shopkeeper who is honest only because dishonesty would cost him customers, while reserving real "moral worth" for people who do the right thing from principle even when nothing recommends it to them. I'm currently taking a seminar that spent two weeks on exactly this material, including the companion example about the naturally warm-hearted person who helps others simply because it makes them happy to do so—which Kant, notoriously, also denies any moral worth to—and the various attempts by later commentators to soften that conclusion: Herman's suggestion that the motive of duty only needs to function as a kind of standby motive, present and ready to act even if it never actually has to, and Henson's distinction between a single-act "battle citation" sense of moral worth and a whole-character "fitness report" sense that requires no inner struggle at all.

What I want to build the toast around is a footnote attached to that very passage, where Kant distinguishes "pathological" love, the kind you feel and cannot be commanded to feel, from "practical" love, the kind involved in the biblical command to love your neighbor, which he locates in the will rather than in feeling. That distinction is, whether Kant intended it this way or not, a fairly exact description of what a wedding vow is for: promising something that mere inclination cannot guarantee will still be there in twenty years. I would like you to write the toast itself: about four minutes long, for a room that is roughly a third philosophy graduate students who will notice immediately if these ideas are used loosely, and two-thirds relatives and old family friends who have never heard the phrase "moral worth" used in this technical sense. It should use the actual content of the debate rather than just invoking Kant's name for effect, it should land on a real answer to whether a love that needs duty to hold it together through the hard years is thereby less romantic than one that runs on inclination alone, or is in fact the only kind of love a vow was ever asking for—and above everything else, it has to work as a toast: warm and funny enough for a wedding reception, not a seminar presentation that happens to end in "cheers."
