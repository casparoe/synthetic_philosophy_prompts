I'm a senior engineer and one of five members of my company's architecture review board (ARB), which has to sign off on any RFC for a significant system change before a team is allowed to start building it. The process has always been informal: someone posts a design doc, the five of us leave comments and an explicit approve or request-changes verdict over a few days, and everything is visible in real time, so by the time the fourth or fifth reviewer opens the doc, they can already see two staff engineers' approvals sitting at the top of the thread.

This came under real scrutiny after we approved an RFC to replace our internal job queue with a different message broker. It picked up four approvals within a day, including two from staff engineers, and shipped a week later. The new broker's delivery guarantees turned out to be weaker than the doc had assumed, and for about two weeks a small fraction of jobs silently vanished under load instead of being retried. In the retro, three of the five reviewers admitted they'd separately noticed the delivery-guarantee mismatch while reading the doc, but each had talked themselves out of flagging it once they saw that the staff engineers had already signed off. The board is now split. Two of us want every reviewer to write and submit an independent assessment before anyone can see what the others said, on the theory that the current setup lets the first couple of opinions quietly determine everyone else's. The other two think that's a solution in search of a problem: deferring to colleagues' judgment instead of re-deriving everything from scratch is exactly what expertise and trust within a team are for, and blind review will mainly slow down the RFCs that most need to move fast. Someone wrote "information cascade" in the retro doc, half-joking, and the phrase stuck, though none of us actually know whether that's the right diagnosis or what would follow from it if it is.

Could you draft the short memo I'll bring to the ARB's next meeting? It should recommend what our review process should actually be going forward -- keep it as is, move to independent blind assessments, or something in between -- with a concrete rollout timeline, and reasoning solid enough that the "this is just healthy deference to expertise" camp would feel genuinely engaged with rather than overruled.
