I'm doing a PhD in philosophy (mostly social ontology / phil language stuff) and we just spent two whole seminar sessions on David Lewis's *Convention*, grinding through the coordination-equilibria-plus-common-knowledge machinery. It's put me in the annoying position of overanalyzing my own house. Three of us live together, and there's this norm where whoever notices we're out of a staple -- coffee, dish soap, toilet paper, whatever -- just quietly buys more themselves, no reimbursment, no discussion. As far as I can tell it started because one housemate did this once back in year one and it just... stuck. Two of us definately think it's a bad system since it obviously screws over whoever burns through stuff fastest, and we'd rather do a shared kitty everyone tops up monthly. Every time it comes up at "house meeting" everyone nods along that the kitty is better, and then nothing changes and we're back to the old pattern within a week.

Using Lewis's actual account of what makes something a convention in the first place -- the arbitrariness condition (there has to be a real alternative regularity that would've worked just as well), the common knowledge requirement, and especially his stuff on precedent as a source of salience that makes one equilibrium self-perpetuating -- what does his theory imply about why we're stuck, and about what it would actually take for a small group like ours to shift onto a new convention? I'm especially curious whether his framework predicts that everyone privately agreeing the kitty is superior should be almost beside the point, and whether "let's all just agree to switch" at a house meeting even counts as the kind of common-knowledge-generating event his theory requires, or whether that's smuggling in more than the theory can actually license. Don't just recap the book's definitions for me -- I want the implications actually worked out for a case like this.
