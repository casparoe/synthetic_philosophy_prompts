Since retiring three years ago after a career in comparative physiology, I have finally had time to read the philosophy I always meant to get around to, and I have lately been working through some of the literature on the concept of disease. A dispute within my own family, however, has done more to sharpen the question for me than any of the books, and I would value an outside opinion on it.

My brother-in-law, who has taken up various "ancestral health" enthusiasms since his own retirement, was diagnosed last year with lactose intolerance. Rather than treating this as an unwelcome diagnosis, he has built a whole position around it: he maintains that lactose intolerance is not a disorder at all but simply the default mammalian condition, and that it is the rest of us, who go on drinking milk happily into old age, who carry the real anomaly. His reasoning draws on genuine biology: the capacity to digest lactose into adulthood is a comparatively recent genetic development, confined to some human populations with a history of dairying, layered on top of a much older and still far more widespread mammalian pattern of losing most lactase production soon after weaning. On this basis, he considers his gastroenterologist's use of the word "disorder" to be little more than a parochial habit of dairy-drinking cultures, mistaking a local statistical majority for a genuine biological norm.

I have not been able to locate the flaw in this argument, even though something about it strikes me as too quick, as though a real biological fact were being made to do more philosophical work than it can actually bear. Is there a principled basis on which one could say that his gastroenterologist, rather than my brother-in-law, has the better claim on the word "disorder" here, and if so, what would that basis be? Or has medicine, as he insists, simply smuggled a value judgement in under cover of an objective-sounding diagnostic label, so that the dispute is really just verbal?
