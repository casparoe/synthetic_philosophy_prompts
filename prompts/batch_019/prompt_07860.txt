Longtime readers of the rationalist blogosphere will know the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as a matched pair: Kyburg introduced the lottery case, Makinson explicitly built the preface case to mirror it, and the two have been cited together ever since as evidence against the principle that justified, high-credence beliefs are closed under conjunction. I've come to doubt that they are, at bottom, the same puzzle in different clothes. Please argue that the preface paradox is not really generated by the same phenomenon as the lottery paradox, so that no proposed solution to one should be expected to carry over to the other. Ground the argument in the disanalogy between the lottery's built-in certainty that exactly one ticket wins (fixed by the very setup of the case) and the preface's merely inductive, defeasible confidence that some claim in the book is mistaken.
