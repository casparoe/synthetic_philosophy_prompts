I've read enough rationalist and effective altruist writing on population ethics to know Parfit's nonidentity problem in the abstract: a choice that determines which particular person will exist can't be said to harm that person, however much worse off they are than some other possible person, since their only alternative was never existing at all. Applying this to a live case is proving less tidy than the theory suggests.

I sit on a fertility clinic's ethics committee. A couple undergoing IVF has several viable embryos; two carry a dominant gene for profound, non-lethal, hereditary deafness that matches both parents' own condition, and the rest do not. The couple, both culturally Deaf, want an affected embryo transferred rather than an unaffected one. Half the committee wants a standing rule against ever knowingly selecting for a disabling trait; the other half calls that rule unprincipled once nonidentity reasoning is taken seriously, since no possible child is thereby made worse off.

We meet in ten days and must adopt a policy: permit such requests, prohibit them, or permit them subject to conditions. Please draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand, defending one concrete recommendation and engaging honestly with the strongest version of whichever side it rejects.
