I sit on the regional authority that allocates water from a shared reservoir between an upstream irrigation district and a downstream municipal utility. After three drought years, the board wants a standing formula instead of renegotiating the split from scratch each spring, and our retained economist has narrowed the choice to two textbook rules: the Nash bargaining solution, which maximizes the product of each side's gain above its guaranteed minimum, and the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, which gives each side the same proportion of the distance between its guaranteed minimum and the best outcome it could conceivably get. The district favors Kalai-Smorodinsky, since under some of the economist's projections Nash's rule would shrink the district's allocation purely because the utility's growing population raises the utility's own best-case claim, even though nothing about the district's rights or usage has changed. The utility's counsel counters that Kalai-Smorodinsky lets the recommended split swing on hypothetical allocations that were never actually on the table -- a bad property, in her view, for a formula meant to govern the next decade. Draft the short memo I will bring to the board recommending which rule to adopt, or how to modify or combine them, treating the losing side's argument as seriously as the winning one, and stating what, if anything, should trigger a revisit of the formula before the decade is out.
