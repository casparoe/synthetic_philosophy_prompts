I'm writing a historical novel set in the winter of 1793–94, during the months the Marquis de Condorcet spent in hiding from the Terror before his capture and death. In one scene he is shown a mathematical result unknown in his lifetime: that any voting procedure for three or more candidates that always produces a single winner must fall into one of three camps — it is effectively a dictatorship, in which one fixed voter's preference prevails regardless of how others vote; it arbitrarily rules out some candidate from ever winning, however the ballots fall; or it can be gamed by a voter who secures a better outcome by misrepresenting his true preferences. This is, in modern terms, the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. Condorcet, in his own work on majority decisions, had already shown that collective preferences among more than two options can cycle, with no candidate beating all the others outright. Before writing the scene, I want to get his reaction right: given what we actually know of his mathematical convictions and his faith in the perfectibility of political institutions, how do you think he would have responded to this result — as a defeat of his life's project of designing a rational, manipulation-proof democracy, or as a problem his own framework might let him sidestep, given his interest in probabilistic reasoning and in information about voters richer than bare rankings?
