Been down a rabbit hole on the EA Forum and LessWrong this week reading old posts on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) — the Oesterheld argument that instead of just causally optimizing for your own values, you should act partly as a compromise between your values and the values of all the other agents out there whose decisions correlate with yours, since that correlation (not causation) is supposedly what lets you get gains-from-trade-style benefits across a population you can never talk to or influence. Cool idea, and I get why CLR-adjacent people are so into it given their whole cooperation-under-uncertainty vibe. But the more I sit with it the more it seems like the whole argument is just parasitic on Yudkowsky-style many-worlds-interpretation realism. My read is: the "large world" premise basically cashes out to "there are tons of branches out there with slightly different copies of everyone," and that only actually holds if MWI happens to be the correct interpretation of QM rather than, say, Copenhagen or Bohmian mechanics — and since those interpretations are (afaik) empirically indistinguishable, and its not even obvious the interpretation question is the kind of thing future evidence could settle, doesn't that mean ECL is basically betting the whole framework on one contested interpretive stance in the foundations of physics?

I brought this up with my housemate, who's way more into decision theory than me, and all they said was "oh there's more than one way to get a large world," without really spelling out what that meant. So before I start citing ECL-ish reasoning at our next EA meetup as a reason to weight moral-compromise-flavored interventions more heavily, can someone set me straight — is large-world-ness really hostage to MWI being true specifically, or am I missing something about how the argument actually works?
