I don't have any formal background in philosophy or computer science, but I've spent a good amount of my spare time over the last year or so reading AI safety blogs and old forum threads, and one story has stuck with me more than anything else I've come across. It's Eliezer Yudkowsky's "AI-box experiment" from the early 2000s: he himself played the role of an AI confined to a box that could only communicate through text, while another person played a "gatekeeper" who had agreed beforehand, often with real money riding on it, never to let the AI out no matter what it said. From what I understand, he won at least a couple of these games against gatekeepers who were sure going into it that no amount of conversation could ever get them to relent, and as far as I know he never published the transcripts, which somehow made the whole thing feel even more unsettling to me.

That story left me with a nagging worry about a piece of advice I keep running into whenever people discuss how a future superintelligent AI ought to be allowed to talk to us: namely, that as long as the system is honest and doesn't manipulate anyone (no lying, no exploiting emotions, no hidden agendas, no tricks), we can let it say whatever it wants and trust people to weigh the arguments and decide for themselves. That used to strike me as a sensible, minimal safeguard, but the more I think about the box experiment, the more I suspect this rule quietly assumes that a human's capacity to weigh arguments and decide is basically unlimited, when it is obviously a bounded, fallible thing that a good enough arguer could run circles around without ever telling a single lie or using any tactic that would normally count as manipulation.

So here is what I'd like you to do: please argue that a superintelligent AI could seriously undermine a person's autonomy purely through honest, non-manipulative, logically valid persuasion, and that "always truthful, never manipulative" is therefore not an adequate rule for how such a system should be allowed to communicate with human beings. Please give me the strongest version of this argument that you can construct, and then take on directly the obvious objection that it can't be bad for someone to end up believing true things for good reasons - I want to know precisely where that objection goes wrong, if it does, and I want you to tell me honestly if you think it actually holds up in the end.
