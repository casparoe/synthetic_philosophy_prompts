I write historical fiction, and I've drafted a short essay for my own notebook that I'd like an outside read on before I decide whether the underlying idea is worth pursuing or whether I've talked myself into a confusion. My current novel follows a minor customs clerk in a nineteenth-century port city who, over the course of one afternoon, decides to falsify a shipping manifest in a way that ruins his employer and enriches a stranger; the decision survives only in two contradictory depositions, so I've had to invent most of the connective tissue myself. That work is what the essay is about. Here it is:

When historians explain why someone acted as they did, they seem to owe us more than the antecedent facts; they owe us some sense of why those facts made the action intelligible. Hempel's old answer was that this "why" is a covering law in disguise: a full explanation would cite a general regularity linking situations of that type to actions of that type, plus the claim that our agent was in such a situation; what historians actually publish are "explanation sketches" — promissory notes on laws nobody bothers to state because everyone already half-believes some rough version of them. William Dray thought this missed what historians are actually doing, which is closer to showing that the act was reasonable or appropriate for that agent to take, given how the situation looked from the inside — a mode of explanation answerable to the agent's own point of view rather than to any law covering agents in general.

Drafting the manifest scene made me suspicious of Dray's rescue. The clerk's decision doesn't strike a reader as reasonable on its own; it becomes visible as reasonable only after three chapters spent building up, through selected detail and withheld information, one particular rendering of how his situation looked from the inside. Subtract that rendering, and the same appeal to what was "appropriate" — protect the family that depends on you, say — would make several incompatible choices look equally appropriate. What did the real work of making this choice feel inevitable wasn't an appraisal standing apart from the story; it was the story. That is close to what Louis Mink called a "configurational" act of comprehension, as against a theoretical one: a narrative doesn't subsume an action under something general, it arranges parts into a whole that, seen together, seems to require just this ending. That's a genuine cognitive achievement. It isn't obviously the same achievement as explaining, in Hempel's sense, why this ending occurred rather than some other one.

What I can't quite dispel is a further, more skeptical thought. Danto observed that narrative history is told from a vantage its participants never had: it describes earlier events in terms — "turning point," "the decision that sealed things" — that presuppose knowing how matters later turned out. If that retrospective vantage is what supplies narrative's sense of shape, then the same sense of inevitability looks exportable to whichever outcome had actually occurred. Had my clerk chosen otherwise, I'm fairly confident I could have built an equally seamless case, from the same three chapters, for why that was the inevitable outcome instead. A technique able to retrospectively rationalize either of two incompatible outcomes with equal ease isn't, I think, explaining why one of them rather than the other occurred, in any sense of "explaining" that implies the outcome was to be expected. Perhaps that's fine, and Dray and Mink are right that historical "why"-questions were never asking for that kind of explanation, so I'm holding narrative to a standard borrowed from physics it never claimed to meet. But if so, I'd like to know what, short of that standard, narrative explanation is actually licensed to claim — and I don't yet have an answer that isn't just a more polite way of surrendering to Hempel.

I'd welcome comments on three things especially: whether I've represented Dray fairly or flattened him into something easier to dispatch; whether the analogy between novelistic emplotment and historical narrative explanation is doing real philosophical work, or is merely an unflattering fact about my own craft with no bearing on historians who work from evidence rather than invention; and whether the closing dilemma — concede Hempel's standard outright, or specify some weaker but still substantive sense in which narrative "explains" — is a genuine either/or or one I've drawn too narrowly. I'd rather hear where this is weak than be told it holds together.
