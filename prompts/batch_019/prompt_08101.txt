I recently joined the board of trustees of a small regional opera company, a rather unexpected turn for someone whose philosophy degree has mostly gathered dust since graduate school. One of my first substantive tasks has been helping the artistic director think through several proposals currently on the table: investing in cinema-relay technology for live simulcasts, adding projected titles to our performances (which, like the English National Opera, we have always given in English translation rather than the original language), and approving a director's request to reset our upcoming production of Rigoletto in a contemporary setting. In preparing for these discussions, I went back to Bernard Williams's On Opera, having remembered that this leading moral philosopher was, rather improbably, chairman of the board of the English National Opera for many years and a serious writer on the aesthetics of the art form. His central and recurring argument, as I understand it, is that opera's power depends on its manifest artifice: the fact that characters sing instead of speak, that ensembles let several people voice conflicting feelings at once, and that the music itself is thoroughly stylized, is not a defect to be minimized through "realistic" staging but the very mechanism by which the form achieves effects spoken drama cannot.

Williams died in 2003, just before several developments that I am now expected to have a view on became standard practice: cinema relays of the kind the Metropolitan Opera has run since 2006, the spread of projected titles even at houses built around performing in the audience's own language, and the now-routine acceptance of "director's theatre" productions that relocate canonical operas to times and places their composers never imagined. Reasoning as strictly as you can from the actual commitments in his opera writings, rather than from a generic traditionalist-versus-progressive script, how do you think Williams would have assessed these developments? I am especially curious whether his argument about manifest artifice would have made him more receptive to radical restaging than a simple defender of "fidelity to the work" would expect, and whether he would have regarded supertitles as a genuine resolution of the translation question he cared about as ENO chairman, or merely a technological way of sidestepping it.
