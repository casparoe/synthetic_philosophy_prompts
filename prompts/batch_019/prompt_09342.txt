Over a long career in experimental physics, I grew used to a certain pattern: when data stubbornly refused to match a theory's predictions, we almost never blamed the theory outright. Instead, we questioned an instrument's calibration, posited some unmodeled systematic effect, or quietly adjusted a boundary condition, and only as a last resort did anyone seriously propose abandoning the theoretical framework itself. Since retiring, I have taken up philosophy of science in earnest, and I now know that this old habit of mine has a name, or rather two names that are typically run together as one: the "Duhem-Quine thesis." Having finally gone back to Duhem's own book and to Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," rather than relying on the secondhand summaries I had previously absorbed, I am no longer sure the two men are entitled to share a single label. A friend from my old department, now also retired, insists the difference is merely one of emphasis; I suspect there is something more substantial at stake, but I cannot yet marshal a precise argument for why.

Duhem strikes me as the more cautious of the two, worried specifically about mathematical physics and reaching for something he calls "bon sens" to explain how physicists nevertheless manage to choose among rival ways of patching a theory. Quine, on the other hand, seems to want the same holism to cover the whole of human knowledge, mathematics and logic included, with no statement standing permanently immune to revision in the face of what he calls recalcitrant experience.

Could you help me pull these two positions apart on their own terms, rather than in the blended form in which they usually circulate? I would like to understand, first, exactly what Duhem did and did not claim about the scope of his holism, and whether he genuinely thought some empirical sciences might be exempt from it. Second, how much further Quine's version reaches, and in what sense he thought even logical truths were negotiable given sufficiently stubborn evidence. And third, whether their two proposed ways of living with the resulting underdetermination, Duhem's invocation of scientific good sense and Quine's appeal to pragmatic virtues such as simplicity and conservatism, are really doing the same philosophical job or something quite different. What I am ultimately after is whether the composite name has smuggled a fairly modest observation about physics into a far more sweeping epistemological doctrine, and which of the two theses, if either, gives us a genuine reason to think theory is underdetermined by evidence, rather than merely a reminder that logic alone cannot settle which hypothesis to blame when a prediction fails.
