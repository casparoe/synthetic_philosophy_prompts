I've been mulling over a case that a former student of mine — now working in the strategy group of a mid-sized tech company — described to me over coffee last week, and I still haven't decided whether her unease is philosophically well-founded or simply confused. I suspect I will end up adapting it for my seminar on collective decision-making, so I would like to think it through properly first. Her company was choosing between two possible directions for its flagship product, redesign A and redesign B, and rather than have management decide by fiat, it set up an internal market in which employees could wager points, redeemable for year-end bonuses, on which redesign, if adopted, would yield higher user retention twelve months out. Two linked markets ran side by side, one on retention "if A is adopted" and one on retention "if B is adopted," and because the A-market settled on a noticeably higher implied probability of success, leadership committed publicly to redesign A. The B-market was simply voided and its wagers refunded, since that scenario never came to pass; A, in due course, did succeed.

What troubles my former student is this. Once the company had publicly staked its future on A, resources, marketing attention, and staff morale all shifted decisively behind it, so its eventual success may tell us little about whether A was really the superior redesign on its own merits, as opposed to merely the option that the market's verdict had caused everyone to rally behind. She also points out that, since bets on B were always going to be voided unless B was actually chosen, people wagering on B had correspondingly weaker reasons to bet carefully or to seek out good information, which makes her doubt that the two implied probabilities were ever genuinely comparable to begin with. Her worry, put sharply, is that the whole procedure is covertly circular: a forecast is used to make a decision whose downstream consequences then retroactively vindicate that very forecast, so that there is no way, even in principle, to tell whether the market discovered a fact about the redesigns or simply manufactured one.

I would be grateful for your help thinking this through: does it point to a genuine and fairly general difficulty for schemes that govern by betting on outcomes, or does it rest on a confusion that dissolves on closer inspection? Is there a principled way of distinguishing cases in which a forecast-driven decision procedure is vulnerable to this sort of self-vindication from cases in which it is not, and does voiding the losing market's bets make the underlying problem worse, better, or simply beside the point? A careful diagnosis would be far more useful to me than a quick reassurance in either direction.
