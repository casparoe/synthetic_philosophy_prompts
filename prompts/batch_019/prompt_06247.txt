I could use an outside, relatively dispassionate take on a dispute that has become surprisingly heated in my household. Years ago, my husband and I set a rule for our two teenagers: if either of them is caught lying to us about where they actually were (as distinct from just missing curfew, which we treat more leniently), they lose the privilege of going out with friends for the following two weekends. The point was never really to prevent any particular bad outcome; it was more that lying to us about something like that is a serious breach of trust that has to cost the liar something. About two weeks ago, our younger son, Theo, lied about where he'd spent a Friday night, and it came out a few days later. The complication is that his two-weekend grounding is set to begin this weekend, which happens to be the one he has been looking forward to all year: a regional chess tournament he qualified for that he can only attend if he isn't grounded. His older sister, Maya, has nothing in particular going on those two weekends and, entirely on her own initiative, has offered to serve the grounding in his place, on the reasoning that "someone in the house is still paying for it, just not him."

My husband won't allow it. His stated view is that letting Maya serve the grounding "doesn't actually settle anything," and that Theo has to be the one who experiences the consequence or the rule stops meaning anything - though he's also separately worried that if this works, Theo will learn that lying costs him nothing personally as long as his sister is willing to cover for him, which strikes me as a different concern from whether the substitution even makes sense in the first place. I've tried to steelman his core objection, but the best reconstruction I can manage is a bare intuition that consequences of this kind are somehow non-transferable, in a way that a debt is not, and I can't get much further than that. My own instinct, for what it's worth, is that if the rule's whole point is that lying has to cost the household something, and Maya's offer is sincere, fully informed, and matches the original sentence exactly, it shouldn't matter whose weekends get sacrificed - but I've spent enough years reading rationalist and EA blogs to distrust an instinct just because it's mine, especially one this convenient for restoring domestic peace.

So I'd like your help thinking this through properly, rather than just siding with whichever of us is more stubborn: does Maya's offer actually accomplish what the rule was for, or is there a principled reason it can't - something my husband is gesturing at but hasn't managed to articulate?
