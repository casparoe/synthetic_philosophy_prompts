I have been working through Keynes's General Theory for a seminar on the history of economic thought, and in a separate course on information economics we recently covered the no-trade theorems associated with Aumann's agreement theorem and, more specifically, with Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 result: if traders are risk-averse expected-utility maximizers who share a common prior, and it is common knowledge that a trade is on offer, then a rational agent should already have incorporated the mere fact of a counterparty's willingness to trade into her own beliefs, so that no exchange motivated purely by private information can actually occur. This sits uneasily next to the sheer volume of speculative trading in real asset markets, and the usual resolutions economists propose — noise traders, heterogeneous priors, liquidity shocks, limits on common knowledge — all work by denying one of the theorem's premises rather than by challenging its underlying logic.

Keynes, of course, died decades before any of this was formalized, but he wrote at length about the psychology and epistemology of speculative markets on his own terms: the newspaper "beauty contest" of chapter 12 of the General Theory, the idea of asset values resting on a precarious and self-sustaining convention, the appeal to "animal spirits" where calculation gives out, and his liquidity-preference account of why anyone would hold idle cash at all. Earlier still, in the Treatise on Probability and the 1937 restatement in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, he had already insisted that our long-run economic prospects are not the sort of thing over which a rational agent can even form a well-defined probability distribution. Taking all of this together, how do you think Keynes would have responded to the no-trade theorems? Would his objection simply reduce to the familiar move of denying that real traders share a common prior, or does his more radical claim — that long-term expectation resists quantification altogether — pose a deeper challenge to the theorems' Bayesian framework than the standard resolutions do? And is there, conversely, anything in his own emphasis on convention, and on trying to guess what average opinion expects average opinion to be, that bears on the theorems' reliance on common knowledge as exactly the condition under which, on Milgrom and Stokey's account, speculation should collapse?
