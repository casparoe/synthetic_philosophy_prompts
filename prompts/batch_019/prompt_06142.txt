In an intro discrete math course, one question gave students five input-output pairs -- (1,3), (2,5), (3,7), (4,9), (5,11) -- and asked them to state "the rule" in closed form and use it to find the value at 50. The key wanted the obvious answer, f(x) = 2x+1, so f(50) = 101. One student turned in a different closed-form rule instead, built with the old trick of adding a term that's zero at 1 through 5 and nowhere else, which fits all five pairs exactly and spits out a wildly different number at 50. Her appeal argues that nothing in the five pairs the instructor actually wrote down fixes his rule as the one in play rather than hers, or any of the other infinitely many rules that also fit -- so marking her "wrong" just means marking her for failing to read his mind. The instructor wants to give her almost no credit for the final answer.

There are five of us on the committee, and the other four are deadlocked two against two, so it's fallen to me to write the opinion that breaks the tie. Something about this case feels like it should have a name -- I've got a nagging memory of an argument along these lines from grad school, but I can't reconstruct it well enough to actually use in a written decision.
