My dad died about three weeks ago, and because I'm the one in the family doing a PhD (criminology, mostly theories of punishment), I've somehow been nominated to give the eulogy this weekend, which everyone assumes will be "the good one" since I supposedly think about this stuff for a living. Here's my actual problem, and it's not just nerves: I spent a big chunk of last semester on Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" for quals, the bit about reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, that whole family) versus the objective attitude, and the difference between pleas that just modify a reactive attitude from inside the practice ("he didn't mean it," "he was pushed") and pleas that pull someone out of the practice entirely and make them an object of management instead ("he's not himself," "he's not really a fit target for that kind of reaction at all"). I cannot stop reading my own family's arguments through this lens and its making the eulogy impossible to write instead of just sad.

My mom (his ex-wife) basically wants a full exemption: he was an alcoholic, the drinking did it, don't be angry at a sick man. My aunt wants something closer to an honest ledger of what he actually did to people over the years. Both feel wrong to me but I cant fully say why without just going "well, Strawson says" at people, which isn't an argument. Part of it, I think, is that "the addiction made him do it" is doing something sneaky: if a general appeal like that were enough to exempt someone, it should exempt everyone from everything, since on some story everyone's behavior is caused by something. So whatever's supposed to be doing the exempting work has to be more local than that, and I don't think my mom's version is that local. To make it messier, he did get sober for his last three years and tried, clumsily, to make amends, including one long email to me I never really answered, so there's actual material for something like forgiveness in there too — and forgiveness is literally on Strawson's own list of reactive attitudes, which feels important but I can't work out how to use it.

Could you write the eulogy itself — something readable aloud in 5-7 minutes at the actual funeral — that keeps my dad inside the reactive, participant relationship (someone whose actions could wrong people and whose late efforts could genuinely count for something) instead of quietly sliding him into the purely objective, disease-explains-it-all bucket, but without turning into a list of grievances either? Most of the room is going to be family and old coworkers who've never heard the phrase "reactive attitude" in their life, but two people from my cohort are coming, and I'd know immediately if I chickened out and just wrote generic Hallmark-eulogy filler underneath.
