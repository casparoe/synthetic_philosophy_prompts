My friend Devin does this thing he calls 'arbing' -- when two betting apps have odds on the same game that don't line up right, he bets both sides and locks in a profit no matter who wins. He was bragging about it at lunch and my friend Lena, who's weirdly into philosophy, said that's literally a Dutch book, and its the exact setup philosophers use to argue that inconsistent probabilities are irrational, not just badly priced. Devin said no -- that just proves the apps are exploitable if someone catches it in time, it doesn't mean their odds are 'irrational,' any more than a weak vault code being easy to crack makes the code irrational instead of just risky. Lena said we already treat exploitability as proof of irrationality elsewhere, like how circular preferences (A over B, B over C, but then C over A) count as irrational because someone can money-pump you out of everything you own. Devin said fine, but then nobody is ever rational, since no one actually checks that their odds on two totally different-sounding statements that happen to be logically equivalent actually match -- so if that's the standard, it convicts everybody and proves nothing. Lena's comeback was that there's an actual theorem showing that if your odds ARE consistent, you literally cannot be Dutch booked, so the connection isn't a coincidence, it runs both ways. Devin said a two-way link still doesn't tell you which side is the 'real' definition of rational and which one just tags along for the ride. They've been going at it in our group chat for two days and want me to settle it. Can you go through this move by move and say who actually has the stronger point at each step, and whether there's something both of them are missing?
