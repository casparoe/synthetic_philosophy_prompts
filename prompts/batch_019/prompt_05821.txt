In the game theory elective I'm taking this semester, we spent a week on two-person bargaining problems, and one detail from that unit has been bothering me ever since. Our teacher first showed us John Nash's classic bargaining solution, which picks whichever feasible split maximizes the product of each side's gain over what they would get if talks broke down, and explained that it can be justified by a small set of axioms, including one called independence of irrelevant alternatives. She then mentioned, almost as an aside, that Ehud Kalai and Meir Smorodinsky proposed a rival solution back in 1975 that drops that particular axiom in favor of a monotonicity axiom instead, and that the two solutions can actually recommend different splits for the very same problem once the feasible set is shaped asymmetrically enough. For our final project, we have to design and defend a formula for splitting a sponsorship payment between our robotics team's build and software sub-teams. Their combined fundraising potential depends in a lopsided, non-linear way on how the credit and visibility get divided between them, and each sub-team could still separately pursue smaller sponsors on its own if the split talks collapsed, which I believe economists call the disagreement point. I want to actually understand what is at stake in choosing between the two solution concepts before I pick one to defend, rather than just repeating whichever explanation I happened to read most recently.

Could you write me a dialogue between two negotiation consultants brought in to mediate exactly this kind of dispute, one of whom is a committed defender of the Nash bargaining solution and the other an equally committed defender of the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, arguing in front of a whiteboard sketch of the feasible set over which split their clients should actually accept? I would like the Nash advocate to press the other on why giving up independence of irrelevant alternatives is remotely acceptable, and I would like the Kalai-Smorodinsky advocate to push back with a genuine, concrete case, not just a slogan, of the sort of situation that made dropping that axiom seem necessary, with each side directly answering the other's strongest objections rather than talking past each other. Please do not let either side come across as the obvious loser of the exchange, and they need not reach an agreement by the end. I am mostly hoping to see the two positions in real tension with each other, since every explanation of this disagreement I have found on my own has made one side sound clearly foolish, and I would genuinely like to understand why a smart person might hold either view.
