I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, but I've recently been reading about Augustine's writings on lying, in *De Mendacio* and *Contra Mendacium*. I was struck by how absolute his position is: he argues that asserting something one believes to be false is always sinful, even as a so-called "officious lie" told purely to protect an innocent person from someone who means to kill them. At the same time, he treats staying silent, being evasive, or letting someone draw their own mistaken conclusion as something other than lying, and therefore sometimes permissible.

Using those specific distinctions, rather than a generic "lying is wrong" intuition, predict how Augustine would rule on four practices from software engineering that obviously postdate him:

1. A warrant canary: a company periodically publishes a statement that it has not received a secret government data request, and quietly stops publishing that statement, without further comment, the moment it does receive one.
2. A honeypot: a fake server or set of credentials deliberately planted to feed false information to an attacker who is actively trying to breach a system.
3. A phishing simulation: a security team sends employees a fake email impersonating an executive, in order to test and train their ability to spot real attacks.
4. A customer-support chatbot instructed to adopt a human first name and speak in the first person, without ever disclosing to the customer that it is software.

For each, state whether Augustine's own framework would treat it as a genuine lie or as permissible misdirection, and give the reasoning he would use rather than just the verdict. Then tell me which of the four sits most awkwardly within that framework, and why.
