At a reading group I'm part of, we'd just worked through a cluster of papers on causation, and two of the members, Nadia and Owen, ended up in a long argument afterward about a case one of the papers used to press the question of whether omissions can be causes. The setup: a woman going away for a week asks her neighbor to water her one houseplant twice while she's gone; the neighbor forgets the one time it actually mattered, and the plant dies of drought.

(1) Nadia opened by saying this is obviously a case of causation: had the neighbor watered the plant as asked, it would have lived, and that counterfactual dependence is all we ever mean by "caused." (2) Owen disagreed: causation, he said, requires some actual physical process linking cause to effect, and no such process runs from a non-event -- the neighbor's simply not doing something -- to the plant's dying; what really caused the death, strictly speaking, was just the plant's own physiological reaction to a lack of water. (3) Nadia replied that this can't be right, since we routinely and correctly say things like "the lifeguard's not jumping in caused the swimmer's death," and denying that omissions can ever be causes would leave us unable to explain why people are held causally, and not merely morally, responsible for what they fail to do. (4) Owen came back with a different problem for Nadia's view rather than conceding: if mere counterfactual dependence were enough for causation, then every person on Earth who didn't water the plant -- the neighbor two doors down, a stranger in Peru who's never heard of any of them -- would be equally a cause of its death, since any one of them watering it would also have kept it alive; something has to narrow the field, and the only candidates on offer, facts about who was asked or whose job it was, are normative facts about obligation, which seems like the wrong sort of thing to be doing metaphysical work in a theory of causation. (5) Nadia responded that ordinary "positive" causation is already just as selective: nobody calls the oxygen in the room "a cause" of a fire even though it's just as counterfactually necessary as the dropped match, so picking out the neighbor via facts about obligation is no more suspect than picking out the match rather than the oxygen. (6) Owen's last word was that the two cases aren't parallel: with the match and the oxygen, everyone agrees a full causal process runs through both, so normality is only being used to pick out which of several genuine causes is worth mentioning; with the neighbor, there's no underlying causal process to begin with, so appealing to obligation or normal practice there doesn't select a real cause among several -- it manufactures one out of nothing.

I couldn't work out who'd actually gotten the better of this. Could you go through the six moves in order and say, for each, who has the stronger position and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's a more defensible view in this vicinity that neither Nadia nor Owen actually stated.
