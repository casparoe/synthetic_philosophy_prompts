Been on a bit of an imprecise-probabilities kick lately (the idea, going back to Isaac Levi and Peter Walley, that your credence in something doesn't have to be a single number - it can be a whole set of probability functions, sometimes called "mushy credences," and which I gather is basically the philosophical ancestor of the "infra-Bayesianism" stuff that shows up on LW every once in a while). Got into it with a friend at a meetup last week about whether the view is even coherent as a theory of rational choice, and he brought up an argument I'm pretty sure is from Adam Elga's "Subjective Probabilities Should Be Sharp" paper (though its been years since I read it, so don't hold me to the details) that I haven't been able to figure my way out of.

Roughly: say your credence that some coin lands heads isn't a sharp 0.5 but the whole interval [0.4, 0.6]. You're offered a bet that only pays off well if your credence in heads is above 0.45 - since 0.45 sits inside your interval, both taking and refusing the bet count as "permissible" (some probability in your set rationalizes each). You refuse it, using the low end of your interval as your excuse. Then you're offered a second bet, a mirror image of the first, that only pays off well if your credence in heads is below 0.55 - again both options are "permissible" by identical reasoning, and this time you refuse using the high end of your interval as your excuse. Thing is, refusing both bets leaves you with nothing, while accepting both would've guaranteed you a small profit no matter how the coin landed. So two individually "permissible" choices add up to a pair that's just strictly worse than an alternative pair that was sitting right there the whole time - something a sharp-credence Bayesian, stuck using one fixed number for both bets, could never be maneuvered into.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people arguing this out - one who's fully bought into imprecise credences as the right theory of rational belief, and one who thinks this exact case shows credences have to be sharp? I want real back-and-forth, not two people talking past each other or one of them being an obvious pushover - the imprecise-credence person should get to try the serious replies (e.g. insisting you have to evaluate whole plans/policies rather than bet-by-bet, or denying that "rationalized by some point in your set" was ever the right gloss on "permissible" to begin with, or turning the tables and asking whether the sharp-credence person has some quietly analogous problem once you look at how they handle updating on evidence whose reliability they're unsure of), and the sharp-credence person should actually engage with those instead of just repeating themselves. Doesn't need to resolve neatly - I'm more interested in whether the imprecise view has any good moves here than in a tidy verdict either way.
