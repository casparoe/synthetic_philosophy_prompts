In discussions of AI alignment, inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is often presented as an attractive alternative to hand-specifying an agent's goals: instead of writing down a reward function directly, you let the agent observe a human's choices and try to recover the reward function that best explains them. I've been working through some of the value-learning literature built on this idea -- from the basic inverse-RL setup, through "Boltzmann-rational" or noisily-optimal models of the human chooser, to interactive frameworks like cooperative inverse reinforcement learning, where the human and the learning agent solve a shared game rather than the agent simply watching a human go about an unrelated task -- and I keep running into a worry that resurfaces in different guises across this literature: that no body of behavioral data, however large, can on its own single out a unique reward function, because any observed choices can be explained equally well by pairing a different reward function with a correspondingly different assumption about how the chooser turns reward into action (how biased, myopic, or noisy their "planning" process is).

The sharpest version of this worry, formalized in results along the lines of those given by Armstrong and Mindermann, is that the ambiguity survives even an appeal to simplicity. For any reward function you might favor, however baroque, there is some model of the agent's decision-making that makes the observed behavior come out as ideally rational relative to that reward; shifting complexity between the "reward" component and the "planner" component leaves the complexity of the pair as a whole roughly unchanged, so Occam's razor cannot break the tie. If that is right, some substantive, independently motivated assumption about rationality has to be supplied from outside the behavioral data before "inferring preferences from behavior" means anything determinate. This has an obvious older cousin in debates over whether attributing beliefs and desires to an agent -- in Quine's and Davidson's sense of radical interpretation -- is ever purely a matter of reading facts off behavior, rather than an interpretive act that presupposes a background standard of rationality (a "principle of charity") from the very start.

Write an essay that takes a definite position on the following question: can an agent's real preferences ever be inferred from its behavior alone, or is such inference always and unavoidably parasitic on a substantive, non-behavioral assumption about the agent's rationality? Lay out the strongest case for thinking the ambiguity is fatal to the whole enterprise of "learning" human values from choices -- rendering talk of the "true" reward behind a policy close to meaningless -- and the strongest case for thinking the problem, while real, is neither special to IRL nor an obstacle to genuine progress (for instance, because it is structurally the same underdetermination that any empirical inference faces, or because interactive settings let the rationality assumption be tested and revised rather than merely posited once and for all). Close by defending a specific verdict rather than leaving the two sides balanced -- something with the shape of "the impossibility result is correct, but it shows less than it is usually taken to show, namely ___," or its denial, whichever your own arguments actually support.
