I've been on a bit of a Maimonides kick lately (it started from an old ACX-adjacent argument about animal welfare and spiraled from there), and there's a specific piece of his thinking I can't stop chewing on. In the Guide for the Perplexed he argues that God only commanded animal sacrifice because the Israelites were so steeped in the sacrificial culture of the ancient Near East that abolishing it outright wasn't psychologically realistic, so the practice got redirected toward the one God as a kind of stepping stone, with the implicit long-run endpoint being pure prayer with no slaughter involved at all. Meanwhile, in a different part of the Guide, he goes out of his way to say there's basically no real difference between animal pain and human pain, and uses that to explain laws like not slaughtering a mother animal and its young on the same day, even though he's also pretty committed elsewhere to the Aristotelian idea that animals sit lower down the hierarchy of creation, partly there for human benefit.

I vaguely remember reading that Rav Kook later took Maimonides own concession-theory move and just kept running with it, arguing meat-eating itself (not only sacrifice) is a temporary accomodation that's supposed to end with a return to Eden-style vegetarianism once humanity's ready for it, citing the wolf-and-lamb stuff from Isaiah. I might be mangling the details there, but that's the shape of the argument as I remember it.

So here's my actual question: imagine sitting Maimonides down in front of an actual industrial broiler farm or a modern slaughterhouse, the scale and mechanization and all of it. Do his own commitments push him toward Kook's conclusion, that this is simply the next rung on a ladder his concession theory already set in motion? Or does he have specific, principled reasons - the idea that Torah law doesn't change once given, the hierarchy stuff, the fact that his animal-welfare concern is about training human character rather than animals having claims against us - to resist that extension and say factory farming is just a different question his theory was never built to answer? I don't want the "he obviously would've been horrified, he liked animals" answer. Pick a side, defend it using his actual specific commitments, and be honest about where his own views end up in tension with each other either way.
