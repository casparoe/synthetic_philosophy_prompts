During my career as a physicist working mostly in cosmology, I only ever met the anthropic principle as a heuristic for model-building; now that I'm reading philosophy in retirement, I've been struck by how contested the underlying reasoning about observer selection really is. Please write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers: one defending the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), on which you should reason as though randomly drawn from all observers who actually exist, and one defending the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), on which you should reason as though randomly drawn from all observers who could possibly exist. Set the scene at Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher": two cosmological theories, so far indistinguishable by any evidence, imply vastly different numbers of observers, and physicists are about to run a costly experiment to decide between them when the SIA-defender interjects that the experiment is superfluous. Have the SSA-defender push back, and let the two work through each other's real difficulty in turn — the presumptuousness of SIA, and the Doomsday Argument that seems to fall out of SSA — with neither side flattening the other's position into a strawman.
