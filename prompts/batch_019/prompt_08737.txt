Random one from the science-journalism side of my job: I'm one of the judges for a mid-size prize that covers long-form science and ideas writing, and this year's shortlist has thrown up an annoying pair of finalists. Both wrote big features built around a not-yet-peer-reviewed result — one on a room-temperature superconductivity claim, one on an exoplanet biosignature claim — going further out on a limb than most science desks would normally risk, with roughly the same level of hedging and source-checking at the time each piece ran. Eighteen months on, one of those results has been independantly replicated and is being treated as a huge deal; the other quietly fell apart once other labs couldn't reproduce it. Half the judging panel wants to just give the prize to the writer who's bet paid off and drop the other from contention; the other half thinks that rewards an outcome neither writer had any way of controlling for, and that we should be scoring the journalism as it stood at publication, not how the science happened to shake out later. Can you draft the short memo I'll put to the full panel before we vote next week — an actual recommendation, not a fence-sit — that takes the "reward what worked" camp seriously instead of just waving it off?
