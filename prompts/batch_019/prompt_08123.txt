I'm a novelist -- which means compressing and reordering lived time is half of what I do for a living -- and I chair the five-person jury for the Bellwether Prize, a foundation's annual award for literary nonfiction. Our presumptive winner, Marta Alsop's memoir The Long Ledger, turns on a deathbed reconciliation with her estranged father; our fact-checker has found, from hospital records and family interviews, that Alsop hadn't seen him in a decade and was three states away the week the book places her at his bedside. Her defense is that the scene is 'true in every way that matters' -- a decade of estrangement compressed into the encounter she needed to write, and no reader is misled about who her father was to her. Two jurors want to rescind the nomination, since a nonfiction prize means nothing if it certifies a scene any competent investigator would reject once the records are in; two others want the award to stand, since memoir has never promised courtroom accuracy, only fidelity to what the years amounted to. We vote Friday. Please draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand, recommending one outcome -- rescind, award with a published note, or award outright -- and engaging honestly with the strongest argument against whatever you recommend.
