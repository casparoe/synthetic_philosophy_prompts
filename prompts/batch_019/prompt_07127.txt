I just finished 10th grade and this summer I did one of those residential math camps where you live in the dorms for a few weeks and take way too many classes. One night after an optional evening talk on the Lowenheim-Skolem theorem, two of the counselors (Iris and Sam, both math grad students) ended up arguing about it the entire walk back to the dorms, and I just kind of trailed behind listening instead of going to bed like I should have. Quick version of the setup since it matters for the argument: ZFC is a first-order theory in a countable language, so if its consistent it has to have a countable model (thats the downward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem). But ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem, so that countable model contains some set which the model ITSELF thinks is uncountable -- meaning no function inside the model witnesses a bijection with the naturals. That's "Skolem's paradox," and apparently everyone agrees it's not an actual contradiction, just deeply weird.

Here's roughly how the argument went. (1) Iris said this shows uncountability isn't some absolute property a set just has, it's relative to whatever model you happen to be looking from -- and that this was basically Skolem's own conclusion, that no first-order theory can ever pin down a single "intended" universe of sets, so naive realism about sets (the idea that there's just one true universe our axioms are describing) is in real trouble. (2) Sam said that's overreaching: there's no actual contradiction, the model is just missing the one specific bijection that would prove its set countable -- that bijection exists just fine outside the model -- so it's the model that's incomplete, not the concept of uncountability itself. (3) Iris pushed back: how do you know there's some privileged "outside" that isn't ALSO just another model missing stuff? By Lowenheim-Skolem going the other direction you can get a model of basically any infinite size satisfying the exact same sentences, so what makes hers "the real one" instead of just another equally valid perspective? (4) Sam said that objection isn't special to set theory at all -- first-order Peano arithmetic has countable nonstandard models too, and nobody thinks that makes "finite" relative or meaningless, we just accept first-order logic can't be fully categorical and lean on second-order logic instead (which does pin PA down uniquely, and pins set theory down a lot more too) to talk about what the intended model even is. (5) Iris said leaning on second-order logic is exactly the move she doesn't trust, since second-order logic only pins anything down if it already has a fixed, determinate notion of "all subsets of the domain" -- and that's precisely the kind of thing in question here, so it's not a neutral fix, it's smuggling the assumption in through the back door.
