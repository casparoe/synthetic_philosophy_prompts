I've been rereading Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" while reporting a longer feature on how the current boom in large language models has resurrected some very old disputes in philosophy of mind. What strikes me on this pass through the text is that Turing didn't simply propose the imitation game and leave the door open for objections; in the section where he works through anticipated criticisms, he directly takes on what he labels the argument from consciousness, built on a claim from the neurosurgeon Geoffrey Jefferson's 1949 Lister Oration that no machine could be credited with genuine thought unless it could feel what it was doing and know that it was the author of, say, a sonnet, rather than having merely produced one through some mechanical process empty of inner experience. Turing's answer amounts to a behaviorist shrug: demanding that sort of first-person proof from a machine, while never demanding it of other people, is just solipsism dressed up as scientific caution, and he suggests we extend machines the same "polite convention" by which we take each other's thinking on faith.

Thirty years later, John Searle's Chinese Room turns up making an argument that sounds like a cousin of Jefferson's but is officially about something else: not whether the man in the room (or the system he's embedded in) feels anything, but whether the correct manipulation of symbols by syntactic rule could ever amount to grasping what those symbols mean, however fluent the output looks from outside. For the piece, I'd like your best-supported reconstruction of how Turing — reasoning from the actual commitments visible in his own 1950 text, not the flattened "Turing test" of popular summary — would have responded had he lived to read Searle's 1980 argument directly. Would he have treated the room as just a syntax-flavored remake of the objection he thought he'd already retired, and reached for the same solipsism rebuttal? Or does Searle's syntax/semantics distinction actually slip past that particular move, leaving a consistent Turing needing a genuinely new line of defense?

If it's the latter, I'd also like your sense of which of the replies Searle himself anticipates and tries to defuse in that same 1980 paper — the Systems Reply, the Robot Reply, the Other Minds Reply — Turing's brand of behaviorism would most naturally commit him to, and where exactly that choice would leave him exposed to Searle's counter. It's a comparison I haven't seen made carefully in any of the AI-and-philosophy coverage I've come across so far, which is part of why I want to get it right rather than just gesturing at "Turing test versus Chinese Room" as though the two men were arguing about the same thing.
