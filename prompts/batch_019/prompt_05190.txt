Quick context: I'm a third-year econ PhD student, and this semester's epistemic game theory seminar just spent two sessions on Aumann's 1995 result that common knowledge of rationality (CKR) implies the backward induction outcome in games of perfect information, plus all the fallout -- Binmore's objections, the Ben-Porath/Reny stuff about what happens once you stop assuming CKR survives off the equilibrium path, Stalnaker's belief-revision framework, etc. Our instructor's line (and I think this is more or less the going view now) is that Aumann was basically right, and that the older "paradox of backward induction" worries -- Pettit & Sugden, Binmore, Bicchieri -- rested on sloppy thinking about what common knowledge of rationality actually requires. Once you formalize it properly the paradox supposedly just dissolves and there's nothing left for the critics to complain about.

I'm not convinced, but its still a pretty half-formed intuition on my end, so I want to see it argued out properly before I embarrass myself in section. Here's the worry: Aumann's theorem is a claim about a single fixed state of his model -- it says IF CKR holds at a state THEN backward induction is played at that state, full stop, no updating or revision anywhere in sight. But the informal paradox was always about something else: what a player is entitled to conclude, or do, once she's actually sitting at a node off the backward-induction path, having observed a "deviation" -- a genuinely dynamic question about belief revision under surprise. My suspicion is that Aumann's result, precisely because it's a synchronic fact about one possible world rather than a claim about revising beliefs on reaching a node that "shouldn't" have been reached, just isn't answering the question Binmore and Pettit & Sugden were asking -- so everyone who says the theorem "dissolves" the paradox is quietly changing the subject rather than resolving it. Can you argue for that position as forcefully as you can: that the CKR-implies-backward-induction theorem and the backward-induction paradox are, underneath the shared vocabulary, two different problems, so that settling the first leaves the second exactly where it was.
