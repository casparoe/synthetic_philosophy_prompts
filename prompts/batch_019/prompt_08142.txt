so my friend Dan (he's basically a walking set theory textbook) and I spent like two hours arguing about this "throwing darts at the real line" thing last night and neither of us backed down. his version: suppose CH is true, so you can well-order the reals so that every real only has countably many predecessors. define f(x) as that countable set of predecessors. now throw two darts independently at the line to get reals x and y -- since f(x) is countable it has measure zero, so y basically can't land in it, and by the same logic x can't land in f(y) either. but the well-order says one of them HAS to be a predecessor of the other, so you get a flat contradiction, and CH has to go. he calls this "Freiling's axiom of symmetry" and says its basically decisive, and that im just being stubborn for not accepting it.

i think it's a slick trick but not an actual argument against CH, because the whole "measure zero = the dart can't land there" intuition is borrowed from ordinary, nicely-behaved sets, and i don't see why it should survive once f is built out of a choice-constructed well-ordering that's about as far from measurable as you can get. i also suspect the "symmetry" premise is quietly assuming exactly the regularity that's equivalent to negating CH in the first place, which would make the whole thing circular. i couldn't get this out clearly enough at 1am and ended up loosing the argument by default. can you write out the strongest, most careful version of my side -- that Freiling's dart argument gives us no real reason to reject CH -- precise enough that it'd actually hold up against a set theory grad student and not just against a tired guy at a bar?
