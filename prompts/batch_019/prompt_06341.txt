For my IB Philosophy Extended Essay, my supervisor has already rejected two research questions for being the kind of thing anyone could answer without reading a single source ("Can AI think?", "Is AI conscious?"), so I want to go narrower still, into the realism debate inside mechanistic interpretability. Here is the background: because language models pack more features into their activations than they have neurons for (a phenomenon called superposition), individual directions in that activation space tend to be polysemantic, firing for clusters of unrelated concepts rather than one clean idea. Sparse autoencoders are used to unpack these into apparently monosemantic "features," yet the very same activations resolve into different, more numerous features as the autoencoder's dictionary is enlarged, so no single decomposition looks privileged. At the same time, intervening directly on one of these features — as when Anthropic clamped a "Golden Gate Bridge" feature and Claude began steering almost every answer toward the bridge — changes the model's behavior exactly as the representational story predicts. I want the essay to use that tension, between real causal traction and the absence of a uniquely correct decomposition, to adjudicate between a Dennett-style "real patterns" realism, on which a feature counts as a genuine representation as long as it earns its keep predictively, and a more eliminativist line, in the spirit of William Ramsey's critique of connectionist representation, on which "representation" here is just vocabulary the system never needed. Please write the essay: lay out the strongest considerations for each side, and end by actually defending one of them rather than concluding that "it depends."
