I've been working through Gongsun Long's 白馬論 ("Discourse on the White Horse") for a seminar paper on the School of Names, and I keep wondering how far his logical apparatus actually travels beyond horses. Gongsun Long defends the thesis 白馬非馬 ("a white horse is not a horse") partly on the grounds that "horse" names a shape while "white" names a color, so the compound term cannot be coextensive with the simple one, and partly via the "seeking a horse" argument: a request for a horse can be satisfied by a yellow or black horse, but a request for a white horse cannot, so the two terms pick out different ranges of things. Suppose Gongsun Long learned of the International Astronomical Union's 2006 decision, made in Prague, to define "dwarf planet" as a category that does not fall under "planet" at all -- a stipulation many found linguistically perverse, since a dwarf star is still counted as a star and a dwarf galaxy as a galaxy, whereas Pluto, once reclassified, is now officially not a planet. Working only from the logical resources of the white horse argument, how do you think Gongsun Long would have evaluated the IAU's move -- as vindicating the very reasoning that makes a white horse not a horse, or as a superficially similar but importantly different inference? Reconstruct the strongest argument you think he would have made, and identify precisely where the analogy between "white horse"/"horse" and "dwarf planet"/"planet" holds and where it breaks down.
