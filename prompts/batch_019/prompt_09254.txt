A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about higher-order theories of consciousness, and the conversation ended without either of us persuaded. My friend defends the view that a mental state is phenomenally conscious just when it is the target of a suitable higher-order thought — roughly, a non-inferential, assertoric thought to the effect that one is right now in that very state — while the specific sensory quality of the state is supposed to be a first-order matter that the higher-order thought does not itself supply, only make conscious. I pressed the familiar worry that, since the higher-order thought is a distinct existence from whatever first-order state it targets, nothing rules out its misfiring: it could occur while misrepresenting its target, or while lacking a target altogether. My friend bit the bullet, insisting that even then the subject would still seem to itself, from the inside, to be having the experience, and that this seeming is all phenomenal consciousness was ever supposed to be. I objected that this leaves the theory unable to say where the determinate quality of the experience — its being redness rather than greenness that seems present — comes from once there is no first-order state left to supply it, and that "the higher-order thought's content just is the quality" looks like it quietly redefines phenomenal character as conceptual content. Neither of us could dislodge the other.

Write a dialogue between a higher-order theorist and this kind of critic that carries the exchange further than we managed. Have the critic press the "where does the determinate quality come from" objection in its strongest form, and have the theorist offer the best available reply — whether by biting the bullet harder, restricting which cases of misrepresentation are genuinely possible, or some other route. Both sides should be equally sharp, with each turn responding to the specific point just made rather than restating a fixed position.
