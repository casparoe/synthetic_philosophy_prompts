Random one from a rabbit hole I fell down for work: I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm currently poking at a piece on scapegoating and vicarious punishment (it started from a totally different angle — corporate fines standing in for actual jail time — and spiraled from there). Somewhere in that spiral I ended up reading a short, weirdly delightful David Lewis paper called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", which threw me because I only knew him as the possible-worlds/modal-realism guy from undergrad, not someone who wrote seriously about atonement theology.

His basic move, as best I can tell, is this: we clearly accept that suffering can be vicarious (someone else bears pain so you don't have to, no issue there), and we clearly accept that debts can be vicarious too (if I owe you a hundred bucks and my sister pays it off, your claim's satisfied, full stop, because money is fungible and you don't actually care who's hands it passed through). But he thinks punishment is a much harder case, because on the standard retributive picture, justice isn't satisfied just by *some equivalent suffering happening somewhere* — it specifically wants the guilty party to suffer for what they did. Punishment, unlike a debt, seems essentially tied to the wrongdoer rather than fungible the way money is. So if an innocent party suffers in the guilty party's place, it's not obvious that anyone's punishment has actually occurred, as opposed to some unrelated suffering that merely resembles it.

This has been bugging me ever since, so I'd like you to push back on it for me: make the strongest case you can that Lewis is wrong, i.e. that punishment really can be discharged by a willing substitute in a way that's continuous with (not just superficially similar to) how a monetary debt gets discharged by a guarantor. I'd rather you engage with the retributivist assumptions directly — what desert actually requires, whether "liability to be punished" might be more transferable than "guilt" even if guilt itself isn't — than just fall back on "well, religious doctrines don't have to be logically tidy." Go after the strongest version of the objection, not a strawman of it.
