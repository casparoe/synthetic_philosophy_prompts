A friend of mine and I spent a long evening arguing about Johannes Kepler after we had both worked through the Kepler chapters of Arthur Koestler's *The Sleepwalkers* for our book club. The disagreement ended with both of us settling on a shared conclusion that I've been turning over ever since: that Kepler's astrological output — the horoscopes he cast for patrons like the general Wallenstein, the yearly prognostica he owed his employer as part of his salaried duties, even a full treatise like *Tertius Interveniens* defending astrology against a skeptical physician — belongs to a completely separate ledger from his astronomy. On this reading, the imperial mathematician's salary needed his astrology to keep flowing, so he supplied the horoscopes the market wanted, while the actual reasoning behind elliptical orbits and the three laws of planetary motion proceeded on entirely independent grounds and would look exactly the same whether or not he had ever touched astrology. By the end of the night we were treating the nested Platonic solids of the *Mysterium Cosmographicum* and the harmonic ratios of the *Harmonices Mundi* as charming packaging Kepler used to make an essentially modern physics presentable to a pre-modern audience, rather than as anything doing real intellectual work.

Since that compartmentalized story seems basically right, I'm puzzled by how much weight historians of science still put on Kepler's astrological writings — treating something like *Tertius Interveniens*, or the extended discussion of harmonic aspects earlier in the *Harmonices Mundi*, as necessary background for understanding how he arrived at that same book's astronomical climax in Book Five, rather than as a separable curiosity that merely happens to share an author with the physics. If the mercenary-sideline picture is correct, why do serious Kepler scholars keep insisting on reading the astrology and the astronomy together instead of just bracketing the former as a biographical detail explained by his need for patronage? Is there some feature of his actual reasoning that this picture leaves out, or is the continued scholarly attention to his "reformed astrology" more a matter of antiquarian thoroughness than anything that should change how we understand the science he's actually remembered for?
