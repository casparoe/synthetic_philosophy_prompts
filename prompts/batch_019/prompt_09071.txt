For the past three years I have volunteered with a mentoring program that pairs adult volunteers with teenagers who have been formally identified by their schools as "at risk of dropping out," with many also carrying a diagnosis of oppositional defiant disorder. Something about this arrangement has started to unsettle me. When a student is first flagged, the label often fits only loosely; it functions more as an administrative placeholder than as an accurate description of the child. Yet within a year or two, many of these teenagers seem to grow into the label: they begin describing their own moods and choices using the clinical vocabulary they picked up from counselors and support groups, and their behavior increasingly conforms to the profile sketched on the original intake form. Meanwhile, when the school district revises its screening criteria every few years, the new criteria are visibly shaped by the patterns caseworkers observed in students who were already carrying the old label, so the very definition of "at risk" keeps shifting, as though it were chasing a population it had a hand in creating.

When I raised this with my program director, she told me I was overanalyzing a simple matter: a diagnosis is a diagnosis, she said, no different in kind from a fracture that looks the same on an X-ray whether or not the patient has ever heard the word for it. That comparison does not sit right with me, precisely because the X-ray image does not change once the patient learns the term for their condition, whereas these students' self-understanding and conduct seem to visibly shift once they are told what they supposedly are, and the categories themselves seem to shift in response to them. I do not have much background in philosophy of science or psychiatry, so I lack a good vocabulary for what is bothering me here. Is this simply confirmation bias on my part, an ordinary case of self-fulfilling prophecy, evidence of sloppy diagnostic practice that better instruments could eventually fix, or is something more fundamentally different going on when we classify people rather than things? I would welcome help thinking through what is actually happening here, and what, if anything, it implies for how the program ought to use these labels going forward.
