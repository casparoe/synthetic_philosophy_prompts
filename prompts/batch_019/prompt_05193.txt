A friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing over David Hume's economic essays and never quite settled it. I'd raised his case against mercantilism: in "Of the Balance of Trade," Hume argues that no nation can hoard specie for long, since any inflow of gold raises domestic prices until its exports lose their edge and the metal drains back out, making a policy of permanent trade surpluses self-defeating. My friend granted that, but pointed to "Of Money," where Hume also holds that a nation with specie still flowing in is more industrious than one where the stock has settled, because wages and prices lag behind the new money for a while, and it's precisely in that "interval" that the stimulus to industry is real, not illusory. He took this as Hume quietly conceding the mercantilists' practical advice — keep the specie flowing in — even while mocking their reasons for wanting it. I replied that a transitional effect which the price mechanism itself guarantees will dissolve again is nothing like the standing treasure mercantilists prized. My friend's harder case came from "Of Interest": there, Hume points to Spain, glutted with New World silver yet stuck with stubbornly high interest, to argue that the quantity of money has nothing to do with interest rates, which track instead the profits of commerce, the extent of accumulated stock, and the demand for borrowing. His challenge: why does an inflow of money get to be a genuine, if temporary, cause of higher industry in one essay, but a merely coincidental companion of lower interest in the other, with commerce doing all the real causal work only when it's convenient for Hume's argument? I had no good answer at the table. Could you reconstruct whatever principle would let Hume draw that line consistently, and say whether my friend has actually caught him in a real inconsistency?
