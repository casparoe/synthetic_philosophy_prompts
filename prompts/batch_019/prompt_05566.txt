In Guide of the Perplexed III:32, Maimonides argues that the commandment to offer animal sacrifice is best understood not as something God desires in itself, but as a concession to the religious psychology of the generation that received the Torah. I am finishing grades for a take-home midterm in my graduate seminar on Maimonides and his medieval critics, and I would like an independent read on one student's response before I finalize the grade. Below is the question, worth 20 points, followed by the student's answer exactly as submitted.

Question (20 points).
(a) [8 pts] Reconstruct Maimonides' argument, including the role it assigns to prophetic critiques of sacrifice such as those in Jeremiah and 1 Samuel.
(b) [6 pts] Explain Nahmanides' central textual objection to this account, as given in his commentary on the opening chapter of Leviticus.
(c) [6 pts] Maimonides rules in the Mishneh Torah that sacrificial worship will resume once the Temple is rebuilt in messianic times. Does this create a difficulty for the account in (a), and if so, how might it be addressed?

Student answer:

(a) Maimonides' claim, laid out in Guide III:32, is that the sacrificial cult was never something God wanted for its own sake. The chapter's larger argument is that every chok — every statute lacking a self-evident rationale — ultimately reduces to a single strategy: weaning Israel off the star- and idol-worship of the surrounding nations, and sacrifice is just the most visible case of that strategy at work. Since a lawgiver cannot ask an entire people to abandon everything it is accustomed to all at once (nature does not change suddenly from one extreme to its opposite), the Torah let Israel keep the outward forms of worship it already knew — altars, offerings, a sanctuary — while rerouting the object of that worship away from the stars and toward God alone, forbidding images and centralizing the practice at one site under one priesthood. Maimonides finds confirmation of this in the prophets, who insist that obedience rather than sacrifice is what God is actually after: he invokes Samuel's rebuke of Saul and Jeremiah's statement that sacrifice was not what God commanded at the time of the exodus as evidence that the sacrifices were always a means and never the point.

(b) Nahmanides' objection is that this history does not fit the biblical timeline: Cain and Abel bring offerings, and Noah builds an altar after the flood, long before there is any idolatry on the scene for God to be weaning anyone off of. If sacrifice is already normal practice before there is a Sabian cult to react against, it cannot originally be a concession to that cult. Nahmanides takes this as evidence that sacrifice answers to a standing human need to draw near to God — he reads korban through the root meaning "to bring close" — rather than to a local and temporary problem.

(c) Maimonides' ruling that sacrifice resumes with the rebuilt Temple does not, in the end, create a real problem for the account in (a). In his legal writing, Maimonides treats the rebuilt Temple as a symbol of restored national sovereignty rather than as a literal institution, consistent with his general willingness to read messianic material non-literally. So the Guide's rationalist account and the Mishneh Torah's ruling are not really in tension.
