I've been down a rabbit hole again reading old MIRI stuff, specifically the Garrabrant et al. logical induction paper (the one with the traders betting on logical sentences, and the market prices eventually becoming calibrated and coherent even about stuff like open conjectures). Every time it comes up on the Alignment Forum or in LW comment threads, people talk about it like it basically dissolves the problem of logical uncertainty — like now we finally have a principled notion of "reasonable credence" in something like the Riemann Hypothesis or the parity of some huge unconfirmed number, even though those aren't random/unknown in the ordinary Bayesian sense, just facts nobody's computed yet.

But here's the thing that's been bugging me, and I brought it up at our local rationalist meetup and couldn't quite articulate it well enough. The actual guarantee logical induction gives you — that no efficiently computable trading strategy can make unbounded profit off the market, ever, as deduction proceeds — is a totally externalist, reliability-style property, kind of like the efficient market hypothesis for beliefs. It doesn't tell you anything about whether the credence output at some particular finite stage is *justified* for the reasoner holding it, only that the whole infinite sequence of markets can't be money-pumped in the limit. And honestly it seems even worse than classic reliabilism in epistemology, where at least the reliable process (like that clairvoyant guy from the thought experiments, I think it's a BonJour example but don't quote me on it) is doing its reliable thing right now, in the moment — whereas the logical inductor's "reliability" is a promise about a limit that literally no finite-stage reasoner, including the market itself on any given day, could ever confirm is being kept, since there's no bound anywhere on the rate of convergence.

So could you help me build this out into an actual argument? I want the position to be: logical induction, whatever its mathematical elegance, is best understood as a hardcore externalist reliabilism about mathematical/logical credence dressed up in market language, and it therefore inherits (and if anything sharpens) the standard objections to reliabilism about epistemic justification — so it hasn't really given us a theory of *rational* credence about logical facts, just of reliable-in-the-limit credence. I'd like the argument to actually engage with the mechanics of the trading framework (efficient computability of traders, the exploitability criterion, the asymptotic nature of the guarantee) rather than just gesturing at "markets are basically reliabilism" and leaving it there.
