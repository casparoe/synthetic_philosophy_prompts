Two students in my graduate seminar on Kantian ethics got into a real disagreement during the break over a case one of them raised about a mutual acquaintance, Renata. Renata bought a used filing cabinet online, found $3,000 taped inside a drawer, tracked down the previous owner through the seller, and mailed the cash back overnight at her own expense. Asked why, she said she never thought in terms of "obligation" — she just couldn't stop picturing the owner's face if that money was rent or medical savings.

(1) Owen said this is a genuine case of moral worth, not merely a right action: Renata was moved by exactly the fact that made returning the money right — the owner's impending loss — even without ever framing it to herself as a duty.

(2) Ines disagreed: whatever credit Renata deserves, it falls short of moral worth, because the feeling that moved her was contingent and could easily have cut the other way. That, she said, is luck, not virtue.

(3) She pressed the point with a case of her own: a landlord she knows always returns deposits promptly because tenants who feel respected don't trash the place on their way out — a motive that also tracks what makes returning deposits right, yet earns him no moral credit at all.

(4) Owen replied that the cases aren't parallel: the landlord's motive (future tenant behavior) is a different consideration from what makes the act right (that it's the tenant's money) — they merely correlate. For Renata, the very fact that made the act right was also, one and the same, what moved her. He added that "it could have gone differently" is true of almost anyone's sense of duty before it's actually tested, so that alone can't disqualify her either.

Neither budged. Please go through the four moves individually and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than jumping to an overall winner, and note anything important you think both of them are overlooking.
