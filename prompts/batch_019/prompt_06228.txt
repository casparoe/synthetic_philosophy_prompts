I host a small biweekly podcast on philosophy of science for a general but philosophically literate audience, and I've just confirmed a guest — a philosopher of biology who has spent her career defending a Cummins-style causal-role account of biological function against etiological, selected-effects theories in the tradition of Wright, Neander, and Millikan. The debate has stopped being purely academic for me: for the past few months I've been the philosophy consultant on a documentary about de novo synthetic organisms, and the production team kept getting stuck on a question none of us could resolve cleanly. If a lab writes a gene from scratch, with no selection history behind it whatsoever, can the protein it codes for be said to have a "function" at all, on a view where a function just is a selected effect? The etiological answer seemed to be no, which struck the whole crew as perverse, but when I tried out the causal-role alternative instead, I ran into the mirror-image worry: if a trait's function is just whatever effect of it figures in some describable capacity of some containing system, what principled thing stops the heart's function from including the thumping sound a stethoscope picks up, given that producing that sound is just as real a causal contribution to the doctor-patient diagnostic encounter as pumping blood is to the circulatory system?

Since my guest has built her reputation on the causal-role side of this fight, I don't want the episode to consist of her restating Cummins against a straw opponent. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me? I'd like at least one question that presses her on that liberality worry directly, on how her view rules out cases like the stethoscope one without quietly smuggling in our own explanatory interests as the thing doing the real discriminating work; at least one that asks her to explain what, on a purely systemic account, it could even mean for a heart to "malfunction" in the ordinary medical sense, given that a component's current causal role doesn't seem to fix, by itself, what that component is supposed to be doing; and at least one that turns her view's biggest selling point back on her — since getting rid of any historical requirement is exactly what lets her handle novel, unselected traits so easily, I want her to say whether that isn't the very same move that generates the liberality problem, just wearing a different hat. For each of these, please add a short, specific follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, rather than a generic "how would you respond to critics of your view" prompt. Close with one question that brings the whole thing back to my own case: what her account actually implies about whether a freshly synthesized, never-selected gene has a function the moment it starts doing something in the cell, and whether she thinks that's a virtue of her view or a bullet she has to bite.
