A friend of mine who is several years into her own pursuit of FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) and I spent most of last night arguing about whether that movement fulfills or betrays Keynes's vision in "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren." She reads FIRE as his prophecy arriving on schedule: capital and technology finally letting ordinary people escape what he called the economic problem. I argued that most FIRE adherents, once they hit their number, don't actually stop — they recalculate for a bigger cushion, or pivot straight into monetizing their own frugality online, which sounds much closer to the "purposive" character type he worried the transition would expose: someone constitutionally unable to enjoy anything except the pursuit of more security. Neither of us budged. So, setting his economics aside and drawing on the ethical outlook he actually held — formed under G. E. Moore's influence in the Bloomsbury circle and revisited later in "My Early Beliefs," which prized love, beauty, and states of mind enjoyed for their own sake in the present — how do you think Keynes would judge the FIRE movement: as the first real test of what he called the permanent problem, or as something his own optimism failed to anticipate?
