I've become fairly interested in philosophy of mind over the past year, mostly through a couple of podcasts I listen to on my commute, since I have no formal background in any of this. One idea that has stuck with me is 'cognitive penetration' -- the question of whether what you believe or expect can change what you actually perceive, rather than just changing how you interpret or react to an experience that would otherwise be identical. I'm bringing this up because a real disagreement about exactly this broke out in my birdwatching group a few weeks ago, first on a Saturday walk and then for days afterward in our group chat, and I still can't work out who came out ahead.

It started when Alina, who had spent nearly a year training herself to tell a pine warbler's musical trill apart from the very similar trill of a chipping sparrow, said that once she'd learned the difference, the warbler's call didn't just become easier to identify -- it started to sound different to her, clearer and more separated from the background noise, 'like my ears changed.' She compared it to a hidden shape suddenly popping out of a magic-eye picture once you know how to look: not a change in interpretation, she said, but a change in the raw experience itself. Dev objected that this doesn't show what she thinks it shows: what she's describing is ordinary perceptual learning, the slow recalibration of a trained ear over a year of practice, which nobody disputes happens; the genuinely contested claim is whether a belief you hold right now, with no new training involved, can reach back and change what you experience in that moment, and her case is silent on that question. Alina replied that Dev's line between 'trained-in' change and 'occurrent' change is blurrier than he was letting on, since what makes her hear the call correctly on any given walk is precisely that she now knows, right then, what to listen for -- so something occurrent is doing the immediate work, however long it took to install it -- and that a move which labels any change 'learning' when it follows practice and 'attention' when it happens in the moment looks designed so that nothing could ever count as genuine penetration. Dev granted that individual cases can be blurry without agreeing that this empties the distinction, and brought up something he'd read about how a genuinely gray photograph of a banana looks faintly yellow to people just because they know bananas are yellow -- a case that's hard to blame on attention, since there's nothing else in the frame to look at instead, and no training taking place during the test itself. Alina's closing point was that resting a claim about perception in general on a couple of lab paradigms involving banana photos is a strangely narrow foundation, and that those cases might just be the easiest to isolate rather than the only genuine ones -- so maybe the testimony of people who have actually built real expertise deserves more weight than Dev was giving it.

Neither of them budged, and both seemed right about something at each stage, so I would like an outside opinion. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- Alina's original claim, Dev's perceptual-learning objection, Alina's blurred-line reply, Dev's banana-photo counterexample, and Alina's closing objection about narrow evidence -- and say who has the stronger argument at each step, not just who comes out ahead overall? I would also like to know if there's something both of them are missing -- for instance, whether there's actually a principled line to be drawn between mere attention or perceptual learning and 'real' cognitive penetration, or whether that whole distinction is shakier than either of them wants to admit.
