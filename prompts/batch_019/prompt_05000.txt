I'm a novelist, and two of my closest friends, Talia and Marco, are getting married next month. They're also two of the most insufferable Borges people I know — they actually met in a graduate seminar arguing about "A New Refutation of Time," and I've been asked to give the toast. Here's what makes them perfect for each other and also makes this speech hard to write: they've never actually resolved the argument they started with. Marco thinks Borges's case against time — deny a persisting self the way Hume does, deny matter the way Berkeley does, and there's no subject left for time to happen to — is a thesis you're supposed to genuinely believe, and that the famous ending, where Borges admits he's still a river and a tiger and stuck living in the world anyway, is just him loosing his nerve on the last page. Talia thinks the concession IS the essay, that the whole point is you can murder time on paper and still not manage to climb out of it, and that this is the honest, actually moving part. They also still fight about whether any of that squares with "The Garden of Forking Paths," where time isn't one thing being denied so much as an infinite branching mess — how do you prove there's no river in one piece and then spend a story mapping all its tributaries in another.

I don't want a toast that just mentions this fight as a cute fact about them, I want one that's built out of it the way a scene is built out of its subject rather than decorated with it — something that takes the disagreement seriously (a wedding vow is basically a bet placed on a future time neither of them can prove is real) and comes down gently on Talia's side by the end, since I think the wedding itself is a pretty good demonstration of her reading. The complication is the room: half of it is their department, who will clock exactly which essay and which lines I'm circling without me having to name them, and the other half is my aunt and Marco's five groomsmen from his Sunday soccer league, who have never heard of Borges and shouldnt have to sit through five minutes of literary theory to feel like they're at the same wedding. Can you write me something like a four-minute toast that pulls this off — it needs to read as a love letter to both of them, not a seminar, and somewhere in it I want one line that's unmistakably, specifically a joke for the Borges contingent.
