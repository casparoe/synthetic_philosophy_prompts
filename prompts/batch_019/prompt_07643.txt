I'm about a third of the way into a novel with a subplot about a seventeen-year-old, Mara, who spends a summer sliding deeper into ADHD-and-autism TikTok, self-diagnosing with one thing and then another, mostly by recognizing herself in creators' descriptions of their own symptoms. I really don't want this to read like a cheap "kids these days, self-diagnosing off the internet" joke, so I've been re-reading my way through Ian Hacking on looping effects to try to get the arc intellectually honest — Making Up People, the essay where he sets up interactive kinds vs. indifferent kinds, the multiple personality material in Rewriting the Soul, the fugue stuff in Mad Travelers.

Here's what I keep going back and forth on with a friend of mine who did a philosophy postdoc. Her instinct is that what happens to Mara ISN'T real Hacking-style looping, because the loop he actually cares about isn't just "label changes the person" — its specifically a two-way street where the classification itself gets revised by researchers and clinicians in response to how classified people start behaving and describing themselves, and nothing like that is going on when a few thousand teenagers watch the same handful of creators and start reaching for the same vocabulary. On her reading it's closer to what he calls something like semantic contagion, just dressed up to look like the real mechanism because it borrows official-sounding diagnostic language.

I'm less sure, mostly because I know his later stuff on autism got interested in cases where the "experts" doing the revising weren't credentialed researchers at all but the classified people themselves, writing and talking publicly about their own experience — which sounds uncomfortably close to what's happening on TikTok, just faster and less curated. Could you argue as hard as you can for my friend's side of this: that platform-driven self-diagnosis doesn't count as genuine looping, even granting the autism precedent, because something else essential to the mechanism is still missing? I'd like it to actually engage with the mechanics of Hacking's account rather than just asserting that internet self-diagnosis is fake and clinical diagnosis is real — that second move is the one I'm trying to avoid accidentally writing my way into.
