Quick one from a rabbit hole I fell into while reporting a piece on personalized medicine (not even the thing the story's about, just a side detail that's been bugging me since). My mother-in-law just recieved results from one of those genetic risk panels for breast cancer, and it came back with a 10-year risk of 14%, calculated by matching her markers and family history against a big database of similar women. Then at her actual oncology appointment, the doctor pulled up a different, older calculator, one that's mostly built off age and reproductive history, and that one spat out 22% for the exact same 10-year window. Both tools are apparently well-validated and peer reviewed, and neither doctor thinks the other is doing junk science - they're just drawing the comparison group differently.

Now she has an actual decision on her hands, whether the side effects from a preventive medication are worth it, and she asked me, since I'm supposedly "the science person" in the family, which number she should go with. I mumbled something about "just ask your doctor," which felt like a total dodge coming from me. So: is there some principled way to figure out which percentage is more true for her, specifically? Or is "what's the real probability that this one particular person gets the disease" just not a well-formed question to begin with? Would love your take - this has been derailing my dinner conversations for like a week now.
