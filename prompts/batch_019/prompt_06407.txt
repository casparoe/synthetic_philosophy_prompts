I'm a philosophy professor on my university's Faculty Senate Rules Committee, and as its one member with any background in social choice theory, I've been asked to draft our recommendation. Last spring's Senate chair election, held under plurality rules, was won by a candidate whom a majority of senators had ranked last, after three candidates split the vote. The committee is now split three ways: one member wants ranked-choice voting, another wants approval voting, and a third, an economist by training, thinks adopting either would be largely theater: the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem guarantees that with three or more candidates, every voting rule is either a dictatorship or manipulable by some voter in some situation, so no rule can make sincerity a senator's dominant strategy. A fourth member points out that our other pending item, a referendum on next year's budget increase (0%, 3%, or 6%), is single-dimensional rather than a contest among unrelated options, and wonders whether that changes things. Please draft the short memo we owe the full Senate next week: it should recommend a specific procedure for each vote, take the manipulability worry seriously rather than dismissing it, and be honest about what, if anything, reform actually buys us.
