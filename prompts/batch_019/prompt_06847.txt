I spent my whole career as a field geologist, lots of time mapping remote backcountry, and only really got into reading philosophy after I retired a few years ago -- mostly stuff on rationality and decision-making, since it scratches an itch a lot like research used to. Last month a few of us old fieldwork buddies had dinner, and my friend Hal told a story from back in the early 80s: he was out doing solo sampling in some genuinely remote country when his truck's axle gave out, hours from anywhere, long before cell phones existed. A rancher happened along eventually and agreed to drive him all the way into the nearest town, a huge detour that cost the guy the rest of his day and a tank of gas, on nothing but Hal's word that he'd pay him back once they reached a bank, since Hal had no cash on him out in the field. They got to town, Hal withdrew the money and handed it over in full, and that was that. The rancher never even got his last name, and Hal's crew pulled out of the area within the week, so there was never any real prospect of the guy tracking him down.

Our friend Meg, who's been on her own philosophy kick lately too, said that once Hal was standing at that bank counting out the cash, actually handing it over didn't do him one bit of good: the rancher had no way to chase him down and they'd never cross paths again, so by any ordinary standard of self-interested rationality, paying was a mistake, even if it was a decent thing to do. Hal shot back that if he'd secretly been planning to stiff the guy, the rancher would've clocked it back at the truck and left him there; the only reason he got the ride at all is that he genuinely meant to pay, and meaning it isn't something you can just switch off once you're safe. Meg said fine, that explains why it was smart for him to want to be, and come across as, the paying kind of guy, but that's a different question from whether the actual handover, on its own, at that later moment, was the rational move -- nothing in physics or logic stopped him from being perfectly convincing back at the truck and then quietly pocketing the cash once in town. Hal answered that 'convincing but secretly planning to defect' isn't really an option on the table for an ordinary person: the rancher had hours to size him up, and most people can't fake that kind of sincerity to order. So the real choice, way back at the truck, was between actually being the paying kind and getting rescued, or being the bailing kind and getting left in the heat, and once he was committed to the first path, calling the last step of it 'irrational' is like calling the final stride before the finish line pointless just because the race is already basically won by then. Meg granted people can't easily fake sincerity, but said that just relocates the puzzle rather than solving it: fine, his character did the work rather than some choice made at the bank, but that's not the same as the payment itself being the rational thing to do right then, its just him getting lucky that his character was already fixed the useful way beforehand. Hals comeback was that praising a policy of always paying up while calling every single payment it produces a mistake seems like an odd position to hold: a policy just is its instances, there's no version of 'always pay' floating around apart from the individual payments that make it up.

They went back and forth like that for a good twenty minutes and never really landed anywhere, and honestly I couldn't tell you who came out ahead. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step? I'd also love to know if either of them is overlooking something that would actually settle it, and where you personally come down on whether Hal handing over that cash was genuinely the rational thing to do, or just felt that way.
