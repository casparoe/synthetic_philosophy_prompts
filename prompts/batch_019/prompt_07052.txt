I spent the better part of four decades as a behavioral neuroscientist, much of it studying how rodents and small mammals reorient within milliseconds after a sudden disturbance in their environment, and I have only taken up philosophy seriously since retiring. A reading group I joined last year worked through several of Daniel Dennett's essays, which is how I first encountered the term "the frame problem." Since then I have noticed that the phrase seems to cover two rather different things depending on the source. In some treatments it is introduced narrowly, as the difficulty John McCarthy and Patrick Hayes identified for formal planning systems: how to specify, without writing an explicit axiom for every fact an action fails to affect, which parts of a described world remain unchanged once an action is performed. In Dennett's own retelling, in his essay "Cognitive Wheels," with its robot that either detonates itself or sits frozen deducing the endless irrelevant consequences of pulling a wagon out of a room, the very same name is attached to a much broader epistemological worry about how any finite reasoner is supposed to bound the space of things worth considering before it acts at all.

I would like a careful explanation of how these two versions of the frame problem relate to one another, since the popular accounts I have come across tend to slide between them without comment. Is the epistemological worry simply a looser restatement of the logical one, or a genuinely separate claim that happens to share a name and an origin story? Do the technical devices developed within AI and formal logic to handle the narrow version -- circumscription, Reiter's successor-state axioms, and the add/delete lists of STRIPS-style planners -- actually bear on Dennett's broader worry, or do they dispose of the narrow representational problem while leaving the deeper question of relevance exactly where it was? And given that the animals I spent my career watching solve relevance problems of just this kind, at speed and with no apparent checking of irrelevant facts, I would be especially interested in your view on whether that everyday biological fact counts as evidence that minds cannot possibly be working the way a symbolic planner does, or whether it is perfectly compatible with the frame problem after all, merely solved by different means.
