I'm taking a seminar on practical rationality this term, and we've just spent two sessions on Parfit's Hitchhiker and what Parfit calls "rational irrationality." For this week's response piece I'd rather dramatize the disagreement than summarize it, so instead of an explainer I'd like a dialogue.

Setting: two strangers, both dying of thirst, were picked up by the same driver after each sincerely promised to pay him once they reached town (he won't take anyone he doesn't believe). The truck is now a few minutes from the town limits, and one of the two has started, out loud, to reconsider. Write the exchange this provokes. The sophisticated chooser, in terms our seminar has taken from McClennen, holds that rationality must be assessed choice by choice, in light of whatever reasons exist right now, and has concluded that since paying will buy nothing once safety is already secured, it is no longer worth doing. The resolute chooser holds instead that what matters is the policy an agent commits to, not the isolated act considered after the fact, and that reneging now, however locally sensible it feels, is the real lapse of rationality, not its expression.

Have them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another. I want the resolute chooser to press the charge that a theory of rationality which predictably strands its own adherents is in trouble on its own terms -- isn't this just Parfit's "rational irrationality," where it is rational to be disposed to do the locally "irrational" thing, since having exactly that disposition is what got them both the ride in the first place? And I want the sophisticated chooser to press back that "resolute choice" is smuggling in a constraint that no genuinely self-interested theory has room for once the relevant reasons have run out. Neither should fold more easily than a real philosopher would; a standoff is a fine place for the dialogue to end.
