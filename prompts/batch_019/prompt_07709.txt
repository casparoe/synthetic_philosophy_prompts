Spent about 35 years as a comparative physiologist before retiring a couple years back, and Ive been filling the time reading philosophy of biology, of all things. Fell into this old argument between what people call the "etiological" account of biological function (goes back to a Larry Wright paper from the 70s, tightened up later by Neander and Millikan) and Cummins's "causal role" account. Wrote up some rough notes for what I'm hoping turns into a short essay eventually, trying to work out the difference for myself using a case from my own corner of biology. Would love your comments -- mainly whether I'm being fair to both camps, and whether my test case is actually a real problem for one side or if I'm missing some standard fix that's already out there.

Etiological, roughly: a trait's function is whatever effect explains why that trait is still around, i.e. what it was selected for. Nice feature: malfunction comes for free. A heart that isn't pumping blood is malfunctioning because pumping blood is what hearts were selected to do, regardless of what the heart happens to be up to right now.

Cummins, roughly: forget the history. A function is just whatever causal contribution a part makes to some capacity of the whole system we happen to be interested in explaining. The appeal is you don't need to know, or guess at, a trait's evolutionary past to say what it currently does.

Here's the test case I kept coming back to. The malleus and incus, the tiny bones in the mammalian middle ear, are repurposed reptilian jaw-joint bones (the articular and quadrate) -- one of the best-documented transitions in the fossil record. Somewhere back in the synapsid lineage, a bone being selected for "lets the jaw hinge" gradually turned into a bone being selected for "transmits vibration to the inner ear." So, question for the etiological side: exactly when does the real function flip from hinging to hearing? It can't be sudden, selection doesn't work that way, so theres got to be a long stretch where the bone is being maintained, to varying degrees, by selection for both effects at once. That seems to make "the" function genuinely fuzzy during the transition, not just fuzzy to us because the data's incomplete. The causal-role view seems fine with this bit, you just ask what the bone's contribution to hearing is at a given moment and get a reasonably determinate answer without touching history at all -- but then it can't say the bone was ever doing something wrong along the way, which feels like giving up too much.

So: am I even being fair to Cummins, is there some standard way for causal-role people to recover a notion of malfunction that I'm not seeing? Does the jaw-bone case actually bite for the etiological side, or is there a fix (I have a vague memory someone, maybe Godfrey-Smith, restricted things to "recent" selection history) that handles it cleanly? And honestly, feel free to tell me this whole example is a well-worn one that everybody in the field already argues about -- my reading here has been pretty scattershot.
