I spent my career doing field ecology and evolutionary biology, and now that I'm retired I've finally got time to read all the philosophy of biology stuff I never got round to (Okasha's book on levels of selection has been keeping me up at night, in a good way). It dragged up a memory of a fight that broke out in the literature around 2010, when Nowak, Tarnita and E.O. Wilson published that Nature paper arguing inclusive fitness theory / kin selection isn't actually needed to explain eusociality, and that plain old natural selection acting on group-structured populations does the job just as well. I remember at the time it felt like half of evolutionary biology signed onto a furious rebuttal defending Hamilton's rule and inclusive fitness as indispensable, but back then I was busy with my own research and never really dug into whether the two camps were disagreeing about actual biology or just about how you like to do your bookkeeping.

So here's what I'd like: write me a dialogue between two evolutionary biologists (philosophers of biology is fine too) who are equally sharp and know the technical literature cold -- one who thinks inclusive fitness / kin selection is the deepest and most necessary concept for understanding social evolution, and one who's sympathetic to the "standard natural selection is more general, relatedness is just along for the ride" line. I don't want the cartoon version of genes vs groups -- get them into the actual weeds of that dispute: whether Hamilton's rule only holds up under additivity and weak selection assumptions, whether that's a fatal limitation or a fixable one, and whether the haplodiploidy story about relatedness asymmetries is doing real explanatory work or is beside the point. Above all I want them to honestly wrestle with the meta-question of wether they disagree about the biology at all, or whether inclusive-fitness accounting and standard-natural-selection accounting are just two equivalent ways of carving up the same causal process -- in which case the whole fight was a lot of heat over what amounts to a translation dispute.

It doesn't need to land on some tidy resolution -- me and an old colleague certainly never managed that when we used to argue about it over beers. I'd rather see them actually locate where the real disagreement is, if there even is one, than watch one side just concede. Make sure they respond to each others specific points as the exchange goes along, rather than each just restating their own side back and forth.
