I'm about two-thirds through a novel where one of my leads is an aging philosophy professor who built her whole career on defending Rorty, and now she has to referee a fight between her grad students over whether the model they've spent all semester fine-tuning for the lab actually understands anything or is just very good at faking it. Writing her half of that argument sent me down a rereading spiral of Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature, and now I'm honestly more interested in the actual question than the scene it was supposed to be background for. My editor thinks this whole subplot is too far in the weeds for a novel and wants it cut to two lines, but I want to get it right for myself first even if 95% of it never makes the page.

Rorty died in 2007, well before transformer language models were something anyone outside a few labs was arguing about, so he obviously never weighed in directly. But his whole antirepresentationalist project was built around denying that "getting reality right" is even a coherent thing for a vocabulary to do -- he wanted to junk the mirror metaphor rather than polish it, and treated truth as basically an honorific we hand out to beliefs that keep working for us rather than a matter of correspondence to the world. So here's what I actually want from you: using his specific commitments -- the rejection of the scheme/content split, what he owes to Sellars on the Myth of the Given and to Davidson on conceptual schemes, his sympathy for something like Dennett's instrumentalism about the mental over Searle's insistence on "intrinsic" intentionality -- tell me what you think he'd actually say about the current fight over whether LLMs understand language. I'm especially curious what he'd do with Bender and Koller's octopus thought experiment (the one where a hyper-intelligent octopus taps a cable connecting two castaways on separate islands and learns to fake conversation through pure statistical pattern-matching, then gets caught out when one of them needs real advice on fighting off a bear with whatever's lying around). Would he think that thought experiment is doing real philosophical work, or would he say its just his old enemy, the picture of meaning as mirroring, sneaking back in through a side door -- and would he rather we dropped the whole "do they really understand" vocabulary and asked a more useful question instead?

Don't give me a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" answer -- the guy wasn't shy about picking fights, so pick a side for him and commit to it. A page or so is plenty, and feel free to drag in Brandom, Putnam, or whoever else you think he'd be leaning on or arguing with along the way.
