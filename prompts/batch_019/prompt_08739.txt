So this is a bit of a random one, but I somehow ended up on my county's oversight commitee for the pretrial risk-assessment tool (long story — my day job touches the county's data infrastructure and they wanted a "numbers person" who wasn't from the vendor). We've got an actual vote in three weeks, and I keep finding myself framing the whole disagreement as the reference class problem, which I first ran into via some Hájek paper years ago (something like "the reference class problem is your problem too"?) and then kept seeing diffrent versions of in the anthropics threads on LW/ACX.

Here's the situation. Our current tool sorts every defendant into one of nine risk tiers using a handful of broad factors (age, current charge, prior record). The vendor wants us to switch to a new model that clusters people into much narrower groups using something like 40 features, and outputs a specific-looking number, e.g. "17.4% chance of new arrest within 24 months," instead of just "moderate risk." Our committee's statistician is against adopting it as-is — her argument is that for the exact same defendant, you could pick several different, equally defensible feature sets and training subsamples and get genuinely different percentages every time, and none of those numbers is privileged as THE probability for that particular person. It's the reference class problem wearing a lab coat. But one of the judges on the committee, who I think has a real point even though my gut sides with the statistician, says the new model is measurably better-calibrated on holdout data than our current nine tiers, that's a real fact regardless of what you think about single-case probability, and that retreating to vaguer categories doesn't make the indeterminacy go away, it just launders the same reference-class-dependence through fuzzier language that happens to sound more humble.

Could you draft the memo I'm going to circulate to the committee before the vote? I need it to land on an actual recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific conditions — with a concrete timeline, not a "more research is needed" shrug, and it has to genuinely engage the judge's calibration argument rather than just re-asserting that reference classes are arbitrary, because he's going to make exactly that argument in the room and "well, all single-case probability talk has this problem" isn't going to fly as a rebuttal on its own.
