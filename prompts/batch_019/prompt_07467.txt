A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing over a single pair of sentences: "If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did," and "If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have." I take it as obvious that the first is true and the second false, even though the two share an antecedent and a consequent and differ only in grammatical mood. My friend, who thinks logic ought to be blind to grammar, insists that any adequate semantics for conditionals must predict this divergence from a single unified mechanism, and that if it cannot, the semantics is what has gone wrong, not her expectation of unity. Write an essay that actually settles this. Weigh Stalnaker's attempt to treat indicative and counterfactual conditionals as the same kind of thing, differing only in which worlds a selection function is permitted to reach, against the more standard view that indicatives are governed by something like Adams' thesis while counterfactuals receive an ordinary truth-conditional similarity semantics. Take seriously both the triviality results that make truth conditions costly for indicatives and the difficulty non-truth-conditional accounts have explaining conditionals embedded under negation or nested inside further conditionals, and end with an actual verdict on whether unification is achievable — not a survey of the positions.
