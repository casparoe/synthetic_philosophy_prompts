Ive been down a rabbit hole on "money pump" arguments this week — started with a rationalist blog post, then I just kept googling until I ended up skimming some old decision theory papers. I think I get the basic idea: if you prefer A over B, B over C, but then loop around and prefer C over A, someone can trade you around that circle over and over, taking a small cut each time, until you're broke and holding the exact same thing you started with. That part makes sense to me as a knock against having circular preferences.

What's actually bugging me is the "diachronic rationality" angle, where its not just about your preferences at a single moment but about being consistent over time. It seems to me like this proves something way bigger than "don't have circular preferences" — it seems to prove that changing your mind about anything, ever, is a kind of irrationality, since a clever enough trading partner could in principle always design a sequence of offers to profit off any shift in what you want, loop or no loop. Like, I sold most of my vinyl records two years ago because I was sure I was done with physical media, and now I've bought some of them back at worse prices because I missed having them. Was that me getting money-pumped, just because a change in my preferences let someone extract value from me over time? That reasoning seems to lead to the conclusion that updating your preferences on new information or just genuinely growing as a person is irrational, which feels obviously wrong, so where's the actual mistake here — is there a principled line between the "bad, exploitable" kind of changing your mind and the normal kind, or is it really just a difference of degree and I'm reading too much into what's basically a metaphor?
