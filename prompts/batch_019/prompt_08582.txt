Over a forty-year career as an experimental physicist working mainly on low-temperature calibration standards, I grew accustomed to worrying about exactly what a measurement scale is entitled to assume: whether it has a non-arbitrary zero, whether its unit is conventional, and whether two instruments' readings can be compared at all without some further convention bridging them. Since retiring I have been working through the welfare-economics and political-philosophy literature on utilitarianism, and I cannot shake the sense that the long-running dispute over interpersonal comparisons of utility is really the same measurement-theoretic problem wearing different clothes, even though almost nobody in that literature frames it that way explicitly. A friend from my old department who retrained in mathematical economics insists the dispute was settled decades ago and that I am overthinking it; having now read enough to have opinions of my own, I would like an outside judgment before deciding whether to take his word for it.

In my reading notes I keep returning to seven recurring approaches: the classical utilitarians' largely unexamined assumption that pleasure and pain are directly commensurable across persons (Bentham, Sidgwick); Robbins's ordinalist argument that such comparisons are not the business of a science and should simply be dropped; the Kaldor–Hicks compensation-test tradition, which evaluates policy changes through potential Pareto improvements precisely so as to avoid ever making the comparison; the "extended sympathy" idea Arrow raised in his later discussion of the problem, grounding comparisons in a person's preferences over what it would be like to occupy someone else's position; Harsanyi's utilitarian aggregation theorem, which derives a cardinal social welfare function from individual von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions plus a Pareto condition; Rawls's substitution of an index of primary social goods, together with the difference principle, for any direct comparison of utility; and Sen's capability approach, which relocates the comparison from subjective utility to capabilities and functionings. What I want to know is how well each of these actually solves, rather than merely relabels, evades, or quietly reintroduces by the back door, the problem of grounding a meaningful interpersonal comparison.

Could you sort these seven approaches into tiers "S" (a genuine, defensible solution to the grounding problem), "A", "B", "C", and "D" (fails to really engage with the problem, or engages with it incoherently), with every approach placed in exactly one tier and tiers free to be left empty?
