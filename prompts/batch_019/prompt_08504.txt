So I fell down a rabbit hole this week trying to settle an arguement from my local rationalist meetup (yes, we are that kind of group) about coherence theorems and whether they really force an agent into having a complete preference ordering. Everyone there already knows the standard Yudkowsky pitch from "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities" -- if you're not representable as an expected-utility maximizer, someone can construct a sequence of trades (a money pump) that leaves you strictly worse off than where you started, so any agent that doesn't want free money extracted from it ends up looking VNM-coherent. Rohin Shah's old rebuttal about this not entailing goal-directed behavior is the standard "yes, but" that usually gets attached to it, but that's not actually what's bugging me.

What I can't get straight is the narrower argument specifically for completeness, as opposed to transitivity (the transitivity pump I feel like I actually get). I recall reading -- possibly it was Elliot Thornley's stuff on the AI shutdown problem, possibly an older decision theory paper, my memory's fuzzy on the exact source -- that you can supposedly construct a money pump against an agent with merely incomplete preferences too, by exploiting the "gaps" where it has no preference at all between some options. But then there's a reply on behalf of incompleteness where the agent dodges the pump by committing in advance to a whole plan rather than choosing trade-by-trade, something like "resolute choice," so it's preferences stay incomplete and yet it never actually gets pumped.

Could you lay out that whole dialectic properly for me? Specifically: (1) how does the money-pump argument for completeness actually work, given that a "gap" between two options seems structurally different from a preference cycle, so I don't get why the same pump-style construction should apply, (2) what does "resolute choice" (or foresight, or whatever the correct term for it is) need to assume about the agent for the pump to actually get blocked, and (3) is there a good reason to think that assumption doesn't just quietly reintroduce a coherence requirement at the level of plans or policies -- which would mean the incompleteness side hasn't actually won anything? I keep flip-flopping on whether this is a genuine hole in the coherence-implies-EU-maximization story or just a definitional dodge, and I'd like to actually understand the mechanics instead of picking a side based on whichever blog post I read most recently.
