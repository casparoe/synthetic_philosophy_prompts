I'm taking a jurisprudence seminar this term, and we've just finished the unit on the Hart–Fuller exchange in the 1958 Harvard Law Review — the grudge informer case, Radbruch's formula, and so on. For my next response paper, I want to push the debate past the Nazi-era cases both men actually discussed and onto a scenario neither lived to weigh in on directly: the prosecutions of former East German border guards, after reunification, for shootings carried out under the GDR's Schießbefehl (shoot-to-kill order), where German courts explicitly invoked Radbruch's formula to ground the convictions.

Assume the guards' conduct was authorized by domestic GDR statutes and internal directives that were, by the regime's own legal criteria, validly enacted. Arguing strictly from the positions Hart and Fuller actually staked out in 1958, rather than from later commentary on this specific case, how would each analyze whether the guards could be legitimately convicted? Where exactly would their reasoning diverge, and is there some feature of this case that would genuinely strain a committed Hartian positivist, given Hart's own caveats about the limits of the separation thesis?
