A friend and I spent much of last evening arguing about the Meno problem — why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief — and ended up disagreeing about the actual text rather than the usual one-line summary of it. We had both been content to describe Plato's answer as an ancestor of the justified-true-belief analysis: true belief becomes knowledge once it is "tied down" by a reason. Checking this against the dialogue itself, though, a few lines after Socrates and Meno agree that a right opinion about the road to Larisa guides a traveler just as well as knowledge of it would, we got stuck on the following exchange (Meno 97e–98a, Jowett's translation) and ended up arguing about it instead:

> Men. I admit the cogency of your argument, and therefore, Socrates, I wonder that knowledge should be preferred to right opinion — or why they should ever differ.
> Soc. And shall I explain this wonder to you?
> Men. Do tell me.
> Soc. You would not wonder if you had ever observed the images of Daedalus; but perhaps you have not got them in your country?
> Men. What have they to do with the question?
> Soc. Because they require to be fastened in order to keep them, and if they are not fastened they will play truant and run away.
> Men. Well, what of that?
> Soc. I mean to say that they are not very valuable possessions if they are at liberty, for they will walk off like runaway slaves; but when fastened, they are of great value, for they are really beautiful works of art. Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause [αἰτίας λογισμῷ]; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more honourable and excellent than true opinion, because fastened by a chain.
> Men. What you are saying, Socrates, seems to be very like the truth.
> Soc. I too speak rather in ignorance; I only conjecture. And yet that knowledge differs from true opinion is no matter of conjecture with me.

My friend reads "the tie of the cause" as Plato's word for something close to justification in the modern sense — having reasons or evidence behind a belief. I doubt that fits the Larisa case, where the only stated difference between the knower and the mere right-opinion-haver is that one of them has actually made the journey before, which looks less like acquiring a justification than like acquiring a further true belief. What really stopped us both is the offhand identification of this "tethering" with recollection, given that recollection was introduced earlier in the dialogue as a specific, fairly exotic thesis about the soul's prenatal acquaintance with the Forms, defended through the slave boy's geometry lesson — not obviously the sort of thing that should also be doing the work whenever someone comes to know an ordinary empirical fact like a route to a town.

Could you help us actually work through this passage, rather than hand us the standard encyclopedia paragraph on "the Meno problem"? I would especially like to know: (1) what "the tie of the cause" secures that mere true opinion lacks, and whether "justification" is really the right modern analogue for it; and (2) whether Socrates's move from tethering to recollection is a claim the argument actually entitles him to, given how recollection is set up earlier in the dialogue — and what his very next remark, waving the whole thing off as "conjecture," does to that claim.
