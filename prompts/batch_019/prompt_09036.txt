Kind of a random thing to have stuck in my head today, but here goes. I just saw that Happy the elephant died -- she's the Bronx Zoo elephant who passed the mirror self-recognition test back in 2005, and who some animal rights nonprofit (Nonhuman Rights Project, I think?) spent years trying to get declared a legal person, before loosing at New York's highest court in 2022. Total coincidence, but my Kant seminar just covered his section on animals: his argument that we have no direct duties to animals because they aren't self-conscious -- can't represent an "I" to themselves like rational beings can -- so cruelty to them is only wrong for what it does to our own character, not because we're wronging the animal.

Except Happy actually demonstrated exactly the capacity, self-recognition, that's supposedly the whole reason animals get left out of Kant's "ends in themselves" club -- which he obviously never had to reckon with in real life. Would he have had to budge on the direct/indirect duties distinction if confronted with something like the mirror test, or is there a principled way for him to hold the line anyway? Or am I misreading what "self-consciousness" is even doing in his argument?
