I've recently been re-reading Kurt Gödel's formal ontological proof — the version he kept private during his lifetime and reportedly shared only with Dana Scott, which only circulated publicly after his death — after it came up in a comment thread on one of the rationalist-adjacent blogs I follow. What struck me is how closely its central move echoes an argument Leibniz once offered to patch what he saw as the central gap in Descartes's version of the argument. Descartes simply assumed that the concept of "a supremely perfect being" is coherent; Leibniz thought this needed to be proven, and tried to prove it by arguing that a perfection, being a simple, positive, unanalyzable quality, cannot conflict with any other perfection, since a contradiction can only be exhibited by resolving concepts into components and finding an affirmation contradicted by a negation — which is impossible for qualities that are already simple and purely positive. From this he concluded that all perfections must be jointly compatible ("compossible") in one subject, so the concept of a most perfect being is at least a possible one, and the rest of the Cartesian argument can go through (he reportedly even tried the argument out on Spinoza in person). Gödel's "positive properties," and his theorem that the positive properties are jointly consistent, look like exactly this Leibnizian move, redone with the resources of quantified modal logic, in a proof that is standardly carried out in the modal system S5.

What I'd like your help with is reconstructing how Leibniz himself might have reacted if he had somehow been handed Gödel's finished construction, axioms and all. Would he have recognized it as the rigorous completion of his own sketch, or would some of Gödel's specific choices have struck him as illegitimate additions — for instance, the axiom that necessary existence is itself a positive property, which is arguably doing much of the proof's real work? And, most importantly, what do you think he would have said about "modal collapse": the result, usually credited to Jordan Howard Sobel and patched in various ways afterward (for instance by C. Anthony Anderson), showing that Gödel's axioms entail that every truth is a necessary truth, so that there is no genuine contingency anywhere. This seems especially worth asking of Leibniz, since he is independently notorious for flirting with a structurally similar necessitarianism via the principle of sufficient reason and the doctrine that God cannot but choose the best of all possible worlds — a charge he tried to fend off with his theory that contingent truths, unlike necessary ones, would require an infinite analysis to demonstrate. Would he have read modal collapse as an unwelcome vindication of that old worry about his own system, or as a narrow, technical defect in Gödel's specific axioms that any good Leibnizian should simply patch, the way Anderson tried to?
