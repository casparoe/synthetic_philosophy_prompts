So a while back I went down this really specific internet rabbit hole that started from a dumb question: why does the sentence "the cat is on the mat" actually say something, but if you just handed someone the raw ingredients -- a cat, an on-ness relation, and a mat -- sitting in a pile, that doesn't say anything at all? Something has to glue those pieces into an actual claim that can be true or false, and I couldn't figure out what that "something" was. Eventually that led me to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (I know, not exactly light reading for someone with zero philosophy background) and I found out theres a real, named problem here -- the "unity of the proposition" -- and there's this whole argument by a 19th century guy named F.H. Bradley, now just called Bradley's regress, that's supposed to show relations themselves can't be doing the gluing. The idea, as best I understand it, is that if a relation R is what connects a and b, then R's connecting-to-a-and-b looks like it needs its own further relation to tie R to a and b, and that new relation needs another one, and so on forever.

What I can't get a straight answer on anywhere is whether this regress is actually supposed to be fatal (people call this a "vicious" regress) or just a weird but harmless side effect of how relations work (a "benign" regress that doesn't actually blow anything up). And the fixes I've read about -- Frege's idea that concepts are "unsaturated" so no extra relation is needed, Russell's later move to make judgment a multi-way relation between a mind and all the bits at once instead of a relation to one unified proposition, or just positing some special "non-relational tie" that holds everything together -- honestly all kind of smell to me like they're just renaming the problem instead of solving it, but I genuinely can't tell if that's a fair complaint or if I'm missing why they actually work.

Could you write me an actual essay that settles this instead of just listing "here's view A, here's view B"? Lay out the strongest case that Bradley's regress is vicious and really does sink the idea that facts and propositions are unified by relations, then the strongest case that it's benign, weigh them against each other including whether the Frege/Russell/non-relational-tie style responses genuinely escape the regress or just relabel it, and land on an actual verdict by the end -- not a "reasonable people disagree" shrug.
