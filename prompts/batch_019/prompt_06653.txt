I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental condensed-matter physics before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen into reading philosophy and old essays I never got round to as a working scientist. Last week I finally read Einstein's 1949 piece "Why Socialism?" and honestly it surprised me – I'd always filed him under "pacifist who signed some letters," but the essay is a real, structured argument, not just a mood. He's making a specific case that private capital, left to competition, tends to concentrate into what he actually calls an "oligarchy of private capital," one powerful enough to control legislatures, the press and education regardless of how democratic the formal voting system looks on paper, and separately that unplanned, anarchic production keeps a standing pool of unemployed workers around that functions almost like a disciplinary tool, keeping everyone else's wages down. What struck me most is that he's no naive cheerleader for central planning either – he explicitly worries a planned economy could just swap capitalist domination for bureaucratic domination, and admits he has no clean answer for how you'd prevent that.

Anyway, I brought this up over coffee with an old collegue from my department (a chemist, not a political theorist, but we like arguing about this stuff) and we got stuck on something: what do you think Einstein would say about companies like Google, Amazon or Meta, which sit on both concentrated capital in his sense and a kind of automated, data-driven power he had no category for at all? Would he read that as his "oligarchy of private capital" argument basically confirmed, just running on data instead of factories? Or would the twentieth century track record of actual planned economies have pushed him to keep his diagnosis of capitalism but drop his own proposed cure? I'd also like your honest take on whether his whole framework, built around industrial-age nouns like "labour," "capital" and "production," even has the conceptual resources to say anything non-superficial about an economy running on attention and automation, or whether that needs more than just swapping in new examples. Please actually engage with the structure of his argument rather then just telling me he was a humanist so he'd obviously have hated big tech – that part I could've guessed myself.
