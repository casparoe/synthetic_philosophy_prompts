I spent most of my working life as a condensed-matter physicist, preoccupied with the sometimes uneasy fit between the continuous mathematics we use to describe matter and the discrete, granular reality it is meant to capture. Since retiring three years ago I have been auditing a philosophy course at the local university, and a recent session on supertasks left me more unsettled than I expected. We discussed Thomson's lamp: a lamp switched on at t = 0, off at t = 1/2, on again at t = 3/4, off at t = 7/8, and so on, toggled infinitely many times as the intervals halve on the way to t = 1 — at which point we are asked whether the lamp is on or off, a question Thomson himself thought had no coherent answer, and took as evidence that such a task could never really be completed.

What unsettled me rather more was a spatial cousin of this puzzle that came up afterward, usually credited to José Benardete and sometimes called the paradox of the gods (a temporal relative of it circulates too, under the name the Grim Reaper paradox). Imagine infinitely many gods standing between a man and a wall, at distances 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 of a mile from the wall, and so on without end, each resolved to throw up a barrier at their own position the instant the man would otherwise reach it — but only if no nearer god has already done so. Because the gods' positions crowd infinitely close to the man's starting point, with no nearest one among them, he cannot take even a first step: any distance he might travel, however small, is preceded by some god's trigger point. And yet no single god ever acts, since each is forever pre-empted by one still nearer. The man is frozen by a committee that never actually convenes.

I would like you to write me a short story of around 1000 to 1500 words that dramatizes this family of puzzles rather than explaining them. Please do not write a dialogue-heavy lecture in fictional dress; I would rather feel the vertigo of the thing than have it summarized for me. You are welcome to invent your own setting and characters — it need not literally involve lamps, gods, or reapers — but I would like the underlying structure to stay intact: a sequence of discrete events, or potential events, packed infinitely densely against some finite limit, so that the state at that limit is never fixed by any single event before it. If the story can end without resolving the paradox — the way, as far as I can tell, the philosophical literature itself has not resolved it — I would count that a success rather than a flaw.
