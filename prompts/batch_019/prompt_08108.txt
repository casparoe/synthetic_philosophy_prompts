I'm a novelist currently stalled on the fourth draft of a book, and in lieu of actual revision I have spent the past week rereading Tolstoy's late writings on aesthetics — partly for material, partly as a form of procrastination that has since curdled into a genuine crisis about the manuscript itself. My writers' group has had to hear me work through this out loud for three sessions running, so before I turn it into the short craft essay I keep threatening to write, I would rather get a stranger's read on it. Below are the notes as I currently have them; I would like comments on two things in particular — where I have gotten Tolstoy wrong or flattened him unfairly, and whether the argument I build in the final point is doing real work or is simply dressing up ordinary writerly anxiety in borrowed authority.

- Tolstoy's "infection" theory: art is any activity that consciously transmits, through external signs, a feeling the artist has genuinely lived through, such that an audience is "infected" by it and comes to feel some version of the same thing. He names three conditions that determine the strength of that infection — the individuality of the feeling, the clarity of its expression, and, above all, the sincerity of the artist in having actually felt it — and he is fairly explicit that sincerity outweighs the other two.
- He then stacks a moral test on top of the aesthetic one, which is the part people tend to leave out: infection alone only makes art effective, not good. Good art spreads feelings that draw people into the brotherhood he takes to be the real content of a Christian age; counterfeit art is produced by and for a leisured class, and specializes in feelings — pride, refined boredom, erotic longing, cleverness admired for its own sake — that only people with money and training ever get to have. By that test he discards most of the European canon he had once revered, and, notoriously, most of his own fiction along with it; I recall, though I would want this checked, that he spares only a couple of his simplest stories as belonging to "good" art of the universal kind.
- The sharpest version of the argument is not in What Is Art? itself but in the 1906 essay on Shakespeare, where he goes after King Lear specifically: implausible plotting, characters who all speak in the same overwrought register regardless of who they are supposed to be, and a tragic ending he judges worse, both morally and dramatically, than the ending of the old anonymous play Shakespeare was adapting. His larger claim is that Shakespeare's reputation is an inherited hypnosis, passed down from German Romantic critics, that almost nobody has ever actually tested against an unprompted reaction of their own.
- My problem is that the book I am stuck on is narrated by a wealthy, over-educated art critic whose voice is dense, allusive, and syntactically pleased with itself — exactly the sort of individual but class-bound voice Tolstoy's universality test looks built to exclude, even though I would argue, conveniently, that the narrator is sincerely felt, and that the book's underlying feeling — his slow, humiliating discovery of his own uselessness — is about as universal as anything I know how to write. Is separating sincerity of feeling from accessibility of feeling like that a real move inside Tolstoy's own framework, or is it just the maneuver he accuses Shakespeare's defenders of making — mistaking a trained response for a natural one?

I would like your comments in two parts: first, line by line, anywhere the notes above misstate or oversimplify Tolstoy's actual position; second, a direct verdict on the closing argument — whether it survives contact with the framework I have just laid out, or whether the essay I want to write ends up talking itself out of existing.
