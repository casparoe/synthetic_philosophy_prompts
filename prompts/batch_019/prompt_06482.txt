Quick one for the intertemporal choice reading group. Everyone loves citing Ainslie's hyperbolic discounting curves as basically having solved Aristotle's old puzzle about akrasia: the addict (or procrastinator) prefers the big reward tomorrow when it's still far off, then flips to wanting the small reward now once its close, and hey presto, thats your incontinent person acting against their own better judgement. I'm not so sure it's that easy.

Compare Aristotle's account of incontinence -- the "has the knowledge but isn't using it" move, plus his split between incontinence of impetuosity (propeteia) and of weakness (astheneia) -- with the hyperbolic/quasi-hyperbolic discounting story. Does the discounting model actually preserve what makes akrasia puzzling in the first place, namely that the agent judges, right at the moment of acting, that she's doing the worse thing? Or does it quietly change the subject by just saying her preferences flipped, in which case Davidson's principle of continence was never even violated? Take a side on whether hyperbolic discounting is a genuine theory of weakness of will or just a different phenomenon that's borrowed its name, and deal with Holton's objection that a mere preference reversal isn't the same as failing to hold to a resolution.
