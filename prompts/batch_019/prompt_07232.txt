I'm putting together next week's session for my seminar on the Vienna Circle, and I want to sanity-check something before I say it out loud in front of 30 students. My working narrative has always been that the verifiability criterion is basically what killed off "big" existential questions in that tradition: Carnap goes after Heidegger's talk of Nothingness and Dasein as strings of pseudo-statements, Ayer is happy to toss most of ethics and all of metaphysics into the meaningless bin, and I've always assumed Schlick, who if anything pushed the verifiability line even harder than Carnap in some of his writing, would've treated "what is the meaning of life" exactly the same way: a grammatically fine but cognitively empty question, since no observation could ever settle it, so the right move is to dissolve it rather than answer it.

Except a student doing her term paper on happiness sent me a scan of an essay literally titled "On the Meaning of Life" by Schlick, from the late 1920s, where he seems to just... answer the question - somthing about play, youth, and the difference between activity done for its own sake versus activity done as a means to something else. That doesn't read like a takedown or an error theory at all, it reads like he's sincerely proposing an account. Is this some kind of inconsistency on Schlicks part, a case of the essay predating his mature verificationism, or is there a principled way for a logical positivist to earnestly answer that question without violating his own criterion of meaning? Would love your read before I update my slides.
