I'm TAing the decision theory unit of a formal epistemology course this term, and one of the problem sets had a question on Good's theorem -- the "expected value of free information is never negative" result. It's a thing I've seen invoked approvingly on rationalist blogs for years as basically a slam dunk for "just go find out more before deciding," but I'd never actually sat down and rebuilt the proof myself until I had to grade people on it. The question I set was: state the theorem precisely, including whatever assumptions it needs, sketch in your own words why its true, and then say whether the Hirshleifer effect (the thing where public information about, say, a farmer's future harvest can wreck the market for crop insurance, leaving everyone worse off) counts as a genuine counterexample to Good's theorem or whether something else is going on. I threw the Hirshleifer part in myself, mostly because I wanted a question nobody could answer just by regurgitating the Wikipedia paragraph on the theorem.

Here's one student's answer (not mine, and anonymized) that I'd like a second opinion on before I finalize a grade:

"Good's theorem (I. J. Good, 1967) says that for a Bayesian agent who updates on evidence by conditionalization and then picks whichever action maximizes expected utility given her current beliefs, getting to see some piece of evidence before choosing can never make her worse off in expectation, as long as the evidence is free and doesn't change which actions are on the table. So the expected value of information is never negative -- you're never worse off, ex ante, for having more data before you decide, though sometimes the data won't end up changing what you'd do anyway, so it won't help either.

The reason this is true comes down to convexity. Once you're allowed to pick the best action for each possible posterior, your achievable expected utility is a convex function of your beliefs, and this is really the same convexity that shows up in Jensen's-inequality arguments about risk aversion: risk is what makes the value function bend the right way. Since averaging a convex function over the different posteriors you might land on beats applying it to the average of those posteriors, and since the average of all your possible posteriors just is your prior (that's the "conservation of expected evidence" idea), it follows that expecting to see the evidence first can never be worse than deciding on the prior alone.

As for Hirshleifer: this is a genuine counterexample to Good's theorem. If a farmer's future harvest size becomes public knowledge before crop insurance is sold, insurers won't sell insurance against a risk that's no longer really a risk, so the market unravels and everyone -- the farmer included -- can end up worse off than if the information had stayed hidden a while longer. That shows Good's "more information is always at least as good" result breaks down once you leave the single isolated decision-maker and put the information into a market with other people reacting to it."
