I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion and modal metaphysics this term, and this week's session was devoted to Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument, in the reconstruction that Dana Scott circulated from Gödel's own notes. We spent most of the meeting working carefully through the derivation — from the primitive, undefined notion of a "positive" property, through the essence-based definition of necessary existence, to the final theorem that a God-like being exists necessarily — and I was struck by how divided my students were about which of the standard objections actually sinks the argument, as opposed to being a mere irritant that a sufficiently ingenious theist could patch around. Since most of the secondary literature treats the whole exercise as a logical curiosity rather than something worth engaging with fully seriously, I would like my students to leave next week's session with a clearer sense of where the real pressure points lie.

(a) the modal-collapse result, first noted by Jordan Howard Sobel and later confirmed via automated theorem-proving (Benzmüller and Woltzenlogel Paleo), showing that the axioms jointly entail that every truth is a necessary truth;
(b) the complaint that "positive property" is never given an independent, non-question-begging characterization, so that we have no way of checking which properties actually satisfy the axioms;
(c) parody constructions, in the spirit of Gaunilo's island but built directly on Gödel's own axioms, that appear to "prove" the necessary existence of things we have no antecedent reason to accept;
