In a philosophy of physics reading group, we recently paired Hume's treatment of causation in the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding with a session on the three leading interpretations of quantum mechanics, and a question came up that none of us could fully resolve: judged strictly by his own epistemological standards rather than by ours, what would Hume have made of Bohm, Copenhagen, and Everett?

Hume's position is not merely that causal connections are hard to observe, but that the very idea of a "necessary connexion" binding cause to effect is one we have no right to. On his account, however closely we examine any single case, all we ever find is one event regularly following another -- events are, in his words, "entirely loose and separate," merely "conjoined," never "connected" -- and the felt necessity we project onto them is supplied afterward by custom, once constant conjunction has done its habituating work, rather than discovered in the objects themselves. This is meant to rule out any appeal to hidden powers or mechanisms that outrun what experience actually gives us. It is worth remembering, though, that in his later discussion of liberty and necessity Hume insists that this same regularity-based necessity holds just as fully for human motives and actions as for colliding bodies, so a thoroughly deterministic universe is no problem for him in itself; his quarrel is specifically with unobservable necessitating glue, not with determinism as such.

With that in mind, work through how Hume's own commitments -- and not a stereotype of "empiricists must prefer instrumentalism" -- would bear on: (a) Bohmian mechanics, whose particles follow fully determinate but experimentally inaccessible trajectories under the guidance of a "quantum potential" that acts instantaneously and without regard to distance; (b) the Copenhagen interpretation, which declines to specify any mechanism beneath the observed statistics and simply takes Born-rule probabilities as basic; and (c) Everett's many-worlds picture, which adds nothing to the deterministic Schrödinger evolution of a single universal wavefunction, at the price of an unobservable proliferation of branching worlds and a still hotly contested story about why squared amplitudes should govern anyone's rational credence. Please reason through each interpretation in turn rather than jumping to a verdict, flag any point at which Hume's own doctrines seem to pull in more than one direction at once, and conclude with whether you think his framework favors one interpretation, forces him to reject all three, or leaves him with no principled way to choose among them.
