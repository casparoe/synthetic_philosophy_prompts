Last week a friend of mine who works as an actuary and I got into a long, ultimately unresolved argument that started from something she tells clients routinely: "your probability of a serious cardiac event in the next ten years is 14%." I pointed out that the client in question belongs simultaneously to a great many overlapping classes — men his age, men his age with his cholesterol level, men his age with his cholesterol level and family history, men of his profession and neighborhood, and so on without any obvious stopping point — and that these equally data-backed classes will in general hand back different percentages for the very same person on the very same evidence. My friend's response was that this isn't a real problem in practice, since actuaries simply use the narrowest class for which they have reliable statistics. I countered that this is essentially Reichenbach's own proposal, and that it doesn't really solve anything: "narrowest class with reliable statistics" is a matter of degree and convention rather than principle, and if you push it all the way you collapse into the single case, where relative frequency stops being defined at all. We spent the better part of an hour dragging in weather forecasts and a hypothetical doctor's five-year survival estimate without landing anywhere, and I'd like a more rigorous outside verdict than either of us could manage over dinner.

Write an essay on the reference class problem for single-case probability ascriptions. Set out the problem precisely, including why Reichenbach's "narrowest class with reliable statistics" move is unsatisfying on its own terms, and address whether the problem is a specific defect of frequentism or whether, as some philosophers of probability (Alan Hájek prominently among them) have argued, it resurfaces in some form for propensity theories, best-system/Humean accounts of chance, and even broadly Bayesian treatments once they have to say which evidence or description of the case is the relevant one. Weigh the main responses on offer — biting the bullet and treating single-case objective probability as incoherent or as covertly interest-relative, versus the various proposed principled fixes — and then commit to a specific verdict rather than concluding that reasonable people simply disagree: tell me whether the reference class problem is fatal to the idea of an objective probability of a single, non-repeatable event, or merely a manageable inconvenience, and say for which theories of probability, if any, it is actually fatal.
