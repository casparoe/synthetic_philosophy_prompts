I'm a backend and ML engineer, and a few months ago I fell into a bit of a rabbit hole that started with an offhand footnote in a paper on calibrating probabilistic classifiers. It mentioned, almost in passing, that "strictly proper scoring rules" like log loss and the Brier score are not just convenient training objectives, but are also the exact machinery some philosophers use to argue that rational degrees of belief have to obey the probability axioms in the first place. That connection stuck with me, so over the following weeks I read around what I now know is called "accuracy-first epistemology" or "epistemic utility theory," and I tried writing up my own understanding of the central argument, plus a worry about it that I can't tell is a genuine problem or just my own confusion. I have no formal training in philosophy, so before I repeat any of this to the coworker who got me started on it, I'd like someone to check where my reconstruction holds up and where it doesn't. The draft is below.

The classic Dutch Book argument for Bayesian probabilism always struck me as a bit thin: it shows that if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie can arrange a set of bets against you that loses money for certain, whatever happens. But why should losing a bet be an epistemic failure rather than a merely practical one? Jim Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument, which I believe is from a 1998 paper, is supposed to fix exactly that by staying "nonpragmatic": instead of bets, it scores a credence function purely on how close it is to the truth at each possible world, using something structurally identical to a machine-learning loss function — the Brier score, say, or log loss. The theorem is that if your credence function is not a genuine probability function, there exists some probability function that is strictly more accurate than yours in every possible world, regardless of which one turns out to be actual — so you are being dominated on purely truth-directed grounds, no betting required. Crucially, though, the proof only goes through for scoring rules that are "strictly proper," in the same technical sense used in forecasting and machine learning: the rule under which reporting your true credence is the uniquely best strategy by your own lights.

That last clause is where I get stuck. The whole force of the argument seems to depend on restricting "legitimate" measures of epistemic accuracy to the strictly proper ones, and propriety is defined by what a reporter's own expected score recommends — which sounds like a fact about incentives for eliciting honest reports, not obviously a fact about what accuracy itself is. So I cannot tell whether the argument quietly presupposes probabilism, by building it into the choice of which scoring rules even count as legitimate, before going on to "derive" it — or whether propriety really is an independently motivated constraint on any adequate accuracy measure, with no essential connection to elicitation or incentives, in which case the objection dissolves. I vaguely recall that someone — Maher, maybe? — raised a version of this circularity complaint against Joyce, and that there is a later, more general proof of essentially the same theorem using tools from information theory, but I don't know whether that later version actually answers the worry or just restates it in more elegant mathematical clothing.

What I would like help with: first, whether my reconstruction of the dominance argument is basically right or whether I have garbled some step along the way; second, whether the circularity worry about "legitimate" scoring rules is a real and recognized problem with the argument, a confusion on my part, or something in between, and, if there is a standard philosophical response to it, what that response actually says; and third — the part I find most interesting — whether "strict propriety" is really a pragmatic notion dressed up in epistemic clothing, in a way that would undercut the "nonpragmatic" selling point of the whole project, or whether there is a genuinely non-circular story for why it is the right condition on any measure of accuracy. I would rather have the gaps in my understanding pointed out directly, including any places where philosophers themselves are simply divided, than be reassured that it is all "a matter of ongoing debate."
