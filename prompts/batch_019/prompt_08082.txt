My grandpa's had Parkinson's for about 13 years now, and his neurologist just cleared him for deep brain stimulation — they'd implant electrodes (subthalamic nucleus, I think, in his case) to get the tremor and rigidity under control. Everyone in the family is thrilled except me, because I'm a neuroscience PhD student and made the mistake of going down a rabbit hole of DBS case reports and patient interview studies last week. Turns out a non-trivial number of patients come out the other side saying they don't feel like themselves anymore — more impulsive, weirdly euphoric, or just "off" in ways their spouse or kids notice before they do. What really got me is that patients flatly disagree with each other about whether the change is even bad: some say things like "I finally feel like the real me," others say "I miss who I used to be," even when their motor symptom scores look basically identical from the outside.

So now my grandpa is asking me, the science person in the family, whether he should go ahead with it, and I dont know what to tell him. I keep starting to give the standard "just weigh the expected benefits against the risks" answer and then stopping halfway, because something about that advice feels like it doesn't fully fit here, even though I can't quite pin down why. I mentioned this to a couple of labmates over lunch and we spent forty minutes arguing about it instead of eating. The best I could come up with is that it feels different from, say, choosing between two job offers, since he can't really picture what its like on the other side and can't test-drive it first — but I don't know if that's a real, principled difference or if I'm just spooked by the case reports and pattern-matching to something that isn't actually there. Is there something genuinely different about how you're supposed to reason through a decision like this compared to a normal high-stakes choice, and if so, what would actually thinking it through well look like for him?
