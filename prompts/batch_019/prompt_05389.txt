I've been down a Frederick Douglass rabbit hole this week (started from an offhand line in a Motte CW roundup, as these things do) and I'm stuck on something nobody in the thread wanted to actually argue about instead of just gesturing at it. Douglass spent the 1840s agreeing with Garrison that the Constitution was, in Garrisons words, a "covenant with death," then flipped hard around 1851 and spent the rest of his career arguing the opposite. His fullest version of the flip is the 1860 Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", and the thing that gets me about that speech is it's doing two interpretive moves at once without ever admitting they might not point the same direction. Move one is basically proto-public-meaning-originalism: don't go digging through Madison's convention notes for what the framers secretly meant, just read the document the way a competent reader of 1787 would've read it -- and since the words "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in it, slavery isn't in there. Move two is a straight-up higher-law/natural-rights appeal: liberty and justice (per the Preamble) are the default setting the text has to be read against, and anything cutting into them needs explicit, unambiguous language. Those two moves can obviously diverge once you're not the one picking the test cases, which he definately was.

So here's the actual question: dress modern-day Douglass up as a time traveler and make him sit through the last decade-plus of Supreme Court terms before he answers. Does he end up siding with the "public meaning" originalists (his move one, grown up and everywhere) or does he decide that whole project is a hostile takeover of a method he only ever deployed in service of move two? I want your actual read on how he'd react to specifics, not just "he'd feel complicated" -- e.g. Shelby County gutting the Voting Rights Act's preclearance regime on basically textualist/federalism grounds, Dobbs using originalist history-and-tradition analysis to kill a right he would've wanted kept, and Bostock, where garden-variety textualism spat out a result he'd probably cheer for. If you have to hedge, fine, but give me an actual leaning and defend it using the structure of his own arguments, not vibes about his general politics.
