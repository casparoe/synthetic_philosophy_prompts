Since retiring from a career in statistical mechanics (mostly modelling phase transitions in disordered systems), I have been filling my afternoons with philosophy rather than crossword puzzles, and I have joined a small reading group of fellow retirees that has lately wandered into decision theory. Some years ago, while I was still working, I read Ole Peters's papers on "ergodicity economics," which use the Kelly criterion to argue that the classical resolution of puzzles like the St. Petersburg paradox — via a concave, typically logarithmic, utility function — treats as a mere psychological taste for risk-aversion something that is really just a fact about multiplicative, non-ergodic processes: an individual's wealth over time does not track the expectation of wealth across parallel possible worlds, so maximizing the growth rate is not a preference at all but the objectively correct criterion. One of my reading-group colleagues recently sent me a long online sequence called "Geometric Rationality," by Scott Garrabrant, whom I gather is known in AI-safety circles for work on embedded agency; he pushes this same idea a good deal further than I expected, treating Kelly betting and the Nash bargaining solution as two instances of one underlying move — maximizing a geometric rather than an arithmetic expectation — and he states outright that a geometrically rational agent thereby rejects the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, since such an agent can be strictly required to randomize between options that a fixed expected-utility maximizer would never bother mixing except at an exact tie.

Having spent decades on the physics side of the ergodic/non-ergodic distinction, I find myself unable to tell whether Garrabrant's proposal is a genuinely new normative principle — on a par with, and possibly displacing, expected utility theory — or whether it is expected utility theory in disguise, with the "geometric" framing doing rhetorical work that a suitably chosen utility function could always do instead. I would like your help thinking this through. Concretely: what, if anything, does "geometric rationality" deliver in the Kelly-betting case that an ordinary risk-averse expected-utility maximizer with logarithmic utility over wealth does not already deliver? And, separately, in the Nash-bargaining case, where the relevant "expectation" is taken over an agent's uncertainty about its own identity rather than over states of the world — what exactly is being averaged, and over what population of "selves"? If there is a genuine, unifying insight here rather than a coincidence of formulas, I would like to understand what it is, and why it is supposed to force abandoning the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms rather than merely restating them in unfamiliar language.
