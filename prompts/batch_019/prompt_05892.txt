I am sketching a minor character for a novel-in-progress — a Cambridge mathematician modeled loosely on Frank Ramsey — and I want his convictions to hold up under real scrutiny rather than merely sound clever. In the "Epilogue" he read to the Apostles in 1925, Ramsey answers cosmic despair by insisting that sheer size is beside the point, morally and emotionally: his picture of the world is "drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale," with human beings filling the foreground and the stars reduced to specks. He died in 1930, decades before anyone spoke of longtermism, so picture him confronted with the version of that outlook built on Bostrom's astronomical waste argument — the claim that because the accessible universe could sustain an almost unimaginable number of future people, reducing existential risk now should dominate our moral priorities precisely because of that scale. Does the Ramsey of the Epilogue have any principled way to resist an argument that revives, in consequentialist dress, exactly the cosmic bookkeeping he waved away, or does his own pioneering treatment of subjective probability and expected value in "Truth and Probability" commit him, whether he liked it or not, to taking that kind of astronomical multiplication seriously? Answer in his voice if you like, but make sure the philosophical moves are ones he could actually have made, not just generic musings on the meaning of life.
