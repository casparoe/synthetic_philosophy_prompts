Two people in my PhD program's formal epistemology reading group got into it after we read the Glymour chapter on the problem of old evidence, and I honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

Quick context: on strict Bayesian conditionalization, if you already know E for certain (P(E)=1), then P(H|E) just equals P(H) -- learning E (again) literally can't raise your credence in any H. But everybody agrees the already-known anomaly in Mercury's orbit counted as strong confirmation of GR once Einstein showed the theory predicted it. So something's gotta give.

(1) Mara said this just refutes naive Bayesian confirmation theory, full stop -- if your formal model says a textbook case of confirmation isn't confirmation, thats the model's problem, not the physicists'.

(2) Deshawn said there's an easy fix: stop using your actual credence function and use a counterfactual one instead. Ask what your credence in H would've been, before you learned E, conditional on E -- that's the real measure of confirmation, and its obviously high for Mercury and GR.

(3) Mara said that's not a fix, it's a different question in a trenchcoat. You're not conditionalizing on anything anymore, you're just stipulating an answer via an agent who doesn't exist, with a background state you have to reverse-engineer until you get the result you want (how much did this hypothetical person already know about celestial mechanics, other tests of gravity, etc.?).

(4) Deshawn switched moves: forget the counterfactual agent, do "the Garber-Jeffrey thing" instead. The real new evidence was never E -- it was the logical fact that H entails E (or raises its probability). Nobody's logically omniscient, so learning about that entailment is genuine news you can conditionalize on like anything else.

(5) Mara said that just relocates the problem: now entailment facts have to be contingent, uncertain propositions living inside your probability space, which means giving up the classical assumption that logical truths get probability 1. And even granting that, it still doesn't match the actual history -- physicists were impressed by how badly the Mercury anomaly resisted every non-GR explanation, not by the bare fact that a valid derivation existed somewhere.

They had to run to class after that. Go through it move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, then close with a one-line overall verdict: Mara, Deshawn, or neither.
