I'm taking a course on classical Indian epistemology this semester, and this week's unit covered the Nyāya school's defense of upamāna ("comparison" or "analogy") as a fourth pramāṇa in its own right, standing alongside perception, inference, and testimony. Our professor used the standard textbook illustration: someone who has never seen a gavaya (a wild-ox-like animal) is truthfully told that "a gavaya is an animal similar to a cow"; later, wandering through a forest, she encounters an animal resembling a cow in just the relevant respects and, for the first time, judges "that is a gavaya." Nyāya treats this final judgment as a distinct item of knowledge (pramā) produced by upamāna, over and above anything perception, memory, or testimony could deliver on their own. After section, two people in my discussion group got into a genuinely unresolved argument about whether that's actually defensible, and I haven't been able to settle it myself.

(1) Elena opened by defending the Nyāya line: "that is a gavaya" can't be a case of perception, since perception delivers only "that animal resembles a cow," and it can't be testimony either, since the only testimony ever received was an earlier, general remark made in a completely different context, not any present assertion about this particular animal. (2) Marcus answered that this is just inference wearing a disguise: the woman is implicitly reasoning that this animal has the features described to her, that whatever has those features is called a gavaya (as she recalls being told), and therefore that this is a gavaya — an ordinary syllogism using a remembered statement as one premise, with no need to mint a new pramāṇa. (3) Elena replied that this can't be a genuine Nyāya-style inference, since inference requires an independently established vyāpti (invariable concomitance) between reason and conclusion, normally confirmed across many observed cases, whereas here there is exactly one data point — the informant's single sentence — so treating it as already licensing a universal premise just presupposes the very word-object connection upamāna is supposed to establish for the first time. (4) Marcus pushed back that demanding repeated confirmation before something counts as an inference proves too much: if someone tells you, for the first time, that poisonous mushrooms have red caps, and you later see a red-capped mushroom and infer it's poisonous, nobody thinks a new pramāṇa is needed to cover that single-premise inference — so mere singularity of data can't be what makes the gavaya case special. (5) Elena answered that the cases aren't parallel: "this mushroom is poisonous" is an ordinary object-level fact, exactly what inference is suited to conclude, whereas — on Nyāya's own account of what upamāna delivers — "that is a gavaya" is really knowledge of a word-meaning relation, namely that the term "gavaya" correctly applies to this kind of thing, and no other pramāṇa is individuated to produce that specific kind of result.

Could you adjudicate this move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner? For each of (1) through (5), say who has the better of it and why. I'm especially keen to know whether Elena's closing distinction between word-meaning relations and object-level facts actually rescues upamāna's independence, or whether Marcus could reasonably fold that distinction back into an inferential story. Please also flag anything both of them are missing that a Naiyāyika, a Buddhist epistemologist, or a Mīmāṃsaka defender of upamāna might press against either side.
