I spent last week at a small workshop pairing philosophers of mind with computational neuroscientists, for a piece I'm writing on the philosophy of neuroscience. The sharpest exchange I caught wasn't on stage but in the Q&A after a talk defending non-reductive physicalism, between the speaker (whom I'll call Daniel) and a neuroscientist in the audience (whom I'll call Sofia). I reconstructed it from my notes:

(1) Sofia said that if Daniel's mental states are fully realized by brain states, and those brain states are already sufficient — given the causal closure of the physical — to produce behavior, then the mental state adds no causal work of its own: it's either identical to the physical state, which is reduction, or it's redundant.

(2) Daniel replied that this only looks threatening if you treat the mental property and its physical realizer as two separate causes competing for credit, like two bullets hitting one target. Realization isn't that kind of relation, so there's no overdetermination — just one cause, describable at two levels.

(3) Sofia said "realized by" has to mean "not identical to," since that's the whole point of rejecting reductionism, so he can't have it both ways: distinct enough to resist reduction, but not distinct enough to be excluded.

(4) Daniel countered that the same reasoning would show temperature doesn't really raise the mercury, since mean molecular kinetic energy is already sufficient on its own — yet nobody concludes thermodynamics is causally idle, so the exclusion principle itself must be too strong.

(5) Sofia answered that the analogy cuts the other way: temperature earns its causal keep precisely because it can be reductively tied to its micro-base in a way Daniel refuses for the mental case, so borrowing that move quietly turns him into the reductionist he denies being.

Neither budged. Could you adjudicate this move by move? Start with a JSON object scoring each numbered turn from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), then briefly justify each score and flag anything important that both of them are missing.
