hey, I'm drafting a chapter on the logical omniscience problem and wrote up some rough notes on why I think the standard "fixes" don't actually fix anything, they just relocate the problem. Can you read through it and give comments? Mainly want push back on (a) whether im being fair to Fagin & Halpern, and (b) whether the "collapse" argument at the end actually works or proves too much. Also curious whether this is even novel enough to build a section around or if it's a well-worn objection I should just footnote and move past.

Notes:

Standard epistemic logic (Hintikka-style: agent knows φ iff φ is true at every world epistemically accessible from the actual one) has the usual closure problems - agents automatically know every tautology, know every logical consequence of what they know, and can't believe P without believing anything logically equivalent to P. Fine maybe as a model of "implicit" knowledge (Levesque's term) but useless as a model of what we'd actually call knowing/believing something ("explicit" knowledge).

Two standard-ish fixes:

1. Impossible worlds (Rantala, later Hintikka too) - add worlds where the laws of logic don't fully hold, and let some of these be accessible. Now an agent can fail to know a tautology because there's an accessible impossible world where it's false.

2. Awareness (Fagin & Halpern) - explicit knowledge = implicit knowledge (the usual truth-at-all-accessible-worlds thing), restricted to propositions in the agent's "awareness set."

My problem w/ (2): it looks like an explanation but I don't think it is, once you ask where the awareness set comes from. If it's just stipulated case-by-case to match the data (agent knows P, doesn't know some Q that trivially follows from P) that's not a theory, thats redescription. If instead you try to ground it in something principled - e.g. "aware of whatever's derivable in <= n steps" - you've basically reinvented algorithmic knowledge (Halpern/Moses/Vardi: knowing just is whatever the agent's algorithm outputs) except now theres a redundant possible-worlds apparatus bolted onto it that isn't doing any work. All the actual work is happening in the algorithm/proof procedure, not the worlds or the accessibility relation.

Tentative conclusion: awareness logic isn't a real rival to the algorithmic/proof-theoretic approach (justification logic - tracking an actual proof/justification term instead of just quantifying over worlds - is maybe the cleanest version of that approach), it's just an unstable way-station on the road to it.

Things I'm unsure about:
- am I strawmanning Fagin & Halpern, is there some non-ad-hoc, non-algorithmic way to motivate the awareness set that I'm not seeing
- does the "collapses into algorithmic knowledge" move also hit impossible-worlds semantics, or is that one safe from it
- is setting this whole thing up via Levesque's implicit/explicit distinction even the right move, or does it just smuggle in the conclusion from the start
