For the better part of a decade, the "ideas" column I write has amounted to a running profile of Margit Ossendorf, the philosopher largely responsible for reviving Moritz Schlick's neglected 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life." Schlick's claim, briefly, is that a life composed entirely of purposeful, means-to-end activity — a career among them, however distinguished — can never actually contain meaning, only defer it indefinitely; genuine meaning, on his view, belongs only to activity valued for its own sake, paradigmatically play, which is why he treats it as most available in youth and steadily crowded out by adult "seriousness." Margit has organized her working life around genuinely believing this, and has warned her department in writing that anyone who toasts her "decades of hard work and dedication" at her retirement dinner will be corrected from her seat, mid-sentence. They have asked me, not a colleague, to give that toast anyway. Write it: five minutes, warm enough for her grandchildren, funny enough to survive her interrupting, faithful enough to Schlick's actual argument that the logical-empiricism specialists at her table don't wince, and affectionate rather than a lecture.
