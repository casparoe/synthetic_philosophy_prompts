So I'm about sixty pages into a first-contact novel, and my protagonist Noor is the team's xenolinguist -- she's the one out in the field with a recorder, pointing at things while the aliens make sounds back, trying to bootstrap a vocabulary with zero shared cognates or grammer to lean on. To make the linguistics feel less made up (my agent keeps gently reminding me this is supposed to be "commercial sci-fi," not a dissertation) I went down a rabbit hole, pun very much intended, on Quine's old "gavagai" example -- the field linguist who hears a native say "gavagai" every time a rabbit hops by, and can't tell if the word means "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit-stage." My takeaway was that this is basically a lesson about how radically underdetermined translation is at the start of contact, but that its fundamentally a data problem: with enough patience, and by cross-checking enough different scenes (the rabbit-thing sitting vs. running, one vs. several, dead vs. alive), a sufficiently careful linguist could eventually triangulate the one correct meaning, sort of like nailing down a physical constant through repeated measurement.

I want to build the book's midpoint scene around exactly that: after months of stalled fieldwork, Noor finally racks up enough cross-referenced data to settle, once and for all, what the aliens' core nouns actually refer to, and her hard-nosed supervisor has to eat his words about the "gavagai problem" being unsolvable -- I even have him quoting Quine at her like scripture earlier in the book. Before I write the payoff scene though, I'd rather find out now than from a philosopher friend after the book's out: is "it just needs more/better data" a fair description of what Quine thought would resolve the indeterminacy, or does his actual argument rule that kind of resolution out even in principle? If it's the latter, I clearly need to rethink what Noor's "victory" scene is even a victory over.
