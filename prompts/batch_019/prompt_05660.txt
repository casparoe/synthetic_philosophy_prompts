I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term built around Kit Fine's and Jonathan Schaffer's work on metaphysical grounding, and we've just reached the question of whether grounding must be well-founded - whether there has to be some fundamental level at which chains of "in virtue of" relations terminate. Most of the assigned readings simply build this in: every non-fundamental fact is supposed to trace back, through a finite and non-circular chain of grounds, to something fundamental. A couple of students pushed back hard on this in discussion, pointing out that well-foundedness is usually just stipulated rather than argued for, and that there is a real minority tradition - Ross Cameron's work on regress and fundamentality, Einar Bohn's discussions of worlds with no maximal whole, and more recent work by Ricki Bliss on distinguishing vicious from benign regresses - that takes the alternative seriously.

For next week's class I'd like to hand the students a genuinely hard case rather than just restating the worry in the abstract. Could you construct a scenario involving some fact, or small family of related facts, where our ordinary grounding judgments seem to force a choice among three uncomfortable options: treating an obviously non-fundamental fact as though it had no ground at all, accepting a chain of ever-more-basic grounds that descends forever without ever reaching bedrock, or accepting that two or more facts end up grounding one another, contrary to the usual assumption that grounding is asymmetric. I'd rather you not just gesture at the standard mereological "gunk" case (an object all of whose parts have further proper parts) without doing any real work on it - either develop that case carefully enough to show exactly which grounding claims about a gunky object generate the trilemma, or find a different case that doesn't rely on gunk at all. Either way, be explicit about which facts are supposed to ground which others, and say which of the three horns strikes you as the least costly to accept, and why.
