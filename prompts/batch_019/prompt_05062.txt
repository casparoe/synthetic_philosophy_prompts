I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental condensed-matter physics before I retired, and since then I've gotten weirdly hooked on philosophy, much to my wife's amusement (she keeps asking when I'll take up gardening instead like a normal retiree). Two totally separate threads of reading have collided in my head lately and I can't shake it. One is a little Leibniz reading group a few of us started at the library, currently slogging through the Monadology. The other is my grandson, who's doing something AI-safety-adjacent for his PhD, sending me links about "acausal trade," which is apparently a real idea some decision theorists take seriously and not just a joke someone made up.

In case you haven't run into the term: acausal trade is the idea that two agents who can never causally affect each other in any way -- say because they're in different, causally disconnected patches of the universe (or multiverse), or otherwise positioned so that no signal could ever pass between them -- can still coordinate for mutual benefit, purely by each one reasoning about what a similar-enough reasoner on the other end would do. Something like: I bear a cost to help you because I predict you'd only help me if you predicted I'd do the same, and it turns out we're both right, even though neither of us can ever check in with the other, or send so much as a photon their way. My first instinct as an old physicist was to reach for entanglement as the model here, but apparently thats the wrong intuition entirely -- there's no shared physical state being exploited, just two seperate minds independently reasoning their way to matching conclusions.

What I can't stop thinking about is how close this sounds to Leibniz's pre-established harmony, minus the God. His monads are famously "windowless" -- nothing causal ever gets in or out of any of them -- yet they all unfold in perfect lockstep because God tuned the whole system at creation, like a huge bank of clocks built so precisely that they never need to be synchronized again once they're wound and set going. Acausal trade seems to be chasing that exact same trick, coordination with zero causal contact, except the syncing is done by the agents themselves, reasoning about each other, with no outside guarantor at all. So here's my actual question: if you sat Leibniz down and explained acausal trade to him on his own terms, what do you think he'd say about it? Would he wave it off as a shaky, homemade substitute for what pre-established harmony hands you for free -- since nothing stops two acausal "traders" from simply misjudging each other and ending up badly out of sync, which his whole system was built specifically to rule out? Or could he turn his own machinery on it -- sufficient reason, the identity of indiscernibles, the best-of-all-possible-worlds argument -- to claim that this kind of coordination isn't even a coherent possibility unless there's an omniscient God arranging it from outside the system to begin with? I'd like your best reconstruction of his actual reasoning, not just a one-liner about him needing a lecture on game theory first.
