I'm a retired physicist (spent about thirty-five years doing condensed matter work, then a lot of time near the end of my career herding grant panels and hiring committees), and about a year ago I started going to a philosophy discussion group that meets at the library near me — mostly retirees, plus a couple of philosophy grad students who keep the rest of us honest. We've been working slowly through a unit on social epistemology, and a couple weeks back we got to Condorcet's jury theorem, which genuinely delighted me, because it's the exact same math I used to half-argue about with colleagues over whether a bigger review panel is actually more trustworthy or just feels that way.

Two people in the group, Greg and Meera, kept going at it over coffee after the session, and I've been turning it over since without landing anywhere. (1) Greg opened with more or less the textbook statement: if each juror (or panelist) is independently more likely than not to be right about a binary question, then as you add more of them, majority vote comes out correct with near-certainty as the group grows — Condorcet proved this back in the 1780s, and it's the reason "get more opinions" is always at least a defensible instinct. (2) Meera pushed back that independence is doing basically all the work in that result, and it's almost never true of real panels: people read the same background material, anchor on whoever speaks first with confidence, share the same disciplinary blind spots, so the errors are correlated across jurors, not independent, and once you let in even mild correlation the "approaches certainty" conclusion falls apart pretty fast. (3) Greg conceded the correlation point but said it doesn't get Meera all the way there: even with correlated errors, adding reviewers can't make the panel worse on average, only less helpful than the idealized case — so "bigger is weakly better" survives even if "bigger is dramatically better" doesn't. (4) Meera then brought up a result she'd read about (Feddersen and Pesendorfer, I think, though she admitted she might be misremembering the names) on strategic voting under unanimity rules: a rational juror should reason about the specific scenario where their vote is the one that decides the outcome, and once you build that "pivotal voter" reasoning in, it can be rational for a juror to discount their own honest read of the evidence, to the point that bigger unanimous juries convict more innocent defendants, not fewer, as you add jurors. That's not just weaker-than-advertised, she said, that's the theorem's whole direction running backwards. (5) Greg answered that this needs jurors sophisticated enough to reason like game theorists about pivotality, which is alot to assume about actual people on an actual jury, who mostly just vote their gut reaction to the evidence — a cute result, maybe, but not obviously one that should shake anyone's confidence in "bigger is better." (6) Meera said you don't need explicit Bayesian reasoning for the effect to show up: a holdout juror learning that eleven other people all think "guilty" is itself evidence, and real people update on that constantly in real deliberation rooms, sophistication or not — and anyway, she said, the entire use of proving a theorem is finding out which of its assumptions are load-bearing, and it turns out dropping "sincere voting" wrecks the conclusion a lot more thoroughly than dropping "independence" does, which should worry us more than Greg's concession lets on.
