A friend of mine, Dan, had his car break down just after one in the morning on an unlit stretch of highway, in one of those dead zones where his phone had read "no service" for the better part of an hour. The truck that eventually stopped was driven by a stranger who, before agreeing to take him anywhere, explained that he'd once picked up a hitchhiker who'd sworn he would pay him back for the detour and the gas once they reached town, and had simply walked into a gas station and never come out. So this time, he told Dan, he wasn't driving anyone anywhere unless he was genuinely convinced they meant it. Dan promised to hand over $200 once they reached an ATM some forty minutes down the road, apparently sounded sincere enough to satisfy him, and got the ride. When they arrived, nothing bound him to actually pay: the driver had no way of finding him afterward, the two would never cross paths again, and the ride itself was, by then, simply a fact about the past. Dan paid anyway, and described it afterward as barely feeling like a decision at all.

When he told the story at a dinner a few weeks later, two people at the table, Sasha and Marcus, ended up arguing about it through most of dessert. (1) Sasha said the payment, whatever else it was, wasn't rational: by the time Dan was standing in the gas station, the ride had already happened, the driver would never learn whether he paid, and handing over $200 changed nothing about the previous forty minutes — it was money spent on nothing. (2) Marcus said this gets the order of explanation backwards. The driver was explicitly trying to screen out people who reason the way Sasha just did, so if Dan really were someone who'd walk away once safe, and the driver was any judge of character, Dan would still be standing on the shoulder of the highway; whatever you want to say about the moment of paying, it was Dan's being disposed to pay that got him the ride at all, and it seems odd to call the disposition rational while calling the very act that expresses it irrational. (3) Sasha answered that this only shows the disposition was worth having beforehand, not that paying was rational once he was safe — she compared it to a poker player whose standing policy of never bluffing on the river earns him more calls over the course of a night, even though on any specific hand where bluffing would win the pot, the policy isn't what maximizes that hand's return. (4) Marcus replied that the analogy undercuts her. Nobody thinks the disciplined player is being irrational on the hands where the policy "costs" him, because the whole value of a policy is that you don't reopen it hand by hand — a policy you'd quietly abandon whenever it stopped paying off in the moment was never really a policy, which means the trust it's supposed to earn, whether from other players or from the driver who'd picked Dan up in the first place, was never really earned either. (5) Sasha then tried a different angle: maybe none of this matters, because Dan had simply made a promise, and promises create a moral reason to keep them independent of any self-interested calculation. Marcus said that might well be true, but that it changes the subject — the question on the table had been whether self-interested rationality alone recommends paying, and swapping in a moral argument once the rational-choice argument got uncomfortable isn't the same as winning it.
