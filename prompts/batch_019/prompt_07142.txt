Write a dialogue, set just after a seminar on conditionals, between two philosophers of language who disagree about the semantics of indicative conditionals in light of Lewis's triviality results. One character holds that a conditional such as "if A, C" expresses an ordinary proposition with truth conditions, and defends Stalnaker's response to the triviality proof: that the proposition a conditional expresses can shift with context, so that no single proposition need satisfy the Equation, P(A > C) = P(C | A), for every probability function the proof requires it to hold for. The other character denies that conditionals are truth-apt at all, siding with Adams and Edgington in holding that their assertability simply tracks conditional credence, and argues that letting content float with credence in this way only relocates the triviality problem rather than solving it. Have the propositionalist press the other on how a truth-valueless conditional is supposed to behave when embedded under negation or nested inside a further conditional, and have the non-propositionalist press back on whether making semantic content this sensitive to credence is anything more than an ad hoc rescue. The two should respond directly to each other's specific moves across several exchanges, and neither should concede more than their position actually requires.
