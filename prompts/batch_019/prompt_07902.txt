Classmate's answer:
"The non-identity problem, discussed by Derek Parfit in Reasons and Persons, comes up in cases where a choice determines not just how well off a future person will be, but which particular person will exist at all. Parfit's own example is a fourteen-year-old girl who decides to have a child now instead of waiting several years. If she waits, she will conceive a different child later on, one who would have a better start in life; but if she goes ahead now, the resulting child, though worse off in various ways, still has a life worth living. The puzzle is that the child she actually has cannot say her decision made him worse off, because if she had waited, he specifically would never have existed at all — a different child would have been born instead. So although we want to say she did something wrong, person-affecting views, which hold that an act can only be wrong if it makes some particular person worse off, seem to have no grounds for objecting to her choice. The same structure shows up in policy debates, such as choosing to rapidly deplete natural resources rather than conserve them, since the individuals who will be alive several generations from now depend on which policy is chosen, and so cannot claim to have been harmed by whichever policy is actually adopted. Parfit's own solution to this puzzle is the Repugnant Conclusion, which he uses to argue that a much larger population whose lives are barely worth living can be impersonally better than a smaller population of very happy people, so that we should evaluate such outcomes impersonally rather than by whether they are worse for specific individuals. Because of this, most philosophers have simply rejected the non-identity problem and concluded that our intuition that the girl did something wrong must be the thing that's mistaken."
