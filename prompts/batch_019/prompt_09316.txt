I don't have any formal background in math or philosophy, but over the last year or so I've fallen fairly deep down an AI-safety rabbit hole -- a lot of Robert Miles videos, old LessWrong posts, and a few of the original MIRI technical write-ups -- and there's one piece of the "embedded agency" material I keep circling back to without ever feeling like I've actually got it: the so-called Löbian obstacle to an agent trusting a future or successor version of itself. My rough understanding is that if you want an agent to only self-modify, or build a successor running on the same kind of reasoning, when it has actually verified that the successor's actions are safe, you'd like some general principle along the lines of "if my future self can prove an action is fine, then it's fine." But apparently Löb's theorem says you can't just have that as a free-standing rule: either the system already had to be able to prove the specific fact directly, or granting the general rule blows the whole proof system up so that it can prove anything at all. I even came across a toy version of this involving an agent that just has to press a button once before some deadline, which somehow ends up "proving" that it's fine to procrastinate forever.

The part I can't get past just memorizing the definitions to see is why this counts as a distinctly embedded-agency problem, rather than a generic paradox of self-referential trust that would turn up whether or not the agent is "embedded" in anything. Could you walk me through the gut-level reason a system generally can't get away with asserting "provable implies true" about its own future reasoning, and how that connects to the broader embedded-agency point that an agent can't step outside its own skin to verify itself the way a "Cartesian" agent sitting cleanly outside its environment supposedly could? I'm not looking for a full formal walk-through of the proof -- I've tried, and mostly bounced off it -- but if there's a clean way to see why softened versions of the idea (bounded reasoners, probabilistic reflection, and so on) manage to dodge the sharpest form of the obstacle, I'd be glad to get a sense of that too.
