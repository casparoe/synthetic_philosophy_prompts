This term I am taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and this week's session was devoted to Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against the possibility of true, or ultimate, moral responsibility. As I understood the reading, the argument turns on three moves: (i) what you do on any occasion flows from the way you are, mentally speaking, from your beliefs, values, and dispositions; (ii) to be truly responsible for what you do, you would therefore have to be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the relevant respects; and (iii) you cannot be truly responsible for being the way you are, since any earlier act by which you might have shaped your own character would itself have had to issue from some still-earlier way you already were, so that securing true responsibility for your present self would require completing an impossible regress of self-authorship — you would have to be, in effect, a cause of yourself, a causa sui. Strawson takes this to hold independently of whether determinism is true, since even a chancy, indeterministic contribution to one's character is not something one has authored either. What struck several of us in the seminar is that Strawson does not confine this conclusion to human beings or other causally embedded creatures: if I am remembering correctly, he explicitly extends it to any possible being whatever, including a maximally powerful, self-sufficient being answerable to nothing outside itself, on the grounds that even such a being could not have chosen the character or values from which its own first act of self-determination proceeded.

It is this "not even a god could satisfy it" extension that I would like your help with, in the form of an argument rather than a summary. Please argue for the position that this very universality gives us good reason to reject premise (ii) — that being truly responsible for what one does requires being truly responsible for the way one is — rather than to accept Strawson's conclusion that true responsibility is simply impossible, full stop. The thought I would like you to develop and defend as rigorously as you can is that a condition on responsibility that literally no possible being could ever meet, not even under the most favorable conceivable circumstances, is thereby disqualified as a genuine condition on responsibility, rather than being a demanding standard that we, tragically, just happen to fail to meet. In making this case, please engage directly with the obvious rejoinder that unsatisfiability alone does not discredit a requirement — some epistemologists, after all, accept demanding standards of justification or knowledge that may be extremely hard or even impossible fully to satisfy, without concluding that those standards were never really in force — and explain what, if anything, makes the case of moral responsibility relevantly different.
