here's a passage from Bentham's unpublished essay on "paederasty" (written around 1785, arguing that consensual sex between men shouldn't be a crime):

"As to any primary mischief, it is evident that it produces no pain in anyone. On the contrary it produces pleasure, and that a pleasure which, by their perverted taste, is by this supposition preferred to that pleasure which is in general reputed the greatest. The partners are both willing. If either of them be unwilling, the act is not that which we have here in view: it is an offence totally different in its nature of effects: it is a personal injury; it is a kind of rape. As to any secondary mischief, it produces not any pain of apprehension. For what is there in it for any body to be afraid of? By the supposition, those only are the objects of it who choose to be so, who find a pleasure, for so it seems they do, in being so."

I get the gist but not really how "primary" vs "secondary" mischief functions as actual technical machinery in his penal theory more broadly - where's that distinction coming from in his system and what job is it doing here exactly? Also, he apparently argues elsewhere in the same essay that adultery is worse than this, mainly because of illegitimate kids - does the same framework explain that consistantly or is he kind of stacking the deck to get the conclusion he wants? can you walk me through the whole argument here, not just paraphrase it.
