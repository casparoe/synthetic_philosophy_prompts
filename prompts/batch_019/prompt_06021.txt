Retired physicist here, only got serious about philosophy after I stopped working, so bare with me. I've been reading about Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's old thought experiment with two symmetric spheres in an empty universe. What I can't wrap my head around is this "weak discernibility" move some people invoke to save the principle: the idea that two things can still count as discernible if some irreflexive relation holds between them (like "opposite spin to"), even if they share every intrinsic property. Apparently this gets used both for Black's spheres and for fermions in a singlet state. Is that actually doing real philosophical work, or just relabeling the brute fact that there happen to be two of them? And is the quantum case really analogous to Black's, given the particles don't even have definite individual spins to begin with — or is that a disanalogy people tend to gloss over? Would love the underlying intuition here, not just the formal definitions.
