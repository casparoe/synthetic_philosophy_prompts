It is a commonplace on rationalist blogs and forums to explain "weakness of will" via hyperbolic discounting: because our discount rate is steeper for delays close to the present than for equally long delays further out, an agent's ranking of a smaller-sooner reward over a larger-later one can flip as the sooner reward draws near, producing behavior that looks like backsliding on one's own resolutions (skipping the gym, breaking the diet, and so on). But Aristotle's original puzzle of akrasia, later sharpened by Davidson, concerned something stricter: an agent who judges, at the very moment of acting, that one course of action is best, and yet knowingly does otherwise — a synchronic conflict between judgment and action, not merely a change of preference over time. Please argue that the hyperbolic-discounting story, whatever its virtues as a predictive model of intertemporal choice, does not actually explain akrasia in this stricter sense, and in fact quietly changes the subject from a puzzle about synchronic irrationality to a considerably tamer story about diachronic preference reversal. Be sure to address the natural rejoinder that talk of "judging one action best while doing another" was always just a confused folk-psychological gloss on time-inconsistent preferences, so that no further explanandum remains once the discounting model is on the table.
