I am several chapters into a novel set between Zurich and Berlin in the years 1912 to 1915, narrated by a fictional junior assistant to Einstein during the period when he was working his way toward the final field equations of general relativity. In the scene I am currently drafting, my narrator, who has read her Leibniz, challenges Einstein about the hole argument that led him to abandon full general covariance for nearly two years before he returned to it in November 1915. Her claim, put rather pointedly, is that his hole argument is not really a new discovery at all, merely Leibniz's old shift argument against Newton and Clarke translated into the language of differential manifolds. I like the exchange dramatically, but I do not know the philosophy of physics well enough to judge whether her charge is fair, and I would rather Einstein's reply not be a strawman.

Could you compare the hole argument, in the form in which philosophers of physics later revived it as a challenge to manifold substantivalism, with Leibniz's shift argument against Newtonian absolute space? I am especially interested in whether the two arguments really trade on the same underlying principle, what work the Principle of Sufficient Reason is doing in Leibniz's version that it may or may not be doing in Einstein's, and whether a substantivalist has anything available in reply to the hole argument that plays the role Newton's bucket and rotating globes played in reply to Leibniz. Then, in whatever answer you think Einstein could honestly and plausibly give my narrator: in what ways, if any, is the hole argument more than the shift argument in modern dress? Does it actually establish something the older argument could not, or is my narrator basically right to be unimpressed?
