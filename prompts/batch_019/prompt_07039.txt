I'm developing a unit on practical reason for my seminar this term, and a real dispute within my own family has been nagging at me as a possible case study, so I'd like your take on it before I decide whether to use it in class. My brother-in-law, Tom, agreed two years ago—back when he and his business partner, Ray, were still on excellent terms—to co-sign a substantial bank loan that allowed Ray to expand his restaurant. Tom and Ray have since had a serious falling-out over an unrelated matter and have not spoken in over a year; Tom insists that whatever loyalty or affection he once felt toward Ray has simply drained away, entirely and, as far as he can tell, permanently. The loan is now up for renewal, and under the terms of their original arrangement, Ray is asking Tom to co-sign again.

My sister thinks the answer is obvious: Tom gave his word, Ray restructured his finances in reliance on it, and reneging now could ruin Ray financially. Tom doesn't dispute any of that, but maintains that these would be excellent reasons for someone who still cared, even a little, about Ray or about his own reputation for keeping commitments—and since he cares about neither anymore, and no amount of reflection brings any of that feeling back, he can't see what reason, as opposed to what other people would prefer, he actually has to sign.

Since I'm the resident philosopher in the family, my sister has asked me to settle who's right, and I confess I don't have a ready answer—I keep suspecting the disagreement runs deeper than the usual story about why promises bind. Does Tom's total loss of concern really dissolve whatever reason the original promise generated, leaving at most an obligation other people can hold him to but not one he himself has? Or does my sister have the better view, on which the reason to honor the agreement survives regardless of what Tom currently feels? I'd appreciate it if you could work out the strongest version of each side before landing on a verdict—I'd like to be able to give my sister something more considered than "reasonable people disagree."
