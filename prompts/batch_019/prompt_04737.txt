Last weekend, a friend of mine — she does data analysis for a public-health research group — and I got into a long argument about how to interpret Simpson's paradox, and neither of us walked away convinced. Her position, which she said reflects how she was trained to think about confounding, was that Simpson's paradox has essentially one moral: whenever an association reverses between the pooled data and every subgroup, you should always trust the subgroup-level, stratified result, because the pooled figure is just an artifact of unequal group sizes. I objected that this can't be the whole story. I recalled reading somewhere — possibly a piece popularizing Judea Pearl's work on causal graphs, though I'm honestly not certain of the source anymore — that whether stratifying gives you the right answer depends on what kind of variable you are stratifying on. My half-remembered claim was that if the variable defining the subgroups is a mediator — one that sits on the causal pathway between the variable whose effect you care about and the outcome, rather than a common cause of both — then stratifying on it can destroy the very effect you are trying to measure, so that it is the pooled, aggregated data that gets the causal story right, not the disaggregated one. She doubted such a case could really arise, or at least not in a way that looks like a genuine reversal rather than mere attenuation toward no association.

I would like your help settling this. Could you construct a concrete numerical example that would make the point? I am picturing an explicit small table of counts, not just a verbal sketch, and ideally something other than the standard UC Berkeley admissions or kidney-stone-treatment setups, since we have both already seen those. The example should involve some treatment or intervention X that shows a positive association with an outcome Y in the aggregated data, but a reversed, negative association inside every stratum of a third variable Z, where Z plays the role of a mediator of X's effect on Y rather than a confounder, so that it is the aggregated figures, not the stratified ones, that reflect the true causal effect of X on Y. It would also help if you could spell out, using a simple causal diagram or an equally explicit causal story, exactly why controlling for Z would be the analytical mistake here, since that is the crux on which my friend remained skeptical.
