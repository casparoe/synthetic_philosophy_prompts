A few weeks ago I had a long, fairly circular argument with my housemate about whether it even makes sense to ask an aligned AI system to represent "everyone's values." He thinks the phrase is close to meaningless; I think it names the actual question the alignment project eventually has to answer, however it gets dressed up. Neither of us moved the other an inch, but the argument left me primed for it, so when a related dispute broke out a few nights later between two people in the AI alignment reading group I sit in on, Dana and Theo, I paid unusually close attention and wrote down roughly how it went, because I still cannot decide who came out ahead.

The group had just read a piece arguing that an aligned system should treat people's values as inputs to be aggregated rather than have its designers settle on a substantive ethical stance on everyone's behalf. (1) Dana defended this as the only non-paternalistic option available given real disagreement: don't impose a theory, aggregate everyone's preferences through some fair procedure instead. (2) Theo objected that "fair procedure" is doing unearned work, since Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that once there are three or more options on the table, no rule for turning individual rankings into a group ranking can satisfy even minimal conditions — working for any pattern of preferences, respecting unanimity, ignoring irrelevant alternatives, and having no single dictator — all at once; so "just aggregate the preferences" isn't a neutral default, it's a decision to quietly drop one of those conditions. (3) Dana replied that Arrow's theorem governs bare rankings, which isn't what reward models actually work with; once cardinal scores are on the table and everyone's preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, Harsanyi's aggregation theorem shows that any social utility function respecting unanimity must be a weighted sum of individual utilities, so the setting alignment actually operates in is exactly the one where the paradox dissolves. (4) Theo said that only relocates the problem: Harsanyi's theorem is silent on what the weights are or how to put different people's utility on a common scale, which is precisely the contested choice in question, and it quietly assumes everyone shares the same beliefs about uncertain outcomes, which they manifestly don't; and even setting all that aside, Sen showed that protecting even a minimal amount of individual liberty — letting each person's preference decide at least the matters that are truly their own business — is flatly inconsistent with honoring unanimous preferences, so an aggregator built to respect unanimity has already sided against that kind of liberty, whether its designers notice or not. (5) Dana fell back to a narrower claim: maybe neutrality across every conceivable value system is impossible, but nobody serious was asking for that; the real target is something closer to Rawls's overlapping consensus, where you look for principles the system can act on that people holding different reasonable views could each endorse for their own reasons, setting aside views that are not reasonable to begin with — a more modest ambition, but a coherent and reachable one. (6) Theo answered that this just relocates the argument one last time: the entire disagreement between, say, a strict traditionalist and a secular egalitarian is usually a disagreement about who counts as reasonable, so whoever ends up drawing that line — a philosopher, a lab, a policy team — has made a first-order ethical decision and then relabeled it a neutral background constraint.
