Question: In 2–3 paragraphs, explain how Molinism's doctrine of divine middle knowledge is supposed to let God be providentially in control of history while creatures still act with libertarian free will. Be sure to explain where middle knowledge fits relative to God's natural knowledge and free knowledge, and what a "feasible world" is. Then explain the grounding objection to middle knowledge and say whether you think it's a serious problem for the Molinist.

Answer: Molinism, developed by the 16th century Jesuit Luis de Molina as a response to Calvinist ideas about predestination, tries to fix the tension between God's providence and human freedom by giving God a third kind of knowledge that sits between his natural knowledge and his free knowledge, called middle knowledge. Natural knowledge is God's knowledge of all necessary truths and every possible world he could create, and free knowledge is his knowledge of the actual world once he's decided to create it, including what will actually happen in it. Middle knowledge sits logically after natural knowledge but before God's decree to create, and it's made up of counterfactuals of freedom: truths about what every possible free creature would do in any circumstance it could be placed in. Since these truths aren't up to God (he just knows them, he doesn't make them true), Molinists call this prevolitional knowledge. Using it, God picks which world to actualize out of all the feasible worlds, meaning the worlds that are consistent with whichever counterfactuals are actually true, instead of choosing from literally every logically possible world, since some possible worlds (ones where a creature does the opposite of what the true counterfactual says it would do) simply aren't options for him. This is how Molinists think God can plan out history in meticulous detail while people are still the ones freely making the choices.

The classic objection to this is the grounding objection, first developed by Alvin Plantinga: since counterfactuals of freedom are contingent and libertarian freedom means nothing about a creature's nature or circumstances necessitates the choice, there's nothing that makes "if S were in C, S would freely do A" true rather than the counterfactual where S does the opposite. There's no truthmaker for it, especially since the creature in question doesn't even exist yet at the point when the counterfactual is supposedly already true. Personally I think this objection basically sinks Molinism, because if there's nothing grounding the counterfactuals then God doesn't really know them, he's just guessing, and "knowledge based on a guess" isn't knowledge at all.
