I keep coming back to the Hart–Fuller exchange of 1958 for a piece on how legal systems handle the wreckage of their own past regimes — especially Hart's "grudge informer" case, in which a postwar German court had to decide whether a wife who had denounced her husband under a Nazi-era statute could still be punished, and his argument that the honest fix was a frankly retroactive law, not a fiction that the Nazi statute had never been law at all. Fuller's reply — that a regime ruling through secret, contradictory, or unevenly enforced decrees forfeits any claim to have made "law," regardless of what it said — struck me as doing real philosophical work, not just restating that unjust laws are no laws.

Write a short story, around 1,000 words, set in an invented country and era (not Nazi Germany, not the Berlin Wall), in which a minor legal official — a clerk, a magistrate, a records-keeper — must decide how to treat one specific rule left behind by a regime that has just fallen. Anchor the dilemma in a concrete case rather than a debate or speech, and write it so that a careful reader could come away persuaded of either Hart's answer or Fuller's.
