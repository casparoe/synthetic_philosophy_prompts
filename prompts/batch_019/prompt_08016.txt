So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole this week after a podcast mentioned, in passing, that Hobbes basically "discovered" geometry as a middle-aged man and it completely rerouted his whole philosophical project. The story (from Aubrey's Brief Lives) is that Hobbes was already around 40 when he happened to see a copy of Euclid lying open in some gentlemans library, at the Pythagorean theorem of all places, read the proposition, said something like "this is impossible," then worked backward through all the propositions it depended on until he was fully convinced - and apparently that experience made him fall completely in love with geometry. From then on he was obsessed with the idea that geometry is the one area of thought where people don't have permanent, pointless, civil-war-causing disagreements, precisely because everyone nails down their definitions before reasoning from them, and that if you could just do the same thing for ethics and politics (define your terms rigorously, then deduce everything else) you'd get that same certainty and put an end to needless conflict. Which is apparently part of why Leviathan and his other big books start with such painstaking definitions of basic terms like matter, motion, and sense before they ever get to politics - he wanted the same stepwise certainty for civil philosophy that Euclid gets for triangles.

Here's the part that really got me though: at basically the same time he was building this reputation as the guy who wanted to out-Euclid Euclid for politics, Hobbes spent the last two-plus decades of his life in an increasingly embarrassing public fight with the mathematician John Wallis, because he was convinced he'd squared the circle (plus a couple of the other classic unsolved construction problems) using his own "rigorous" method, and he was just flatly wrong about it, in ways Wallis demonstrated clearly and repeatedly. Hobbes never once backed down - he kept publishing revised "proofs" that were also wrong, and eventually just declared that Wallis's whole algebraic style of doing geometry was illegitimate trickery rather than admit a single mistake. So the same guy, with the same underlying faith in rigorous deduction from clear definitions, produced both his most famous and enduringly-debated political philosophy and a decades-long case study in a smart person being confidently, publicly, permanently wrong.

This feels super relevant to a pattern I see a lot in rationalist-adjacent spaces (mine included - a few of us have an ongoing group chat where we keep trying to hash out a shared ethical framework from a handful of agreed-on axioms, "utilitarian-ish," and keep getting stuck arguing about the axioms themselves): the recurring hope that if you formalize a messy domain like ethics or disagreement or group decision-making rigorously enough, the persistent disputes just dissolve the way they supposedly do in math. So I'm curious what you'd say are the most important implications of Hobbes's views on geometry, and of how that whole story actually played out for him personally, for how much weight we should put on "formalize it and the arguments go away" as a strategy - whether for ethics broadly, or smaller-scale stuff like my group chat's project.
