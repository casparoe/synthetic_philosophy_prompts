I'm a third-year Philosophy, Politics, and Economics student, and this term's Philosophy of Economics seminar has spent several weeks on revealed preference theory: the view, central to modern consumer theory, that an agent's preferences — and the "utility function" sometimes said to represent them — should be defined entirely by a consistent pattern of choices made under budget constraints, rather than by anything introspectible. We worked through the consistency conditions, WARP and SARP, that let you infer a preference ordering from choice data alone, and we spent a whole session on the standing worry that a choice pattern's being formally rationalizable by some utility function tells you nothing about whether that function tracks anyone's well-being, once framing effects, adaptive preferences, and plain weakness of will are allowed to affect what gets chosen.

That seminar has turned out to be strangely relevant to my other role on campus: I sit on the Student Activity Fee Board, which this spring has a one-time $40,000 surplus to allocate between two rival proposals — expanded evening hours at the counseling center, or a longer late-night dining and shuttle schedule. To settle the question, the Board commissioned a ranked-choice survey of the whole student body and, separately, pulled two years of anonymized usage logs (ID-swipe records for dining and the shuttle, appointment data for counseling) showing how students have actually spent their own limited time and flex-dollars on the services already on offer. The two sources disagree sharply: the survey ranks mental-health programming as students' clear top priority, while the usage logs show existing counseling slots chronically under-filled next to dining and shuttle services running consistently over capacity. About half the Board wants to fund whatever the survey ranks highest, on the grounds that people are the best reporters of what they value; the other half wants to fund whatever the logs show, on the grounds that a real trade-off against a real constraint is the only kind of evidence about preference — and therefore utility — worth taking seriously.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Wednesday's vote? It should recommend one specific allocation of the $40,000 (a split is acceptable, but only if you argue for that particular split rather than offering it as a way of dodging the question), and it should be explicit about what work the concept of "utility" is actually doing in each side's argument. Please also represent the losing side's position well enough that its proponents on the Board would recognize their own argument in your reasoning rather than feel straw-manned.
