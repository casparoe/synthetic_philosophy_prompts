Write a short story about a research team that has just obtained the first detailed anatomical scans of a newly discovered alien organism. The data reveal a conspicuous, energetically costly structure that the organism clearly puts to good use, and the team splits over how to explain it: one scientist reasons backward from the structure's present usefulness to a confident account of the selective pressure that must have built it, while a second — invoking Gould and Lewontin's critique of the adaptationist programme — calls this a textbook "just-so story," arguing that current utility proves nothing about origins and that the structure looks just as plausibly like a spandrel: a byproduct of some unrelated developmental or structural constraint that was only later co-opted for use. Tell the story through a third team member who must decide, under real deadline and funding pressure, what the official announcement will say about why the structure exists, and let the story register the fact that nothing available to the team can decisively settle the question. Avoid having any character simply lecture the others; the disagreement should surface through what the characters do and notice, not through exposition.
