A friend of mine and I belong to a small reading group on classical American pragmatism, and after last week's session on Peirce and James we ended up arguing about their respective theories of truth for most of the walk home, then picked the argument back up by text for another hour after we'd both said goodnight. Her position, roughly, is that Peirce's talk of a "final opinion" that inquiry is fated to converge on is just a more solemn, more metaphysically encumbered way of saying what James says plainly when he writes that "the true is the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief, and good, too, for definite, assignable reasons" -- that the two men are gesturing at the same thing, and Peirce simply disliked James's breezier packaging of it. I pushed back, arguing that Peirce ties truth to an idealized, communal endpoint of inquiry in a way that James's more individualist, moment-by-moment story about what "works" for a given believer does not, and that this is a substantive difference and not merely one of tone. When she asked me to say exactly what work the "final opinion" is doing that "good in the way of belief" couldn't also do, though, I didn't have a crisp answer ready, and we left it there.

This morning I went back to what I think is actually the load-bearing passage on my side of the argument, from Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (Popular Science Monthly, January 1878), where he raises an objection to his own definition of truth and reality and tries to answer it. Here it is in full:

"No modification of the point of view taken, no selection of other facts for study, no natural bent of mind even, can enable a man to escape the predestinate opinion. This great hope is embodied in the conception of truth and reality. The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real. That is the way I would explain reality.

But it may be said that this view is directly opposed to the abstract definition which we have given of reality, inasmuch as it makes the characters of the real depend on what is ultimately thought about them. But the answer to this is that, on the one hand, reality is independent, not necessarily of thought in general, but only of what you or I or any finite number of men may think about it; and that, on the other hand, though the object of the final opinion depends on what that opinion is, yet what that opinion is does not depend on what you or I or any man thinks. Our perversity and that of others may indefinitely postpone the settlement of opinion; it might even conceivably cause an arbitrary proposition to be universally accepted as long as the human race should last. Yet even that would not change the nature of the belief, which alone could be the result of investigation carried sufficiently far; and if, after the extinction of our race, another should arise with faculties and disposition for investigation, that true opinion must be the one which they would ultimately come to. 'Truth crushed to earth shall rise again,' and the opinion which would finally result from investigation does not depend on how anybody may actually think. But the reality of that which is real does depend on the real fact that investigation is destined to lead, at last, if continued long enough, to a belief in it."

Could you help me actually work through this? First, state as precisely as you can what objection Peirce thinks he is answering, and then walk through how the distinction between being independent of "what you or I ... think" and being independent of "thought in general" is supposed to defeat it; I can parse the sentences one at a time but keep losing the thread of why that particular distinction, rather than some other, does the job he needs it to do. Second, the aside about the human race going extinct and being succeeded by other investigators suggests Peirce wants the "final opinion" to be some kind of counterfactual or dispositional fact rather than a straightforward prediction about what will actually happen; if that is the right way to read him, tell me whether this move actually rescues his account from circularity or merely relocates the problem. Third, now that you have gone through it carefully, tell me whether you think my friend or I had the better of the argument: is Peirce doing real philosophical work here that James's formula can't also do, or is the disagreement between them mostly verbal? I would rather you defend your own reading of the two of them than just summarize how commentators usually divide up the dispute.
