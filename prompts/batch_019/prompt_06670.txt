I'm drafting a short paper on the Epicurean case against the badness of death, and before it goes to colleagues I'd like an outside pair of eyes on one move in particular. The occasion was a work-in-progress session last month: a junior colleague defended a Nagel-style deprivation account of why death is bad, and someone in the audience pressed the old Lucretian symmetry worry against her. I thought I had a tidy way of shoring up her position, but the more I write it out the less sure I am that it actually works, and I'd rather have it judged by someone other than myself before I take it any further. Here is the relevant section.

Epicurus's argument trades on the thought that so long as we exist death is not yet present, and once it is present we no longer exist, so there is never a time at which its badness could be borne by anyone. Lucretius sharpens this into a symmetry argument: the stretch of non-existence after I die mirrors the stretch of non-existence before I was born, and since the latter troubles no one, consistency demands that the former trouble no one either. The reply that seems closest to Nagel's own denies that the two stretches are really parallel. I might have gone on living for another twenty years, and this would have been the very same person, me, merely continuing further; but I could not have been born twenty years earlier, since who I am depends essentially on my particular biological origin — that egg, that sperm, that moment of conception. "Earlier birth" does not name a possibility for me at all; at best it names someone else's life. Because the extra years after death are years I could have had and the extra years before birth are not, only the former count as a genuine deprivation, and our asymmetric attitudes are vindicated rather than exposed as inconsistent.

What worries me is that this move treats biological origin as a fixed, dateless event, when it is nothing of the kind. Take a cryopreserved embryo: the same zygote, with no change whatsoever in genetic origin, could be implanted and brought to term now or twenty years from now. If sameness of origin is what secures sameness of person across possible birth-dates, this case seems to show that a person could, after all, have been born considerably earlier than they actually were while remaining numerically the same person — precisely the possibility Nagel's reply needs to rule out. The obvious rejoinder is that the timing of implantation is itself partly constitutive of an individual's origin, so that shifting it shifts which possible person results, in which case the embryo case never gets off the ground. But then I don't see why the same should not hold, symmetrically, of a life's terminus: why treat when a life ends as biographically negotiable while treating when it begins as metaphysically frozen?

I'd welcome real pushback rather than polite agreement, particularly on three things: whether the embryo case actually threatens the asymmetry or just trades on an equivocation over "origin"; whether there is a principled way to hold the origin/terminus distinction fixed that survives the case; and whether I have represented Nagel's actual view fairly or built a straw version of it for the sake of having something to knock down. And if the whole origin-essentialist detour turns out to be beside the point — if the deeper trouble is simply that deprivation accounts need an existing subject to be deprived, and death is precisely what removes the subject — I would like to be told that too.
