A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about Aquinas on tyrannicide and never quite settled it. My friend's shorthand was that "Aquinas thought it was fine to kill tyrants," but his actual view seems more layered to me: in De Regno, and again in his remarks on sedition in the Summa, he distinguishes a ruler who never held legitimate title to rule — a usurper, whom a private person may resist or even kill, absent recourse to a higher authority — from a ruler who acquired power legitimately but has grown oppressive in exercising it. In that second case, Aquinas insists that removal is a matter for the public authority or community that installed the ruler, not for private individuals acting on their own judgment, partly because he feared that licensing private tyrannicide would give the ambitious cover to murder decent rulers on false pretexts.

Here is the case that split us. Imagine a head of state who wins power in a genuinely free election, then hollows out the courts, jails independent journalists, and rigs or cancels every subsequent election to stay in office indefinitely; and imagine that a foreign government, acting alone and in total secrecy, with no UN authorization or other recognized public sanction, has this leader killed in a drone strike. Working from Aquinas's actual principles rather than the popular gloss on them: would he treat such a leader as a usurper, on the grounds that the mandate which made his rule legitimate has been subverted from within, or as a legitimate authority removable only through due public process? And should a covert strike ordered by a foreign power be judged by the standard Aquinas applied to a lone citizen's dagger, or does the change of actor change the analysis? Tell me what verdict you think Aquinas's own principles commit him to, and why.
