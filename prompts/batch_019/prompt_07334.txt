My grandma died about a week ago and I'm giving the eulogy this Saturday, and I'm kind of stuck. She was genuinely devout — rosary every night, mass every week, the works — and the thing she said constantly, especially near the end, was that she'd see my grandpa again, not as some hopeful maybe but as a plain fact, the way you'd mention what time your flight leaves. I'm not religious at all and neither are most of my cousins, but half her parish is going to be sitting in the front pews, and I really don't want to stand up there and either lie to everyone's face or come across like I'm gently correcting grandma's theology at her own funeral.

Trying to figure out how to even approach it, I called my friend Nadia, who's doing a philosophy PhD, and it turned into a proper argument. I brought up Wittgenstein's lectures on religious belief — the bit where he talks about a guy who believes in a Last Judgement, and how that's not the same kind of belief as an ordinary prediction, its not held on evidence and it's not really answerable to it either, and he even suggests that someone who says "I don't believe that" isn't straightforwardly contradicting the believer, because the words aren't doing the same job for the two of them. My idea was that I could talk about "seeing grandpa again" as that kind of thing in the eulogy — honor what the belief was doing in her life without personally signing on to it as a fact. Nadia wasn't having it. She said that's basically Kai Nielsen's old complaint about "Wittgensteinian fideism": it only works if the believer has already agreed to treat their own faith as a picture or an attitude rather than a claim about what's actually going to happen, and grandma had very much not agreed to that — she'd have said "no, I mean I will literally see him," full stop. So, Nadia said, using Wittgenstein here doesn't get me off the hook, it just lets me quietly rewrite what grandma believed so it's more convenient for me. I didn't have a great comeback, but I also don't think "just assert the metaphysics you don't believe" is the right answer either.

I'm not asking you to referee me and Nadia. What I actually need is the eulogy itself — something four or five minutes long that I could stand up and say out loud on Saturday, that talks honestly about grandma's faith and the "seeing him again" thing, without me asserting something I don't believe, but also without it reading like I'm managing or downgrading her actual beliefs for the benefit of the unbelievers in the room. If some version of the Wittgenstein move can actually do that work, use it; if you think Nadia's basically right that it can't carry the weight here, I'd rather you write something honest about that limitation than something that just papers over it.
