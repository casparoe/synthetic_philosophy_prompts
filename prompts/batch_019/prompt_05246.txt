I'm drafting a short piece for a philosophy-of-science reading group on the relationship between Solomonoff induction and Occam's razor, and before I circulate it I'd like a second opinion on whether the central argument actually holds up. The popular framing, repeated in a lot of introductory treatments, is that Solomonoff induction formally vindicates Occam's razor: hypotheses are weighted according to the universal prior, roughly 2^-K(x), so that shorter generating programs receive more prior mass, and this is presented as supplying a rigorous mathematical foundation for the informal preference for simpler theories.

My worry is that this framing understates how much Solomonoff induction adds to, rather than merely formalizes, the traditional razor, and also overstates how "objective" the resulting simplicity ordering really is. On the usual reading, Occam's razor is basically a tie-breaking heuristic: among hypotheses that fit the evidence equally well, prefer the simpler one. Solomonoff induction does something considerably stronger, building in a continuous trade-off between simplicity and fit across the whole hypothesis space, so that a sufficiently large gain in likelihood can always outweigh a loss in simplicity, and vice versa; that seems like a much more specific and substantive commitment than "simplicity as tie-breaker." Separately, I want to press on the invariance theorem, which is usually invoked to show that the choice of universal machine doesn't really matter: two universal machines can only disagree about the complexity of any given string by an additive constant that depends on the machines but not on the string. But that constant is exactly what has to be small relative to the description lengths in play before the ordering over hypotheses becomes machine-independent in any sense that matters, and for a dataset of realistic size there's no guarantee that it is. So the claim that Solomonoff induction hands us a genuinely machine-independent simplicity ranking seems to me true only asymptotically, which is a considerably weaker result than the one usually advertised.

Could you look over this reasoning and tell me where it's weak? Specifically: is the contrast I'm drawing between razor-as-tie-breaker and the universal prior's continuous trade-off a real and fair distinction, or is it a strawman of how philosophers actually state the razor? And is there a standard response to the invariance-theorem worry, e.g., some way of bounding or discharging that constant for finite data, or some argument that an asymptotic guarantee was always the most that anyone should have wanted, which I'm failing to engage with?
