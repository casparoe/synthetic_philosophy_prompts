I'm taking a senior seminar on Aristotle's practical philosophy this term, where we've spent most of the semester working closely through the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics, and our final paper is supposed to develop a narrow, text-based argument rather than survey secondary literature. I've decided to write on Aristotle's treatment of money and exchange, and I think I've noticed a tension between two passages that none of our assigned readings mention. When I raised it briefly in office hours my professor seemed interested but didn't have time to dig into it with me, so I'd like an outside sanity check before I commit the rest of my page limit to it.

Here is the argument as it stands. In Nicomachean Ethics V.5, Aristotle solves the problem of how to make radically dissimilar goods, like shoes and a house, commensurable for the sake of just exchange: he says it is "demand" or "need" (chreia) that functions as the real common measure holding the whole exchange together, and that money (nomisma) is only a conventional stand-in for that need, adopted because need itself cannot always be carried around or stored. Taken on its own, that looks like a fairly sophisticated demand-based theory of value, closer to something a subjectivist economist like Menger could accept than to any theory of intrinsic worth. But when Aristotle turns, in Politics I.8-10, to condemning retail trade and especially usury (tokos) as the most unnatural mode of acquiring wealth, the argument seems to depend on a completely different, quasi-naturalistic picture of money as a sterile instrument whose sole legitimate function is to facilitate the exchange of use-values; interest is monstrous, on this view, because it makes money itself "breed" more money, contrary to the purpose for which it was introduced. My claim is that these two accounts don't sit comfortably together: if money is really just a conventional proxy for need, nothing in the Book V analysis explains why need itself (and so money that merely represents it) could not be lent, priced, and made to grow over time; the condemnation of interest has to be imported from outside the exchange theory. I also want to bring in Marx's well-known footnote in the first chapter of Capital, where he credits Aristotle with being the first to see that exchange presupposes some common substance among commodities, yet argues Aristotle could not identify that substance because a society built on slave labor could not yet conceive of human labor-power as fundamentally equal. I'd like to use this to argue that Aristotle's chreia-based solution is a principled stopping point rather than a confusion, which would let me push back against commentators who treat his economic thought as simply inconsistent.

Could you comment on this before I turn it into a full draft? I'm especially interested in: (1) whether my reading of NE V.5 and Politics I.8-10 is accurate, or whether I'm eliding some distinction Aristotle relies on to keep the two discussions consistent; (2) whether bringing in Marx's footnote strengthens the paper or risks anachronism and dilutes what would otherwise be an internal critique; and (3) whether the tension I've identified is a genuinely interesting point worth building a paper around, or a fairly well-known observation that I should cite rather than present as my own.
