A former PhD student of mine is now an interpretability researcher at one of the AI labs, and she keeps sending me papers that I think raise a real philosophy of mind question, not just an ML curiosity. The interpretability crowd constantly says things like "we found a feature that represents X" -- the example everyone brings up is Anthropic's sparse autoencoder work on Claude, where they isolated a direction in activation space that fires on anything related to the Golden Gate Bridge, and when they cranked it up the model started bringing up the bridge in almost every reply, at one point insisting it literally *was* the bridge. Cool result, but I can't tell if "represents" is doing real philosophical work there or if its just folk-psychological gloss, the same way we say a thermostat "wants" the room warm. It's basically Ramsey's old job description challenge again -- does positing a representation actually earn its keep, or could the same behavior be explained by mere causal covariation, the same way tree rings covary with age without representing it -- except now applied to a system whose internals you can actually poke around in, which Ramsey never had.

Anyway, could you write me an essay that takes an actual stand on this, rather then just concluding "it's complicated"? Lay out the strongest realist case (these features/circuits really are representations in a fairly robust sense, maybe because they play the right causal role in guiding downstream computation -- something like Cummins/Gallistel-style structural representation) against the strongest deflationary case (it's all instrumentalist talk, no different in kind from calling the principal components of a dataset "representations," and Egan-style deflationism about content is the more honest description of what's going on). I want a real verdict, defended, even a hedged one like "realism is warranted for X but not Y" -- just not a wishy-washy both-sides thing.
