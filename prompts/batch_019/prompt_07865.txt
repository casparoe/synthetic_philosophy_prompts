question (15 pts total): "In On Liberty, Mill says that opinions can 'lose their immunity' from interference when the circumstances of their expression amount to a positive instigation to some mischievous act -- his example being someone who denounces corn-dealers as starvers of the poor to an angry crowd gathered right outside a corn-dealer's house. (a) [8 pts] Explain why Mill can hold that restricting this particular speech act is consistent with the harm principle, even though he thinks publishing the very same opinion in a newspaper should be tolerated. (b) [7 pts] Does the corn-dealer example show that the harm principle ultimately collapses into a general "bad tendency" test for restricting speech -- i.e. that speech may be suppressed whenever it has some tendency to cause harm? Why or why not?"

here's the student's answer:

"(a) For Mill, whether a speech act can be restricted depends heavily on the circumstances of its utterance, not just its propositional content. Printing the opinion that corn-dealers starve the poor in a newspaper just contributes it to public discussion, which any reader can accept or reject at their leisure. But saying the very same thing to a hungry, already-agitated mob standing outside a particular dealer's house is to incite an imminent harmful act against a specific person. So what's being punished isn't the opinion, its the act of instigation -- a paradigm case of harm to others, exactly what the harm principle is meant to cover.

(b) Yes, I think it does. Mill's point is basically that once speech can be shown to cause harm, the harm principle takes over from the liberty principle, and speech forfeits its protection whenever it has a sufficient tendency to bring about bad consequences. The corn-dealer case is just a vivid illustration of that more general rule: the speaker's words had a strong tendency to provoke violence, so restricting them was justified. This puts Mill's view in basically the same family as the old "bad tendency" tests English and American courts historically used to suppress seditious or heretical speech, just applied more even-handedly."
