I'm a retired atmospheric chemist (spent about 35 years modelling stratospheric ozone chemistry before I hung up my lab coat), and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy pretty hard, mostly through a little reading group of other retired academics that meets every couple of weeks. We've been working through consequentialism this year, and a few weeks back we hit the "cluelessness" objection: Lenman's old paper on it, and then Hilary Greaves' distinction between "simple" cluelessness (where the unforeseeable effects of an act are basically a wash, no reason to think they skew one way or another) and "complex" cluelessness (where you do have some reason to think the unknown long-run effects are systematically good or bad, but no real way to weigh that against what you can actually foresee). It rattled me more than I expected, and it definately didn't help that in my old field we spent entire careers modelling systems where the real, ultimate consequences of an intervention only show up decades later.

Which is basically my actual problem right now. An old colleague wants my name on a public letter backing more government funding for small stratospheric aerosol injection field trials, basically outdoor geoengineering experiments in solar radiation management. The direct, short-run cooling effect is reasonably well modelled at this point, so on a naive utilitarian calculus it looks like a clear win, fewer heat deaths pretty much straight away. But the second I start thinking about the second- and third-order stuff, shifts in monsoon patterns, knock-on effects for agriculture and migration, "termination shock" if we ever stopped, the moral hazard of it sapping political will for actual decarbonization, who ends up in control of the global thermostat, I honestly dont have a clue whether the overall sign is positive or negative, let alone the size of it, and it feels dishonest to just sign the thing and pretend otherwise.

So here's my actual question: assuming consequentialist cluelessness is a genuine problem and not just a clever debating trick, what are the most important implications of it for how someone in my position should reason about signing (or not) that letter? I don't want a flat yes or no, please walk through the implications properly. Does cluelessness push you toward precaution or reversibility as decision rules instead of raw expected value? Does the simple/complex distinction actually change the verdict here? And does the fact that cluelessness seems to infect nearly every long-range decision (donating to a malaria charity included) mean it can't give us any special reason to be extra cautious about geoengineering specifically, as opposed to just being a general reason to distrust consequentialist reasoning about the far future? If you can, rank the implications by how much they should actually move someone who currently leans towards signing.
