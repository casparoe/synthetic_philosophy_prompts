Okay so in my epistemic game theory seminar we just spent two weeks going through Aumann's old "agreeing to disagree" proof and then Harsanyi's type-space setup for games with incomplete information, and my professor kept emphasizing that the whole "you literally cannot rationally disagree once your posteriors are common knowledge" result only goes through because of the common prior assumption -- i.e. any leftover disagreement between two people has to be traced back to differences in private information, never to differences in the prior itself. That felt kind of off to me. Isn't the whole point of calling probabilities "subjective," in the Savage/de Finetti sense, that two equally rational and equally coherent people are allowed to just start from different priors, with nothing irrational about it as long as each of them individually satisfies the coherence axioms? So slapping a "common prior" requirement on top of that seems like its smuggling in a much stronger, extra requirement about what rational belief has to look like, not just some harmless technical simplification.

I brought this up in office hours and my professor basically said most economists just treat the common prior assumption as an innocent modeling convenience rather than a real epistemic commitment, which honestly made me more suspicious, not less. So can you argue the strongest possible version of the opposite view: that a genuine subjective Bayesian has no principled basis for imposing a common prior, and that once you take subjectivism seriously, two people can be fully rational, have completely identical information, and STILL rationally disagree forever, even after repeatedly telling each other exactly what they believe? If it's relevant feel free to bring in stuff like the no-trade results too, but mainly I don't want a wishy-washy "there are considerations on both sides" essay -- I want the actual best case for that specific side.
