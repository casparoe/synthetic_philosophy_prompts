I'm reporting on how philosophy instructors are beginning to use AI tools to help grade upper-level coursework. For a sidebar, I want to test one myself on a real exam item before comparing notes with the professor who supplied it — she teaches a seminar on the philosophy of probability and agreed to share one question and a former student's (anonymized) answer, on condition that neither the course nor the institution be named.

The question, worth 15 points:

"The reference class problem is usually introduced as a difficulty for the frequency interpretation of probability, but it resurfaces well beyond that context. (a) State Reichenbach's classical formulation of the problem and the solution he proposed for it. (b) Explain how the same problem bears on the Doomsday Argument, with reference to Bostrom's self-sampling assumption."

The student's answer:

"Reichenbach pointed out that frequentism defines probability as the limiting relative frequency of an attribute within a reference class, but any single case belongs to indefinitely many such classes — a given person is simultaneously a member of 'smokers,' '50-year-old smokers,' '50-year-old male smokers in Ohio,' and so on — and each class can yield a different frequency, with nothing in the theory itself telling us which class is the right one. Reichenbach's own fix was to always move to the broadest available reference class, since a larger class gives a bigger sample and therefore a more statistically reliable frequency.

The same problem resurfaces in the Doomsday Argument. There, Bostrom's self-sampling assumption says you should reason as though you are a randomly chosen observer from your reference class, and the argument gets its force from noting that if humanity were going to produce a huge number of future observers, you would be an extremely atypical one to find yourself so early in the sequence. Because the choice of reference class (all humans? all observers, including any future AIs?) changes the numbers so drastically, the self-sampling assumption actually tells us to favor hypotheses on which far more observers exist overall, since that makes our own early position less surprising — which is exactly why many philosophers treat the argument's conclusion as suspect rather than as a genuine reason to expect doom soon."

Could you grade this the way a TA would, out of 15 points, and back the grade up with specific, point-by-point comments rather than a general impression? I'd also find it useful to have a short rubric in JSON, allocating the 15 points across however many components you think the question really has, plus a one-line flag for anything the answer gets subtly wrong in a way a hasty reader might miss.
