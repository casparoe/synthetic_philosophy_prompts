I'm developing a longer feature on how newsrooms talk about "conspiracy theories," and in reporting it I've fallen down a rabbit hole in the philosophical literature that strikes me as more interesting than the psychology-of-belief angle most science journalism defaults to. There's a live dispute between what philosophers call particularists — who hold that "conspiracy theory" is not itself a mark of epistemic disrepute, and that each such theory has to be assessed on its individual evidential merits, since real conspiracies (Watergate, MKUltra, the tobacco industry's internal research on its own products) are periodically confirmed — and generalists, who argue that conspiracy theories as a class tend to share structural, epistemically corrosive features (self-sealing explanations that reinterpret disconfirming evidence as further proof of the cover-up, a standing rejection of institutional and expert testimony, an overreliance on pattern-matching in place of positive evidence) that justify treating the category itself with suspicion, independent of any particular case's details. This came up at an editorial meeting, where a colleague argued we should simply retire the phrase "conspiracy theory" altogether because it does the generalist's dismissive work for it; I want something more rigorous to bring back to that conversation than my own intuitions.

Could you write a dialogue between a sophisticated particularist and an equally sophisticated generalist who are debating this, using the trajectory of the COVID-19 lab-leak hypothesis as their running example — a theory that was widely labeled a conspiracy theory and dismissed by many scientific and media institutions in 2020, only to be treated as a serious open question by some of those same institutions a couple of years later? I'd like the two speakers to be genuine epistemic peers who respond directly to each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another: the generalist should have to say something substantive about whether the early dismissal was nonetheless epistemically reasonable given the information available at the time, and the particularist should have to address the worry that "just evaluate each case on its evidence" is cold comfort when the relevant evidence is classified, contested, or otherwise inaccessible to ordinary reasoners. Please keep the exchange focused on these underlying epistemological questions rather than on relitigating where the virus actually came from.
