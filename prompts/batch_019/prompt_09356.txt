I've been reporting a feature on the intellectual ancestry of "AI safety via debate," the scalable-oversight proposal, first outlined by OpenAI researchers in 2018, in which two AI systems argue opposing sides of a disputed claim in front of a judge who cannot evaluate the underlying question directly and must rule on the exchange itself. In conversations with a couple of alignment researchers, I kept being pointed back to John Stuart Mill's chapter on the liberty of thought and discussion in *On Liberty*, and specifically his argument that even a correct opinion, if it is never seriously contested, decays into what he called "dead dogma" rather than "a living truth" — his claim being that a belief stays justified, and stays truly understood by the person holding it, only if it has been made to survive real opposition.

The more I reread that chapter, though, the more it seems to me that Mill's confidence in open contest rests on conditions the debate proposal deliberately does without. Mill seems to assume that disputants and audience are working with roughly comparable faculties, that a bad argument can eventually be run down because everyone involved is, in principle, capable of following it to its flaw, and that there is time for the sounder position to accumulate an advantage through repeated exposure. The AI version is built for the opposite case: a judge stipulated to be weaker than the debaters, a single adjudicated round rather than an open-ended process, and a training signal that rewards winning rather than being correct — so a fluent but dishonest debater could in principle prevail by exploiting the judge's specific blind spots (the failure mode researchers sometimes call the "obfuscated arguments" problem) rather than being exposed the way an incompetent disputant would be in Mill's picture.

Setting aside the thin "marketplace of ideas" slogan people usually extract from Mill, and going instead by what he actually argued, how do you think he would judge AI safety via debate as a way of keeping increasingly capable systems honest? Would he recognize it as his own argument simply scaled up, or would he see the specific ways it departs from his assumptions — the competence gap between judge and debaters, the manufactured rather than organic character of the contest, the optimization pressure aimed squarely at persuasion — as undermining the very mechanism he thought made open argument trustworthy in the first place?
