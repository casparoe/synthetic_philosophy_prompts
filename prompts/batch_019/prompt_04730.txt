Leibniz's monads famously have "no windows"—they can't causally affect each other at all, but they stay perfectly in sync because God pre-programmed a "pre-established harmony" into all of them at creation. That's always kind of reminded me of entangled particles violating Bell's inequality: you get correlations between two separated measurements that hold up even though nothing seems to actually pass between them. If Leibniz had somehow lived to see Bell's theorem and the loophole-free experiments confirming those correlations, do you think he'd read it as vindicating his rejection of action-at-a-distance, or would something about it cause real trouble for his system? Bell's proof also leans on the assumption that experimenters are free to choose thier measurement settings independently of whatever's determining the particles—and given how deterministic Leibniz's whole setup already is (every monad's future already baked into its concept from the get-go), I wonder if he'd just bite the bullet on that assumption instead.
