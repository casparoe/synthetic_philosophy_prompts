I've been slowly working through Pascal's Pensées for a seminar (not the Wager stuff everyone always brings up, but the weirder political fragments), and I keep getting stuck on the one usually titled something like "Justice, force." The gist is that justice without force is powerless, because there will always be wicked people who just ignore it, while force without justice looks like naked tyranny — so people try to unite the two, and since force is easy for everyone to recognize while justice is endlessly disputable, what actually happens is we don't make what's just strong, we just declare whatever's strong to be just. My professor mentioned in passing that some people read this as basically an early legal-positivist move, or even a theory of legitimacy as pure convention, which seems like a strangely cynical thing for a devout Catholic apologist to be saying, though maybe it fits once you factor in his stuff about the limits of unaided reason.

Anyway I can't stop thinking about how weirdly well this maps onto crypto governance, which obviously didn't exist yet in the 1600s. Take the "code is law" ethos in a lot of blockchain communities, or the actual 2016 DAO hack on Ethereum, where someone drained a huge chunk of funds (tens of millions of dollars worth of ETH) by exploiting a bug in the smart contract, and the community had to choose between saying "well, the code allowed it, so the outcome is legitimate" (which is basically what happened on the chain that became Ethereum Classic) versus hard-forking the whole network to reverse it because it felt intuitively unjust even though it followed the rules exactly as written. More generally, in a proof-of-work or proof-of-stake system, whoever controls the most hashpower or stake just is the one whose version of transaction history counts as "true" — force isn't merely correlated with justice there, its practically built in as the definition of it.

So what I'm actually curious about is: if you explained all this to Pascal, what do you think he'd say? Would he see blockchain consensus as just an unusually honest, unembarrassed version of the exact dynamic he already diagnosed — people finally admitting out loud that they've given up on making justice strong and settled for making strong things "just"? Or is there something about procedural/computational force that doesn't fit his picture, since he seems to have coercive, personal force in mind (armies, kings, mobs) rather than something transparent and rule-governed that everyone agreed to in advance? I'd also be curious whether you think he'd side more with the "code is law" purists or with the pro-fork camp, given his own fairly bleak resolution of the justice/force problem.
