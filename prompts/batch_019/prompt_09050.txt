In my computability theory course, we recently proved that Turing machines, Church's lambda calculus, and Kleene's general recursive functions all pick out exactly the same class of functions on the natural numbers. Since these three formalisms were developed completely independently yet converge on the same notion of computability, it seems to me that we have effectively produced a rigorous mathematical proof of the Church-Turing thesis, rather than merely gathering evidence for it. Given that, I don't understand why my textbook still describes the thesis as something that can never, in principle, be formally proven, and insists it remain a "thesis" rather than a theorem. Could you explain what is supposed to be missing from this convergence argument, and, more generally, how a computer scientist should think about the epistemic status of a foundational claim that apparently can never be settled by proof?
