For an independent presentation in my Theory of Knowledge class, I have been reading about how probability and rational choice can behave strangely once an agent is uncertain not about the external world but about "where" it is within a sequence of decisions. That is how I came across the absent-minded driver problem, originally posed by the economists Piccione and Rubinstein: a driver wants to take the second of two consecutive highway exits home, but the two intersections look completely identical from inside the car, so upon reaching one he has no way of telling whether it is the first or the second. Exiting at the first intersection pays off 0, driving past both pays off 1, and exiting at exactly the second, which is the actual goal, pays off 4. Because the intersections cannot be told apart, the driver cannot simply follow a rule like "continue, then exit"; whatever he does at one intersection, he is stuck doing at the other as well. Worked out as a plan in advance, the best policy turns out to be a genuine randomization — continuing with probability 2/3 at whichever intersection he happens to be at — and this beats every non-random policy outright, which is already strange, since in ordinary decision problems with perfect recall no randomized strategy can ever strictly beat the best deterministic one.

What I cannot yet get a solid grip on is the further question of what the driver should believe, in the moment of actually sitting at an intersection, about whether it is the first one or the second one, and why this "in the moment" perspective is supposed to sit uneasily with the plan that looked optimal from outside the decision altogether. I have seen this contrast described as "action optimality" versus "planning optimality," and I gather it is a close relative of the dispute over the Sleeping Beauty problem, but so far I only understand that people disagree, not why the disagreement is supposed to be philosophically deep rather than just a quirk of this one driving story.

Rather than have me simply recycle the highway example in my presentation, could you construct a different, concrete scenario with the same underlying structure — some agent who, for a specific and well-motivated reason, and not mere carelessness, genuinely cannot distinguish between successive decision points in a repeated task — and use it to explain exactly why the ex-ante-optimal plan and the credence that seems natural to hold from inside the decision do not obviously line up? I would like a real, vivid replacement for the highway story, developed in enough detail that I could actually present it to my class instead of the original.
