Last month I served on a jury for a criminal trial that turned almost entirely on the testimony of a single eyewitness, and it left me thinking harder than I expected about what actually justifies believing someone else's word. During deliberations, one juror argued that we had no business crediting the witness's account unless we had some independent, non-testimonial reason to think she was reliable on this particular occasion -- otherwise, he said, we were just taking her word for her own trustworthiness. Another juror shot back that this standard is impossible to meet in ordinary life: almost everything we believe about matters beyond our immediate perception -- names, dates, distant places, other people's motives -- rests on simply trusting what others tell us, and that this trust seems no less basic an epistemic source than perception or memory themselves. We reached a verdict, but the disagreement stuck with me, and some reading afterward turned up a real fault line in epistemology that I hadn't known existed.

As far as I can tell, that fault line runs between reductionism about testimony, which traces to Hume and holds that testimonial justification must ultimately bottom out in the hearer's own perception, memory, and inductive inference regarding testifiers' reliability, and anti-reductionism, which traces to Reid and treats our default trust in testimony as a basic, non-inferential source of justification in its own right, on a par with perception. The debate has apparently been refined considerably since Hume and Reid -- I've come across names like C.A.J. Coady, Elizabeth Fricker, Tyler Burge, and Jennifer Lackey attached to sharper versions of each side, plus hybrid positions that try to split the difference -- but I don't have the background to reconstruct their arguments myself, and I'd rather not settle for a summary that flattens the disagreement into a platitude like "well, testimony matters, but so does evidence."

Could you write me a dialogue between a contemporary reductionist and a contemporary anti-reductionist about testimony, perhaps two philosophers asked to weigh in on a case much like my jury's? I want both to be genuine experts defending the strongest form of their view, not a strawman on either side. Have the reductionist press the worry that anti-reductionism collapses into licensing gullibility, and have the anti-reductionist press cases that seem to make strict reductionism impossible to satisfy in practice -- small children who trust testimony long before they can reason about anyone's reliability, or testimony about matters (remote history, other people's private mental states) that no hearer could ever independently corroborate. Rather than have either side simply concede, I'd like the exchange to end with both characters agreeing on exactly what, if anything, is still substantively in dispute between them once any merely verbal confusions have been cleared away.
