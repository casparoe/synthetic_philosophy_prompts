I write crime novels and brought a heist scene to my writing group last week that sparked a weirdly heated argument. In the scene a five-person crew robs a bank, but the mastermind has lied to two of them about the point of the job -- she told them its a straightforward cash grab, when really she's there to burn a set of documents in the vault and the cash is just cover. Dana said the scene doesn't work as written because you can't really call it "the crew's heist" if two of the five don't even know whats actually happening -- for her, a real joint action means everyone's in on the same plan, not just going through motions someone else understands and they don't. Marcus pushed back, saying that's way too strict: soldiers in a platoon don't know the full battle plan either, and we still say the platoon fought the battle together, not just "the general's battle." Dana said being on a need-to-know basis is completely different from being flat-out lied to about the goal. Marcus answered that the two deceived crew members still know exactly what they're doing -- robbing this bank, right now, together -- and that the lie is only about their personal reason for it, and reasons don't change whether an action counts as joint. Dana wasn't having it: she said if the goal two of them think they're pursuing isn't the real goal at all, then they're not actually party to the real plan, just tools being used by the people who are. It went in circles from there and nobody budged. I can't tell who's actually right here. Can you go through it move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, and whether there's something both of them are missing?
