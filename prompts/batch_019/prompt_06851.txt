"Cases of misleading higher-order evidence are often said to generate a puzzle about epistemic akrasia. Using an example of your own choosing, explain: (a) what would make a belief-state in such a case count as 'akratic'; (b) why many epistemologists have thought a fully rational agent cannot be akratic in this sense; and (c) what cost this creates for the view that higher-order evidence should have no bearing on what's rational to believe at the first-order level. (~350 words)"

And here's the student's answer:

"(a) Higher-order evidence is evidence about how reliable you're being in responding to your other, first-order evidence, rather than evidence about the world that first-order evidence concerns. Take a doctor near the end of a twenty-hour shift who has to work out a drug dosage. She's told, truly, that doctors this exhausted get this kind of calculation wrong about a third of the time without being able to tell when they've slipped. She does the math twice and gets the same number both times. She ends up believing the dosage she calculated is correct, and, if asked, would also say that given the statistic about exhausted doctors, her evidence doesn't really support trusting that number. Holding both of those at once is the akratic combination: not a contradiction, but a state nobody could recommend to someone in advance, the way nobody could sincerely tell someone in advance to believe it's raining and also believe that their evidence doesn't support that it's raining.

(b) The reason this looks impossible for a rational person is that belief and confidence-in-your-evidence seem to be the same state looked at from two angles: to believe P at all just is to be treating your evidence as supporting P, so believing P while believing your evidence doesn't support P is Moore's paradox relocated from assertion to evidence-assessment. This is also why the same puzzle shows up in the peer disagreement literature: conciliationism, the view that discovering an epistemic equal disagrees with you should move your credence toward theirs, is standardly treated as the version of this same principle that other cases like fatigue or drugs get generalized from.

(c) The view that higher-order evidence shouldn't affect first-order rationality at all -- that the doctor should just believe whatever her checked arithmetic supports, full stop -- runs into the bootstrapping problem: since the fatigue statistic is itself only more evidence, a view that lets it be screened off entirely means a reasoner could always dismiss any amount of evidence about her own unreliability, which makes the position awkwardly immune to correction by exactly the kind of evidence it's supposed to be able to use."
