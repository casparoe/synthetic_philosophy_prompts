Ok so we did the St. Petersburg paradox in stats class (the coin flip game where the expected payout is infinite) and I thought that was the weirdest thing ever, until I fell down a rabbit hole and found this "Pasadena game" thing from a 2004 Nover and Hajek paper that's supposedly even worse. Same basic setup: flip a coin till it lands heads, but now if the first heads is on an odd flip number n you win 2^n/n dollars, and if n is even you pay 2^n/n dollars. Add up the expected value in the order the flips would actually happen (n=1, then n=2, then n=3...) and you get the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln(2), so about +0.69, a totally normal-looking answer. Except that series is only "conditionally" convergent, not absolutely (we literally just did that distinction in calc), and apparently that means you can relist the exact same infinite set of outcomes in a different order and make the expected value come out to whatever you want, including +infinity or -infinity. Same probabilities, same payouts, different order, different answer. They even build a sister game called "Altadena" that pays you one extra dollar no matter what happens, so it obviously has to be worth more -- except expected value theory can't even guarantee that much, since both games' "expectations" are just as up for grabs.

Nover and Hajek's own conclusion is that there's no priveleged order, so the game just doesn't have an expected value, period. I want you to argue against that. Make the case that the order the coin flips actually happen in (1st flip, then 2nd, then 3rd...) IS the priveleged order, so the Pasadena game does have a perfectly well-defined expected value after all. Don't just assert it though, you have to deal with their comeback that this smuggles in irrelevant procedural info a decision problem shouldn't care about (only probabilities and payouts are supposed to matter), and with their "black box" move where they show that calling flip-number "the" natural index is kind of arbitrary too, since you can relabel the same outcomes in groups of three and get a totally different "natural" total (3/2 ln 2) out of the identical mechanism. Actually engage with those two points instead of dodging them, and land on an actual verdict, not a "well its complicated" shrug.
