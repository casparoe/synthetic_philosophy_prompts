So I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy and I've been part of a reading group that just spent way too long on Books 1 and 2 of Plato's Laws, and I keep getting stuck on the section about drinking parties. Quick recap in case its not fresh in your memory: Megillus (the Spartan) and Kleinias (the Cretan) both come from cities that basically ban that kind of drinking for anyone involved in military life, and the Athenian Stranger spends most of Book 1 talking them out of their suspicion of it. His argument is basically that just as Spartan kids are deliberately exposed to fear and pain so they can practice courage, ordinary citizens need some cheap, low-risk way to practice resisting pleasure, and supervised drinking under a sober symposiarch is the ideal tool for this, because it temporarily knocks out your inhibitions and lets you (and your legislator) see and train your actual capacity for shame and self-control. Book 2 piles an age-graded system on top of this: zero wine under 18 because you shouldn't "pour fire on the fire" already burning in a young soul, moderate wine without drunkenness up to 30, and then increasing license after that, with old men getting the most license of all as a dedicated "chorus of Dionysus," on the theory that wine softens the stiffness of old age (their souls go "soft like iron in fire") enough that they'll actually sing and dance in public, which matters because that kind of choral performance is supposed to be doing a lot of the community's moral education.

What's been bugging me is that this sits really badly with the Republic, where the guardians are trained to handle pleasure precisely by not being exposed to gluttony, lust, or drunkenness, and Socrates censors a bunch of the Homeric material partly because of how it depicts gods and heroes losing control. There's a paper by Nicholas Baima that basically asks whether Plato changed his mind, and if so, whether the Laws version can be defended on its own terms, or whether "deliberately lose control so you get better at not losing control" is just a bad argument dressed up as political theory. I can sort of construct a charitable reading in my head (the fear/courage analogy really does generalize to pleasure/temperance, and the safeguards -- sober supervisors, strict age limits -- are doing real work) and a skeptical reading (unlike fear, intoxication doesn't just test the rational part of the soul, it disables it, so there's nothing left to do the "training" once the wine actually kicks in) but I can't get the two versions to actually engage with each other in my head.

Could you write me a dialogue between two contemporary philosophers of ancient ethics who both know the text cold but land on opposite sides of this -- one defending the Athenian's proposal as basically sound moral psychology, the other thinking it's incoherent, or at best a rationalization of a practice Plato was personally fond of? I want real back-and-forth, not two monologues, so have each one respond to the other's actual strongest move (e.g., whatever the defender says to the "you can't train a faculty by disabling it" objection, and whatever the critic says to the fear/pleasure symmetry argument), and please don't make either of them a pushover. If it amuses you to have them get a little tipsy themselves over the course of the conversation, that's a fun bonus, but not required.
