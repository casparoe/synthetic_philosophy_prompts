I'm prepping next week's seminar for my medieval Jewish philosophy course and I keep going back and forth on how much to get into the Maimonides/Nachmanides fight over animal sacrifice, because the standard one-line version ("Maimonides thought sacrifice was just a primitive concession, the end") really doesn't do justice to either side, though my TA keeps telling me I'm overthinking this for a bunch of undergrads who mostly just signed up for the gen-ed credit. Quick recap so we're on the same page: in the Guide (III:32), Maimonides argues that God didn't actually want sacrificial worship for it's own sake. The Israelites coming out of Egypt were so steeped in a culture where worship just meant sacrificing animals that yanking the practice away entirely wasn't psychologically realistic, so the law redirects that impulse toward Him instead of idols, as a transitional measure whose real point is knowledge of God and the eradication of idolatry. He gets way more granular a bit later in book III (around ch. 46 if memory serves, don't hold me to the exact number), where specific sacrificial rules, like which animals are used and which combinations are banned, get mapped onto specific Egyptian or "Sabian" idolatrous practices he wants repudiated.

Nachmanides, in his Torah commentary on Leviticus, thinks this whole picture is basically untenable. His best shot is that Cain and Abel, Noah, and the Patriarchs were all sacrificing long before the Israelites ever set foot in Egypt, so how can the practice be a concession to specifically Egyptian (or Sabian) idol-worship habits that didn't even exist yet? His own account gives sacrifice real, positive content instead: something about the ritual enacting the sinner's own deserved fate onto the animal (substitution, atonement), plus some hints at a deeper rationale he's cagey about that sounds distinctly kabbalistic.

What I'd like from you is an actual comparison of the two views, not just "concession theory vs. intrinsic-value theory," but one that gets into the reasoning. In particular: is Nachmanides' Abel/Noah/Patriarchs point actually fatal to Maimonides, or is there a decent Maimonidean comeback available (maybe about what's really doing the accommodating work)? And then commit to an answer: in your judgment, whose theory does a better job explaining the specific, sometimes weirdly granular content of the sacrificial laws, rather than just the bare existence of sacrifice in general?
