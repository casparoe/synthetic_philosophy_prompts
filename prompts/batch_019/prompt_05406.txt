Ok, this has been bugging me for weeks and I can't let it go. A few of us on my team started this dumb rotating fund for end-of-quarter drinks: every Friday, whoever's "holding" the pot can either chip in another $20 (which grows what's available down the line) or cash out right then and keep most of it for themselves, splitting whatever's left over among the rest of us. Going in, we all knew the whole thing had to wrap up after week 6, since that's when half the team rotates to a different floor. One guy on the team, who's got a stats background and is normally really sharp about this stuff, announced before we'd even started that the whole setup was "obviously" pointless: his reasoning was that whoever's holding the pot in week 6 will clearly just cash out rather than pass it on to nobody, so the week 5 holder should see that coming and cash out first instead of ending up with nothing, and so on all the way back down the chain — meaning the truly "rational" move was to cash out on day one, before anyone had even put a dollar in.

Except that isn't remotely what happened. We passed it back and forth for something like four rounds, it kept growing, and nobody felt like a chump for passing along, even though by his logic we all should of seen the ending coming from day one. When I brought it up with him afterward he just shrugged and said we'd all been "irrational" and gotten lucky. I've been mildly obsessed with decision theory podcasts since this happened, but haven't heard anyone name this exact issue. I'm on the design side of things, not stats, so I don't really have the toolkit to push back on him properly, but something about his argument still nags at me even though I can't find the flaw in any individual step. Was he actually right that passing the pot was irrational the whole way through, or is there something off about that chain of reasoning itself, even if each link looks fine on its own? I'd genuinely like to understand what's going on here rather than just having a vague hunch he's missing something.
