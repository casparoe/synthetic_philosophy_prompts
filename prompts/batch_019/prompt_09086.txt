I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mostly on practical reason and decision theory, and Kavka's toxin puzzle came up again in my reading group last week -- I've now explained this puzzle to people a bunch of times and I think my go-to gloss on it might just be wrong. What I usually say is: the puzzle shows that a rational agent literally cannot be trusted to follow through on a costly promise once the payoff for making the promise has already been banked -- of course you wouldn't actually drink the nasty toxin once the eccentric billionaire has already wired you the million, since by that point drinking it just makes you sick for a day for no reason. To me this always seemed like a fancy philosophical dressing on something game theorists already know cold: self-interested agents defect once the enforcement mechanism disappears, same reason a contractor ghosts you after final payment or a country walks away from a climate pledge with no teeth attached. So I've been telling people it's basically a wordy restatement of an end-game/backward-induction problem, not some deep unsolved puzzle about intention.

My advisor thinks this misses the actual point of the puzzle entirely and got kind of exasperated with me about it, but I still don't fully see what's supposedly left over once you take the "people break promises when there's nothing left in it for them" part out. Is she right that theres a genuinely seperate paradox here, or is this one of those cases where philosophers have dressed up an obvious bit of strategic behaviour in fancier clothes than it needs?
