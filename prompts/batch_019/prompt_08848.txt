After a forty-year career in experimental physics, I have spent much of my retirement working through the game-theory literature, and I recently came across the notion of "program equilibrium": games in which players submit computer programs rather than actions, with each program able to inspect its opponent's source code before deciding how to play. As I understand it, this is meant to show that in a single round of the Prisoner's Dilemma, mutual cooperation can be a genuine Nash equilibrium of the resulting "program game." This strikes me as too good to be true: whatever conditionally cooperative program my opponent submits, I remain free to submit the trivial program that outputs "defect" no matter what code it inspects, and since defection strictly dominates cooperation in the underlying Prisoner's Dilemma, this trivial program should do at least as well as any more elaborate one. How, then, can mutual cooperation count as a stable equilibrium, rather than an artifact of theorists having quietly excluded the unconditional defector from consideration?
