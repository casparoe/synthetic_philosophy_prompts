I am currently taking a seminar this term on Wittgenstein and hinge epistemology, and this week's session worked through the "hinge" sections of On Certainty. I could follow the more widely discussed remarks about hinges and the river-bed of thought reasonably well, but I have found myself stuck on a shorter passage a little further on that we passed over quickly in discussion. Here it is, §358–359:

"Now I would like to regard this certainty, not as something akin to hastiness or superficiality, but as a form of life. (That is very badly expressed and probably badly thought as well.)

But that means I want to conceive it as something that lies beyond being justified or unjustified; as it were, as something animal."

What I cannot work out is how the two characterizations here are meant to fit together. "Form of life" elsewhere in Wittgenstein seems to pick out something shared and practice-based, even normative — a matter of what a community of language-users does together. "Something animal," by contrast, sounds like a pre-social, purely instinctual reflex, closer to biology than to culture. Is Wittgenstein running together two different pictures of what grounds a hinge proposition, or is there a reading on which the social and the animal converge here? And does the answer bear on whether hinges are best understood as a special class of (non-epistemic) beliefs, or as not really propositional at all?
