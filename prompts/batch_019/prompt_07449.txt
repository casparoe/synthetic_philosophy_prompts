I never studied philosophy or theology formally -- everything I know about this stuff comes from library books and, for the last year or so, from a small podcast that a couple of friends and I record in someone's garage. I fell down a rabbit hole recently on John Rawls that I haven't been able to shake. Long before "A Theory of Justice" made him famous, Rawls wrote a 1942 undergraduate thesis, "A Brief Inquiry into the Meaning of Sin and Faith," defending what I gather was a fairly devout, anti-Pelagian Christian position: sin as a kind of self-isolating egoism, faith as restored community rather than merit earned before God. Then, apparently, near the end of his life he wrote a short private memoir, "On My Religion," published only after his death, describing how that faith had quietly come apart on him during the war.

What I can't stop turning over is that the Rawls everybody actually reads and cites -- the one behind "overlapping consensus," "public reason," and the "duty of civility" -- treats religion only from the outside, as one "comprehensive doctrine" among others that citizens have to bracket in political argument. I don't know whether that later, apparently secular framework is a clean break from the early theology or whether it is quietly still doing the same work in different words.

For next month's episode I've booked a guest who has spent much of her career on exactly this question. She argues that ideas like "reasonableness," the priority of the right over the good, and even the notion of a "well-ordered society" are secularized descendants of the sin-and-community theology in the 1942 thesis rather than a break from it, and her standard response whenever someone points out how thoroughly Rawls scrubbed theological language out of his mature work is that "an inheritance doesn't need the heir's permission." I don't want the episode to consist of her restating the thesis and that line back at me. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the position rather than invite a summary of it? I would like at least one question that forces her to say what kind of textual or historical evidence would actually count against the continuity reading, rather than being merely compatible with it; one on whether "On My Religion" should be allowed to change how we read the neutrality claims of "Political Liberalism" at all, or whether that is biography dressed up as textual argument; and one on how a genuinely devout citizen -- say, someone holding the natural-law Catholic position on abortion that Rawls's own "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" treats as testing the limits of the reasonable -- is supposed to recognize herself in a theory that this same guest says descends from Christian ethics. For each of these, and for the rest of the set, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she would most likely give if she reached for the "heir's permission" line again.
