My mother is retiring in June after thirty-one years as a state appellate judge, and I have been asked to give the toast at her retirement dinner. I should admit up front that I never went to college, let alone law school — I run a small landscaping business — so whatever I understand about the legal side of who she is, I picked up the slow way, mostly by half-listening to an argument she has been having with my uncle Frank at every family gathering since I was a child. Frank is a devout Catholic with strong natural-law convictions, and my mother is, and has always been, a committed legal positivist of the H.L.A. Hart variety, careful always to add that this has nothing to do with "might makes right" or thinking a law deserves obedience just because it happens to be on the books. Her actual position, as best I can render it, is that a law can be perfectly valid and binding and still be wicked, and that pretending a wicked law was never really a law at all — however comforting that sounds — is a kind of dishonesty dressed up as virtue.

Their argument always circles back to the same historical example, one she brought up so often when I was growing up that I could probably recite it from memory: the German courts after the war that had to decide what to do with people who had used Nazi-era laws to get rid of neighbors or spouses they disliked, and who then argued afterward that they had only been reporting a crime under the law as it stood at the time. Frank thinks the courts were right to declare, after the fact, that those statutes had never been valid law to begin with, since nothing that wicked deserves the name — he would probably tell you he is just quoting Aquinas. My mother has spent decades on the other side of it: that the honest, and ultimately safer, thing was to admit those were real, properly enacted laws, and that they were also monstrous, and that a legal system able to hold both thoughts at once without flinching is worth more than one that quietly defines evil out of existence whenever it becomes awkward to admit having enacted it. She has always treated this as the least dispensable idea she owns, not as a technicality.

The dinner is going to be an odd room to write for. Half of it will be fellow judges, former clerks, and a couple of law professors who will know exactly what is being referenced the moment I say "the grudge informers" or "Hart and Fuller," and who will wince if I flatten her position into anything resembling "she believed the law must always be obeyed" — which she would consider a betrayal of everything she has argued for thirty years, including, I am told, in at least one dissent. The other half will be my aunts, her book club, and my own children, who have absorbed the shape of this argument for decades without ever quite learning what it is actually about, and who mostly want to laugh, maybe tear up a little, and feel that the speech was truly about her. I would like the toast built around this argument of hers, since it is the truest thing I know about how her mind works, rather than around some safer and more forgettable story. Could you write me something in the five-to-seven-minute range that does right by both halves of that room?
