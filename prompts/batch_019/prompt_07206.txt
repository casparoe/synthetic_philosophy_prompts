I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology, and I want a sharper sense of what's really at stake in the debate over the common prior assumption. Write a dialogue between two equally rigorous game theorists. One holds, in the spirit of Aumann and the so-called Harsanyi doctrine, that rationality itself requires a common prior, so that persistent, mutually known disagreement between equally informed agents signals irrationality on someone's part. The other is a subjective Bayesian who denies that ordinary probabilistic coherence (Savage, de Finetti) ever demanded shared priors, and holds that two fully rational people can simply start from different priors. Set the exchange right after the two have worked through a proof of Aumann's agreement theorem together, only to notice that they still disagree about the probability of some real-world event of their choosing. Have the first argue that such unexplained divergence is arbitrary and epistemically suspect, and the second respond by invoking persistent expert disagreement and the routine occurrence of speculative trade — a phenomenon no-trade theorems say shouldn't occur if traders share a common prior — as evidence that rational priors can differ. The two should directly engage each other's strongest arguments, and neither should win outright.
