"Parfit's hitchhiker case goes like this: you're stranded in the desert, dying of thirst, and a driver comes along who happens to be an extremely reliable predictor of what people will do. She offers you a deal: she'll drive you into town, but only if she predicts that once you're safe and have access to money again, you'll actually pay her (say $1000) for the ride. If she predicts you won't pay, she leaves you there. Say she picks you up -- which means she's predicted you'll pay. Now you're safe in town. The question is whether you should actually hand over the money.

The problem for causal decision theory is that once you're already in town, paying can't causally affect anything that's already happened -- the ride is a sunk cost from a causal point of view, and refusing to pay just saves you money with no downside, since (let's assume) you'll never see this driver again. So CDT says: don't pay. But if you're the kind of agent who, predictably, won't pay once safe, then a good enough predictor would have foreseen that and left you in the desert. So an agent who reasons causally at the moment of choice is exactly the kind of agent who dies in the desert. That's the paradox: doing the locally rational thing is what makes you unable to get the good outcome in the first place.

This is what's meant by rational irrationality: it can be rational, according to a theory like the self-interest theory, to cause yourself to become disposed to act in ways that the theory itself will later call irrational. In other words, the theory recommends that you make yourself, by its own lights, into someone who will sometimes act against it -- which is what's meant by calling such a theory 'indirectly self-defeating': satisfying it perfectly at every moment is worse, even by its own standard, than being disposed to occasionally violate it.

Gauthier's answer to this is to distinguish sophisticated choice from resolute choice. A sophisticated chooser picks a plan in advance but re-evaluates at each later decision point and abandons the plan whenever deviating looks locally better -- which is exactly the CDT behavior that gets you left in the desert, because the driver can predict that you'll abandon the plan once safe. A resolute chooser, instead, picks the best overall plan ex ante and then just carries it out later even when, taken in isolation, the later step looks like a bad deal -- so a resolute chooser pays, gets picked up, and ends up better off than the sophisticated chooser who never gets picked up at all. Gauthier's claim is basically that resolute choosers do better by their own lights than sophisticated ones whenever they might face predictors like this, so it's rational to be resolute even though resoluteness sometimes requires doing what looks locally irrational.

You can also frame this in evidential terms instead of causal ones. An evidential decision theorist looks at paying as good news: choosing to pay is the kind of thing only a payer-type would do, and payer-types are exactly the people who get picked up, so conditioning on 'I pay' is good news in a way that conditioning on 'I don't pay' isn't. That gives EDT an easier time with the hitchhiker case than CDT, without needing to bring in resolute choice at all.

My own take is that this doesn't actually show CDT gives the 'wrong answer' once you're standing in town with certainty that you were already picked up -- CDT is answering the question 'what should I do now, given the causal structure from now on,' and given that structure, not paying really is better for you now. What the case shows instead is that it can be bad to be a CDT-follower, i.e., bad to be an agent that only ever reasons this way, in a world with predictors -- which is a claim about which dispositions are good to have, not a claim that CDT mis-evaluates the causal facts at the moment of choice. So I'd say the case is less a refutation of CDT as a local decision rule than a demonstration that the rationality of an act and the rationality of a policy or disposition can come apart, and that we have reason to want the latter even at some cost to the former."
