I run a small reading group on philosophy of perception this term, and a couple weeks back we spent two sessions on the cognitive penetration debate -- Siegel and Macpherson making the case that perceptual experience can genuinely be shaped by belief, expectation and affect, versus Firestone & Scholl's big methodological broadside arguing that pretty much none of the classic demonstrations survive scrutiny once you control for confounds. Afterwards two of my students, Nadia and Theo, kept arguing about it over beers while I mostly just listened (and payed for a round, which I'm still annoyed about). I've tried to reconstruct their exchange as fairly to both sides as I can below, because I honestly can't tell who came out ahead and its been bugging me since.

(1) Nadia opened with Proffitt's hills research: induced fear, tiredness, or being out of shape all make a hill look steeper, and this isn't just measured by asking for a number -- people also visually match the slant to a tilted disk, which seems to tap appearance rather than opinion. She said that's about as clean a case of affect penetrating visual experience as you're going to find. (2) Theo said a matching task is still a deliberate, conscious judgment, and conscious judgments are exactly the kind of measure most liable to demand characteristics -- and, tellingly, the very same research shows that a more automatic, action-guiding measure (adjusting a tilted board by feel, without looking, to match the slope underfoot) barely budges with fear or fatigue, if at all. That dissociation, he said, is exactly what you'd expect if the effect lives downstream of perception, in judgment, rather than in perception itself. (3) Nadia replied that this shows less than Theo thinks: the action-guiding system was never a plausible home for phenomenal, "what it looks like" experience to begin with, so showing that it's unaffected doesn't demote the conscious measures to "mere cognition" -- it just confirms something nobody was denying, that the two systems can be dissociated. (4) Theo said Nadia has the burden backwards: she needs some independent reason to trust the explicit measure as a readout of experience rather than belief, especially given that explicit, reflective measures are, again and again in this literature, exactly the ones that carry the effect whenever a "penetration" result shows up, which is the pattern Firestone & Scholl built their entire case around.

Could you referee this properly? Go through moves (1) through (4) and say, for each one, whether the point is basically right, basically wrong, or right about something narrower than the person thinks -- and why. Then stand back and tell me what you think both of them are missing, something that would actually bear on whether this is a genuine case of cognitive penetration rather than just a dispute about which measure to trust.
