I'm the philosopher on my hospital's ethics committee. A patient, several months after a severe brain injury, remains unresponsive on every bedside exam and meets criteria for unresponsive wakefulness syndrome. A research fMRI protocol nonetheless found that, on command, she produces the same sustained activity in the supplementary motor area that healthy volunteers show when asked to imagine playing tennis -- replicated on a second scan a month later. Her family wants the hospital to commit to a defined further course of these sessions, aimed at establishing yes/no communication, before any conversation about her long-term care plan resumes. The attending neurologist objects that this only shows a first-order sensorimotor network doing what it's told. On any theory in the higher-order family -- from Rosenthal's higher-order-thought view to Lau's perceptual-reality-monitoring account -- what would actually settle whether there is something it is like to be her is whether that activity is accompanied by anything resembling a further state representing her as imagining tennis, and no single fMRI contrast can show that. He wants the standard family meeting to proceed unchanged. Our care-planning policy mentions only the bedside behavioral scale and is silent on neuroimaging evidence. Please draft the short opinion I'll circulate before the committee meets: recommend a concrete course of action with a timeframe, and engage the neurologist's objection on its own terms rather than either deferring to it as decisive or dismissing it as pedantry.
