I'm putting together a philosophy of AI seminar for next term, and I've spent way too much of my summer reading mechanistic interpretability papers trying to figure out what I actually think about them. The question that won't leave me alone is this: when researchers train a sparse autoencoder on a model's activations and pull out a direction that reliably fires on, say, the Golden Gate Bridge (there's that demo where they clamped it way up and the model started bringing up the bridge in almost every response, even to questions that had nothing to do with it), have they *discovered* something the network represents, in the way a neuroscientist discovers that a cell in visual cortex encodes edge orientation? Or have they just picked out one convenient decomposition among a huge space of equally good ones, and all the "representation" talk is doing more work than its earned? Raw neurons are famously polysemantic -- the standard story is that the network is packing in more features than it has dimensions for and leaning on their sparsity to avoid too much interference (people call this "superposition") -- and the classic illustration is a neuron that fires for cat faces and the fronts of cars, for no principled reason anyone has found. That's the whole reason people reach for these autoencoders in the first place, so its not as if there's some obvious ground truth sitting there waiting to be read off.

I brought this up over dinner with Dmitri, a colleague from CS who works on interpretability, and Renata, who's in my own department and works on philosophy of neuroscience, and it turned into a real argument I keep replaying. Dmitri kept leaning on the causal evidence: ablate the feature and the specific capability drops out, clamp it and you get the behavior on demand, features "split" into more specific children in an orderly way as you grow the dictionary, and similar features keep turning up in models trained completely independently -- which is exactly the kind of manipulable, convergent structure that earns realist talk about orientation columns or place cells. Renata wasn't buying it: sparse autoencoders are dictionary learning, and dictionary learning finds *a* sparse basis that minimizes a loss, not *the* basis the network "really" uses -- tune the sparsity penalty or the dictionary size differently and you get a different, equally valid decomposition, so nothing forces "Golden Gate Bridge" to be a real unit of computation rather than a human-flattering artifact of wherever we happened to shine the light. At one point she told him he'd discovered Rorschach blots and mistaken them for anatomy. He said that if that's all this was, steering vectors wouldn't transfer to held-out prompts the way they demonstrably do.

Could you write out a continuation of this dialogue, maybe five or six more exchanges, where each of them actually has to answer the other's last point instead of just restating their own? I want Dmitri to say something better than "but steering works" when the underdetermination worry comes back around, and I want Renata to really engage with the causal and cross-model evidence instead of retreating to "it's all just matrices" -- I don't want either of them winning on a technicality. If they end up circling the question of whether "representation" requires something beyond reliable causal-and-predictive usefulness -- i.e., whether this is a real metaphysical disagreement or just a fight over how loosely to use a word -- great, but don't let them resolve it; I want the shape of the actual disagreement, not a tidy ending.
