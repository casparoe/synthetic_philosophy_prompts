I'm about two-thirds through a novel whose central figure is a fictional logician modeled loosely on the great logicians working around the turn of the twentieth century — a man who spent forty years trying to scour every trace of psychology out of arithmetic, insisting that a thought is what it is regardless of who thinks it or how they happen to feel. After his death, the woman who was his favorite student, now his literary executor, finds a private notebook full of the kind of unguarded antisemitism and contempt for democracy the old man never let anywhere near his published work. I want that discovery, and her reckoning with it, to be the emotional spine of the book's second half, and I don't want to let the philosophical question underneath it off easy.

The premise is lifted from a real case: Gottlob Frege's 1924 diary, kept the year before he died and not seriously reckoned with by English-language philosophers until long afterward, which turns out to be full of contempt for Jews, for parliamentary democracy, and for the Weimar Republic generally, alongside a wish for some strongman to sweep it all away. Michael Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to install Frege as the founding father of analytic philosophy, later wrote candidly about how much the discovery unsettled him. The detail I can't stop turning over is this: Frege's entire case for logical objectivity rests on there being, in his own phrase, a "third realm" (drittes Reich) of thoughts existing wholly independently of any thinker's psychology — a coincidence of naming that looks uncomfortably pointed only in hindsight, since "Drittes Reich" is of course also the name German nationalists would soon claim for the authoritarian state his diary seems to be hoping for.

Before I draft the executor's confrontation scene, I want the argument she has with a skeptical colleague to be a real one rather than a strawman on either side. So: lay out, as strongly as you can, both the case that none of this should touch our assessment of Frege's logic (the genetic-fallacy response, available to anyone) and the case that it is specially awkward for Frege in particular — that a philosophy staked entirely on the thinker's irrelevance to the thought becomes, in an interesting way, harder rather than easier to defend once the thinker's own non-logical judgment turns out to have failed so badly. Then tell me, if you're willing to commit, which way you actually lean.
