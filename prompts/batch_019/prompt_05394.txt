I was appointed last month to the Professional Conduct Committee at the school of nursing where I teach clinical skills, and my very first case has turned out to be more philosophically fraught than I expected. A second-year student runs a monetized, age-verified adult-content subscription page under a pseudonym. It does not feature the school's name, a uniform, patients, or any clinical setting, and nothing about her academic standing or clinical performance has ever been in question. A classmate who recognized her filed a complaint describing the page as "unbecoming of a future nurse," and the case now turns entirely on our code's catch-all clause barring conduct that "brings the profession or the institution into disrepute" -- language we have previously applied only to things like exam cheating or a DUI arrest, where the connection to fitness to practice was obvious. Here it isn't. The committee has split cleanly: some members argue that nursing depends on a public trust that itself rests on a shared, ordinary sense of propriety, and that protecting that shared sense is exactly what the clause is for, even absent a single identifiable victim; others think that, absent harm to a patient, a colleague, or the school, invoking "disrepute" here simply launders moral distaste into policy. I sat through enough legal philosophy as an undergraduate to recognize the shape of this disagreement, but I never expected to be the one drafting a resolution to it.

Could you write the memo I will circulate to the full committee before we vote next week? It should recommend one specific disposition of the case -- dismissal, an informal warning, a formal hearing, or some other concrete step -- along with a timeframe for whatever follows, and it should separately advise whether the disrepute clause needs to be narrowed before it is invoked again. I would like the strongest version of the public-trust argument set out on its own terms, not as a stand-in for disgust, before you explain whether it actually carries the weight its proponents on the committee want it to bear, and I would like you to say plainly what, if anything, would distinguish a legitimate appeal to the profession's shared standards from simply relabeling distaste as harm.
