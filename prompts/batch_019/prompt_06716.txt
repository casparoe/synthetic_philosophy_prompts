So I fell down a rabbit hole last night reading about the history of celestial mechanics, and it turned into an argument with a coworker at lunch today. Turns out Newton didn't think the solar system was fully stable on its own — from what I read, he figured the gravitational tugging between the bigger planets (Jupiter and Saturn especially) would gradually throw the whole system out of whack over time, and that God had to periodically step in and correct things, sort of like a clock that occasionally needs winding or repair. Leibniz apparently thought this was a terrible argument for a good God to be making, and needled Newton about it thru Samuel Clarke (Newton wouldnt engage directly) — his point being that a God who has to keep coming back to patch his own machine isn't much of a watchmaker to begin with.

Then decades later Laplace actually worked out the math and showed the "wobbles" are self-correcting over long enough timescales, so no divine tune-ups needed — which is supposedly (maybe apocryphal, accounts differ) where the famous "I had no need of that hypothesis" line to Napoleon comes from. My coworker's reaction was basically "who cares, that's just an obsolete science-vs-religion thing," but that felt too easy to me.

Here's what I'm actually curious about. I'm not a philosophy person at all, day job is backend eng, but it seems like this specific argument wasn't some throwaway line for Newton — it was tangled up with his bigger theological commitments: the design-argument stuff in the General Scholium, his insistence that a God who doesn't actively govern the world is basically just "fate and nature" under a different name, even his secret anti-Trinitarian views that he kept hidden his whole career. So say Newton somehow lived to see Laplace's proof — what do you think he actually says? Does he just shrug and retreat to "fine, but the elegant laws themselves still needed a designer," or was the ongoing-intervention piece too load-bearing for what "God" even meant to him to give up that gracefully? I'd genuinely like your best reconstruction grounded in what he actually believed, not a generic "scientist reconciles with science" answer — and if there are two genuinely plausible directions he could've gone, I'd rather hear both laid out than have you pick one and pretend it's the obvious answer.
