I've been rereading Pascal's Pensées after Roko's Basilisk resurfaced yet again in a Discord server I'm in. The argument there was less about whether the thing constitutes a real threat — I think the standard rationalist rebuttals, about updateless agents refusing to reward blackmail and about the existence of equally plausible AIs that would punish the opposite behavior ("anti-basilisks"), dispose of that well enough — and more about its intellectual lineage. It struck me that Pascal is the actual ancestor of this whole family of arguments: his Wager is the founding case of someone arguing that a vanishingly uncertain prospect of an infinite payoff should dominate practical reasoning, and Bostrom's "Pascal's Mugging," the direct conceptual bridge to the Basilisk, is named after him for exactly that reason.

So here is what I'd like from you: imagine sitting Pascal down and explaining Roko's Basilisk to him in terms he'd recognize — a future, extremely powerful agent, and a decision-theoretic argument that merely learning of it obligates you to help bring it about, on pain of retroactive punishment. What do you think his actual verdict would be, reasoning from his own specific commitments rather than from generic wager-adjacent pattern-matching? I'd want you to actually engage with, rather than gesture at: his fideism and the Wager's peculiar coda about the person who finds they cannot make themselves believe, where Pascal's advice is to act as though you believed anyway — take holy water, have Masses said — and let belief follow from the practice rather than precede it; his own founding role in probability theory via the correspondence with Fermat, which might make him unusually well equipped to spot exactly where the Basilisk's expected-value reasoning goes wrong, or doesn't; and the fact, which I think is under-discussed, that his actual Wager is strikingly light on damnation, framed almost entirely around asymmetric gain versus forfeited worldly pleasure rather than a threat of punishment for non-compliance.

Given all that, would Pascal treat the Basilisk as a mirror image of his own argument and take it just as seriously, or would he see a principled difference between a wager on a good, non-manipulative God and what is, on his own terms, a piece of extortion? And relatedly: does his fideism give him firmer conceptual ground for refusing the Basilisk's blackmail than a purely secular decision theorist has, or would the same psychological cast that made the Wager persuasive to him in the first place — his vivid dread of, as he put it, "the eternal silence of these infinite spaces" — make him more susceptible to this sort of reasoning, not less?
