I've spent a lot of time reading LessWrong-adjacent blogs and forums where the Bayesian treatment of conditionals is treated as close to definitional: your credence in "if A, then B" just is your conditional credence P(B|A). A friend who does formal epistemology recently disabused me of the idea that this is just obviously correct, pointing me to David Lewis's 1976 triviality proof, which shows that this equation — sometimes called Adams's Thesis, or just "the Equation" — cannot hold across the board without collapsing into absurdity. The proof itself is almost embarrassingly short: partition on B and not-B, apply the Equation to each of the two resulting conditionalized credence functions, recombine by the law of total probability, and you're forced to conclude that P(B|A) = P(B) for essentially any A and B whatsoever — obviously false for any interesting probability function. Lewis revisited the result a decade later in a sequel paper and didn't back down from it.

What I keep going back and forth on is what to actually conclude from this. Stalnaker wants to preserve indicative conditionals as ordinary propositions, with truth conditions given by something like the selection-function semantics he uses for counterfactuals, and to block the proof by insisting that the Equation only has to hold for an agent's actual credence function at a time, rather than across the whole family of credence functions reachable from one another by conditionalization. Adams and Edgington, on the other hand, take the result at face value and conclude that indicative conditionals simply lack truth conditions: their assertibility tracks conditional probability, but there's no proposition there for an unconditional probability to attach to in the first place. The restriction move is attractive because it keeps ordinary compositional semantics intact, but it has always struck me as a little ad hoc, given how natural the closure-under-conditionalization assumption seems for anyone who thinks rational updating just is conditionalization — and I recall Hájek having catalogued further results showing that even quite minimal versions of that assumption suffice to reinstate triviality, though I don't remember the details well enough to reconstruct them myself.

Please argue for the position that Lewis's triviality result is best read as evidence that indicative conditionals do not express propositions at all — that something in the vicinity of the Adams/Edgington view is correct — rather than as a technical constraint that merely tells us to restrict the scope of the Equation. In making the case, reconstruct the triviality proof precisely enough that your argument doesn't just gesture at the result, engage with the strongest version of the Stalnaker-style restriction strategy rather than a strawman of it, and address the standard objection to "no truth value" views: that conditionals seem to embed perfectly felicitously under negation, conjunction, and as antecedents of further conditionals, which seems to demand exactly the kind of propositional content that such views deny them.
