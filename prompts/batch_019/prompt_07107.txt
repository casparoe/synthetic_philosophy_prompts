I'm a novelist, and I'd like help thinking through an episode from a writers' residency last spring that I still haven't resolved for myself — not least because a version of it has since worked its way into the novel about literary ambition I'm currently drafting. A considerably more established writer, whose blurb could plausibly decide whether my next book gets reviewed anywhere that matters, offered to write me one on the condition that I first supply a blurb for his own new novel. I read his manuscript twice, in good faith, genuinely hoping to find something to praise, and concluded that I couldn't: the prose is inert, the structure mistakes withholding for depth, and the characters exist mainly to illustrate a theme rather than to live outside one. When I told him, as gently as I could, that I wasn't able to write something I didn't believe, he took no offense; he said that given what the exchange could do for my career, I had every reason to admire his novel, and that most writers, in his experience, came around to admiring a book quite sincerely if they sat with it long enough under those conditions.

That response has stayed with me longer than an insult would have. I clearly have overwhelming reason to want to admire his novel, and perhaps even reason to work at bringing myself around to it, but I can't make myself believe that wanting to admire it for those reasons is the same as its deserving admiration — even though I can't cleanly say why the two should come apart, especially if he's right that people in my position often do end up sincerely convinced. My writing group split down the middle over this: half think he made a manipulative but basically valid argument, half think he only redescribed an ordinary self-deception in courteous terms. Before I decide whether to write the character based on him as making an honest mistake or being quietly cynical, I'd like to know which half is closer to right. Does the fact that so much rides on this really give me a reason to admire the novel — the same kind of reason his prose or his plotting would give me, if either were any good — or has he made a genuine error, and if so, could you help me locate it precisely?
