I spent thirty-five years doing experimental condensed-matter physics and, aside from the general relativity course I took as a graduate student, never had much occasion to think about the foundations of the theory. Since retiring, I have joined a small philosophy-of-science discussion group at the local university, and this term we have been working through the literature on spacetime substantivalism, culminating last week in a session on Earman and Norton's "hole argument." I believe I have the technical setup straight: take a solution (M, g_ab) of the Einstein field equations, choose some small, source-free region H (the "hole"), and construct a diffeomorphism d that is the identity everywhere outside H but differs smoothly from the identity somewhere inside it. Because the field equations are generally covariant, dragging the metric along by d produces another solution, one that agrees with g_ab everywhere outside H, including on any Cauchy surface lying to its past, while disagreeing with it inside H. So the entire causal history leading up to the hole apparently fails to fix what the metric does inside it.

What I haven't been able to talk myself out of is the sense that this is a genuinely alarming result about general relativity's predictive content, not so different in kind from the indeterminism physicists invoke to describe quantum measurement outcomes, rather than a curiosity confined to metaphysicians. If two models can be made to agree on literally everything up to the boundary of a region and still disagree inside it, shouldn't relativists running, say, a numerical simulation of a black hole merger worry that their initial data leaves the outcome genuinely unsettled, in the way an experimentalist would worry if two runs with identical initial conditions could yield different readings on the apparatus? Our facilitator suggested that this worry dissolves on closer inspection, but, before our next meeting, I would like to understand exactly why: is there a rigorous sense in which the hole argument's indeterminism has no bearing on anything a physicist would actually predict or measure, or have I simply been shown an elegant philosopher's way of making peace with a real gap in the theory's determinism?
