I've long been a reader of the rationalist blogs and forums where precommitment devices are a recurring topic -- Schelling on the strategic value of binding oneself, Beeminder-style commitment contracts for beating akrasia, and so on -- and a recent conversation with some friends about that literature sent me down a path I hadn't expected: noticing that the term "Ulysses contract" is actually used for two quite different things, and wondering whether the comparison between them is as clean as it's usually assumed to be.

The first is the individual-level use from psychiatric ethics: a patient with a condition like bipolar disorder, while lucid and legally competent, signs an advance directive instructing doctors to have her involuntarily hospitalized and medicated the next time she becomes manic, overriding whatever objections she raises at the time. The second is Jon Elster's well-known extension of the same Odysseus-at-the-mast image to constitutions: bills of rights, supermajority amendment rules, and judicial review as devices that let a polity's more reflective self bind its own future majorities against momentary passion. Elster developed this application across _Ulysses and the Sirens_ and then substantially qualified it in the later _Ulysses Unbound_, growing considerably more doubtful that constitution-makers are really doing anything like what Odysseus did.

Here is what I can't settle on my own, and what I'd like you to work through carefully rather than gesture at. The standard objection to the psychiatric case is that a manic episode might not be mere irrationality being restrained by a wiser self, but could express something more like a genuine, if unwelcome, shift in what the person values -- making the directive less like Odysseus overruling a temporary delusion and more like one person overruling another. The standard objection to the constitutional case, going back to Jefferson's argument with Madison over whether the dead can bind the living, is that a later generation is not even numerically the same agent as the one that wrote the constraint -- unless, that is, one follows Bruce Ackerman in relocating the relevant agent from a past generation to "the People" acting in its higher, constitution-making capacity, as opposed to its ordinary-politics capacity, a move that starts to look structurally similar to distinguishing a patient's lucid, planning self from her manic, acting self. The question, then, is whether these two objections are really the same worry wearing different vocabulary -- personal identity in one case, popular sovereignty in the other -- such that whatever rescues one case ought to rescue the other, or whether one of the two uses of "Ulysses contract" is resting on much shakier ground than the other for reasons that have nothing to do with the other case at all. I'd like an actual verdict rather than a survey of considerations: which of the two is the more defensible way to bind a future self against its own contemporaneous wishes, and does Elster's retreat between his two books track a genuine asymmetry between the cases, or was he simply being overcautious?
