So in my philosophy of physics seminar we spent the first couple weeks on the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence (the whole debate over whether space is a real container or just a system of relations between things) and now this week we've moved on to Bell's theorem and the whole locality/realism mess. I can't stop thinking about how weirdly relevant Leibniz's own system might be to this, even though obviously he never heard of entangled photons or anything like that.

Here's the thing that's bugging me: Leibniz's monads famously don't causally interact at all, each one just unfolds according to its own internal principle, and the fact that they all seem to correspond with each other (mind and body, one monad's perceptions and another's) is down to pre-established harmony - God set the whole thing up in advance so it all matches without any real influence crossing between substances. That sounds a lot like a "local" story about how two seperated particles could be correlated without any signal passing between them. But then I remembered Leibniz is also totally committed to the Principle of Sufficient Reason, which seems to rule out the idea that an experimenter's choice of measurement setting is ever some kind of free, uncaused event - everything has to trace back to reasons/causes going all the way back. So it kind of seems like Leibniz's system might actually commit him to something close to superdeterminism (denying measurement independence) rather than just an innocent local hidden-variable story, if he wants to keep pre-established harmony in light of Bell's theorem.

What I'd like from you is an actual reconstruction of the Leibnizian position here, not just a "he'd probably say X" one liner - which of his actual doctrines (PSR, pre-established harmony, the relational theory of space from the Clarke letters, his views on contingency and possible worlds) would he lean on, and where does the analogy between pre-established harmony and local hidden variables get stretched too thin or break down completely. Would he happily bite the superdeterminism bullet since he never believed in libertarian free choice anyway, or is there something else in his metaphysics that gives him an escape route Bell never considered? my professor joked that basically every "solution" to Bell's theorem is just a different flavor of giving up on locality, realism, or free choice/measurement independence, and im curious which flavor you think Leibniz ends up stuck with.
