A few weeks of burnout and sleep deprivation left me with a stretch of depersonalization-derealization: my perception and thinking kept functioning normally, but the "felt" quality I ordinarily attribute to seeing colors, tasting food, or feeling emotions seemed to drain away, leaving me oddly convinced that my experience wasn't quite what it presented itself as. Trying to make philosophical sense of this afterward, I came across illusionism about phenomenal consciousness -- the view, developed by Keith Frankish and embraced in some form by Daniel Dennett, that our sense of having rich, intrinsic qualia is itself produced by an introspective mechanism that misrepresents our mental states rather than accurately reporting them.

What I can't get past is an objection I keep running into, one pressed forcefully by critics such as Galen Strawson: if phenomenal consciousness is only an illusion, doesn't the very fact that it seems to me that I have rich qualia already constitute a phenomenally conscious event, so that illusionism just relocates the explanatory target rather than dissolving it? I'd like a careful explanation of how illusionists try to block this self-undermining charge -- specifically, what is supposed to license treating the introspective representation of an experience as separable from the experience's actually possessing the represented quality -- along with your own assessment of whether that move escapes the objection or merely restates it in more technical language.
