And here is the answer I need graded, reproduced exactly as submitted:

"According to the free-energy principle, any system that maintains itself in a limited repertoire of states over time must act so as to minimize its free energy, which is essentially another name for the prediction error generated by the mismatch between an organism's internal model and the sensory evidence it receives. Since minimizing free energy just is minimizing prediction error, and prediction error is smallest in an environment that never changes, critics have argued that the theory seems to predict that any organism should retreat to a dark, silent room and stay there, since once it adapted to such an environment almost nothing would ever violate its predictions again. This is the dark-room problem: real organisms do more or less the opposite, leaving comfortable and predictable environments to explore, forage, and seek out novel stimulation, which looks like the reverse of what surprise-minimization should produce.

Active inference theorists respond that organisms do not minimize prediction error against their sensory states alone; they minimize it against prior expectations that are already highly informative, for instance about being fed, warm, and safe. A dark room does not satisfy priors like these, so although it minimizes moment-to-moment sensory prediction error, it generates large prediction errors against the interoceptive and homeostatic expectations built into the organism's model, and the organism is driven to leave. What ultimately does the work here is precision: the brain assigns low precision, meaning low reliability or weight, to the prediction errors generated inside an unchanging room, since a signal that never varies carries little useful information, and low-precision errors cannot compete with the precision-weighted errors generated by unmet homeostatic priors elsewhere. So the organism leaves in search of the more precise evidence it needs to keep those deeper priors satisfied.

I think this response succeeds, although it is a little unsatisfying, since a critic could reply that the theorist is always free to posit whatever prior preference is needed after the fact to explain whatever behavior is actually observed, which threatens to make the free-energy principle unfalsifiable rather than genuinely predictive."
