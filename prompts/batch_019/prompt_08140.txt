Two people in an early modern philosophy reading group I'm part of (not one of my own classes, so I'm just an eavesdropper here) got into a real back-and-forth about Hobbes a couple weeks ago, and I honestly cant tell who came out ahead. We'd been working through the early chapters of Leviathan alongside some of the Six Lessons material, and somebody brought up Hobbes's decades-long insistence that he'd squared the circle, trisected the angle, all of it, even after Wallis and the Savilian crowd at Oxford spent years picking his "proofs" apart in one pamphlet after another. Two of the regulars, Dan and Priya, ended up arguing about how seriously to take this, and its been bugging me since.

Dan's opening move was that the whole episode is basically an embarrassing footnote: a genuinely brilliant guy getting stubborn and out of his depth in a technical field he'd only fallen for late in life (there's that Aubrey story about him discovering Euclid I.47 in his forties and getting hooked). On this view it tells you something about Hobbes's ego, not about his philosophy, and treating it as philosophically significant is over-reading a personality quirk. Priya pushed back that it's not incidental at all, because Hobbes's constructions only came out looking valid to him because he'd quietly rejected the standard definitions -- he wouldn't grant that a point has literally zero extension or a line zero breadth, since for a thoroughgoing materialist nothing without body can exist, so his bad geometry is downstream of his metaphysics rather than separate from it. Dan granted the heterodox-definitions point but said it proves less than she thinks, since even on Hobbes's own revised definitions Wallis was still able to locate flat-out errors in the actual constructions -- so there are two different mistakes being run together, a principled metaphysical one and a completely mundane one, and only the first is really "philosophical." Priya's comeback was that the two aren't so separable: once Hobbes had decided that geometry (like civil philosophy) is demonstrable precisely because we construct the objects ourselves -- his own line, more or less, about the two of them being the only genuinely demonstrable sciences for exactly that reason -- he'd licensed himself to treat any refutation as just a dispute over starting definitions rather than a real error, which is why he never once actually conceded to Wallis, decades of humiliation notwithstanding. So for her the persistence itself is evidence, not just the original slip. Dan's last word was that this cuts against Priya too: if "I get to redefine the primitives because I'm the maker" is a legitimate move, it's a license for any crank to insulate any claim from refutation, so maybe the real lesson isn't about Hobbes's geometry specifically but about how much weight that "we made it, so we can demonstrate it" idea can bear anywhere, geometry or politics.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just picking an overall winner? And if you think Dan and Priya are both missing some more accurate way of reading the Hobbes-Wallis affair, I'd like to hear that too. Feel free to get technical about the math/metaphysics side of things, this particular group won't mind.
