I spent most of my working life running a low-temperature transport lab, measuring the same quantized Hall plateaus that have supplied one side of a friendly argument I've been having since graduate school with Elias, a theorist who is retiring from our old department next month. Elias has never let me forget that the reason those plateaus sit at exact integer multiples of e²/h, in samples messy enough with disorder that no honest experimentalist would expect anything so clean, is that the conductance is quietly computing a topological invariant — a Chern number, the very piece of differential geometry Shiing-Shen Chern worked out in the 1940s with no thought of electrons at all. To him, the Thouless–Kohmoto–Nightingale–den Nijs result isn't just a beautiful explanation; it's a glimpse of mathematics dictating terms to physical reality rather than merely describing it.

Only since retiring myself, and finally having the time to read the philosophy that thirty years of grant applications never left room for, have I understood how deep this disagreement actually runs. I don't doubt that the result is one of the most startling in twentieth-century physics; I doubt that it needs a metaphysics. Hamming's rejoinder to Wigner's old paper on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, with its considerably less mystical list of explanations — that we discard the mathematics that doesn't fit, quietly retrofit our theories until it does, and were, in any case, produced by an evolutionary process that had every reason to make us good at noticing exactly this kind of pattern — has left me thinking the effectiveness is real without being a small miracle. Elias regards that whole line of reasoning as a way of refusing to look the miracle in the eye.

I've been asked to give the toast at his retirement dinner, to a room that will be roughly half theorists and mathematicians who could referee this argument themselves, and half spouses, former students, and at least one dean who has never heard of a Chern number and shouldn't have to before dessert. I don't want to stand up and concede an argument I haven't actually lost, and I don't want to turn his own party into a seminar either — but pretending the disagreement away would feel like toasting someone other than the Elias I actually know. Could you write me a five-minute speech, built around the quantum Hall story, that keeps both of our positions honestly on the record, includes at least one line the theorists will get a beat before everyone else does, and still closes, in the end, as a tribute rather than a draw?
