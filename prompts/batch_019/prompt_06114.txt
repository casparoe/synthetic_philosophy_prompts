"Reconstruct Williamson's margin-for-error argument against the luminosity of 'feeling cold' (the gradual-warming case), and explain what the argument shows about the KK principle. Say a word about whether the argument turns on 'feels cold' being a vague predicate."

And here's the students answer, verbatim:

"The KK principle says that whenever you know P, you're in a position to know that you know P. Williamson's luminosity thesis is just this same idea generalized from knowledge to any condition at all: whenever a condition obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. His argument against luminosity goes like this. Suppose a subject sits outside from a freezing morning into a hot afternoon, and the temperature rises in increments so small that no two consecutive instants feel any different to her. At the first instant she's freezing and obviously knows it. Now, knowledge requires a margin for error: if she knows at instant i that she's cold, she must actually still be cold at instant i+1, because i and i+1 feel exactly alike to her, and if she could so easily have been wrong she wouldn't have known in the first place. If feeling cold were luminous, she'd know at every instant that she's cold for as long as she really is cold, and together with the margin-for-error step that means she has to stay cold at the next instant, and the next, all the way to the far end of the series — but by then she's hot, which is a contradiction. So feeling cold isn't luminous, and nothing in the reasoning depends on facts peculiar to coldness, so no contingent mental state is luminous. This is also why 'feels cold' has to be a vague predicate here: somewhere in the series there's an instant where it's genuinely indeterminate whether she feels cold, and that's what the argument is exploiting. Applied to knowledge itself, the moral is that Kp does not entail KKp in general, since knowing is itself a condition that isn't luminous."
