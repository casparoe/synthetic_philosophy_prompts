During a long career as an experimental physicist I had the physicist's usual mild impatience with questions that struck me as unanswerable even in principle, so it is a source of some private amusement that I have spent much of my retirement working through Wittgenstein with a small reading group at the local university. What is currently occupying us is whether his early and late remarks on religion really express one and the same underlying position. In the Tractatus he seems content to dispose of the whole territory in a handful of gestures: ethics, and by extension whatever falls under religion, is "transcendental," cannot be put into a meaningful proposition, and belongs among the things about which, per the famous closing line, one must simply keep silent. Yet in the 1938 lectures published long after his death as the Lectures on Religious Belief, he engages with religious belief at considerable length and in surprisingly concrete terms: he worries at length over what it would mean to believe in a Last Judgement, insists that such a belief does not function as an ordinary empirical hypothesis about which one could straightforwardly turn out right or wrong, and even suggests, disconcertingly, that a believer and a non-believer need not be understood as asserting and denying one and the same proposition. He is also notably unimpressed by Father O'Hara's attempt to defend the reasonableness of faith on broadly evidential grounds, treating this move as a kind of category mistake about what religious conviction even is.

I would like you to compare these two treatments directly. Do the austere silence of the Tractatus and the much more detailed, almost anthropological treatment of religious language in the later lectures express one and the same underlying attitude — namely, that religious and ethical commitments are simply not the kind of thing that could be true or false in the way ordinary empirical claims are — or does something more substantial change between the two periods, for instance because the vocabulary of "language-games" and "forms of life" that Wittgenstein was developing in this later period gives him a positive, descriptive way of talking about religious discourse that the picture theory of the Tractatus could never have supplied? I would especially value your view on whether the "no genuine contradiction between believer and non-believer" idea from the lectures is a natural extension of the earlier silence or whether it actually goes well beyond anything the Tractatus could have licensed, given that the Tractatus does not obviously leave room for meaningful, if non-fact-stating, discourse at all. Please justify whichever reading you ultimately settle on.
