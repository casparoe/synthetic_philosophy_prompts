I spent thirty-five years as a population geneticist, mostly running experimental-evolution work in Drosophila, and only came to philosophy after I retired, when a former colleague talked me into auditing a philosophy of mind reading group at the university where I used to keep an office. The group is mostly graduate students with a couple of retired scientists like me rounding things out, and this term we have been working through teleosemantics: the project, associated above all with Ruth Millikan, of explaining what makes a mental state or signal about anything at all by appealing to the biological function it was selected to perform. I find the basic move attractive — it explains misrepresentation cheaply, since a state can have the function of indicating rain and simply fail to obtain on some rainless Tuesday, and it fits my general preference for naturalistic accounts of the mind over anything that helps itself to a brute, unexplained relation of "aboutness." But something about it has been nagging at me as a working biologist, and I have written up the worry as a short piece I'd like your comments on before I bring it back to the group, who I suspect will be too polite to a retiree to tell me if I have embarrassed myself.

My complaint is not the two objections that come up first in every seminar — Swampman, and the "disjunction problem" of whether the frog's snapping reflex is really about flies or merely about small, dark, moving things. Those get plenty of attention already, and I don't have much to add to them. Mine is further back, about the notion of "proper function" itself. Here is what I have so far:

Teleosemantics and the Ghost of Adaptationism

Millikan's notion of proper function is explicitly historical: a trait token has the proper function of doing F just in case it belongs to a "reproductively established family" whose earlier members were reproduced because they did F, and their doing F is what accounts for that reproductive success. A mental representation means what it does, on this view, because the mechanisms that produce and consume it were shaped by selection to traffic in states correlated with some particular worldly condition. The theory earns its keep by making misrepresentation free: a state can be selected to indicate a condition and simply fail to obtain on a given occasion, without anything in the state's intrinsic makeup having to change for that failure to count as error rather than a change of subject.

The trouble is that "was reproduced because it did F" is a far more delicate historical claim than the theory's proponents seem to notice. Population genetics spent the back half of the twentieth century absorbing Gould and Lewontin's complaint that biologists reach for adaptive stories too readily, when a trait's presence may equally be explained by drift, by hitchhiking alongside a linked locus that was under selection, by a correlated response to selection acting on some quite different, pleiotropically linked trait, or by developmental constraint. And even a bona fide adaptation is very often a co-opted one: Gould and Vrba's notion of exaptation is built for exactly the case where a structure shaped by selection for one role — feathers for thermoregulation, say — is only later, without any selection "for" the new role at the time of the switch, pressed into service for another.

Apply this to a cognitive mechanism whose present job is representational. Even granting that it is an adaptation rather than a spandrel, teleosemantics needs the finer-grained fact that it was selected for tracking the particular condition we want it to represent, rather than being a by-product of selection for tracking something else entirely, or a capacity whose current use simply hasn't yet accumulated enough of a selective history of its own to fix any proper function at all. Millikan has resources here — what she calls derived proper functions, letting a device inherit a function from the mechanism that produced it rather than needing its own selection history, and perhaps other apparatus I have not come across in my reading — but this machinery looks to me built mainly for novel tokens of an already-settled type (this year's beaver dam, my belief that it is raining right now), not for the harder case of a whole capacity whose adaptive history is mixed, recent, or reconstructed after the fact to fit its present usefulness. My suspicion is that for many of the capacities philosophers most want teleosemantics to underwrite — general-purpose inference, abstract concept formation, perhaps most of human belief fixation — we are nowhere near being able to say which of these historical stories actually obtains, and may never be able to. If that is right, teleosemantics faces something worse than the familiar residual indeterminacy about which of several coextensive properties a state represents: for a large class of interesting cases, it faces indeterminacy about whether there is any determinate proper function on the table to interpret in the first place.

I would be grateful for your comments on a few specific things. First, have I got Millikan's apparatus roughly right, or is derived proper function doing more work than I am giving it credit for? Second, is this actually a distinct problem from the standard disjunction and indeterminacy worries, or have I just redescribed them in the vocabulary of population genetics to make them sound more threatening than they are? Third, what is the strongest reply available to a committed teleosemanticist — Millikan herself, or a later defender like Karen Neander — and does it actually succeed, or only relocate the difficulty? I would rather hear now, from you, exactly where I am overreaching, whether on the philosophy or on the biology, than find out in front of the reading group.
