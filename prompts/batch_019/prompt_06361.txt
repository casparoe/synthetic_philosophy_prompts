The exam question read: "In 'The Meta-Problem of Consciousness' (2018), David Chalmers distinguishes the meta-problem from the hard problem of consciousness. Explain this distinction, say why Chalmers regards the meta-problem as tractable in a way the hard problem allegedly is not, and explain what he takes to be at stake in the debate between illusionists and phenomenal realists once the meta-problem is solved. Answers should be no more than 450 words."

Here, reproduced exactly as submitted, is one student's answer:

"Chalmers's hard problem of consciousness asks why physical processes in the brain are accompanied by subjective experience at all -- why there is 'something it is like' to be a functioning brain rather than only unconscious information-processing. This is supposed to resist the kind of functional analysis that works for the 'easy problems,' such as explaining discrimination, reportability, or attention, because no matter how much functional structure we describe, it always seems coherent to ask why that structure should be accompanied by experience.

The meta-problem, by contrast, does not ask why we are conscious; it asks why we believe, or report, or find it so compelling to say, that we are conscious in this hard-to-explain way. That is, it asks for a functional/causal explanation of our 'problem intuitions' -- the judgments that experience seems ineffable, intrinsic, and irreducible to anything physical. Because this is fundamentally a question about behavior and judgment, it counts, in Chalmers's own terminology, as one of the easy problems: in principle, cognitive science could trace the causal story that leads us to produce these reports, without ever having to settle whether the reports are true.

Chalmers thinks this matters because it gives illusionists like Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett a way to dissolve the hard problem rather than solve it: if we can give a complete causal explanation of why people insist that experience is irreducible, there is nothing further left over to explain, and phenomenal consciousness turns out to be, in Frankish's terms, no more real than a stage magician's illusion -- a trick the brain plays on itself, with no actual subject behind the curtain having any experience whatsoever. This is in fact Chalmers's own preferred resolution: once the meta-problem is solved, the hard problem simply disappears, because it was never a problem about some extra ingredient in the world, only ever a problem about why the brain generates the reports it does.

For realists about consciousness, solving the meta-problem is still valuable, because it isolates exactly what a theory of consciousness would additionally have to explain, but they deny that it removes the hard problem's force, since even a full causal account of our reports leaves open why any processing is accompanied by experience in the first place."
