I don't have any background in philosophy — I'm an electrician by trade — but a few years ago, after a long stretch on medication for anxiety, I got interested, in a completely untrained way, in questions about how something like a change in brain chemistry is even supposed to relate to a change in what you feel and do, as opposed to just running alongside it without actually producing it. That eventually led me to Jaegwon Kim's writing on mental causation, and I've since spent a fair number of evenings on it relative to how little of it I actually understood the first time through.

A friend of mine, Reema, is taking a philosophy of mind module as part of her cognitive science degree, and since I'm the only person she knows who reads this stuff for fun, she asked me to look over her answer to a practice question on Kim's causal exclusion argument before she has to answer something similar under exam conditions next week. I'd like to give her feedback that's actually useful rather than just reassuring, but this is exactly the kind of material where I can follow an argument well enough to feel like an answer sounds right without being confident I'd personally notice if a step in it were subtly off. So before I write back to her, I wanted a second, more reliable read on it.

Here is the question, copied exactly as she received it: "Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument purports to show that non-reductive physicalism is an inherently unstable position. (a) Reconstruct the argument, making clear the roles played by the causal closure of the physical and the principle against systematic causal overdetermination. (b) Describe two strategies a non-reductive physicalist might use to resist the argument's conclusion, and briefly assess whether each one succeeds."

And here is what Reema wrote in response:

"(a) Non-reductive physicalism holds that mental properties are not identical to physical properties, but that they nonetheless supervene on physical properties: there can be no mental difference without some physical difference. This is usually combined with multiple realizability — the idea that the same mental property can be realized by different physical properties in different creatures, or even in the same creature at different times — which is precisely what stops the non-reductive physicalist from simply identifying mental properties with their physical bases.

Kim argues that this position cannot make sense of mental causation. Suppose a mental event M, realized on this occasion by physical property P, causes a physical event P*. The physical domain is causally closed: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. So P* must also have a sufficient physical cause, and the natural candidate is P itself, the very physical property that realizes M on this occasion. It looks, then, as if P* has two distinct sufficient causes, M and P. We should not accept that this kind of overdetermination happens systematically, the way we might reluctantly accept it in a rare case like a firing squad where two bullets strike a heart at the same instant. Since P is already doing all the necessary causal work, M's contribution becomes redundant, and generalizing this reasoning to any case where a mental event causes a physical event, Kim concludes that mental properties, if genuinely distinct from physical properties, cannot be causes at all: non-reductive physicalism collapses into epiphenomenalism about the mental.

(b) One strategy, going back to Donald Davidson's anomalous monism, is to identify mental events with physical events at the level of tokens while denying that there are any strict laws linking mental and physical types. Since M just is (token-identical to) some physical event, and that physical event is a cause of P*, M is a cause of P* too; there are not two events competing to cause P*, only one event under two descriptions. This directly defuses the overdetermination worry that drives Kim's argument, since the worry only arises if we think of M and P as distinct causes in the first place.

A second strategy accepts that M and P are both, in some sense, sufficient for P*, but denies that this is the objectionable kind of overdetermination, since M and P are not independent of one another the way the two bullets are: P realizes M, so M depends entirely on P for its existence on this occasion. Whether this succeeds is unclear, since it is exactly this question — whether 'dependent' overdetermination is any less mysterious than the independent kind — that Kim's argument was meant to press in the first place, so the response risks simply restating the problem as if it were a solution."
