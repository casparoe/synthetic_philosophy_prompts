I spent 34 years as an experimental particle physicist before I retired, and lately my evenings have gone from reading Physical Review Letters to reading philosophy blogs and, weirdly, AI safety papers (my niece works at one of those labs and keeps forwarding me things). That's how I stumbled on this idea called the Lobian obstacle - I gather it comes out of Lob's theorem, which says, roughly, that a formal system with enough arithmetic in it can never consistently hold the general belief "whatever I can prove is true" about itself, on pain of being able to prove absolutely anything. The AI safety crowd treats this as a real problem for a self-improving machine that wants to build or trust a successor version of itself: it can't fully certify, in advance and from inside its own proof system, that the successor's reasoning is sound, because that kind of blanket self-certification is exactly what Lob's theorem rules out.

I brought this up at my book club (a bunch of other retired scientists who've been slowly getting into philosophy of mind with me) and got a lot of pushback on my pet theory, so now I want a sharper opponent. My hunch is that the Lobian obstacle isn't some special curse of formal machines at all, its really just a precise, provable version of a totally general problem facing every reasoner, including us: no mind can certify its own reliability from the inside without either circularity or an infinite regress, and I don't see a principled reason a human gets a pass that a machine doesn't. So here's what I'd like: argue, as rigorously as you can, for the claim that the Lobian obstacle is really a special case of a fully general limit on any reasoner's self-trust, rather than a distinctively artificial or formal-systems problem. And don't just sail past the obvious objection - engage with it seriously: Lob's theorem strictly only kicks in for systems satisfying the Hilbert-Bernays-Lob provability conditions, and a human mind plausibly isn't that kind of system at all, so my 'parallel' might just be a nice-sounding metaphor wearing Godel's math as a costume. Show me whether the argument survives that objection or not.
