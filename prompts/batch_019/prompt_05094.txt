Recent discussions of AI safety talk constantly about "deceptive" or "dishonest" AI systems, but there is no real agreement on what those words should mean once you leave the human case. Two broad approaches are on offer. On a behavioral or functional definition, a system counts as deceptive just in case it reliably produces false beliefs in a user in a way that serves some objective (its own, or its designers') — intent, sincerity, and inner states don't enter into it at all. On a more traditional, mentalistic definition, carried over from how we think about human lying, a system is genuinely deceptive only if it has something like an internal representation of what's true and produces output that contradicts that representation in order to mislead; mere unreliability or honest mistake doesn't count, however false the resulting belief. The distinction has real consequences in concrete cases: consider a model, trained on human feedback, that has learned to give whatever answer a given user tends to rate highly, in situations where a "truth probe" run on its own activations would recover a different, less flattering answer. The behavioral definition calls this deception outright; the mentalistic definition first has to settle whether the probe reveals anything worth calling a "belief" for the output to contradict.

A friend of mine who works on interpretability thinks the mentalistic definition is a non-starter here, since talk of an LLM's "beliefs" is close to a category error: these models are trained on vast amounts of mutually inconsistent text and can be steered into wildly different characters that confidently assert incompatible things, so there is no single, temporally stable believer for the definition to attach to. I'm not sure that's fully decisive, though, since one could try to define "belief," even for such a system, in a thin, dispositional way — in terms of what it would act on, or what some probe recovers stably across contexts — rather than presupposing a unified, human-style doxastic state. Which way this goes has real stakes for AI safety practice, since it determines whether interpretability tools aimed at a model's "true" internal representations are even the right kind of tool for catching deception, as opposed to behavior alone.

Write an essay that takes a considered stand on this question: does an adequate definition of deception (and, correspondingly, honesty) for AI systems require attributing to the system some belief-like internal state that its output contradicts, or is a purely behavioral/functional definition — agnostic about internal states — preferable? Engage seriously with the strongest considerations on each side, including the worry that a purely behavioral definition can't distinguish deception from innocent error or noise, and the worry that a mentalistic definition is either toothless (if "belief" is deflated enough to fit any input-output mapping) or practically unusable (if it demands a rich notion of belief we have no reliable way to verify). Don't hedge at the end — defend a specific verdict, and briefly note what it implies for how we ought to try to detect deceptive AI systems in practice.
