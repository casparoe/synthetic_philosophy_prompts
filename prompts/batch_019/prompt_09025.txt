I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and together with a couple of people from my cohort I run a small departmental podcast in which we interview philosophers working on the more analytic end of philosophical theology. I came across David Lewis's short, often-overlooked 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" while chasing a footnote for my dissertation on divine and human retribution, and was struck that a philosopher best known for modal realism and counterpart theory had ever ventured into atonement theology at all -- let alone by trying to rescue the doctrine with an analogy to the transfer of a debt.

Our guest next month has just finished a monograph that revives exactly that move as the strongest going defense of penal substitution, and in the only interview of hers I've read so far, she brushed off the standard retributivist objection -- that, unlike a debt, a penalty can't simply be discharged by whoever happens to bear it, since punishment is supposed to target the wrongdoer specifically -- by saying the objection just "begs the question about what punishment is even for."

I'd like eight or nine interview questions that don't let her simply restate the debt analogy. Please include at least one question on each of the following: (a) why the debt/desert disanalogy should count as a genuine objection rather than a question-begging assumption about what punishment is for; (b) what is actually doing the licensing work in Lewis's swap and substitution scenarios -- the substitute's consent, the wrongdoer's, or the relevant authority's -- and whether the Christological case can help itself to whichever answer she gives; and (c) whether her defense quietly trades a retributive theory of punishment's point for a more consequentialist or "vindication of the law" one, and if so, whether that concedes exactly what her critics were asking for in the first place. For each, add a brief follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close the sequence with a question that returns to Lewis's own parting doubt -- whether a logically tidied-up version of the doctrine is still recognizably what believers take themselves to be committed to -- so that the "begs the question" deflection has nowhere left to retreat.
