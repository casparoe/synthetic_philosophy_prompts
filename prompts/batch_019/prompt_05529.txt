I fell down a youtube rabbit hole last night watching videos about AI decision theory stuff and ended up on this old philosophy example called Parfit's hitchhiker. The setup is basically: you're dying of thirst out in the desert, a driver pulls up and says he'll only give you a lift into town if you sincerely promise to pay him once you get there, and he's supposedly near-perfect at telling when someone's lying so he won't be fooled by an empty promise. Fine so far. But then once your actually in town, safe, there's nothing left to gain from handing over the cash since the ride already happened, so the "rational" thing (just maximizing your own good from that point on) is to keep the money and walk away.

Someone in the comments called this "rational irrationality" and it kind of clicked for me that this basically proves true rationality is just cold self-interest all the way down, and the only reason any of us actually pay up (or keep promises like that in general) is because evolution gave us some kind of irrational glitch, like guilt or honor, that overrides our real rational judgement in a lucky way. So decent, trustworthy behavior only survives because most people aren't fully rational, and "rational irrationality" is basically philosophers admitting that rationality and being a good person are opposites. Is that basically the right way to read it, or is there something about the thought experiment (or that phrase) that I'm missing?
