As a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, I've never been fully convinced by Eliezer Yudkowsky's proposed escape from the Euthyphro dilemma in the old LessWrong metaethics sequence. Rather than grounding rightness in divine command, he locates it in one specific, extremely complex computation -- roughly, whatever it is our brains are imperfectly running when we reason about fairness, suffering, and autonomy -- and argues that the word "right" rigidly designates that computation rather than whatever any authority (a god, a future superintelligence, one's own extrapolated volition) happens to output. Because the referent is fixed independently of any authority's say-so, he takes the arbitrariness horn to be dodged. Because he denies there is any further, independent standard the computation needs to answer to, he takes the independence horn to be dissolved rather than resolved. His parable of alien "pebble-sorters," who prize heaps of prime-numbered pebbles the way we prize fairness, is meant to illustrate that this can be done without lapsing into full relativism.

Please argue for the position that individuating "the right computation" by its content -- the fact that it happens to care about fairness and welfare rather than prime-numbered heaps -- covertly reintroduces exactly the independent standard the dissolution was supposed to make unnecessary, so that the rigid-designation move does no real work against the original dilemma. Engage with the strongest Yudkowskian reply available before explaining why it ultimately fails.
