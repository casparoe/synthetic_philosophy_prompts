I'm teaching a graduate seminar on conditionals this term and I've hit a wall with an exercise I keep trying to make work. We spent the first couple of weeks on the standard cases for the indicative/subjunctive split -- Adams' famous pair about Oswald ("if Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" vs. "if Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have"), plus a few of the other classics from Gibbard and Lewis. Problem is, by now my students have basically memorized the standard gloss -- indicatives track whats left open given what we actually know, counterfactuals hold the actual past fixed and just vary the antecedent world, and that's supposedly why the truth values come apart -- without being able to generate the phenomenon themselves. Every time I ask for a new example on a problem set they just recycle Oswald and Kennedy with the names swapped out. I was grumbling about this to a colleague over coffee and neither of us could produce a clean non-Oswald example on the spot either, which is a little embarrassing given what we do for a living.

So here's what I actually want from you. Construct a brand new minimal pair for me: same antecedent, same consequent, one phrased indicatively and one counterfactually, set in some domain that has nothing to do with assassinations, crime, or politics (I am so tired of every example in this literature being about someone getting shot). It should be a pair where a careful, logically competent speaker would judge the two conditionals to have different truth values, or at least wildly different assertability, even though on the surface they look like "the same conditional" just in a different mood. Then walk me through exactly what's doing the work in your example -- is it a difference in which background facts get held fixed, a difference in the similarity ordering over worlds, an epistemic-vs-metaphysical-modality thing, or something else entirely.

One more thing, purely out of curiosity. Every version of this pair I've ever seen, including the ones my colleague and I tried to cook up over coffee, has the indicative come out true and the counterfactual come out false. Is that a deep fact about how the two constructions work, or just an accident of which examples people happen to have invented? If you can construct a case that goes the other way round, where its the counterfactual that's true and the indicative that sounds off, that would be amazing -- but don't force it if it just doesn't exist.
