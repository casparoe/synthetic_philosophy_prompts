For a column on the hidden assumptions behind "utilitarian" arguments in welfare economics, I've been working through Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: if each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, the social planner's preferences do too, and the social ranking also respects a Pareto condition over those same lotteries, then social welfare must equal a fixed weighted sum of the individual utility functions. An economist I interviewed keeps raising Peter Diamond's 1967 objection to this: because the Pareto condition is imposed on individual utilities before any uncertainty is resolved, even an equally weighted version of Harsanyi's rule cannot prefer an arrangement that gives people a fair chance at an indivisible good over one that simply hands the good to a single person outright, whenever the two arrangements happen to yield the same expected sum of utility. Rather than the two-person, coin-flip case that shows up in most treatments of this objection, could you construct your own concrete scenario — a specific, real-world-flavored setting, at least three people, and explicit numerical utilities — that makes this indifference vivid? Please show the arithmetic confirming that the two arrangements really do tie under Harsanyi's rule, and then explain, in a sentence or two, exactly which premise of the theorem is responsible for that tie.
