Over the past year a personal preoccupation of mine has collided with a professional one, and I haven't quite been able to shake the collision. On the personal side, I've been sitting with a small vipassana group and slowly working through Vasubandhu's ninth-chapter treatise usually rendered in English as the "Refutation of the Theory of a Self" (the section appended to his Abhidharmakośabhāṣya), mostly to understand what the no-self doctrine actually commits you to beyond the self-help-book gloss of "there is no self." On the professional side, I write software for a lab that is, quite seriously, pursuing whole-brain-emulation research, and a recent internal argument about whether an emulated colleague's upload would "be him" in any sense that matters left me wanting to bring the two threads together.

What strikes me is that Vasubandhu already had to fight a structurally similar battle in his own context, just without the silicon. Arguing against the Pudgalavādins (the Vātsīputrīya-Sāṃmitīya school), who held that some kind of pudgala — a "person" that is neither identical to nor different from the five aggregates — was needed to make memory, moral responsibility, and rebirth across lives intelligible, Vasubandhu insisted that "person" is only a conventional designation (prajñapti) laid over a causally connected but strictly momentary series (santāna) of aggregates, with nothing that persists as a single real (dravya) entity even within one lifetime, let alone between two. One image the Pudgalavādins themselves offered for how a pudgala could depend on, yet not reduce to, the aggregates was fire in relation to its fuel; Vasubandhu spends real effort arguing that the analogy gets them nothing they couldn't already explain with bare causal succession.

I'd like you to reconstruct, as carefully as you can from these actual commitments rather than from the one-paragraph popular version of "Buddhism denies the self," how Vasubandhu would most plausibly rule on a case he never had to consider: a whole-brain emulation that goes on speaking, remembering, and acting like the person who was scanned, or a Parfit-style teleporter that reconstructs a body and brain at the destination while destroying the original at the source. Does his framework give any principled basis for saying the resulting continuant is owed the same concern, inherits the same obligations, or bears the same karmic responsibility as the original — or would he say that the whole question of "is it really the same person" is exactly the confusion the Pudgalavādins fell into, so that the honest response is to dissolve the question rather than answer it? I'd also be curious, if you have a view, whether his position ends up closer to or further from Derek Parfit's own reductionism about persons, given that Parfit reaches some superficially similar conclusions from an entirely different, non-karmic starting point.
