After forty years in soil chemistry, I retired three years ago and have since been volunteering with a watershed group that replants native sedges along eroded stream banks. In checking this year's figures, I found that bare-root plugs beat containerized plugs on our sandy-soil sites (70 percent one-year survival versus 60 percent) and also on our clay-soil sites (25 percent versus 15 percent) — yet when our coordinator pools every site together, containerized plugs come out ahead overall, 51 percent to 34 percent, and she wants us to switch entirely to those. Her arithmetic checks out. As best I can tell, the reason is that bare-root plugs happened to be planted mostly on our worst, clay-heavy sites, while containerized plugs went in mostly on the easier sandy ones. I've started reading a little philosophy since retiring, and I have a nagging feeling that this is a specific case of some more general problem rather than a fluke of our small dataset. Could you help me see what is actually going on here, and tell me which of the two recommendations — the coordinator's or my own instinct — the numbers genuinely support?
