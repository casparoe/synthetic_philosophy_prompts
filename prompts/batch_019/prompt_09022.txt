I'm assembling a collection of short fiction in which each story is built around a single, precisely stated philosophical problem, in the tradition of writers like Borges, Le Guin, and Ted Chiang. For the next piece, I want to work with Wittgenstein's notion of a "hinge proposition" from On Certainty: propositions such as that I have two hands, that the earth has existed for a long time, or that I have never been to the moon, which are not held fast because they have been checked and justified, but instead form the fixed framework within which doubting, checking, and giving reasons become possible at all — so that unsettling one of them isn't like unsettling an ordinary belief, but threatens to make the whole practice of doubt spin without anything to catch on. Could you write me a short story, under roughly 1,500 words, that dramatizes one character's hinge propositions coming loose? I want the wrongness to feel structural and specifically Wittgensteinian rather than a repackaged Cartesian brain-in-a-vat plot, so please steer clear of the well-worn "hands" example, and let the story convey the vertigo through texture and incident rather than through a character explaining the theory out loud.
