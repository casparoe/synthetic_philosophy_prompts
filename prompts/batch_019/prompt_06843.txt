I'm drafting a historical novel featuring Jeremy Bentham, and I'd like one scene to turn on the actual structure of his ethics rather than on invented conflict. Bentham grounds all morality in a calculus of pleasure and pain meant to secure the greatest happiness of the greatest number, and I understand he privately wrote essays defending the decriminalization of homosexual acts between consenting adults on precisely these utilitarian grounds. But it seems to me his own principle should cut the other way: if the aim is to maximize happiness across the greatest possible number of people, shouldn't procreative sex always count for more than acts that can never add new people, and their potential happiness, to the total? On that reading, his tolerance for such "irregularities," as he called them, looks like a lapse from his principles rather than an application of them. Before I write the scene, I'd like to know whether that's a genuine tension in his thought, or whether I've misunderstood how his felicific calculus is actually supposed to work.
