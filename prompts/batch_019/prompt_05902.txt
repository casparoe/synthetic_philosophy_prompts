During a long career studying alarm-call systems in vervet monkeys, I had little occasion for philosophy, but retirement has given me time to work through David Lewis's Convention, and one feature of it troubles me. Lewis holds that a regularity counts as a convention only if it is sustained by common knowledge within the population: everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and everyone knows this, knows that everyone else knows it, and so on without end. His own preferred illustration is a simple sender-receiver signaling game. Yet the evolutionary game theorists who later took up that very game - Skyrms's replicator-dynamics models, Peyton Young's adaptive-play learning - showed that a population can converge on one of several possible signaling conventions through nothing more than differential reinforcement of successful pairings, with no individual ever forming a belief about anyone else's beliefs, let alone an infinite regress of them. The alarm-call systems I spent my career studying display exactly the arbitrariness and self-perpetuation that a theory of convention is supposed to explain, in a species with no plausible capacity for the iterated mutual knowledge Lewis's account demands. Please argue that Lewis's common-knowledge condition is not actually necessary for a regularity to constitute a convention in the philosophically relevant sense - that evolutionary dynamics alone deliver everything his more demanding apparatus was meant to secure - and take on the strongest reason a defender of Lewis could give for thinking that mutual knowledge, rather than mere stable behavioral correlation, is indispensable to convention properly so called.
