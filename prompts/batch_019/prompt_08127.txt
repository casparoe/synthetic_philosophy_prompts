A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about whether Bentham's felicific calculus can really underwrite the tolerant conclusions he reaches in "Offences Against One's Self: Paederasty," the essay he drafted around 1785 but left unpublished for fear of scandal, and which was not printed in full until Louis Crompton edited it for the Journal of Homosexuality in 1978. My friend's position was that a consistent hedonistic utilitarian has to count the widespread disgust and antipathy Bentham's contemporaries felt toward 'unnatural' acts as a genuine pain, one that should enter the calculus and aggregate like any other, so that on Bentham's own terms the arithmetic could end up favoring suppression whenever that aggregate disutility is large enough. I couldn't tell whether Bentham actually has an answer to this worry or simply sidesteps it.

The passage that seems most relevant is this one:

"If this were admitted we should be forced to admit the propriety of applying punishment, and that to any amount, to any offence for instance which the government should find a pleasure in comprising under the name of heresy. I see not, I must confess, how a Protestant, or any person who should be for looking upon this ground as a sufficient ground for burning paederasts, could with consistency condemn the Spaniards for burning Moors or the Portuguese for burning Jews: for no paederast can be more odious to a person of unpolluted taste than a Moor is to a Spaniard or a Jew to an orthodox Portuguese. Besides this, the antipathy in question, so long as it subsists, draws with it in course, and without having recourse to the political magistrate, a very galling punishment, and this punishment is the heavier the greater the number of persons is by whom the antipathy is entertained and the more intense it is in each person."

Could you help me reconstruct exactly what argument Bentham is making here against antipathy as a 'sufficient ground' for punishment? I can't tell whether he means to exclude antipathy-based pain from the calculus altogether, or whether his point is instead that such pain is already 'priced in' elsewhere, through the informal punishment antipathy inflicts on its own, so that formal legal punishment on top of it becomes redundant. On either reading, does the argument actually block my friend's worry, or does it just relocate the problem?
