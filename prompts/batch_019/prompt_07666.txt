A few months ago I went down a Wikipedia rabbit hole that started with Hume's problem of induction and, to my surprise, ended up several centuries earlier with the medieval theologian al-Ghazali. What caught my attention wasn't just that he seems to have gotten there first, but why he cared about it in the first place. In the Incoherence of the Philosophers, he uses the example of cotton touching fire: we see the fire, and we see the cotton burn, but according to him we never actually witness the fire causing the burning, only the two things occurring one after the other, reliably enough that we've taken to calling it "necessary." His conclusion is that the fire has no power of its own at all; the real agent behind the burning, and behind literally every other event in the world, is God, acting directly on each individual occasion, and the only reason any of it looks like a law of nature is that God happens to act that way out of sheer habit. (I gather this general position even has a name, occasionalism, though obviously he wasn't calling it that himself.) As far as I can tell, he needed it to come out this way because if cause and effect were truly, logically necessary, there would be no room left for miracles: for fire that, on some particular occasion, simply fails to burn.

I can't stop turning over what it would actually be like to live inside that picture, not as a metaphor but as the literal architecture of the world: nothing has any causal power of its own, "natural law" is just God's extraordinarily consistent habit, and every event, a struck match, a dropped glass, someone's next heartbeat, is a fresh act of direct creation that merely resembles the one before it rather than following from it. So here is what I'd like: a short story, maybe 800 to 1500 words, that takes this completely seriously from the inside, rather than treating it as a curiosity. I don't want a story that's really about something else (AI, physics, or anything else) dressed up in al-Ghazali's ideas; I want the actual logic of his argument, habit rather than necessity, the fire that could fail to burn without any contradiction, to be doing the real narrative work, ideally through a character who starts noticing the "seams" in causation and has to decide what, if anything, to do about it. I'll leave the setting, period, and characters entirely up to you.
