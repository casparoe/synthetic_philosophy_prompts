so about two months ago I started a new job at the agency that decides whether our national drug plan pays for new treatments, and the tool we use for basically everything is the QALY (quality-adjusted life year) — you take some outcome, convert it into a slice of "healthy life" gained, and compare it against other outcomes regardless of who's getting them or how. Last week we had a case thats been bugging me: a very expensive drug for something like 30-40 patients a year with an awful degenerative condition, versus spending the same budget on a marginally better inhaler that gives a tiny quality-of-life bump to a couple million people with mild asthma. The QALY math wasn't close — fund the inhaler. I said "that feels wrong" in the meeting and my supervisor basically laughed and said I was just being scope-insensitive, same as everyone who flinches at Yudkowsky's old torture-vs-dust-specks post, the one where he argues you have to pick fifty years of torture for one guy over a dust speck landing in each of some absurdly huge number of people's eyes (3^^^3, I think, whatever that notation means exactly), because if you actually multiply it out the specks come out worse, and refusing to accept that is just your brain being bad at big numbers, not a real moral insight.

Here's the thing: I don't think I can just say "the rule of rescue matters to me" and leave it there, because thats exactly the move she's dismissing as bias. But I also don't think our drug-plan case and the torture/specks case are actually the same shape of problem, even if I can't yet say why without it sounding like special pleading. In the toy case there's one person choosing, once, between two fully specified outcomes that already involve fixed, named quantities of harm. What we do is set a policy in advance, for a population that doesn't exist as individuals yet, where the cost side stays statistical forever and the benefit side sometimes turns into an actual kid on the local news. Somthing about identified people vs. statistical people, or deciding ex ante versus judging ex post, or just persons not being fungible containers of utility, feels like it should matter here — but I can't get past the first "the numbers are still the numbers" objection on my own.

So I want you to actually make the argument for me, properly, not a wishy-washy both-sides thing. Grant, for the sake of argument, that Yudkowsky's right and you must choose torture over dust specks in his exact toy case — I don't want that part relitigated. Given that, I want the strongest case you can build against the generalization from that conclusion to "therefore a health system should let QALY aggregation override rule-of-rescue exceptions." Show me there's a real structural disanalogy doing legitimate philosophical work, not just an intuition I happen to like — and if the honest conclusion is that there isn't one, fine, but earn that conclusion, don't just default to "well, it depends."
