I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology, and this term the decision theory reading group I belong to has been slowly picking apart Nover and Hájek's 2004 paper introducing the "Pasadena game." I've followed the construction closely enough to reproduce it myself: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, on toss n; the payoff for that outcome is (−1)^(n+1) × 2^n / n; and since each such outcome has probability 1/2^n, the naive "expected value" of the game is the sum, over all n, of (−1)^(n+1)/n — the alternating harmonic series, which converges only conditionally, to ln 2, rather than absolutely. From there it is a short step, via the Riemann rearrangement theorem, to the conclusion that reordering those same countably many outcomes can make the series sum to any real number at all, diverge to +∞ or −∞, or oscillate forever without settling down.

What I can reproduce mechanically but still can't articulate well in my own words is why this is supposed to show that the game's expectation is genuinely undefined, rather than merely showing that one particular way of adding up the outcomes — in order of the number of tosses — happens to be an unusually delicate calculation. In the St. Petersburg game the expectation is likewise infinite, yet nobody feels the need to call it undefined, because every rearrangement of that all-positive series still diverges to +∞: the pathology there doesn't depend on the bookkeeping. When my reading group pressed our discussion leader on why the natural, toss-by-toss ordering isn't simply the privileged way of computing Pasadena's expectation too, the best answer on offer was that an expectation is supposed to be a single number representing the gamble's worth, and that such a number shouldn't be hostage to how we happen to partition an infinite set of mutually exclusive outcomes — which struck several of us as more a restatement of the puzzle than a diagnosis of it.

Could you offer a genuinely intuitive account (as opposed to a merely formally correct one) of what conditional convergence is doing here, and why it — rather than mere infinitude — is the real source of the indeterminacy? I would find it especially useful if you could explain why summing "in the order the tosses would actually occur in time" does not settle the matter the way it intuitively seems like it should, and whether there is a principled sense in which finite gambles and absolutely convergent infinite ones like St. Petersburg really do have determinate expectations while Pasadena-style gambles do not — or whether that distinction starts to look less stable the more closely one examines it.
