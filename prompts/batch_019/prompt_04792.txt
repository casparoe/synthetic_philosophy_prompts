While reading through some early cybernetics literature alongside more recent AI-safety papers, I noticed a striking continuity that I'd like your help thinking through. In his 1960 Science article "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation," Norbert Wiener argued that machines fast enough to act on their own conclusions before we can review them pose a distinctive danger, illustrating the point with the fable of the sorcerer's apprentice, whose enchanted broom cannot be recalled once set going, and warning more generally that "we may not know, until too late, when to turn it off." Read today, this looks like an almost exact statement of what contemporary AI-safety researchers call the shutdown problem, a specific instance of the broader corrigibility problem: how to design an agent that will neither resist being shut down nor manipulate the humans who control the shutdown button, without those safeguards costing you an agent that is otherwise less capable or less useful in every other respect.

One influential strand of recent formal work on this problem — descending from MIRI's 2015 "Corrigibility" paper and developed further in Elliott Thornley's more recent writing on an "incomplete preferences" solution — arrives at a striking diagnosis: an agent whose preferences are complete in the classical von Neumann–Morgenstern sense (meaning it has some preference, however slight, between literally every pair of outcomes) cannot be made safely shutdownable, because completeness forces it to weigh shutdown against every other goal it has, generating exactly the incentive to resist shutdown, or to manipulate its timing, that we want to avoid. The proposed remedy is to deliberately withhold completeness: to build agents with no preference at all between trajectories that differ only in when shutdown occurs, governed instead by a weaker "timestep dominance" principle rather than by expected-utility maximization across the board. The fix, in other words, requires giving up one of the very axioms of rational choice under uncertainty that von Neumann and Morgenstern had formalized within the same mid-century mathematical milieu that Wiener himself helped shape.

Given Wiener's own diagnosis of the control problem and his broader cybernetic commitments — feedback, homeostasis, and communication as the basis of purposeful behavior, in some tension with treating rationality as expected-utility maximization in the first place — how do you think he would have assessed this incompleteness-based solution? Would he likely have welcomed it as a natural formalization of the very worry he raised, or would he have judged it a partial or even mistaken remedy given his own theoretical commitments, and if the latter, what alternative kind of solution might his own framework have pointed toward instead? Please ground your answer in the actual substance of Wiener's arguments rather than his general reputation as an early worrier about automation.
