I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist is a logician, and her defining trait is a commitment to dialetheism -- the real philosophical position that some statements are both true and false -- which she applies in earnest to a case she's investigating, including to a piece of evidence about her own whereabouts on the night in question. While researching the view for the book, I learned that in classical logic, accepting even a single contradiction as true lets you validly derive absolutely any statement whatsoever, which seems to mean that, to remain consistent, she would actually have to believe every proposition there is. Assuming that's correct, should I write her subsequent reasoning in the novel as essentially arbitrary from that point onward, since none of her later deductions could be any more justified than their opposites once she has, in effect, committed herself to believing everything?
