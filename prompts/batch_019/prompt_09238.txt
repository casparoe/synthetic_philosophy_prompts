My aunt Miriam died ten days ago, suddenly, of a stroke, and I have been asked to give the eulogy at her memorial this coming Saturday. She was effectively a second mother to me growing up, and she was also, from the time she first read Peter Singer's drowning-child argument as a medical student — in a copy of his book that I, at nineteen and rather pleased with myself, gave her — the most thoroughgoing utilitarian I have ever known. She pledged early on to live for the rest of her life on a fixed sum, adjusted only for inflation, and to give away everything she earned above it; she spent long stretches abroad on outbreak-response and vaccination campaigns she considered more urgent than almost anything waiting for her at home; and she would, with no apparent bitterness, work out loud what a given expense cost in QALYs before deciding against it. She had read Susan Wolf's essay on moral saints and Bernard Williams's objection that a life like hers corrodes a person's integrity, and she had considered answers to both, including something close to Peter Railton's point that the right kind of consequentialist should love the people close to her without calculation, precisely because that disposition does more good than constant calculation would. None of this ever fully closed the wound at home: her son did not have his mother at his own wedding, because she was mid-deployment during an outbreak she would not leave, and as far as I know she never pretended to him that this had cost nothing.

She left instructions, relayed to me by her sister, that whoever spoke should not call her a saint or a martyr, and should not use the eulogy to apologize on her behalf for any particular choice, the wedding included. The room on Saturday will have that son, and a family that is genuinely divided on whether her life was admirable, sitting a few rows from people from the public-health and effective-altruism world who regarded her as close to a model of how to live, and who will notice immediately if I go soft on any of it. I took one ethics course a long time ago and am otherwise out of my depth here, but I don't want to write either a hagiography or a lecture, and above all I don't want the eulogy to quietly settle, in either direction, a tension she lived inside of for thirty years and never fully settled herself. Could you write it for me — about five minutes read aloud, warm rather than academic — so that it takes the cost to the people who loved her exactly as seriously as it takes the good she did, without letting either one cancel the other out?
