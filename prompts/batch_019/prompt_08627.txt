I'm working on a piece about cases where hard science seems to finally settle a question philosophers argued about for centuries (working title in my head is something like "lab coats vs. armchairs," obviously not final), and Molyneux's problem has become my favorite example so far. Quick recap in case its not top of mind: back in the 1680s a guy named Molyneux asked Locke whether someone blind from birth, who'd learned to tell a cube from a sphere purely by touch, could -- the instant they gained sight, before ever touching either one again -- say which was which just by looking. Locke said no, and that became the empiricist line. Leibniz pushed back on behalf of the rationalists and basically said the man would be able to work it out just fine.

What's making me actually want to write this up is that it's not hypothetical anymore. There's a research program that does cataract surgery on kids and adults in India who've been blind since birth, and researchers tested newly-sighted patients on exactly this task right after their vision kicked in. From what I've read, they flopped -- matching shapes by sight that they already knew cold by touch, no better than random guessing. So as far as I can tell, the 300-year argument is just over: Locke was right, and Leibniz wasn't.

What I actually need help with is the framing: why did it take three centuries of extremely smart people arguing about this when one well-run experiment nailed it down, apparently for good? Was Leibniz's reasoning simply bad, or is this more a case of philosophy being stuck until the relevant experiment became technically possible? I'd love your take on how to explain, for a general audience, how a modern vision-science study managed to close a case that had stumped Locke, Leibniz, Berkeley, and everyone after them for three hundred years.
