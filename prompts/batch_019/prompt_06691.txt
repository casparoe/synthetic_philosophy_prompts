I was up far too late last night arguing with a friend over a single paragraph in Part Two of Franklin's Autobiography — the section where he lays out his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection." We had both just reread it for a reading group on early American moral thought, and the sticking point was the "speckled axe" anecdote, which Franklin tells immediately after confessing that Order was the one virtue that got the better of him. Neither of us could talk the other down, so I'd like a more careful outside read than either of us managed after a bottle of wine. Here is the passage in full, since I think the axe story and the reflection that follows it have to be read as a single unit:

"My scheme of Order gave me the most trouble; and I found that, tho' it might be practicable where a man's business was such as to leave him the disposition of his time, that of a journeyman printer, for instance, it was not possible to be exactly observed by a master, who must mix with the world, and often receive people of business at their own hours. Order, too, with regard to places for things, papers, etc., I found extremely difficult to acquire. I had not been early accustomed to it, and, having an exceeding good memory, I was not so sensible of the inconvenience attending want of method. This article, therefore, cost me so much painful attention, and my faults in it vexed me so much, and I made so little progress in amendment, and had such frequent relapses, that I was almost ready to give up the attempt, and content myself with a faulty character in that respect, like the man who, in buying an ax of a smith, my neighbor, desired to have the whole of its surface as bright as the edge. The smith consented to grind it bright for him if he would turn the wheel; he turned, while the smith pressed the broad face of the ax hard and heavily on the stone, which made the turning of it very fatiguing. The man came every now and then from the wheel to see how the work went on, and at length would take his ax as it was, without further grinding. 'No,' said the smith, 'turn on, turn on; we shall have it bright by and by; as yet it is only speckled.' 'Yes,' said the man, 'but I think I like a speckled ax best.' And I believe this may have been the case with many, who, having, for want of some such means as I employed, found the difficulty of obtaining good and breaking bad habits in other points of vice and virtue, have given up the struggle, and concluded that 'a speckled ax was best'; for something, that pretended to be reason, was every now and then suggesting to me that such extreme nicety as I exacted of myself might be a kind of foppery in morals, which, if it were known, would make me ridiculous; that a perfect character might be attended with the inconvenience of being envied and hated; and that a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance.

In truth, I found myself incorrigible with respect to Order; and now I am grown old, and my memory bad, I feel very sensibly the want of it. But, on the whole, tho' I never arrived at the perfection I had been so ambitious of obtaining, but fell far short of it, yet I was, by the endeavor, a better and a happier man than I otherwise should have been if I had not attempted it; as those who aim at perfect writing by imitating the engraved copies, tho' they never reach the wished-for excellence of those copies, their hand is mended by the endeavor, and is tolerable while it continues fair and legible."

My friend's reading is that Franklin quietly does exactly what he mocks the axe-buyer for doing. On this view, Franklin catches himself mid-rationalization, names his own excuses outright as "something, that pretended to be reason," lists the very considerations the axe-buyer would recognize as his own (avoiding "foppery" in morals, avoiding envy, keeping friends in countenance), and then gives up on Order anyway, so the "engraved copies" comparison at the end is just a more dignified way of describing the same capitulation. I argued that this runs together two different things: correctly diagnosing a piece of reasoning as self-serving is not the same as being moved by it, and the engraved-copies analogy is doing independent work — it's a claim about the instrumental value of aiming at a standard one cannot reach, which doesn't stand or fall with the excuses Franklin has just finished discrediting in himself.

So, two questions I'd like your help with. First, on the most careful reading you can give it, does Franklin's own account of his reasoning actually commit him to the same move he attributes to the man in the anecdote, or is there a principled difference between recognizing a rationalization and acting on one, as I claimed? Second, how should we understand the relation between his admission that he was "incorrigible with respect to Order" and his claim, one sentence later, that the whole project nonetheless left him "a better and a happier man" — is that a real tension in his position, or does the passage supply, even if only implicitly, a coherent picture of how striving toward an unreachable moral ideal can be worthwhile despite, or even because of, persistent failure at particular virtues? I would rather have your independent reading of what the text supports than a verdict on who won the argument.
