The aesthetics seminar I'm taking this term just spent two weeks on the problem of aesthetic testimony: whether you can be justified in believing a work is beautiful, moving, or successful purely because someone whose judgment you trust reports that it is, or whether an aesthetic verdict is the sort of thing you have to arrive at yourself, through direct encounter with the work. My professor kept returning to Hume, who wanted room for expert critics whose verdicts the rest of us should generally defer to, but who also seemed to think the sentiment of beauty has to be actually felt by the person forming the judgment, not merely accepted on report -- which sits oddly next to how unproblematic it is to take an expert's word for, say, the load-bearing capacity of a beam.

This turns out to matter to me outside the seminar room, because I'm the student representative on the five-person jury for our art department's annual juried exhibition, whose top prize includes purchase of the winning work for the permanent collection. The submitted pieces went up for a single afternoon walkthrough before the gallery had to be cleared for a touring show, and two of our five jurors -- not coincidentally, the two who were added specifically to give the panel expertise outside painting and photography, one in sculpture and one in sound art -- missed the walkthrough because of a transit strike and never encountered in person the piece everyone is now arguing about: a large sound-and-light installation meant to be experienced by walking through it. They have only the detailed notes, photographs, audio recordings, and enthusiastic verbal accounts the rest of us produced afterward. The installation has since shipped to its next venue and can't be reassembled before we finalize the shortlist. Three jurors want the two absent members to vote as normal, since excluding them would just hand the decision to a panel of painters and photographers judging a piece outside their training -- precisely what their positions on the jury were meant to prevent. Two jurors think a ballot cast on secondhand description isn't a judgment of the work at all, and want those two votes recorded as abstentions, with the piece's fate settled by the remaining three. Our charter requires jurors to "give due consideration to every work in competition" but says nothing about what that requires when the juror hasn't seen the work.

I've been asked to draft the memo we'll circulate before Friday's final meeting, where the shortlist has to be locked in for the catalog printer. Could you write it? I need it to come down clearly on how the two contested ballots should be handled, propose a concrete procedure we can actually run by Friday, and take the abstention side's objection seriously on its merits rather than waving it away as pedantry -- ideally in language that will make sense to jurors who have never taken an aesthetics class, since I'd rather the memo do the philosophical work quietly than announce it.
