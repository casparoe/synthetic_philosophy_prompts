A friend and I spent the better part of a long dinner arguing about John Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" after the conversation drifted there from a news story about a rescue boat that could only reach one of two capsized groups in time. I found myself defending something close to Taurek's own position: that when a rescuer can save only one of two sets of equally situated strangers, and owes none of them anything beyond the general duty to help if she can, the bare fact that one set contains five people rather than one does not, by itself, give her more reason to save the five. My reasoning followed his: there is no single subject who undergoes the "sum" of five people's deaths in the way that one person undergoes his own death, so treating the five as automatically outweighing the one smuggles in an illegitimate kind of aggregation across separate lives. I even defended his proposed fallback of settling the matter by some chance procedure, such as a coin toss, so as to give each side's claim equal standing when there is no other principled way to decide.

My friend, unconvinced, took a broadly Scanlonian contractualist line: that a principle telling rescuers to save the greater number whenever they can is exactly the kind of principle that no one, reasoning from their own point of view, could reasonably reject, and that this conclusion can be reached without ever adding welfare across people or treating the five as a single super-person. We spent a long time stuck on whether that contractualist argument really escapes the charge Taurek levels against straightforward aggregation, or whether "no one could reasonably reject a policy of saving the greater number" is ultimately just the same aggregative judgment restated in the vocabulary of reasonable rejection. Neither of us produced a knockdown response to the other, and we ran out of time before working out where exactly the disagreement bottomed out.

I would like to see the strongest version of this disagreement played out properly. Please write a dialogue between two characters crewing a single rescue boat that arrives at the scene of exactly this dilemma: one stranded stranger on one side, a group of five equally stranded strangers on the other, and enough time or fuel to reach only one side before conditions become fatal, with no special obligations or prior relationships pulling either character toward one side over the other. One speaker should be a committed Taurekian, defending the permissibility (or even the requirement) of a non-aggregative procedure like a coin toss against the objection that this simply throws away four extra lives for no good reason; the other should be an equally sophisticated Scanlonian contractualist, defending the claim that saving the greater number is what no one could reasonably reject, against the charge that this is just utilitarian aggregation smuggled back in under a different name. Have each speaker engage the other's actual argument rather than a simplified version of it, and do not let either one win by default.
