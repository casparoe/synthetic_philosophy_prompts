I spent thirty-odd years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I've fallen into reading philosophy of mind, mostly because Integrated Information Theory looked like the first theory of consciousness with actual math behind it instead of just hand-waving. I've been working through Tononi and Koch's Φ (phi) formalism on and off, and this past weekend I got into a friendly argument about it with my grandson, who's finishing a CS PhD and is very keen on mind uploading.

My position was this: take the classic gradual-replacement thought experiment, where you swap out your neurons one at a time for functionally identical silicon units, more or less the way Chalmers sets it up. As long as each artificial unit reproduces the exact same input-output behavior as the neuron it replaces, the brain's overall causal/functional organization stays the same, so under IIT the integrated information Φ has to come out the same too, meaning "you" carry on into the machine with no fading or altering of experience along the way, just on a sturdier substrate afterward. My grandson kept insisting this was too fast but couldn't quite pin down where it broke, which is unusual since he's normally sharper then me on anything computational.

So here's my actual question: is my reasoning solid, or is there something about how Φ actually gets computed that stops "same input-output function" from guaranteeing "same conscious experience" under IIT? I'd like to know if I genuinely have the theory on my side here before I get any more invested in the idea that uploading is a live option for people like me.
