I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but for the past year or so I've been working my way through Rousseau on my own -- the Confessions first, then the Social Contract and the two Discourses -- and a few weeks ago I finally got to the "Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre," a text I'd only ever seen mentioned in passing footnotes before. It's turned into the centerpiece of a small reading group I help run at my local library, and next month I'm interviewing a guest for our group's recorded discussion: someone known for defending Rousseau's case against the theatre as a serious, internally consistent position rather than the puritanical overreaction it's usually written off as.

I'd like help drafting eight to ten interview questions that go beyond a plain summary of the letter and into the places where I suspect that defense is hardest to sustain. At minimum, I'd like one question that presses on the apparent hypocrisy of a man who'd already had a hit opera performed for the king and a comedy staged in Paris before he decided the whole art form was corrupting, with a follow-up ready for whatever distinction she draws to escape it. I'd also like a question built around his reading of Molière's Misanthrope -- since as I understand it he thought the play makes Alceste's sincerity look ridiculous next to Philinte's polished dishonesty -- with a follow-up asking whether that same argument doesn't equally indict any story where an unappealing character happens to be the one telling the truth. Please include a question on his proposed alternative of open-air civic festivals where the citizens are the show rather than an audience watching professionals, pressing on whether that alternative only works for a small, still-uncorrupted republic like his own Geneva, with a follow-up on whether this quietly turns the whole argument relativist -- fine for Paris precisely because Paris is already lost. And I'd like one question tying the letter to its role in the rupture with Diderot, since I gather Rousseau read a line in Diderot's Fils naturel as an attack on his own reclusiveness, asking whether the two men actually disagreed about what drama can do or just about each other. For every question, including whichever others you come up with, please add a short note on the answer she's most likely to give and how you'd follow it up, the way a good interviewer would.
