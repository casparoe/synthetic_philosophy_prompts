My dad was in a really bad car accident about a month ago and ended up on a ventilator in the ICU for what turned into the strangest week of my life. After a bunch of tests (an apnea test, and then some kind of scan to check blood flow to the brain) the neurologist sat me and my mom down and told us he was "brain dead" and that, legally, he had already died — even though his chest was still rising and falling with the vent, his skin was warm, and his heart was beating away on its own. My mom couldn't accept that and wanted to wait until his heart actually stopped by itself before anyone did anything, which made things tense because the transplant coordinator called about organ donation that same afternoon, and to her it felt like they wanted to take his organs from someone who might still be alive. We ended up going along with the brain death diagnosis, and he was taken off support a couple of days later, at which point his heart stopped too.

Its been weeks now and I keep getting stuck on one question: in those two days between the "brain dead" declaration and his heart actually stopping, was my dad alive or dead? I've since read a bit and it turns out this isn't just my family being difficult — there's a real, ongoing disagreement, even among doctors and neurologists, about whether literally the whole brain has to be gone for someone to count as dead or just the parts responsible for consciousness, and a separate disagreement about whether brain-based tests are even measuring the right thing at all versus just waiting for the heart and lungs to permanently give out. I saw someone mention a doctor who tracked "brain dead" patients whose bodies kept doing integrated things on their own for months or years afterward — healing from infections, growing, one even carrying a pregnancy — which apparently gets used as an argument that the whole brain-death framework is shakier than it's usually presented as. So I guess what I actually want to know is: is there a real fact of the matter about when someone dies in a case like my dad's, and is one of these views actually more defensible than the others, or is "death" here basically just a line that gets drawn for practical reasons (like making transplants possible) rather than something being discovered? I'd genuinely like an honest breakdown of the positions and what's at stake in each, not just "here's what the law happens to say," because the law is exactly the part that's been bugging me.
