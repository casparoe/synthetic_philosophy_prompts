I have spent the last month working as a research assistant for a torts professor who is rewriting the causation-in-fact chapter of her casebook, and the job has pulled me much further into the philosophy of causation than I expected when I signed on mostly to check citations. The doctrinal workhorse is the ordinary "but-for" test, which is really just David Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation in legal dress: an act counts as a cause of an injury just in case the injury would not have occurred without it. But my professor keeps sending me back to the same handful of hard cases that this test seems to mishandle -- the "two fires" scenario, where each independently set fire is on its own sufficient to destroy the house, so that neither one, taken alone, comes out as a but-for cause of the loss; late-preemption cases, where a backup cause would have produced the very same injury a moment later if the actual cause hadn't gotten there first; and "double prevention" cases, where a factor merely removes some obstacle to an outcome instead of producing it. She handed me Woodward's Making Things Happen and Pearl's The Book of Why and told me to work out whether the interventionist alternative -- causation analyzed through hypothetical interventions on a structural, causal-graph model rather than bare subjunctive conditionals -- actually fares any better on these cases, or whether it just relocates the same puzzles into the choice of variables and equations. I've now read enough on both sides to reconstruct the main moves, but I keep changing my mind about who wins, and I'd like a second opinion before I write this up for her.

Could you write me an essay that actually adjudicates this dispute, rather than laying the two theories out side by side and calling it a draw? I'd like you to work through the overdetermination and preemption cases explicitly (bring in double prevention, switching cases, or trumping preemption too, if you think they sharpen the argument), show concretely how a naive counterfactual analysis and an interventionist analysis handle each one, and assess whether the usual patches on the counterfactual side -- chains of stepwise dependence, Lewis's later move to treat causation as a matter of degree in "Causation as Influence," or NESS-style tests (asking whether a factor was a necessary element of a set of conditions jointly sufficient for the injury) imported from tort scholarship -- actually close the gap. Please end with a genuine verdict about which theory gives the better general account of causation. A conditional verdict is fine (something like "interventionism wins for type-level, scientific causal claims, but counterfactual dependence still captures something distinctive about singular actual-cause judgments" would count), but it needs to be an argued-for position, not a list of considerations with no resolution.
