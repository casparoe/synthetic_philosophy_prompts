I cover debates at the foundations of physics for a living, which mostly means watching smart people disagree with each other and then trying to work out afterward who actually had the better of it. The best specimen I've collected in a while came out of a public panel I recently moderated on interpretations of quantum mechanics, for a feature I'm reporting. Two of the panelists — a theoretical physicist with a long-standing sympathy for Bohmian mechanics, and a philosopher of physics who is instinctively wary of adding any machinery to the bare quantum formalism — kept going at the bar afterward, well after the room had emptied, and I ended up typing most of it into my phone because it was sharper than anything either of them had said on stage.

It started with him complaining that Bohmian mechanics gets treated as the disreputable, nonlocal option while textbook quantum mechanics passes for the safe default, even though the two are less different than that reputation suggests. She answered that the comparison doesn't hold: ordinary collapse, on the standard reading, is only an update to a probability assignment, not a claim that some real physical disturbance crosses the universe, so it doesn't posit the kind of nonlocal causation that Bohm's guiding wave does, which genuinely makes one particle's trajectory respond, instantaneously, to what happens to its distant partner. He replied that this "merely epistemic" reading isn't free: if collapse doesn't describe anything physically real, then something owes an account of what is real and of how a single, definite, classical-looking outcome ever shows up at all — and textbook quantum mechanics has never supplied one, which is just the measurement problem under another name; so orthodox quantum mechanics buys its appearance of locality only by declining to be a complete description of anything, which isn't obviously a cheaper purchase than what Bohmian mechanics pays openly. She conceded that much, but said the honest response is to drop the collapse postulate rather than keep patching it: let the Schrödinger equation govern everything, with no privileged outcome and hence no measurement problem — essentially the Everett interpretation — on which nothing ever receives a real nonlocal shove, since distant correlated outcomes trace back to a shared local origin rather than to any influence crossing the distance between them. He objected that this conceded too much too quickly, on two counts: first, whether the branching structure can actually be made to line up consistently across space, rather than merely asserted to, is itself disputed, so "local" was carrying more weight than she'd let on; and second, whatever one concludes about locality, the Everett interpretation buys it by letting the universe's contents multiply without limit — a full, causally sealed copy of everything for every occasion on which more than one outcome was possible — and calling that the economical option, just because the core equation has no added term, looked to him like relabeling one extravagance as a virtue. Her closing reply was that the two costs aren't comparable: adding new variables, new fields, or new dynamical laws is the sort of thing a fundamental theory should answer for, but "how many times the one, untouched law applies" is not, any more than classical field theory is charged for assigning a value to infinitely many points in space; and if the branching is simply what that single, unedited equation looks like from the inside, it is a consequence of the theory rather than an extra assumption smuggled into it, and shouldn't appear on the same bill.

Neither of them budged, the bar closed around us, and I went home no wiser about who had actually had the better of it, move by move. Could you adjudicate the exchange for me — not by declaring an overall winner, but by going through it turn by turn and saying who was more right at each step, and why? For reference, here are the moves in order: (1) his opening charge of a double standard over nonlocality; (2) her distinction between epistemic collapse and Bohm's guiding wave; (3) his reply invoking the measurement problem; (4) her pivot to Everett as a way of dropping collapse without reintroducing nonlocality; (5) his two-part objection about contested branch-locality and unbounded ontology; (6) her closing distinction between adding a postulate and one postulate simply applying more often. Please open your answer with a JSON object scoring moves 1 through 6 on a scale from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), then justify each score in a short paragraph, and finish with a few sentences on what, if anything, both of them are overlooking.
