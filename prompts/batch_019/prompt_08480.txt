I spent most of my working life as a comparative anatomist, studying limb morphology in birds and their extinct relatives, and it is only since retiring three years ago that I have allowed myself to read philosophy of science properly, rather than in the fragments that graduate training leaves one with. A former colleague recently lent me her copy of Elliott Sober's anthology *Conceptual Issues in Evolutionary Biology*, and I have been working slowly through the section on adaptationism. Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper on the spandrels of San Marco was already familiar to me in outline — one cannot spend a career in evolutionary morphology without having the word "spandrel" thrown at one eventually — but reading it carefully for the first time, alongside Gould and Vrba's 1982 paper introducing the term "exaptation," has left me genuinely puzzled about how the two notions are meant to relate to one another.

The puzzle, as I understand it, is this. In the 1979 paper, a spandrel is introduced as a structural byproduct forced on an organism by other design decisions, with no immediate implication that it serves any purpose at all. By 1982, however, "exaptation" seems to cover both such byproducts, once they are pressed into some new service, and also features that were shaped by selection for one function and only later co-opted for another — Gould's preferred example, if I have understood him rightly, being feathers, apparently favoured first for thermoregulation and only subsequently recruited for flight. That second sort of case does not look like a spandrel at all in the original architectural sense, since the feature was adaptive from the start, merely for a different job. I would very much value your help comparing the two ideas directly. Are all spandrels exaptations? Are all exaptations spandrels, or only some of them? And which of the two concepts, if either, is doing the real philosophical work in Gould and Lewontin's broader case against what they called the Panglossian paradigm, with the other perhaps serving as little more than useful housekeeping vocabulary? I would also be curious to hear your view on why Gould apparently felt the need to introduce a second, broader term only three years after the first.
