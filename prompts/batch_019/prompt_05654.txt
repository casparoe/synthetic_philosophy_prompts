Ok, this is a slightly ridiculous reason to be reading philosophy but here goes. For the past few weeks I've been stuck between two offers I can't seem to run through a normal pros-and-cons list: a two-year research fellowship abroad in exactly the field I want, versus staying put for a relationship that's actually going somewhere for once. Everyone I ask just says "follow your gut" or tries to force the two options into some common unit so they're comparable, and neither response has helped, so I went down a rabbit hole reading about why certain choices resist that kind of treatment. Somewhere in there (I'd half-seen a TED talk on this years ago and never really absorbed it) I landed on the idea that some options aren't better, worse, or equal to one another but "on a par" -- supposedly a genuine fourth relation -- and that this is the actual reason some choices feel hard rather than just difficult to calculate.

Anyway this became useful in an unexpected way: I co-run a tiny philosophy podcast out of my apartment, and we've got a guest booked in a couple weeks who's built her career defending exactly that view of parity and hard choices. I definitly don't want the episode to just be forty minutes of her re-explaining the thesis, since I could get that off the SEP page myself at this point. Could you draft me eight or nine interview questions that push on the actual pressure points in the literature instead of inviting a restatement? Specifically I want: one on the small-improvement argument and why a tiny sweetening of one option is supposed to reveal parity rather than just more vagueness; one on the objection (the sort associated with John Broome) that "on a par" collapses into ordinary rough or vague equality once you look at precisifications closely enough; one that presses on whether a case like mine is really about incomparable value or whether I'm just ignorant of which option is better and "parity" is a fancy word for that ignorance; and one on her claim that in a genuinely hard choice we can "create" reasons for an option through sheer commitment, since that sounds suspiciously like picking arbitrarily and calling it agency after the fact. For each of those four, can you also add a sharp follow-up keyed to whatever reply shes most likely to give, so I'm not just nodding along on the recording.
