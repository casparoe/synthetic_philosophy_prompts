So I've been kind of obsessed lately with this idea called superrationality (it's the Hofstadter thing about the one-shot prisoners dilemma: if you and the other player are both genuinely rational and reasoning in exactly the same way, then whatever conclusion you reach, they'll reach too, so you might as well each go with whichever joint outcome is best for you both - which ends up meaning cooperate instead of defect). The whole "imagine everyone reasoned exactly like you and act on that" move feels really close to Kant's categorical imperative to me, universalizability and all.

Here's what I keep wondering: if you actually sat Kant down and explained superrationality and the prisoners dilemma to him properly, would he recognize it as basically his own idea turning up again in game-theory clothes, or would he say its actually a pretty different (maybe even lesser) kind of reasoning, and reject it? Don't just give me a "he'd probably approve" type answer - actually work through where the two lines of reasoning genuinely line up and where they'd come apart once you take his real ethics seriously, not just the "universal law" slogan version of it.
