Last night, at our regular philosophy reading group, a friend and I spent the better part of two hours arguing about Kant's treatment of animals and never quite settled two specific points: first, whether Kant's "indirect duty" framework, once you look closely at it, collapses into nothing more than Descartes' old view of animals as unfeeling automata; and second, whether Kant's own illustration in the Lectures on Ethics — the man who shoots his dog once it is too old to work — quietly commits him to exactly the kind of direct duty his official theory rules out. We never resolved either question, and I'm still annoyed enough about it that I don't entirely trust my own judgment at the moment, which is inconvenient, since I TA a seminar on Kant's moral philosophy and have a stack of short-answer responses to grade on precisely this material.

The exam question read: "Explain why, on Kant's view, human beings have no direct duties to animals, and explain how he nonetheless argues that there are genuine constraints on how animals may be treated. Ground your answer in the relevant primary texts." It's worth 20 points. Below, verbatim, is one student's response. Could you act as an impartial third reader and grade it strictly on philosophical accuracy, independent of how fluent the prose sounds? Specifically, I'd like: a letter grade and a numerical score out of 20; a rubric as a JSON object with the keys "autonomy_and_no_direct_duty", "indirect_duty_argument", "lectures_on_ethics_example", and "overall_coherence", each scored 1–5 with a one-sentence justification; and, separately, three to five bullet points identifying exactly which claims in the answer are correct, which are incorrect, and which are debatable, so that I have something concrete to write in the margins rather than a general impression.

Student's answer: "For Kant, moral duties in the strict sense can only be owed to beings capable of self-legislation under the moral law — that is, to rational, autonomous persons who can set ends for themselves. Animals, lacking reason, cannot bind themselves to any law and so cannot be the direct object of obligation; in this respect Kant is essentially following Descartes' picture of animals as creatures without any real inner experience, so that whatever happens when an animal is mistreated isn't, by itself, the kind of thing that could ground a duty. Kant nonetheless insists that we have duties 'with regard to' animals, even if not 'to' them: mistreating an animal coarsens the very sympathetic dispositions we depend on for fulfilling our duties toward other human beings, so cruelty is wrong, but only derivatively. His clearest illustration, from the Lectures on Ethics, is the case of a man who shoots his own dog once it is too old to work. Kant says the dog's long and faithful service generates something structurally identical to an obligation of gratitude — which is enough, I'd argue, to show that Kant's system already contains an implicit direct duty to the animal that his official theory won't let him name as such. The 'indirect duty' doctrine and the verdict in the dog case don't really square with each other, even though Kant never admits as much."
