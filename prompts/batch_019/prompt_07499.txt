I'm working on a magazine feature about how demanding morality can reasonably be, and in the course of reporting it I keep running up against Bernard Williams' well-known distinction between "internal" and "external" reasons for action. As it has been explained to me, an internal reason is one you have simply because you already want something, in the way that wanting to avoid pain or preserve a friendship gives you a reason to act; an external reason, by contrast, is supposed to bind you regardless of what you happen to want, the way a duty to send a large share of your income to strangers dying of malaria would presumably bind you even if you felt nothing at all toward people on the other side of the world. Williams was famously skeptical that external reasons of that second sort make any real sense, and this strikes me as a serious problem for the more demanding wing of the effective-altruism movement: if there is no such thing as a reason that holds regardless of your actual desires, then a person who genuinely feels nothing for distant strangers would seem to be, on Williams' own terms, off the hook, and philosophers such as Peter Singer would need some entirely different resource to show that such a person is making a rational error rather than simply following an unfortunate but coherent set of priorities.

Before I put this argument in front of readers who have never encountered the distinction, I would like to make sure I have the philosophy right. Am I correct that Williams' internalism amounts, in effect, to a rejection of demanding, impartial obligations toward people we have no personal tie or feeling for? I have a nagging sense that "internal" is not simply a philosopher's word for "self-interested," and that the machinery of the "subjective motivational set," together with whatever counts as a "sound deliberative route" leading out from it, is doing more work than my summary allows — though I cannot quite reconstruct the argument on my own. Could you explain what the internal/external distinction actually turns on, correct my summary wherever it misfires, and say whether one can consistently accept Williams' skepticism about external reasons while still maintaining that we owe a great deal to people we will never meet?
