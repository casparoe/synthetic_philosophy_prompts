A friend of mine—a physics grad student with a stubborn streak—and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about superdeterminism after she had been reading Sabine Hossenfelder's popular case for it. Her position was that superdeterminism is simply the logically disciplined response to Bell's theorem: the theorem's "no local hidden variables" conclusion only goes through once you assume that the hidden variables governing a particle's state are statistically independent of the experimenters' later choice of measurement settings (sometimes called the "measurement independence" or "freedom-of-choice" assumption), so denying that assumption lets you keep both locality and realism, at the price of a fully deterministic universe. I countered that this is an overcorrection: it doesn't just patch quantum mechanics, it corrodes the logical basis of every controlled experiment ever run, by positing an eerily fine-tuned correlation between measurement choices and particle states with no independent motivation beyond saving the theory. Neither of us budged, and I'd like a more careful adjudication than either of us managed at midnight.

Write an essay that takes a side on this dispute. Start by explaining what work the measurement-independence assumption is doing in the derivation of the Bell inequality, and why rejecting it is a logically available way of preserving both locality and realism. Then engage seriously with the strongest objections on each side: the charge that superdeterminism is unfalsifiable and therefore outside physics proper; the "conspiracy" objection, and whether it is really just a restatement of the cost of determinism rather than an extra cost; the worry that denying measurement independence proves too much, since the same move would seem to license explaining away any inconvenient experimental result in any science, not just Bell tests; and the relevance, if any, of experiments that use light from distant astronomical sources such as quasars to push the timing of any required "conspiracy" further into the past. Feel free to bring in serious research programs built on superdeterminism, such as 't Hooft's deterministic cellular-automaton proposals, rather than treating the position as a purely ad hoc dodge invented to save appearances. Don't just lay out both sides and shrug: tell me whether superdeterminism deserves to be taken seriously as a way of blocking Bell's usual nonlocal conclusion, or whether it fails for principled reasons that go beyond mere unfamiliarity or inconvenience, and defend that verdict against the strongest form of the view you reject.
