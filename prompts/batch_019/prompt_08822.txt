Here's the question, more or less as she gave it to us:

"In Mūlamadhyamakakārikā XVIII.8, Nāgārjuna writes, in the standard rendering, that 'everything is real, or is not real, or is both real and not real, or is neither real nor not real — this is the teaching of the Buddha.' Using this verse together with the related fourfold analyses in chapters I, XXII, and XXV, explain what Nāgārjuna is doing when he denies all four of the alternatives (the catuṣkoṭi) with respect to a given subject such as the Tathāgata or nirvana. Does this procedure violate the law of excluded middle? Why or why not? Your answer should address (a) how the doctrine of the two truths bears on the question, and (b) whether the fourfold structure itself is best understood as Nāgārjuna's own logical invention or as continuous with earlier material."

And here's the classmate's answer in full:

"Nagarjuna's central logical tool is the catuskoti, or four-cornered negation: for any claim P, he considers P, not-P, both P and not-P, and neither P nor not-P, and in the key passages — I.1 on the origination of things, XVIII.8 on 'everything,' XXII on the Tathagata, and XXV on nirvana — he rejects all four corners rather than affirming any one of them. Applied to the Tathagata, for example, he refuses to say the Tathagata exists after death, doesn't exist, both, or neither, which is exactly the kind of question the Buddha himself was already famous for declining to answer. Nagarjuna's innovation was to devise this fourfold logical structure as a tool for undermining any position that assumes svabhava (essence, or intrinsic nature), and then to apply it systematically to every topic in the text — causation, motion, the self, the aggregates, the fire-and-fuel analogy, nirvana — rather than reserving it for the handful of traditional 'unanswerable questions.' Because he denies all four corners instead of asserting one, he avoids simply swapping one substantialist claim for another, and this is where the two truths doctrine earns its keep: conventionally (samvrti-satya) we can still say the chariot exists or that the Buddha taught for forty-five years, while ultimately (paramartha-satya) no predicate of existence or non-existence sticks to anything, because nothing has the fixed essence such predicates would require. As for the logical worry: denying all four corners does look like a flat contradiction, since 'P or not-P' should be a tautology and 'neither P nor not-P' is its negation. This is exactly why Graham Priest and Jay Garfield's paraconsistent reading has become the standard way scholars now understand the tetralemma: Nagarjuna is telling us that at the very limits of thought, some claims really are both true and false at once, and classical logic simply stops applying there. It's a philosophically serious position, not a cop-out, and it fits Nagarjuna's reputation as someone willing to bite the bullet on contradiction rather than dodge it with qualifications."
