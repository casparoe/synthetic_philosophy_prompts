In my school's philosophy club, we just spent several meetings working through the Vienna Circle, and the idea that has stuck with me most is Schlick's verification principle: the claim that a sentence only counts as genuinely, cognitively meaningful if some possible observation could confirm or refute it, which the positivists used to dismiss most traditional metaphysical and ethical questions as pseudo-problems rather than real questions awaiting an answer. Our club advisor mentioned, almost as an aside, that Schlick himself wrote an entire essay titled "On the Meaning of Life," which surprised me given everything else we had read about him.

Since he was so strict about ruling out meaningless questions everywhere else, I assumed the essay must basically be a demolition job: he poses the question "what is the meaning of life?", shows that no observation could ever settle it one way or the other, and concludes that the question dissolves into nothing once the verification principle is applied honestly to it, the same treatment he and Carnap gave to a lot of classical metaphysics. A friend in the club thinks that can't be right, since it seems like an odd thing to devote a whole essay to if the entire point were just to declare the question empty, but neither of us actually knows what the essay says. Could you help settle this for us: is that basically how Schlick's argument goes, and if not, what is he actually arguing?
