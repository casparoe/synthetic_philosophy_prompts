Ok so I've spent way too much of this week down an experimental philosophy rabbit hole, after someone in a rationalist-adjacent discord dropped "well, intuitions about knowledge vary across cultures so they can't be good evidence for anything" as a total mic-drop in an argument about how to define knowledge. My prior going in was that this would turn out to be mostly overblown, and it kind of is, except where it really isn't, which is the confusing part. The whole thing traces back to Weinberg, Nichols and Stich's 2001 paper "Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions," which ran Gettier-style vignettes (the classic setup where someone has a justified true belief that isn't knowledge because of some weird lucky coincidence) plus a few other cases, like Fred Dretske's old "zebra case" - you're at the zoo, you see what's obviously a zebra, do you "know" it's a zebra given that the zoo could in principle have stuck a cleverly-painted mule in the pen and you'd have no way to tell the difference - on East Asian, Indian, and mostly-US Western college students, and found the groups answers diverged way more than chance would predict. This became more or less the founding document of the "negative program" in x-phi: if intuitions vary this much by culture, the argument goes, they can't be the kind of stable, near-universal bedrock evidence philosophers have traditionally treated them as.

Except almost none of the *other* specific gaps WNS reported have survived later, better-powered replications - a 2015 rerun of the Gettier "car case" with a much bigger East Asian sample found basically identical knowledge-denial rates in both groups (85% vs 86%, no gap at all). The zebra case is the one weird holdout: a 2021 replication with a couple hundred Indian and Western participants found the same US/India split again, and the authors explanation for it is the part that actually got me thinking - they argue it's not generic "holistic vs analytic cognition" noise a la Nisbett, but that skepticism about the reliability of perception specifically (Cartesian-demon, Humean-senses-can't-be-trusted type worries) is a distinctively Western epistemological preoccupation that's largely absent from classical Indian epistemology, where the Nyaya tradition treats ordinary perception as reliable by default. So the "raw" intuition here looks like it's downstream of which sophisticated epistemological tradition you happened to inherit, not some culture-free gut reaction at all.

Given that picture - most cross-cultural gaps turning out to be noise, but the one that's real being theory-laden all the way down rather than some shallow "cognitive style" thing - what do you think are the most important implications here for how much weight I should put on my own snap intuitions when I lean on a thought experiment to argue for or against some philosophical claim, especially when I'm arguing against people who might have inherited pretty different background assumptions than mine?
