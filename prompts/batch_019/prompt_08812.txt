For thirty-five years I ran an experimental condensed-matter physics lab, and it is only since retiring that I have had time to sit in properly on philosophy courses, rather than merely reading around in them on evenings and weekends. This term I am auditing a seminar on existentialism, and the instructor, aware that I spent a career drafting rubrics for graduate qualifying exams, has taken to letting me mark a handful of the short written responses on the bad-faith unit alongside her, so that she has someone to check her own marking against. Before I hand mine back to her, I would like an independent read on one particular answer, since I am still building my own sense of how finely Sartre's distinctions need to be drawn, and would rather not wave an answer through simply because it sounds fluent and uses the right vocabulary.

The question set to the students was: "In Being and Nothingness, Sartre argues that bad faith (mauvaise foi) is a peculiarly human possibility rooted in the ambiguous unity of facticity and transcendence. Using Sartre's own example of the café waiter, explain (a) what distinguishes bad faith from ordinary lying to another person, (b) how the waiter's comportment illustrates a flight from one pole of the facticity-transcendence pair toward the other, and (c) why Sartre holds that bad faith can never simply succeed once and for all, but must instead be continually re-achieved."

Here is the student's answer, transcribed exactly as submitted:

Bad faith, for Sartre, is a form of self-deception, but it differs from ordinary lying because a lie requires two consciousnesses: one that knows the truth and hides it, and another who is meant to be deceived. In bad faith there is only one person, so the same consciousness must somehow simultaneously know and not know the truth it is fleeing. Sartre thinks this is possible because consciousness is not a fixed substance but a kind of translucent nothingness that is always at a distance from itself, which is what makes both freedom and self-deception possible in the first place.

The waiter example brings this out nicely. Sartre describes a waiter whose movements are a little too precise and a little too rapid, and who generally overplays the part of "being a waiter" the way an actor overplays a role. For Sartre this is bad faith because the waiter is trying to convince himself that he simply is a waiter in the way an inkwell simply is an inkwell — that is, he is fleeing his transcendence, his freedom to always be other than his current role, by collapsing himself into pure facticity, into a fixed thing. Sartre's implied prescription is that the waiter would be acting in good faith if he simply quit and found work that was a more authentic expression of his true self, rather than hiding behind a social role.

Finally, bad faith can never fully succeed because sustaining it requires the very consciousness that is fleeing the truth to keep at least a pre-reflective grip on the truth it is fleeing — otherwise it would not be in flight from anything at all. This is the same structure Sartre uses to explain the shame we feel when caught looking through a keyhole: we suddenly grasp ourselves as a fixed object for the Other, and no amount of self-persuasion lets us settle comfortably into being purely a "Peeping Tom" or purely a free subject. Bad faith, like shame before the Other's look, is therefore a perpetually renewed project rather than a state one can simply achieve and rest in.

I would be especially interested in your view on whether the answer keeps the facticity/transcendence discussion of bad faith properly separate from the later discussion of the Look in Part Three, since that is a distinction I keep losing my own grip on.
