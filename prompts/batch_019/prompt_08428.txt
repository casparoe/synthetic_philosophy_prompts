For my AP Government class, our unit on judicial interpretation ended with Yates v. United States — the "fish case" — which our teacher used to show how textualism and purposivism actually play out on real facts instead of in the abstract, along with a few of the interpretive canons judges use to fill in the gaps. Quick recap in case it helps: a commercial fisherman named John Yates was caught with undersized red grouper in federal waters, and when a wildlife officer told him to keep the fish on ice for inspection back at the dock, he had his crew throw them overboard instead. Federal prosecutors charged him under 18 U.S.C. § 1519, a Sarbanes-Oxley Act provision enacted after the Enron collapse that criminalizes knowingly destroying "any record, document, or tangible object" to obstruct a federal investigation. The question for the Supreme Court was whether a fish counts as a "tangible object," and the vote split 5-4 in a way that scrambled the usual alignments: Justice Kagan wrote the dissent, joined by Justices Scalia, Thomas, and Kennedy, on the view that the statute's ordinary text plainly covered the fish, while Justice Ginsburg wrote the lead opinion — technically a plurality, since Justice Alito supplied the fifth vote separately — holding that it didn't.

Two of my classmates, Naomi and Theo, kept arguing about it on the walk home afterward, and I mostly just listened. Naomi said Kagan obviously had the better argument: the statute says "any tangible object," a fish plainly is one, and Ginsburg's opinion is really just the Court deciding that twenty years in prison for tossing back a few fish felt excessive and then working backward to a reading that avoided the result — which isn't interpretation, it's rewriting. Theo objected that this is unfair to Ginsburg, because "tangible object" doesn't sit alone in the sentence — it comes right after "record" and "document" — and one of the canons our teacher had mentioned, noscitur a sociis ("a word is known by the company it keeps"), says you're supposed to read it as the same general kind of thing, so the narrow reading is just as textual as the broad one, not a purpose-driven rescue. Naomi shot back that this actually proves her point: if a canon can turn an unambiguous word like "object" into something that quietly excludes fish, then no text is ever really plain, and "textualism" becomes a license to reach for whichever canon delivers the answer a judge already wanted — exactly what she thought purposivism was supposed to be guilty of. Theo answered that canons aren't arbitrary in that way, that Scalia relied on exactly this kind of contextual canon constantly without anyone doubting his textualist credentials, so the real lesson isn't that textualism collapsed into purposivism here, but that there were two competing textualist readings, and the dissent's was the lazier one for stopping at the dictionary instead of reading the sentence Congress actually wrote. Naomi replied that the same sentence also criminalizes someone who "mutilates" or "conceals" the object, and that both verbs fit a fish far more naturally than they fit a hard drive, so if surrounding words are supposed to be the guide, they don't obviously point where Theo thinks they do.

Neither of them budged, and honestly I could see real merit on both sides. I'm especially interested in whether Theo is right that leaning on a canon like this is compatible with being a committed textualist, or whether Naomi is right that it quietly smuggles purposivism in under a Latin name, and I'd like you to flag anything both of them seem to be missing.
