I'm writing a novel whose central set-piece is a formal philosophical debate, staged in the hall of a monastic university in classical India. The two disputants are a Naiyāyika logician trained in the lineage of Vātsyāyana and Uddyotakara, and a Buddhist monk who has absorbed the epistemology of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti. I want the scene's intellectual stakes to feel real rather than decorative. Over the past few weeks I've been working through some of B. K. Matilal's writing on classical Indian epistemology, and I keep circling back to what seems to be the crux of the disagreement between the two traditions: whether śabda, verbal testimony, is a genuinely independent pramāṇa, as the Naiyāyikas insist, or whether, as the Buddhist logicians argue from Dignāga onward, it simply collapses into anumāna, inference, once one notices that believing someone's word is really just inferring the state of the world from evidence of that person's trustworthiness.

Before I put words in these two characters' mouths, I would like help thinking the disagreement through on its merits rather than staging a caricature. Could you compare the Nyāya defense of testimony as an irreducible source of knowledge with the Buddhist reductive analysis, setting out as precisely as you can what each side takes to be doing the actual epistemic work when a hearer comes to know something purely on another's say-so? I am especially interested in how well each side handles hard cases — long chains of relayed testimony, or testimony about matters, such as remote history, that could never in principle be checked independently by inference — since those seem like exactly the terrain on which the "testimony is just disguised inference" argument should either shine or break down. And because my scene needs a loser as well as a winner, please also tell me, in your own judgment, which position survives this scrutiny better, and why; I would much rather write a real intellectual defeat than a strawman.
