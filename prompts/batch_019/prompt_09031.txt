So a few weekends ago I went down a rabbit hole that started with the Two Generals Problem from a distributed systems course (the thing about two armies needing to coordinate an attack over an unreliable channel, and how you can never actually get "common knowledge" that the attack is on no matter how many acks you send) and somehow, through a chain of wikipedia tabs, I ended up reading about Aumann's argument that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in games like the centipede game. You know the one, alternating chances to grab a growing pot or pass it along, where the "rational" answer is apparently that player 1 should just grab it on turn 1, which feels insane. What's been bugging me for days is the objection that seems to come up everywhere once you read past the first summary: if everyone truly has common knowledge of rationality, reaching turn 30 already means somebody deviated from what the theory says should happen, so what is anyone actually supposed to believe once they're standing at turn 30 having watched 29 "irrational" moves go by? That seems like it either breaks the whole argument or requires some sleight of hand about what "rationality" even means in Aumann's technical sense vs. the normal sense.

Have them actually respond to each other's points instead of talking past each other, let the defender's position shift a little under pressure without fully caving, and don't wrap it up with a neat resolution — my understanding is this is genuinely still contested, but tell me if I'm wrong about that.
