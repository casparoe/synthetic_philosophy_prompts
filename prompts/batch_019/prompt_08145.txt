Here is the question, worth 20 points total:

"Higher-order theories of consciousness hold, roughly, that a mental state is phenomenally conscious just in case the subject has, in addition, a suitable higher-order representation of herself as being in that state.
(a) [5 pts] Explain David Rosenthal's actualist Higher-Order Thought (HOT) theory, and explain how it differs from the higher-order perception (HOP), or 'inner sense,' model associated with Armstrong and Lycan.
(b) [10 pts] Present the objection to actualist HOT theory that turns on the possibility of 'targetless' (or 'empty') higher-order thoughts. Explain how Rosenthal responds to this objection, and why some critics (e.g., Block) have thought the response leaves the theory incoherent.
(c) [5 pts] Does Rosenthal's response give HOT theory a genuine advantage over first-order theories of consciousness in accounting for phenomena like confabulation? Defend your answer in no more than two paragraphs."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"(a) On Rosenthal's picture, what makes a mental state M conscious is that the subject has, at more or less the same time as M, a higher-order thought (HOT) to the effect that she is in M. This HOT must be assertoric and non-inferential: it can't be a conclusion the subject consciously reasons her way to, or something she infers from her own behavior the way she might infer someone else's mental states. Rosenthal's version is usually called 'actualist' HOT theory because it was Rosenthal who first suggested that the requirement of an actually occurring higher-order thought could be relaxed to a mere standing disposition to produce one whenever appropriate, a move meant to avoid the implausible picture of the mind constantly generating a fresh explicit thought about every single conscious state. Carruthers then developed this dispositional strategy more fully, building it into a broader story about a 'theory of mind' mechanism that can in principle target any suitably poised first-order state. Armstrong and Lycan's rival HOP model agrees that consciousness requires a higher-order state directed at the first-order state, but models that higher-order state on perception rather than thought: on their inner-sense view, the mind contains something like a scanning mechanism that monitors internal states in an analog, quasi-perceptual fashion, rather than conceptualizing them in a discrete thought the way Rosenthal's HOTs do.

(b) The targetless (or 'empty') HOT objection points out that if an actually occurring HOT to the effect that I am in M is sufficient for a conscious state, nothing rules out the HOT occurring without the first-order state M ever occurring too, say because of some malfunction upstream in whatever system normally produces such HOTs. In that case it would still seem to the subject, from the inside, that she is having experience M, even though no such state exists. Rosenthal accepts this consequence rather than trying to block it: he holds that the higher-order thought alone fixes what it's like for the subject, so a targetless HOT still yields a subjective appearance of being in M. Block is really pressing the same worry behind the classic 'problem of the rock': if a free-floating higher-order representation is all it takes to generate a conscious state, then in principle a thought about a rock's 'mental states' should generate consciousness in the rock too, which is absurd; the targetless-HOT case is just this same problem showing up inside a single subject's own mind rather than being projected onto an external object. Rosenthal's only real defense is to point to confabulation research, such as Nisbett and Wilson's work on subjects misreporting the causes of their own choices, as independent evidence that people can be radically wrong about their own mental states, but since this does nothing to actually block the rock-style generalization, it looks more like a concession than a rebuttal.

(c) I think this does give HOT theory a real advantage. Because the theory keeps the monitoring state and the monitored state entirely separate, it predicts exactly the kind of radical mismatch we see in confabulation, whereas first-order theories, which identify phenomenal consciousness with the first-order state itself, have no principled way to explain how someone could be conscious of having an experience she isn't actually having. So first-order theories simply cannot accommodate confabulation, and on this specific point Rosenthal's view should be preferred."
