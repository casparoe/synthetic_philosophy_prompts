The question was: "Aristotle claims in the Politics that lending money at interest is 'the most unnatural' way of making money — more unnatural, even, than retail trade. Reconstruct the argument that gets him to this conclusion. Your answer should explain (a) why, on Aristotle's account, money comes to exist in the first place, and what problem its existence solves; (b) how that account of money connects to his distinction between oikonomia and chrematistike; and (c) why usury specifically, rather than exchange or trade in general, ends up as his paradigm case of an 'unnatural' way of acquiring wealth."

Here is the answer, transcribed exactly as the student wrote it: "Money, for Aristotle, isn't wealth in itself but a conventional solution to a practical problem. In the Nicomachean Ethics he illustrates the problem with the case of a shoemaker and a housebuilder: for these two producers to exchange justly, their products have to be exchanged in some fair proportion, but a house and a pair of shoes aren't naturally comparable the way two pairs of shoes are. Aristotle's solution is that the exchange ought to track the relative labor each producer has put into his product — so many shoes for one house, in proportion to how much harder or more skilled the housebuilder's labor is than the shoemaker's. Money is introduced as the conventional token that stands in for this ratio, which is why Aristotle says it is nomisma, existing by nomos (convention) rather than by nature. Because this proportional exchange between producers is what allows a city's different specialists to depend on one another, Aristotle treats it as an instance of distributive justice: each producer is meant to receive a share of the other's goods in proportion to what he has contributed, in the same way offices and honors are properly distributed in the city according to merit. This account of money as a conventional measure is what grounds Aristotle's distinction, in the Politics, between oikonomia and chrematistike. Oikonomia, household management, uses money only instrumentally, to acquire the finite set of goods a household needs; chrematistike is Aristotle's name for the unnatural pursuit of wealth as an end in itself, where money stops being a means to getting use-values and instead gets chased for its own sake, with no natural stopping point. Usury is the worst case of chrematistike because it cuts out even the pretense of exchanging goods: ordinary trade still passes money through some object that has a use, but interest — tokos, which literally means 'offspring' — makes money breed directly from money, as though it were alive. That, for Aristotle, is why lending at interest isn't just one more instance of unnatural acquisition but the most unnatural one: it asks a barren medium of exchange to reproduce itself."
