I've always known the Turing test from the standard CS-101 version everyone tells, but this week I actually sat down and read Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" paper front to back, and section 5 threw me completely. He runs through a list of objections to his imitation game, and one of them is literally the "argument from extra-sensory perception" - he doesn't wave it off as pseudoscience, he says the statistical evidence for telepathy is basically overwhelming, and then seriously proposes sticking the human and the machine in a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic interrogator can't just read the human's mind instead of judging the conversation. This feels kind of insane coming from someone that rigorous, and none of the popular writeups of the test I've seen ever mention it. Can you explain what his actual reasoning was there, why a scientist of his caliber would take ESP seriously in 1950, and whether this is just an embarassing footnote historians politely ignore or if it actually matters for how we should read his broader argument for machine intelligence?
