Okay so over the last few weeks I've fallen into a proper rabbit hole about the liar's paradox (you know, "this sentence is false"). It started as a youtube video and somehow ended with me reading half the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy on it, and what's kind of blowing my mind is that there isn't one agreed "fix" among logicians, there's a whole zoo of totally different theories and each one seems to bite some weird bullet. Like you've got Tarski's move of saying a language just can't contain its own truth predicate, so you stack everything into seperate levels of language. Then Kripke basically lets the liar sentence fall into a gap where it's neither true nor false (it's "ungrounded"). Then Gupta and Belnap have this "revision" theory where you keep re-evaluating the sentence against itself and the truth value just flips forever instead of settling down. Then Graham Priest just says screw it, it's true AND false at the same time (a real contradiction) and builds a whole logic where that doesn't blow everything up. Then there's the more "contextualist" line, guys like Parsons and Simmons, who argue the sentence can't actually be quantifying over literally every sentence including itself, there's a hidden restriction. And then Hartry Field's newer fix bolts on a whole hierarchy of "definitely true" operators plus a new conditional to patch the holes in Kripke's version. None of these feel like the obvious winner to me, they all dodge the bullet in one spot and get shot in another (especially once you bring in "revenge" versions of the paradox, or cousins like Curry's paradox, which some of these seem to handle way worse than others).
