I'm giving one of the toasts at the wedding reception of two close friends from my graduate program, Priya and Daniel, and I'd like to build it around Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, which has been a private joke of theirs since Daniel proposed by taping the ring to the last page of a proof of it that he'd typeset himself. (I've heard secondhand that the officiant is already planning something built on Bayesian updating for the vows, so I'd like to steer clear of that territory and stay with aggregation.)

For anyone who hasn't sat through the relevant seminar: Harsanyi showed that if two people are each expected-utility maximizers, and you want their combined "household" preferences over uncertain outcomes to likewise satisfy the expected-utility axioms while never overriding something the two of them already agree on (the Pareto condition), then the household's utility function is forced to be a weighted sum of the two individual ones. This gets cited constantly, including in the more utilitarian corners of the rationalist blogosphere, as a clean proof that something like utilitarian aggregation falls straight out of individual rationality plus unanimity. What almost nobody mentions at the point where they invoke it approvingly is that the theorem is quietly assuming the two people already agree on the probabilities of everything. It aggregates utilities, not beliefs, and once the two individual probability assignments come apart, the neat "just take a weighted average" result stops going through cleanly, which is more or less the content of Mongin's later impossibility result on Bayesian aggregation under heterogeneous priors.

I don't want the toast to lean on "weighted sum of two utility functions" as a stand-in for compromise, since that's the part everyone already gets right and using it that way would just be decoration. I want it built around the overlooked clause instead: that loving someone enough to respect their preferences isn't sufficient on its own, and that what the theorem quietly needs, beyond goodwill, is two people who already see the world the same way, or who are willing to keep updating on each other until they do. About half the room did the same graduate program and will know exactly where I'm going by the second sentence; the other half is family who has never heard the phrase "expected utility" and just wants a few warm, funny minutes before the meal is served. Could you write the toast? It should run about five minutes, land as an actual blessing rather than a lecture, and contain exactly one joke that the theorists and the grandparents can each laugh at, for different reasons. A little poetic license with the math is fine in service of the metaphor, but only knowingly: it shouldn't drift into actually misstating what the theorem shows.
