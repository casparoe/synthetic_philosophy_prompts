So I went down a rabbit hole this week that started with re-reading Eliezer's old "37 Ways Words Can Be Wrong" / tabooing-your-words stuff, and ended with me discovering that professional philosophers have basically the same question (when is a disagreement "merely verbal" rather than substantive) as an entire subfield called metaontology, with it's own cast of characters -- Hirsch, Sider, Chalmers, etc. Eliezer's answer is basically "swap the word for what it actually cashes out to and see if the argument survives," which felt satisfyingly deflating to me at the time, but apparently a bunch of serious metaphysicians think that move doesn't actually settle things, at least not for their favorite disputes.

I'd like you to write me a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about a concrete test case: whether the debate between compositional universalists (anytime you have two or more objects, there's automatically some further object that's their fusion -- so somewhere out there is the scattered object made of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower) and compositional nihilists (nothing composite really exists, only mereological simples -- tables are just simples arranged tablewise) is a genuine, substantive disagreement or merely a verbal one. Character A should run something like Eli Hirsch's quantifier variance line: there's no uniquely correct meaning of "exists" or "there is," the universalist and nihilist are simply using different but equally legitimate quantifiers, so once you're careful about which quantifier is in play, both of them are saying true things and there's nothing left to actually fight about -- basically a fancier, technical version of tabooing the word "exists." Character B should push something like Ted Sider's response: fine, maybe ordinary English "exists" is ambiguous or imprecise, but that doesn't mean all the candidate meanings are on a par -- there's still a further, substantive question of which quantifier meaning actually carves reality at its joints, and doing metaphysics properly (in "Ontologese," if you like) means trying to figure out which one that is.

Don't let either side just state their view once and stop, I want them actually responding to each other's strongest pressure points. In particular Character B needs to face the obvious comeback of "okay, but how would you even tell which quantifier is the joint-carving one, and isn't THAT dispute just as likely to turn out merely verbal," and Character A needs to face the worry that if quantifier variance is right about this case, it's hard to see why it wouldn't dissolve basically all of metaphysics into verbal disputes, which a lot of people (including Hirsch himself, I think?) find like an uncomfortable amount of terrain to concede. No need to wrap it up with a neat resolution at the end, since as far as I can tell nobody in the actual literature has one either.
