I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and for the past year a small philosophy discussion group I joined at the local library has been slowly working through the philosophy of science I never had time for during my career. We began, as such groups apparently always do, with Popper: falsifiability struck me as basically the right instinct, since it matched my own working sense of what separated my field from the occasional pseudoscientific proposal I watched get quietly filtered out of grant panels. Then we read Larry Laudan's "The Demise of the Demarcation Problem," and I confess it rattled me. Laudan goes through every proposed criterion, Popper's falsifiability included, produces historical cases each criterion misclassifies in one direction or the other, and concludes that "science" and "pseudoscience" are not principled categories at all — merely honorific and pejorative labels — and that philosophers should stop asking whether a claim is "scientific" and start asking only whether it is well or poorly warranted by the evidence available.

What keeps me from simply accepting this is that I lived through, from just outside the field, the so-called string theory wars: Peter Woit's and Lee Smolin's public complaints that string theory had gone decades without producing a single novel, falsifiable prediction, met by physicists and philosophers of physics (Richard Dawid most prominently) who argued that something like non-empirical theory assessment — absence of any workable rival, unexpected mathematical coherence between initially unrelated parts of the theory, and so on — could still rationally underwrite confidence in it. Everybody in that dispute talked as though falsifiability, or something close to it, was exactly the right thing to be arguing about, which makes me wonder whether Lakatos's more sophisticated methodology of scientific research programmes — built to survive the Quine–Duhem problem by relocating falsification from single theories to whole programmes with a hard core and a protective belt, judged progressive or degenerating over time rather than confirmed or refuted in any one test — manages to survive Laudan's critique where Popper's original version doesn't, or whether Laudan's argument sinks the Lakatosian version too, in which case the string theorists and their critics were, without quite realizing it, disagreeing about something other than what they thought they were disagreeing about.

I would like you to write an essay that addresses this question directly: does any falsificationist criterion of demarcation — Popper's or a Lakatosian descendant — survive Laudan's argument that the whole demarcation project is misconceived? Please take Laudan's strongest points seriously rather than simply restating Popper, and use the string theory case as a real test rather than a decorative example. I would rather you commit to an actual verdict than close on a diplomatic "both sides have a point": tell me plainly either that some specific falsificationist criterion still does genuine demarcating work (and say which one, and exactly what it does and does not rule out), or that Laudan is basically right and "is it science?" should be retired in favor of "is it well warranted?" — and if the latter, say what that would actually mean for how we ought to talk about a case like string theory.
