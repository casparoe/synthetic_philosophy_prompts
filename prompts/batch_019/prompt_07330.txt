Two people in my decision theory reading group (both further along in the program than me), Marcus and Ilana, got into a real argument last week and I still can't decide who won. We'd just gone through the Gibbard/Harper "Death in Damascus" case: you're in Damascus, Death shows up and tells you he's coming for you tomorrow. He's a near-perfect predictor of whether you'll be in Damascus or Aleppo, and wherever he predicted is where he shows up -- you die iff you're there too. The textbook line is that causal decision theory (CDT) says neither "stay" nor "flee" is self-ratifying: whichever you settle on, the fact that you settled on it is evidence Death guessed right, so you immediately wish you'd picked the other city. But if you're allowed to flip a coin instead, the 50/50 mixed strategy IS self-ratifying, since Death (so the story goes) can't predict a coin flip.

The argument was over whether that coin-flip move actually rescues the theory.

1. Marcus said it's a genuine solution, not a dodge: the "instability" was only ever an artifact of restricting the guy to two pure options. Widen the menu to include randomizing and CDT delivers a stable answer, same as game theorists are happy with mixed-strategy equilibria in matching pennies.

2. Ilana said thats cheating: Death is stipulated to be a near-perfect predictor of you, and a coin flip is a physical event like any other -- nothing in the original case says Death can predict your character but not a quarter. If Death can predict the coin too, you're back in the exact same loop, one level up, and the "solution" only works because we quietly weaken the predictor exactly when it's convenient.

3. Marcus answered that it isn't ad hoc: Death predicts you via your psychology and dispositions, the way the predictor in Newcomb's problem is usually imagined to work, by scanning your brain, not via magic. A genuinely random physical device doesn't supervene on your character the way your choices do, so there's a principled line between "predictable because it flows from who you are" and "not predictable because it's physically random," and the coin sits on the far side of it.

4. Ilana said fine, grant the line -- something's still fishy. Run the numbers before telling any story about ratification, using your plain prior credence that Death's in each city with equal probability: CDT actually rates paying for the coin as worse than just picking a city outright, since randomizing only adds a fee without changing your 50/50 odds of dying. So the theory's own expected-utility formula says don't randomize, while its "self-ratifying" story says randomizing is the one stable thing to do. A theory giving two different answers depending on which of its own criteria you consult hasn't solved the instability, it's just moved it somewhere less obvious.
