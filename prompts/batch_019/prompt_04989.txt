I co-teach an interdisciplinary graduate seminar that pairs early modern social-choice theory with contemporary debates in algorithmic ethics, and this term the two halves of the course collided in a way I have not been able to resolve to my own satisfaction. We had just finished several sessions on Condorcet — his jury theorem, and especially his discovery that majority rule can generate cyclical, incoherent collective preferences even out of perfectly transitive individual ones — when we turned to the recent "impossibility" results in algorithmic fairness, and several students insisted that the two cases were, at bottom, the same phenomenon in different guises. I would like your help thinking through whether they are right, and in particular what Condorcet himself would have made of the modern result.

For context: work in 2016 and 2017 most associated with Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan on one hand, and with Chouldechova on the other, established that when a risk score is used to predict some outcome across two groups whose base rates differ, it is mathematically impossible — barring a perfect predictor — to satisfy both calibration within groups (a given score means the same thing, probabilistically, regardless of group membership) and equalized error rates across groups (the same false-positive and false-negative rates for each). This is precisely the structure behind the public dispute over the COMPAS recidivism tool, in which ProPublica's charge that the tool was biased against Black defendants, via unequal error rates, and Northpointe's defense that the tool was equally well-calibrated by race, turned out to be simultaneously correct and, given how base rates actually diverged in the data, jointly unsatisfiable as demands. Neither side was using a corrupted notion of fairness; the two notions are simply incompatible once base rates diverge.

What I would like from you is not a generic "Enlightenment philosopher confronts AI" essay, but a reconstruction that takes seriously Condorcet's actual intellectual habits when he ran into the first impossibility result of his own career. On discovering the voting paradox, he did not conclude that collective decision-making lay beyond rational reconstruction; he spent years designing pairwise-comparison procedures to make the best of it, all while insisting, right up to his death in a revolutionary prison in 1794 — not long after finishing the most optimistic work of his career while in hiding from the Terror — that questions of justice, including the rights of women and of the enslaved, were exactly the sort of thing "social mathematics" could adjudicate. Faced with a proof that two independently compelling formalizations of fairness cannot both be honored whenever base rates differ, would he have read this as exposing the whole project of formalizing justice as a kind of category error, or would he have done what he actually did with the voting paradox: treat the impossibility as a fixed constraint and argue, on separate moral grounds, for a principled ranking of the two criteria? I would like your best-supported answer, anchored in what we actually know of how he reasoned rather than in what a generic rationalist of his era might be assumed to have said.
