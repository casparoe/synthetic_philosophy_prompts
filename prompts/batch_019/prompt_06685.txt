I've been a longtime lurker on LessWrong and its assorted descendants, and one piece of community lore I had more or less taken on faith is that Solomonoff induction quietly disposes of Goodman's grue problem: since "grue" (green if observed before some future time T, blue thereafter) takes more bits to specify than plain "green," a Kolmogorov-complexity-weighted prior should favor the green hypothesis over the grue one without needing anything like Goodman's own appeal to "entrenchment." Recently I fell down a citation trail that led, among other places, to a 2022 Philosophy of Science paper by Sven Neth on Solomonoff prediction, which argues that this is too quick: Kolmogorov complexity is only well-defined relative to a choice of universal Turing machine, and a machine whose primitive operations are built out of grue and bleen rather than green and blue will make the grue hypothesis the simpler one instead. The standard rejoinder is that different choices of machine converge on the same predictions given enough data, so any arbitrariness in the choice of machine is supposed to matter less and less as evidence accumulates. That defense is less reassuring than it first sounds, though, since by construction the green and grue hypotheses are empirically indistinguishable on any data collected before T — which is exactly the case where the disagreement bites.

I'd like an essay that takes a definite position on this rather than sitting on the fence. Specifically: does the appeal to algorithmic information theory (Solomonoff induction, or informally a Kolmogorov-complexity-based prior) provide a genuine, principled solution to Goodman's new riddle of induction, or does it just relocate the arbitrariness Goodman identified in "natural" predicates to the choice of description language or universal machine, one level up? Along the way, please (a) explain why Goodman's own entrenchment proposal is usually judged inadequate on its own terms, (b) assess whether the "converges in the limit" defense actually helps with the pre-T case, as opposed to only the long-run case, and (c) say whether, in your view, this is ultimately Hume's original problem of induction wearing a computational costume, or a genuinely separate difficulty that a Bayesian or algorithmic treatment of Hume's problem leaves completely untouched. Please don't hedge into "both sides have a point" — defend a specific, arguable verdict, even if a qualified one.
