So I've been teaching the Hart-Fuller exchange in my jurisprudence seminar for years now, and every single time we do the grudge informer and the "wicked legal system" material and everyone more or less already knows where they're going to land before we even start. This term I want to actually surprise them (and myself, honestly), so I've been hunting for a case that neither Hart nor Fuller could possibly have had in mind when they wrote, and I think I've got one: smart contracts and the "code is law" style of governance you see on blockchains.

Here's roughly the case, in case it helps to set the scene (forgive me if I get a detail or two slightly off, this really isn't my area). Back in 2016 there was a thing called the DAO on Ethereum, basically a big pool of investor money governed entirely by self-executing code, and someone found a bug and drained a huge chunk of it out - completely within the letter of what the code allowed, note, nothing was "hacked" in the sense of breaking any rule the code itself specified. The community split down the middle: one camp said the code's output just is the rule, full stop, theres nothing to appeal to over and above what it actually did ("code is law"); the other camp, including most of the core developers, pushed through a hard fork that effectively overrode the code's literal behaviour to match what everyone had intended the contract to do. It struck me that this is basically a rerun of Hart vs. Fuller with the serial numbers filed off: is a perfectly determinate, self-executing rule that nobody can interpret, excuse, or apply equitably still "law" in any sense worth having (Fuller's challenge, more or less), or does whether something counts as law just turn on pedigree and acceptance, full stop, regardless of whether its morally decent or humanly workable (Hart's challenge back)?

What I actually want from you is a real reconstruction, not the seminar-room caricature where "Hart says yes because he's a positivist and Fuller says no because he's a natural lawyer." Go through Fuller's eight desiderata of legality one at a time and tell me which of them a rigid, unappealable piece of code arguably satisfies more or less automatically (promulgation? generality?), and which ones it structurally can't satisfy (congruence between the rules as declared and official action, maybe, given there's no room left for the kind of equitable correction human officials normally provide). Then, separately, work through Hart's actual apparatus and tell me whether he'd say the relevant "rule of recognition" here is simply whatever the miners or core devs happen to converge on accepting, and whether that causes any real trouble for his insistence that a rule of recognition is a matter of social fact rather than fidelity to some further ideal of legality. Basically: once you push the real machinery of each position onto a case they never got to consider, who ends up looking better off?
