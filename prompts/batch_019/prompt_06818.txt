I've been half-obsessed with the ProPublica/COMPAS fight since it first broke in 2016, and I'm finally pitching my editor on a piece revisiting it now that a bunch of states are writing "algorithmic accountability" laws that mostly dodge the hard part. I had this exact argument with two data-scientist friends over drinks a few weeks ago and neither of them could actually explain where the disagreement bites — most people, including plenty of journalists who covered it the first time round, still seem to think ProPublica simply caught Northpointe being dishonest and that's the whole story. It isn't. I went back and actually read the Chouldechova paper and the Kleinberg/Mullainathan/Raghavan one this time, and the genuinely annoying truth is that once reoffense rates differ between groups, a risk-assessment tool literally cannot be both equally calibrated across race and equally likely to falsely flag people as high-risk across race — not because nobody engineered it well enough, but because it's a theorem.

So here's what I want, partly for my own notes and partly because a version of it might end up in the piece: write me a dialogue between two expert witnesses testifying in front of a state sentencing commission that's deciding whether to keep using a recidivism tool like this one. One witness defends the tool because it's well calibrated across race; the other attacks it because its errors fall unevenly across race. Both of them need to know the underlying math cold and clearly respect each other as adversaries — I don't want either one to be the naive strawman who's never heard the other's best point, and I definitly don't want it to resolve into "the calibration guy was secretly just defending racism" or the reverse. What I really want is for each of them to get pinned down on which of the two conditions they'd actually sacrifice, and to admit, out loud, that this is a value judgement dressed up as a technical one — since that's exactly the part that never survives into the news coverage I'm trying to fix.
