So I've been down a rabbit hole on the "White Horse Dialogue" from the old Chinese School of Names (the Gongsun Long text behind the slogan 白馬非馬, "a white horse is not a horse") for a class on comparative logic, and I can't decide if it's actually clever or just a cheap trick dressed up in old prose. The bare-bones argument goes: "horse" is the name for a shape, "white" is the name for a color, and naming a color isn't the same as naming a shape, so "white horse" and "horse" name different things; plus, if you ask someone for "a horse," a yellow or black one satisfies you, but if you ask for "a white horse," it doesn't, so the two terms can't be equivalent after all. Every intro anthology I've found just says this equivocates "is" as identity with "is" as class-membership and moves on, which feels too quick, especially once you learn that people like Chad Hansen think classical Chinese nouns work more like mass terms (think "water," not "a water"), so "white horse" and "horse" might genuinely name different mereological wholes rather than a subclass and its class -- or that Fung Yu-lan read the whole thing, plus the companion "Hard and White" dialogue, as a real (if clumsy) defense of independently existing universals, more Plato than sophistry.

I want an essay that actually settles this instead of hedging. Reconstruct the argument in its strongest form, walk through at least two of the serious rival readings of whats really going on -- logical, linguistic, metaphysical, whatever framing earns its keep -- and then commit to one: is Gongsun Long guilty of a straightforward fallacy, making a real if narrow point that ordinary class logic can't capture, or doing something "valid/fallacious" doesn't even cleanly apply to? Don't tell me it's ambiguous and reasonable people disagree -- pick a side and defend it like you've got to convince my seminar tomorrow.
