I spent about thirty years as a cell biologist before I retired, and lately I've been getting into philosophy of medicine, which is how I ended up down a rabbit hole on Xavier Bichat -- the French physiologist who wrote that treatise around 1800 on life and death (Recherches physiologiques sur la vie et la mort, if I've got the title right). His big idea was that life is just the sum of functions that resist death, and that dying is never really one instant -- its more like the heart, lungs and brain prop each other up, so that once one of them fails for good the others follow, and death spreads through the body rather than striking everywhere at once. Given that picture, what do you reckon Bichat would make of our modern fight between brain death and circulatory death as the "real" marker of death, now that ventilators can keep a heart beating and lungs cycling long after the brain has permanently shut down? Would his own framework push him toward the neurological criterion, the cardiopulmonary one, or would he say the whole debate rests on a confusion he'd already have spotted two centuries ago? Try to actually reason it out from his own concepts instead of just giving me the standard modern answer.
