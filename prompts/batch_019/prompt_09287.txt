the question was:

"Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual morality argue that English law's treatment of what he calls 'irregularities of the venereal appetite' cannot be justified by the principle of utility. Reconstruct Bentham's argument for this claim, and explain what he thinks actually accounts for the existence and severity of these laws, if not utility."

and here's the answer i have to grade:

"Bentham thinks the felicific calculus has to be applied consistently to every kind of act, sexual ones included, and that the legislator has no business asking whether an act is 'natural,' only whether it produces a surplus of pain over pleasure for the people affected by it. Since the acts he's discussing happen between consenting adults in private, there's no victim: nobody's pleasure is destroyed, and pleasure is actually produced for the people involved. So punishing the act (originally by hanging) creates enormous pain -- to the person executed, to their family, and through the standing risk of blackmail to everyone who could ever be accused -- without any pleasure on the other side of the ledger to offset it. Against the standard argument that such acts threaten population, Bentham points out that this proves too much, since celibate monks depopulate just as effectively as sodomites do, so if population were really the issue we'd have to punish monasticism too. Since the laws can't be justified by utility, Bentham traces them to the principle of asceticism: the same instinct that makes people admire self-denial and mortification for its own sake also makes them want to see other people's pleasures stamped out, especially pleasures that the majority happens to find disgusting. He eventually worked a cautious version of this reasoning into the published Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, though he still left out the most explicit material, which is part of why the full essay wasn't printed until long after his death."
