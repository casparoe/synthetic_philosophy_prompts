I've been working through Kant's ethics on my own for a while now, mostly through secondary sources and translated excerpts since I never studied philosophy formally, and one thing that has stuck with me is his claim that we have no direct duties toward animals at all — only "indirect" ones. The idea, as I understand it, is that because animals aren't rational, self-conscious beings, they can't be wronged in the way a person can; the clearest illustration I've come across is his remark that a man who shoots his old dog, once it's no longer of use to him, doesn't wrong the dog itself, since a dog isn't capable of judging its treatment in any morally relevant sense, but does damage his own capacity for kindness — the very trait his duties toward other people depend on. So on Kant's view, cruelty toward animals is troubling mainly because of what it does to us, not because of anything we owe the animal directly.

What I keep turning over is what he would have made of everything we've since learned about octopuses. They solve puzzles, manipulate objects, seem to have distinct temperaments from one individual to the next, and manage all of this with a nervous system that isn't even centralized the way ours is — a large share of their neurons sit out in the arms rather than in anything resembling a brain. Enough researchers are now willing to call this evidence of real sentience that it's stopped being a purely academic question: there's been a genuine public fight over companies wanting to farm octopuses commercially at scale, with critics arguing that a solitary, cognitively sophisticated animal like this has no business being crowded into tanks and slaughtered on an industrial line.

So rather than just guessing at his conclusion, I'd like you to reconstruct the actual reasoning. Given the specific grounds Kant offers for excluding animals from the moral community — the material about rational nature, autonomy, and being an end in itself, not just the bottom-line conclusion — do you think octopus cognition research would put real pressure on that framework, or is it flexible enough to absorb the evidence without changing anything important? And reasoning strictly from within his own theory, without quietly importing Bentham- or Singer-style arguments about suffering to do the actual work, what do you think he would ultimately have concluded about the ethics of farming them at scale?
