I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and this term's seminar on rational belief change spent several weeks on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- roughly, the requirement that if I am now certain my credence in some proposition A at a later time will be x, my present credence in A (conditional on that supposition) should already be x. Van Fraassen's original defense ran through a diachronic Dutch book argument modeled on the synchronic argument for probabilism, but the seminar spent a long time on Christensen's objection that this analogy is misleading: whether a "clever bookie" can actually exploit me across time depends on the contingent, empirical fact that my future credence will turn out as predicted, whereas synchronic exploitability follows from the sheer structure of my beliefs at a single moment, independent of how the world unfolds. We also worked through van Fraassen's fallback position -- illustrated with a case in the style of Ulysses and the Sirens, where I know in advance that a future credence of mine will result from manipulation, memory loss, or some other epistemically disreputable process, and so should not defer to it -- which restricts Reflection to future credences I expect to arise through some properly "rational" or evidential route. Several of us came away suspecting this restriction quietly does all the normative work, leaving the principle either toothless or covertly dependent on an independent, unstated theory of rational updating.

I'd like you to write an essay that settles this dispute rather than just surveying it. Please state the diachronic Dutch book argument precisely enough to assess whether Christensen's contingency objection actually defeats its normative force (as opposed to merely showing that it isn't exactly parallel to the synchronic case), address whether the restricted version of Reflection can be given a non-circular rationale, and consider whether the comparison to Lewis's Principal Principle -- which faces a structurally similar problem in specifying "admissible" evidence -- helps or hurts Reflection's case. Reach and defend a specific verdict: for example, that Reflection is defensible only relative to an independently motivated theory of updating (and say what that buys us), that it should be abandoned as a distinct rationality constraint, or some other precise position -- but please don't retreat to "both sides have a point."
