A close friend of mine was recently accused, quite credibly, of something entirely out of character for her. My first reaction was to resolve that I would discount any further evidence against her that might surface — not evidence I had already weighed and rejected, but any future evidence whatsoever, on the grounds that I already know her well enough that such evidence would have to be misleading somehow. A colleague with philosophical training pointed out that, without quite meaning to, I had reconstructed the structure of the dogmatism paradox: if I know that P, I thereby know that any future evidence against P must be misleading, and so seem entitled to disregard it in advance, however compelling it looks when it finally arrives. I already know the one-line version of this puzzle, but I would like to understand it properly. Could you explain why the argument is genuinely difficult to resist rather than an obvious sophistry, and then walk me through how the main proposed responses to it — for instance, giving up fallibilism about knowledge, rejecting the closure principle that lets "I know P" deliver "I know any future counterevidence is misleading," or building a defeasibility condition into the analysis of knowledge itself — are each supposed to block the inference, and whether, in your view, any of them fully succeeds?
