It has made me want to get this particular batch of grading right rather than work through it on autopilot, so I would like a rigorous second opinion on one answer before I finalize the mark.

Here is the question, worth 20 points:

"Drawing on the Discourse on Method and Descartes' correspondence, reconstruct his case for denying that non-human animals have minds. Your answer should (a) explain the two tests he proposes for detecting the presence of thought in a body, (b) situate his position against the Scholastic account of the animal soul that it replaced, and (c) address whether, on his view, animals are entirely without sensation or feeling."

And here is the student's answer:

"Descartes denies that animals have minds, arguing instead that animal bodies are elaborate machines, no different in kind from a clock or an ornamental fountain, which only appear intelligent. In the Discourse on Method he offers two tests for telling a mere automaton from a genuine thinking thing. First, a machine could never use language as we do: it could be built to cry out when touched in one spot and stay silent when touched in another, but it could never arrange signs so as to answer appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, the way even the dullest human being can, including those born deaf, who invent gestures of their own to make themselves understood. Descartes is careful to note that this has nothing to do with the vocal organs specifically, since parrots and magpies can be trained to utter words without meaning anything by them. Even so, passing the test in practice requires an anatomy capable of producing the full range of articulate speech, which no animal possesses, so the test is really as much about the throat as about the mind. Second, a machine's competence never extends beyond the specific task it was built for, whereas human reason is a 'universal instrument' usable in any circumstance; a clock keeps better time than we do, but only because it is built for that single job, not because it thinks. Animals are the same way: however skilled a species is at one thing, its skill deserts it the moment the task changes, which shows that it is not consulting reason but simply being driven by the arrangement of its organs.

This is a sharp break from the Scholastic Aristotelian picture Descartes was displacing, on which animals possess a sensitive soul: a lower, non-rational grade of soul that nonetheless gives them genuine perception and feeling. Descartes keeps this same layered picture but subtracts the top tier, so that animals retain the sensitive soul and lose only the rational one, which is why their bodies can produce hunger, pain behavior, and the rest without any reasoning behind it.

On the question of feeling, Descartes is admirably consistent: since sensation is a mode of thought and he denies thought to animals outright, he holds that they are wholly insensate, no more experiencing their injuries than a bell experiences the clapper that rings it. He states this plainly in his 1649 letter to Henry More, settling a question his mechanistic account of animal behavior might otherwise have left open."
