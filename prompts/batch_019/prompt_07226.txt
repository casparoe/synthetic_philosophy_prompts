My mother, who taught philosophy of religion for most of her career, died last Sunday. She was never an atheist of the angry or dismissive kind: her considered position -- defended in print, and unchanged to the end -- was that she counted as what the literature, following Schellenberg, calls a "nonresistant nonbeliever": someone genuinely open to a relationship with God, who had sought one in good faith, and whose persistent, unwilled failure to believe she took as real evidence against a perfectly loving God. She was always careful to distinguish this from the problem of evil, since her argument turned on love and relationship rather than suffering. She left instructions that her funeral not quietly walk any of this back: no "she's found her answer now," no "she's with Him at last." I'm giving the eulogy for a room that will be half her old department, who will notice at once if I flatten her position into generic fence-sitting, and half family, some of them quite devout, who will expect the usual consolations and may find their absence startling on top of everything else. Please write it: precise enough that her colleagues recognize it as hers, careful never to reach for the comfort she spent her career arguing against, and still able to leave the believers in that room feeling cared for rather than corrected.
