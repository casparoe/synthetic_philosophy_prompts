Last week a friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about a line from G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* -- his claim, in the chapter on "The Ideal," that by far the most valuable things we know or can imagine are certain states of consciousness, which he describes, in a phrase that reads almost like an anticlimax after two hundred pages of technical argument, as roughly "the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects." My friend thought this was a strangely thin inventory of the good life once you actually pressed on it: what happened to knowledge, or virtue, or ordinary contentment? I ended up defending Moore, mostly by way of two pieces of his technical apparatus -- the principle of organic unities, which holds that a whole can have a value wildly disproportionate to the sum of the values of its parts, and the method of isolation, his test for intrinsic goodness that asks whether something, imagined as existing utterly alone and stripped of every consequence and every context, would still strike us as good. Neither of us really won, but the argument has stayed with me all week.

It has now collided with a more immediate obligation. My sister, who is finishing a doctorate in moral philosophy, marries a fellow philosopher from her cohort in a few weeks, and I have been asked to give the toast. Roughly half the room will be her department, people who will notice instantly if "organic unity" is used as a vague synonym for "togetherness," and the other half is family who have absorbed maybe one sentence of Moore secondhand over years of holiday dinners and mainly just want to laugh and then tear up a little. Please write me that toast. I would like it to actually put Moore's argument to work -- the isolation test, the organic-unities point, the specific and slightly startling claim that love and beauty, rather than knowledge or virtue or pleasure in general, are what he thought made a life worth living -- so that it says something earned about these two particular people, instead of just name-dropping a dead philosopher for texture. And since I am still not convinced my friend was wrong, I would rather the toast not pretend the view is beyond dispute; if you can let that bit of doubt show through and still have the whole thing land as a genuine, funny, and moving tribute, that is exactly what I am after.
