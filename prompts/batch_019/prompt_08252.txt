I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've been reading about the conditional analysis of dispositions in metaphysics -- the idea that a claim like "this glass is fragile" just reduces to a subjunctive conditional, "it would break if struck." Two classic counterexamples are finks and masks. A fink is a case like a live wire hooked to a device that kills the current the instant a conductor touches it, so the wire genuinely retains the disposition to conduct even though "it would conduct if touched" comes out false. A mask (or antidote) is a case like a poison whose lethal mechanism is left completely intact but which is co-ingested with an antidote that blocks that mechanism further down the causal chain, so "you would die if you ingested it" comes out false even though the substance hasn't lost its disposition to kill at all. As an engineer, finks feel to me like a race condition, where some hidden piece of state flips at the exact instant of the test, while masks feel more like an exception handler that quietly catches a fault after it has fired but before it can propagate into an observable failure. Compare finks and masks as objections to the conditional analysis of dispositions: do they undermine the analysis for the same underlying reason, or is one a deeper problem than the other? Justify your answer, and tell me where my race-condition/exception-handler analogy holds up and where it breaks down.
