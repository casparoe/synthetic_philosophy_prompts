I am currently plotting a detective novel and would like some philosophical help before I commit a key scene to paper. My sleuth, a meticulous former forensic pathologist named Adelaide Voss, prides herself on a method she privately calls "reading for elegance": whenever several hypotheses fit the physical evidence equally well, she settles on whichever one is simplest, most unifying, and most satisfying as a narrative, on the assumption that the most beautiful account of a crime is thereby the most likely to be true. A friend of mine who teaches philosophy of science heard me describe this habit of hers and immediately pointed me toward the literature on Inference to the Best Explanation, insisting that Adelaide's confidence in her own method was exactly the kind of thing philosophers of science have already found good reason to distrust. Two worries in particular seem tailor-made for my purposes: Peter Lipton's distinction between the "likeliest" explanation (the one best supported by the evidence and our priors) and the "loveliest" explanation (the one that would furnish the most understanding if it were true), which need not coincide; and van Fraassen's "bad lot" objection, that being the best of the explanations we have actually thought up is no guarantee of being anywhere close to the truth, since the real explanation may simply not be among the candidates under consideration at all.

What I need is a single, fully worked-out case that Adelaide could plausibly investigate: a crime, a small closed set of suspects, at least two rival explanations of what happened, and an ending in which her commitment to "loveliness," or her blindness to the bad-lot problem, leads her to name the wrong culprit even though her reasoning is impeccable by her own method's lights at every step. Please lay out the case, including all the clues a careful reader would need, in enough detail that the puzzle is genuinely fair, and then, once you have revealed the true explanation, say explicitly which explanatory virtue was doing the misleading work, and why the correct account struck everyone, including Adelaide, as the less elegant of the two right up until the very end.
