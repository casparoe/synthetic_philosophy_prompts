"The man came every now and then from the wheel to see how the work went on, and at length would take his axe as it was, without further grinding. 'No,' said the smith, 'turn on, turn on; we shall have it bright by-and-by; as yet it is only speckled.' 'Yes,' said the man, 'but I think I like a speckled axe best.'"

and then Franklin turns it right back on himself: "I believe this may have been the case with many, who ... have given up the struggle, and concluded that a speckled axe was best; for something, that pretended to be reason, was every now and then suggesting to me that such extreme nicety as I exacted of myself might be a kind of foppery in morals ... and that a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance."

what i cant decide, for the character's sake, is whether Franklin means this as an actual confession of weakness or as a pretty dry joke on himself, like he knows its a rationalization and is showing us the seams on purpose. is there a real "sincere" reading here or is the irony basically the whole point? also wondering if its the same trick as the later line about pride, where he says even fully beating it would just leave him proud of his humility - same move twice, or two different things? not looking for the standard "here's franklin's 13 virtues" rundown, more a close read of what this specific bit is doing.
