My sibling is getting married in a few weeks, and I've been asked to give the toast at the reception. My sibling and their partner met three years ago in a graduate reading group on Bayesian epistemology, where, according to family legend, they spent the first several months of their acquaintance in an escalating disagreement over a point in that week's reading before anyone else in the group noticed they had started dating. My sibling is finishing a philosophy PhD on the epistemology of disagreement, and one dissertation chapter argues that the common-prior assumption is doing most of the real work in results like Aumann's, so that such results smuggle in exactly the kind of shared background that makes them uninteresting as models of how actual strangers resolve actual disagreements. Their partner is a game theorist. I'm a PhD student in economics myself, which is apparently why I've been deputized to get this right: something like half the room will be people from their two departments who will notice immediately if I misstate the theorem, and the other half is family who have never heard "common knowledge" used in the technical sense and shouldn't have to.

I'd like the toast to be built around Aumann's agreement theorem, but I don't quite trust my first instinct for how to use it. My working line was that the theorem "proves" two sufficiently rational people who love each other can never really disagree, and the longer I sit with it the less sure I am that this is true to the theorem, flattering to either of them, or even consistent with what my sibling has spent years arguing in print. As I understand it, the theorem only forces equal posteriors given a common prior and common knowledge of each other's posteriors, and both of those are substantial, hard-won conditions rather than automatic facts about being intelligent and fond of one another; even granting them, the theorem promises agreement, not correctness. Could you write me a five-minute toast that uses the theorem honestly, including that fine print, that doesn't quietly contradict my sibling's own scholarly position for the sake of a tidy line, that stays funny and specific to these two people rather than turning into a lecture on information partitions, and that still lands as a toast for the relatives who will only be listening for whether it's sweet?
