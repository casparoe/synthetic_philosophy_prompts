I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly on philosophy of mind, and this term I'm taking a graduate seminar on premodern theories of self-awareness that has just turned to Avicenna. For next week's meeting we were assigned the "flying man" argument, which comes near the end of the first chapter of his treatment of the soul in the Kitab al-Nafs (the psychological part of the Kitab al-Shifa'), and before I say anything in seminar I want to make sure I actually have a defensible reading of it rather than just the received gloss. Here is the passage, in the rendering we were given, based on Fazlur Rahman's edition:

"One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects — created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence."

Most people in my cohort are content to file this away as "Avicenna's version of the cogito" and move on, but that comparison has started to bother me, and I would rather work out what the argument is actually doing than borrow Descartes as a crutch. My worry is that, stated baldly, the inference runs from a purely intensional premise — the flying man is aware of his self without being aware of his body — to an extensional, metaphysical conclusion, namely that self and body are really distinct. That has the shape of the masked-man fallacy, and it sits uncomfortably close to the objection Arnauld later pressed against Descartes' own real-distinction argument with the right-triangle case in the Fourth Objections.

So there are three things I would appreciate help sorting out. First, is that really the argument's logical shape, or is there a more charitable reconstruction on which "affirm the existence of" is not functioning as the kind of opaque, belief-like context I am assuming it is? Second, what work is the closing sentence about imagining a hand actually doing — is it Avicenna's own attempt to head off exactly this kind of worry, and if so, does it succeed, or does it just restate the phenomenological premise in slightly different words? And third, if the argument cannot be rescued as a direct proof of dualism, is there a more modest conclusion in its vicinity — something about the immediacy or non-observational character of self-awareness, rather than its metaphysical upshot — that the passage does support, and would that weaker conclusion actually have sufficed for whatever work Avicenna needs the flying man to do at this point in his psychology, or is he unambiguously after the stronger result?
