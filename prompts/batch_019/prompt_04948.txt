Over a forty-year career as a particle physicist, I grew accustomed to the idea that whatever layer of physical description I had been taught was "fundamental" would eventually be superseded by a deeper one: molecules gave way to atoms, atoms to nuclei and electrons, nuclei to protons and neutrons, and those in turn to quarks and gluons, with some of my more speculative colleagues still betting on preons or some finer structure beneath the Standard Model. Since retiring, I have been reading my way through contemporary analytic metaphysics, and I was struck to find that this very pattern -- one supposedly fundamental layer forever giving way to a deeper one -- is treated there as a serious problem in its own right, quite independent of anything physics could in principle settle.

As I understand it, most philosophers who work with the notion of metaphysical grounding (the relation that is supposed to hold between a fact and the more basic fact or facts in virtue of which it obtains) tacitly assume that grounding chains must be well-founded: any grounded fact must, after however many steps, terminate in something fundamental and ungrounded, on pain of an unintelligible regress. This assumption is sometimes labeled, by analogy with the epistemic regress problem, "foundationalism" about ground. But I gather it has been seriously contested: some philosophers defend a kind of metaphysical "infinitism," on which a fact can be perfectly well grounded even though its grounds never bottom out (reality being, as it were, "gunky" all the way down, with every layer resting on some further, deeper layer); others defend a "coherentist" picture on which the grounding order need not even form a strict hierarchy, but could instead be circular or mutually supporting. When I raised this with an old colleague from my physics reading group, he waved it away as a manufactured problem: philosophers, he said, simply can't stand to leave a hierarchy without a foundation. The arguments I have since found on both sides, however, seem to me to have real teeth, and I have not been able to settle the matter for myself.

I would like you to write me an essay that adjudicates this dispute properly. Please set out the strongest case for foundationalism about grounding, including whatever force the regress, or "unintelligibility," argument actually has, and the strongest case for the infinitist and coherentist alternatives. In assessing the latter, please weigh whatever evidential relevance should be assigned to the fact that, in physics, each supposedly fundamental layer has so far always been succeeded by a deeper one -- a worry I understand Jonathan Schaffer has pressed against simply assuming that a fundamental level exists. I would then like you to reach, and explicitly defend, a definite verdict of your own as to which structure -- foundationalist, infinitist, or coherentist -- grounding should be presumed to have; or, if you think the correct verdict is conditional on further empirical or metaphysical commitments, please state exactly what it is conditional on.
