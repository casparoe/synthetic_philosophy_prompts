I'm a junior and one of five student members on my high school's Honor Council, the panel that hears academic-integrity cases and forwards a recommended finding and sanction to the Dean of Students, who as far as anyone on the council can remember has never overruled us. I'm also taking our school's Constitutional Law elective this semester, where we spent a week comparing textualism and purposivism, using Justice Gorsuch's opinion in Bostock as the main example and touching on how Justice Scalia treated the Second Amendment's prefatory clause in Heller. Since then I've started noticing the same fight playing out on my own council, just without anyone calling it that. We vote this Thursday on a case I've been asked to write the draft opinion for, and I'd rather bring the council something rigorous than an opinion that just announces a side.

The facts aren't in dispute. A sophomore used an AI chatbot at home while finishing a physics problem set, asking it to re-explain one concept and check two algebra steps before she wrote up her own final answers. She saved the transcript and handed it to her teacher unprompted, so the only real question is whether what she did counts as a violation. Section 4 of our Honor Code bars submitting work "prepared, in whole or in part, by another person" — language written before chatbots existed. The section on world-language homework, by contrast, specifically bans "translation software" by name. Two council members take that contrast as proof the people who wrote the code knew how to reach beyond human-to-human help when they meant to, and chose not to here. The handbook's opening Statement of Purpose — never before treated as a rule anyone could actually be found to violate — says the Honor Code exists so that "grades reliably reflect each student's own understanding." The other two members think that once you ask what the rule is actually for, a chatbot solving her algebra obviously defeats it, whether or not a chatbot counts as a "person." I find the first camp too literal and the second too willing to rewrite the rule we were actually given, and I don't know how to adjudicate between them.

Could you draft the opinion I'll bring to Thursday's meeting? I need it to reach a clear finding on whether the sophomore violated Section 4, recommend a sanction from the options our handbook allows (no action, a reduced-credit redo, or a formal violation that goes on her record and is reported to colleges if they ask), fairly present the strongest argument for whichever side loses before explaining why it still loses, and close with a general rule for which interpretive default — the specific words on the page, or those words read against the purpose behind them — this council should reach for the next time a new technology outpaces our handbook, since this won't be the last case like it.
