So a few weeks back I went down a youtube rabbit hole that started with a video about the "dollar auction" game -- the one where an auctioneer sells a dollar bill, but the twist is BOTH the winning bidder and the second place bidder have to pay whatever they last bid (only the winner actually gets the dollar though). Because of that rule, once two people are neck and neck, whoever's currently in second is basically staring down a guaranteed loss unless they bid again, so both sides keep nudging it up and the final price ends up way higher than a dollar, sometimes like $10 or more in classroom demos apparently. I got kind of hooked on this and started reading about "escalation of commitment" and sunk costs, and it turns out theres an actual live disagreement among decision theory people about whether bidding "just one more increment" once your already stuck in the trap is genuinely irrational in that moment, or whether it's totally rational given where things stand and the only irrational bit happened way back when you placed your first bid at all (or maybe nothing was ever irrational and it's more like a war of attrition where holding out can pay off). Somewhere in there I also read about this idea that real rationality might be less about recalculating your best move at every single step and more about being the kind of person who commits to a plan ahead of time and doesn't reconsider, kind of like Ulysses tying himself to the mast -- but I'm honestly fuzzy on how that's actually supposed to settle anything.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists at, say, a conference mixer, who both know this stuff cold and end up as the last two bidders in a dollar-auction party trick someone starts as a joke? As the bids climb past a few bucks I want them to actually go back and forth for real -- one keeps insisting that bidding again is still the rational move given where things stand right now and that backing out is what would actually be irrational, the other insisting the real mistake was ever treating this as a series of separate decisions instead of committing to a rule up front, and that "staying open to reconsidering" is precisely the design flaw that's bleeding them both dry. I want them responding to each other's actual objections as the numbers climb (maybe even calling out that the other's position conveniently lines up with wanting to win the dollar) rather than just each repeating their opening speech, and neither one should come off as the obviously-wrong one -- my understanding is actual game theorists are genuinely split on this.
