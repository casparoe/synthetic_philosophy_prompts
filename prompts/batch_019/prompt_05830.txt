A friend and I spent a long car ride arguing about whether Occam's razor has any real justification, or whether it's just a convention that scientists find convenient. He kept insisting that "prefer the simpler hypothesis" is an aesthetic bias dressed up as a methodological principle, with nothing behind it but habit. I countered that there's supposedly a rigorous version of this idea -- something about assigning hypotheses higher prior probability the shorter their code is on some universal Turing machine -- but when he asked why the choice of machine wouldn't just smuggle the same arbitrariness back in, I had no real answer. Some digging afterward turned up the term "invariance theorem" as the standard reply, but I still can't tell whether it actually resolves the worry or just relabels it. Could you explain, with some precision, how Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity are meant to formalize Occam's razor in the first place, what the invariance theorem does and doesn't establish in response to the choice-of-machine objection, and whether a residual conventionalism survives even so?
