Ok so my housemate Dev (he's a few years into a philosophy PhD, works on decision theory, so he fights dirty) and I got into a whole thing last night about money pumps and I need backup, because I know Im right but I could not close the argument in the moment. Setup in case its useful: I like chocolate better than vanilla, vanilla better than strawberry, and — the "irrational" bit — strawberry better than chocolate. Classic money-pump story: an ice cream guy starts me off with vanilla, sells me chocolate for my vanilla plus a dime (I prefer chocolate to vanilla, so I pay), then sells me strawberry for my chocolate plus a dime, then sells me vanilla back for my strawberry plus a dime, and now I'm holding the exact scoop I started with, just thirty cents poorer. Standard moral: cyclical preferences are irrational because they're exploitable like that.

Dev's line was that once you actually see the pump coming there's only two things on the menu: "sophisticated" choice, where you foresee the whole loop and just refuse to get on the merry-go-round at all (which, he says, is really just caving and acting transitive), or naive choice, where you don't see it coming and get fleeced every single time. He thinks "resolute choice" — deciding on your whole plan up front and then actually sticking to it, including declining the final trade even though, right there in the moment, you'd locally rather take it — isn't a real third option, because at that last node you either (a) don't actually have the preference we said you had, so there's no puzzle left, or (b) you do have it and you're just knowingly acting against your own preference, which is exactly the kind of thing the money pump was suppose to convict you of in the first place. I kept insisting resolute choice is a genuinely different, defensible thing and not just relabeled irrationality, but every time I tried to explain why, I ended up sounding like I was redefining "rational" to mean "whatever avoids the pump," which is obviously question-begging, and Dev called me on it. Twice.

So — can you make the actual, non-question-begging case for resolute choice? I want it to engage head-on with option (b) above instead of dodging it, i.e. explain why sticking to a plan you'd locally rather abandon isn't just akrasia wearing a nicer outfit, and I'd rather you not just assert "plans get evaluated as wholes, not choices in isolation" as though that alone settles it, since that's the exact move that got shot down at dinner. Bring in Kavka's toxin puzzle or the Allais paradox if they're useful for making the case — I've got a feeling my ice cream example is really a baby version of something bigger, I just can't see the whole shape of it yet.
