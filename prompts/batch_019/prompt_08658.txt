I'm a PhD candidate in political theory writing a dissertation on judgment-aggregation theory. This year my university's Graduate Research Integrity Panel created a non-voting "procedural adviser" seat for me, since none of the seven faculty members who actually vote have a background in social choice theory. Under the panel's bylaws, a doctoral student is "responsible for research misconduct" only if the panel finds, by a preponderance of the evidence, that (A) the disputed figures were altered from their originally recorded values, (B) the alteration was intentional or reckless rather than an honest error, and (C) the altered figures materially affected a central claim of the dissertation rather than a peripheral one; all three are required for a finding of responsibility.

We just closed the vote on a doctoral student from another department. Each member balloted on A, B, and C, plus a bottom-line vote that the bylaws fix to equal that member's own A-and-B-and-C. Ballots (A/B/C → vote): M1 Y/Y/Y → responsible; M2 Y/Y/N → not; M3 Y/N/Y → not; M4 N/Y/Y → not; M5 Y/Y/Y → responsible; M6 Y/N/N → not; M7 N/Y/N → not. Tallied issue by issue, A, B, and C each pass by majority (5-2, 5-2, 4-3), which under the bylaws' own rule yields "responsible." Tallied by each member's bottom-line vote instead, "not responsible" wins 5-2. Nobody miscounted, and every member's own three answers are internally consistent; the two procedures simply disagree.

The chair wants a short memo from me, to circulate before the panel reconvenes Thursday, resolving this case and specifying how such splits should be handled going forward. Please draft it. Reach a specific verdict for this respondent under whichever counting procedure you recommend (or defend a principled, case-by-case way of choosing between them, if that is your view), propose concrete next steps and a timeline, and engage seriously with the strongest objection to your recommendation: if you count issue by issue, address the worry that this can brand someone "responsible" when a direct majority, asked the ultimate question, would have cleared them; if you count bottom-line votes, address the worry that the panel's verdict then rests on premises its own majorities rejected.
