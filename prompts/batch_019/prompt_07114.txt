I am writing a novel that includes a scene in which a time-displaced Frank Ramsey is brought up to speed on present-day longtermism: the effective-altruist view, downstream of Bostrom's "astronomical waste" argument, that reducing existential risk is the overriding moral priority given the astronomical number of future people potentially at stake. I want his reaction to be faithful to the real Ramsey rather than a mouthpiece for either side, and the difficulty is that his own body of thought pulls in both directions at once. His 1928 paper on saving called discounting the welfare of future generations "ethically indefensible," which is exactly the anti-discounting move longtermists depend on; yet the philosophical essay he wrote in his twenties, published posthumously as "Epilogue," waved off cosmic vastness — including the eventual heat death of the universe — as irrelevant to how one should live, since for Ramsey a star is not rendered more significant than a person merely by being larger, and being impressed by sheer scale struck him as a confusion rather than an insight. Would the economist in him make him receptive to arguments that lean on astronomical stakes, or would the essayist in him treat the whole framing as a category error, mistaking a very large number for a very large reason? Give me your best reconstruction of his actual verdict, one that takes both strands of his thought seriously rather than quietly dropping whichever proves inconvenient.
