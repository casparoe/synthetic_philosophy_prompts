I've spent the past few weeks reporting a long-form piece on "authenticity" as a commodity in the influencer economy — the manufactured spontaneity of reality television, synthetic personas like Lil Miquela, and, most recently, virtual performers driven entirely by generative AI with no human being behind the persona at all. Digging into the prehistory of arguments about performance and public morals, I went back to Rousseau's 1758 Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles, his furious rebuttal to d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article on Geneva, which had floated the idea that the city should finally build itself a theatre. What struck me on this re-read is how structural Rousseau's objections are, rather than merely puritanical. He argues that the actor's trade is corrosive because it is, at bottom, a trade in counterfeiting the self; that the pity tragedy induces is a kind of currency we spend on fictional sufferers precisely because it costs us nothing and demands nothing further of us afterward; that whether public spectacle is safe for a given society depends on whether that society is already corrupt (his Paris) or still simple, egalitarian, and virtuous (his Geneva); and that the healthier alternative to the theatre is the open-air civic festival, where the distinction between performer and public dissolves entirely.

I'd like your best reconstruction of how Rousseau would size up today's landscape of reality TV, influencer culture, and especially AI-generated performers — entities that are, quite literally, nothing but a counterfeited self with no person behind the mask left to be corrupted by the performing. Does his own framework suggest he'd treat this as the culminating, most extreme case of the harm he diagnosed in eighteenth-century actors, or as something different in kind, precisely because there's no human sincerity left to lose? Would his "corrupt societies may as well have their theatres" logic lead him to a kind of resigned indifference toward a culture he'd already write off as lost, or would the sheer scale and algorithmic reach of the attention economy strike him as a new category of harm his framework wasn't built to handle? And is there anything in participatory, everyone-films-everyone platforms like TikTok that would strike him as closer to his beloved festival-of-citizens than the passive theatre he condemned, or does the profit motive behind the algorithm poison that resemblance? I'm not looking for a survey of scholarly opinion so much as a genuinely reconstructed, specific answer, grounded in the actual architecture of his arguments rather than a general gesture at "Rousseau distrusted artifice."
