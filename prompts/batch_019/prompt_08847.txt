I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole the last few weeks reading old posts from the AI safety/rationalist corner of the internet (LessWrong, a couple of MIRI PDFs, that kind of thing), even though I've got zero background in logic or CS, I just find this stuff genuinely fascinating. The one idea that's been stuck in my head is this thing people call the "Löbian obstacle," about why an AI supposedly can't ever fully trust it's own future self, or a smarter successor it builds, because of some theorem by a logician named Löb. From what I can peice together, the gist is that if a system tries to reason "if I can prove X, then X is true," that setup actually blows up into forcing X to be provable already, so you can't bootstrap trust in your own future reasoning out of nothing.

Here's where I get stuck though: doesn't this basically prove that the whole dream of "safe self-improving AI" is dead on arrival, mathematically? Like if a totally formal system running on pure logic can't even certify that its own future self (or a successor it designs) is trustworthy, how could any AI lab ever build something that keeps rewriting its own code, or hands off control to a smarter version of itself, without that trust just being fake in the end? It kind of feels like this is basically the same punchline as Gödel's incompleteness stuff, just re-skinned for the AI safety crowd - i.e. "no system can ever vouch for itself, full stop." I brought this up with a friend who codes for a living and even he agreed it sounded damning, but he couldn't point to where the reasoning actually breaks down. So which is it: is "provably trustworthy self-improving AI" basically a myth that alignment people haven't fully owned up to, or am I missing something about what this obstacle does and doesn't actually rule out?
