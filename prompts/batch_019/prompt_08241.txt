In my philosophy elective this term, we spent a couple of weeks on Berkeley's immaterialism, working through the Three Dialogues and getting used to the idea that ordinary objects like trees and chairs have no existence apart from being perceived -- esse est percipi, as our teacher wrote on the board more times than I can count. Curious about what else this same philosopher had written, I went looking on my own and found something I didn't expect: a strange little work called The Querist, made up of hundreds of numbered questions about the Irish economy that Berkeley never bothers to answer outright. Many of these queries deny, in one way or another, that money is wealth: he keeps suggesting that gold and silver coin are really just tickets or counters used for reckoning and moving goods around, and that a country's actual wealth lies in the industry of its people -- their labor and what it produces -- rather than in how much specie happens to be sitting in its coffers. He even sketches a plan for a national bank that could issue paper credit for a country like Ireland, which chronically lacked coin partly because absentee landlords kept draining it off to England.

This left me stuck on a question I can't settle on my own: is the monetary theory in The Querist actually connected to the immaterialism, or am I just noticing a coincidence because both involve Berkeley insisting that some familiar thing -- matter, money -- is less "real" than people assume? There's a real case for a connection: in the Dialogues, Berkeley's whole strategy is to strip away a supposed hidden substance behind the ideas and qualities we actually perceive, and in the Querist he seems to run a similar play on money, denying it any intrinsic value apart from its social function, while redefining wealth itself as a kind of power or relation -- power to command other people's labor -- rather than as a stockpile of stuff. That looks like the same move: trading a substance for a relation. But there's also a case against reading too much into this: Ireland in the 1730s had a genuine, specific monetary crisis, driven by ordinary causes like an unfavorable trade balance and landlords who spent their rents abroad, and Berkeley had plenty of contemporaries who argued that money isn't the same thing as wealth for purely practical reasons, with no idealist metaphysics anywhere in sight. So maybe the resemblance is just two separate uses of one common rhetorical move ("X isn't really what you think it is"), rather than evidence that one theory grows out of the other.

Could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this: should Berkeley's economic writing in The Querist be read as a genuine, if undeclared, extension of the metaphysics he laid out in the Principles and the Three Dialogues, or is the resemblance mostly superficial, resting on two quite different senses of "not real"? Please lay out the strongest version of each side before settling anything, and don't let yourself off the hook with a flat "it's a bit of both" -- I want an actual verdict, defended on its merits, even if it ends up being a qualified one (something like "yes, but only for this specific piece of the theory" would still count as a real answer).
