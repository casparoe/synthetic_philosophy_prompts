Last weekend a friend of mine, who's midway through a PhD in economics, sent me a blog post arguing that any sufficiently rational agent, human or artificial, must end up with transitive preferences on pain of being "money-pumped" -- strung along through a cycle of trades, each one a locally attractive improvement, until it has handed over an arbitrarily large amount of money while ending up exactly where it started. I was unconvinced, and the conversation quickly left AI behind and turned into a two-hour argument, over dinner, about how much weight the classical money-pump argument for transitivity can really bear.

My position was that a "resolute" chooser -- someone who, in Edward McClennen's sense, settles on a plan for an entire sequence of trades in advance and then sticks to it even at a later point where the locally preferred move would be to deviate -- can walk straight through the cycle without ever being pumped, all while still ranking the three options intransitively when they're considered pairwise and out of context. If that's right, the money pump would show only that myopic, un-resolute agents are exploitable, not that intransitive preferences themselves are irrational. My friend's reply was that this concedes the game rather than winning it: for a resolute plan to be well-defined at all, the agent needs a consistent ranking over whole sequences of trades, and if that higher-order ranking must itself be transitive to avoid a second-order pump among plans, then transitivity hasn't been refuted so much as relocated onto options individuated by the paths that lead to them. We argued about that for a while and never even got to the "sophisticated" alternative -- the chooser who reasons backward and simply declines to enter the cycle in the first place -- which in hindsight seems like it changes the shape of the whole dispute.

I'd like you to give this the fuller hearing we didn't manage. Write an essay that first lays out the money-pump argument for transitivity in its strongest form, then works through the sophisticated and resolute responses in turn, addressing head-on whether either one shows that cyclical preferences over the base options can survive as rational, or whether my friend is right that the transitivity requirement simply resurfaces at the level of plans. I'd also like your take on the outcome-individuation move -- the idea that once options are individuated by the paths that produced them, the "cycle" was never really a cycle over the same three things -- and on how much the whole argument trades on the exploited agent's lacking foresight about the sequence of offers to come. Please don't hedge with "reasonable people disagree": reach an actual verdict (something like "transitivity binds only agents who can't or won't precommit" would count as an answer, and so would "the money pump succeeds without qualification, resolute choice notwithstanding") and defend it against the strongest version of whichever reply you end up rejecting.
