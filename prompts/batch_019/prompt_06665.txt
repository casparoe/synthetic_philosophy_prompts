I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, and this week's session was given over to the "unity of the proposition" problem and its sharpest formulation, Bradley's regress. The puzzle, as the instructor put it, is that a proposition such as "Desdemona loves Cassio" cannot simply be the collection of its constituents — Desdemona, the loving relation, and Cassio — because a mere collection isn't the sort of thing that can be true or false, while the proposition is. Something has to bind the constituents into a single unified fact rather than leaving them an inert list. But if a relation is what does the binding, we can ask how that relation itself is connected to the two things it relates; supplying a further relation to answer this only recreates the original question one level up, and so on without end. We spent a long time on why Frege's doctrine that concepts are "unsaturated," Russell's multiple-relation theory of judgment, and Armstrong's insistence that states of affairs are non-mereological wholes are each supposed to stop the regress at a different point, and on why nobody in the room thought any of the three fully succeeds.

Write me a short joke built out of this material. It needn't be laugh-out-loud funny, but it should be recognizably about Bradley's regress rather than a generic "philosophers can never agree" joke: the punchline should turn on the actual logical shape of the problem — a relator that itself turns out to need relating to what it relates, generating one relator too many — rather than just name-dropping the term. An analogy is fine as a vehicle (a matchmaker, a piece of glue, a translator, whatever suits) provided it actually reproduces that structure rather than just gesturing at "things needing connectors." Someone who has taught this material should hear the joke and know exactly which move in the debate it is sending up.
