"Sharon Street's Darwinian Dilemma argues that realist theories of value, on which evaluative truths hold independently of our attitudes, run into trouble once we take seriously that natural selection has profoundly shaped which evaluative judgments we're disposed to make. Street asks what relation holds between these selective forces and the independent truths realism posits. If there's no relation, it would be a wild coincidence for our evolved evaluative dispositions to line up with the independent truths, so realists are stuck with a sweeping skepticism about their own moral judgments. If instead there is a relation, natural selection favored ancestors who made true evaluative judgments because those judgments were fitness-enhancing, realism can explain the correlation. But Street argues this 'tracking' story, also known as the third-factor reply, isn't scientifically credible: it's much more parsimonious to say selection favored whatever judgments promoted survival and reproduction directly, without any need to mention independent truth at all. Since neither horn is comfortable for the realist, Street concludes we should give up robust realism in favor of an error theory, on which none of our moral judgments come out true, since terms like 'good' and 'wrong' fail to refer to any real property, natural or otherwise."
