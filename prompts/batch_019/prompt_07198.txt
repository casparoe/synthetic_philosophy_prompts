I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and this fall we spent two sessions on anthropic reasoning: the Sleeping Beauty problem, the Doomsday Argument, Bostrom's Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), its rival the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), and the "presumptuous philosopher" case Bostrom uses against SIA. Two of my students, Julian and Nora, got into an argument after the second session that neither would let drop; both turned up separately to my office hours the following week still trying to relitigate it, and I suspect each was hoping I'd declare a winner. I've reconstructed their exchange as best I can from what each told me, and I'd rather get an outside read on it than referee it myself, since my own sympathies keep flipping from one line to the next.

(1) Julian opened by defending the Doomsday Argument: if, per SSA, you treat yourself as a random sample from all humans who will ever live, then finding your birth rank to be somewhere in the first hundred billion or so is mildly surprising on hypotheses where humanity spreads through the galaxy for eons and trillions more people are born, and unsurprising on hypotheses where it ends relatively soon — so your birth rank alone should nudge you toward "soon," quite apart from anything you know about pandemics or asteroids. (2) Nora objected that this can't be the whole story, since it would let you extract a substantive fact about civilization's future from nothing but your place in a list, which smells like a free lunch; she invoked SIA, on which hypotheses with more observers were always more likely to produce an observer like you in the first place, a consideration that exactly cancels the Doomsday shift once you also condition on your own existence, leaving you with whatever your ordinary, non-anthropic estimate of humanity's prospects was to begin with. (3) Julian replied that SIA is not a free rebuttal, because it inherits the presumptuous philosopher problem: if two cosmological theories are equally well-supported by the physical evidence, but one entails a vastly larger universe — and hence vastly more observers — than the other, SIA says you should already be nearly certain of the "large" theory before anyone points a telescope at the sky, purely because more people would exist to ask the question under it; letting headcounts of hypothetical people settle physics from the armchair is absurd, so if one of SSA and SIA has to go, it should be SIA, Doomsday conclusion included. (4) Nora answered that the two cases aren't parallel: the philosopher's case is stipulated so that no ordinary evidence bears on the question, which forces all the weight onto anthropics, whereas actual reasoning about civilization's prospects is soaked in ordinary evidence about technology and risk; using SSA there doesn't add a small anthropic correction, it lets a purely self-locating fact override a question we could otherwise investigate on the merits, which is presumptuous in its own way. (5) Julian countered that this is a difference of degree, not of principle: once you grant self-locating facts any evidential weight at all — which Nora must, to run the SIA rebuttal in the first place — nothing said so far explains why that weight should be negligible in one case and overwhelming in the other; someone owes an account of what fixes the size of the effect. (6) Nora's last word, before they both had to leave, was that the real culprit might be the reference class: "humans" is a reasonably natural class to sample from, whereas SIA's "all possible observers, weighted by how many exist under each hypothesis" looks like a class gerrymandered precisely to produce the cancellation, and that this is where the philosopher's presumptuousness might creep back in. She admitted this was more a hunch than an answer to (5).

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just handing one of them an overall win? I'd especially like to know whether move (5) actually succeeds against Nora, whether there's something about anthropic reasoning that both of them are overlooking, and whether you think there is a principled way to retain enough of it to block the Doomsday Argument without also licensing the presumptuous philosopher — or whether, as Julian suggests, we are simply stuck choosing which bullet to bite.
