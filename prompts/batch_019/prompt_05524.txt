I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and this semester I'm TAing an undergraduate seminar that traces theories of truthfulness and deception from antiquity to the present. We just spent two weeks on Augustine's two dedicated treatises on lying — the early *De Mendacio* and the later *Contra Mendacium* (the latter written against a correspondent, Consentius, who had proposed that Catholics use deceptive infiltration to expose the Priscillianist sect) — and the take-home midterm asked students the following:

"Reconstruct Augustine's argument for the claim that lying is never morally permissible, not even to save an innocent life. Explain how this absolute prohibition is supposed to be compatible with Augustine's own claim that some lies are far graver than others. Finally, apply his framework to the following case: a person is asked, by someone bent on killing an innocent third party, where that third party is hiding. Does Augustine's view leave this person any legitimate 'way out,' and if so, what is it?"

I have a stack of these to get through and would rather have a second, independent pass on this one before I calibrate my grading for the rest, instead of relying purely on my own first read. Below is one student's answer in full. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me, for each substantive move, whether it holds up — and exactly where and why if it doesn't (please also say explicitly where it's genuinely strong)? Please end with (i) a total score out of 20, (ii) a corresponding letter grade, and (iii) a rubric as a JSON object with the keys `definition_and_setup`, `argument_for_absolutism`, `classification_of_lies`, `hard_case_and_way_out`, and `coherence` (worth 3, 5, 5, 5, and 2 points respectively), each mapping to an object of the form `{"score": <int>, "out_of": <int>, "comment": <string>}`.

The student's answer:

"Augustine's absolutism about lying is often traced to the same impulse that later drives Kant: a maxim permitting lying could not be consistently willed as universal law, since a universal license to lie would destroy the very practice of assertion that any individual lie depends on for its effectiveness. Augustine's own contribution is to complicate this bare prohibition with a hierarchy of lies rather than a flat one. Following the well-known division into jocose lies (told in play, where the shared understanding that one is joking removes any real intent to deceive), officious lies (told to benefit someone else, e.g. to protect their life, chastity, or property), and malicious lies (told to injure someone), Augustine ranks these from least to most grave. Crucially, none of this changes the underlying verdict: even the most sympathetic officious lie remains, for Augustine, a genuine sin, differing from a malicious lie in degree rather than in kind. This is presumably why, when Augustine turns to the hard case of someone asked by would-be killers where an innocent person is hiding, his considered view is that the right response is to say nothing, or to refuse the question outright, rather than assert anything false, since his own definition of lying requires an assertion made with intent to deceive, and silence involves no assertion at all. In practice, though, Augustine treats this line as thin enough to bend: in Contra Mendacium he allows that, if silence is not a live option and an answer is forced, a direct denial that the person is present is the lesser evil and does not compound the original wrong, since the intention behind it is protective rather than malicious. The one place Augustine will not bend, even here, is truthfulness in matters of Christian doctrine, since a falsehood there endangers the hearer's soul rather than merely their body, which is why doctrinal lies outrank every lie told for a bodily motive, however grave. Underlying all of this is Augustine's deeper premise that lying damages the liar's own soul regardless of any good it does for someone else — which is exactly why no degree of benefit to another party could ever be sufficient to tip the balance in favor of speaking falsely."
