"State the Church-Turing thesis and explain why it is standardly regarded as a thesis rather than a proven theorem. Then assess the claim -- sometimes made in popular discussions of quantum computing -- that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could refute the thesis."

And here's the student's answer, also verbatim:

"The Church-Turing thesis holds that any function that is 'effectively calculable' -- computable by a human clerk following a fixed set of mechanical instructions, with unlimited time and paper -- is computable by a Turing machine. It's normally called a thesis rather than a theorem because 'effectively calculable' is an informal, pre-formal notion, while 'Turing-machine computable' is a precisely defined mathematical one, and you can't rigorously prove that an informal notion lines up with a formal one -- you can only accumulate evidence for it. Historically, that's exactly why the thesis is so convincing: Turing machines, Church's lambda calculus, and Kleene's general recursive functions were all designed independently to capture the same intuitive idea, and when they turned out to define exactly the same class of functions, that convergence is usually treated as having effectively proved the thesis, since it's hard to imagine what more evidence anyone could ask for.

Because the thesis is about computability in principle rather than speed, most computer scientists think it's untouched by ordinary algorithmic speedups. Quantum computing is often raised as a possible threat to it, though: algorithms like Grover's exploit superposition to check every branch of a search space at once, which is why a large enough quantum computer is expected to solve NP-complete problems -- intractable for classical machines -- in polynomial time. If that's ever realized at scale, it would mean quantum computers can compute functions no Turing machine could compute even in principle, which would actually falsify the thesis rather than just outrun it."
