I've been going down a rabbit hole of AI-risk / rationalist blog posts lately and kept running into this idea called "Pascal's mugging" - some mugger tells you they've got the power to do something absurdly bad (like torture some ridiculously huge number of simulated people) unless you hand over your wallet, and even though you think the odds of them being serious are like one in a trillion, the sheer size of the badness they're threatening swamps the tiny probability, so straight expected-value math says you should pay up anyway. People treat this as a problem for naive expected utility reasoning once you let payoffs be unbounded.

It hit me that this is basically the same move as Pascal's own wager, just aimed at a mugger instead of God - a huge (functionally infinite) payoff swamping whatever probability you assign to it. So here's what I actually want to know: if Pascal got mugged like this, would he have to pay up to stay consistent with his own wager logic, or could he resist the mugger without quietly abandoning the reasoning that makes the wager work in the first place? Don't just give me the standard modern fixes people bring up now (bounded utility, leverage penaltys, etc) - I want your best guess at how Pascal specifically, given his own theology and how he thought about infinity and probability, would try to wriggle out of it, or whether he'd have to just admit he can't.
