So I'm putting together a collection of short stories that each try to smuggle in a real, specific piece of philosophy without turning into a lecture with dialogue tags -- think the way Ted Chiang does it, where the idea IS the plot. The one I'm stuck on right now is anthropic reasoning, specifically that split between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption. I went down a rabbit hole on this a while back (started with the Doomsday Argument, ended up in Bostrom's "Anthropic Bias") and the bit that really got me was the "presumptuous philosopher" -- the setup where physicists have two rival theories about the size/population of the universe, equally supported by all the evidence so far, except one theory just happens to imply there are way more observers in existance. A philosopher who accepts self-indication reasoning can supposedly sit in his armchair and tell the physicists not to bother building the expensive telescope, because the mere fact that he exists already makes the bigger-population theory astronomically more likely. Which seems insane! But the alternative (sampling only, no self-indication) is what generates the Doomsday Argument and its own mess of reference-class problems, so nobody gets to feel smug here.

I'd love a short story (2-3k words, but I'm flexible) that actually dramatizes this rather than just having two grad students argue about it over beers -- I want the choice between these two ways of reasoning about your own existence to be load-bearing for the plot, ideally tied to some decision a character has to make where the "correct" empirical answer can't arrive in time (a generation ship, a founding population, a bet, whatever you think works). Feel free to invent the setting entirely, I'm more attached to the reasoning having real teeth than to any particular backdrop. Only hard no: please don't just make it a "we might be in a simulation" story, that's not actually the same puzzle and it's the first place everyone's mind goes.
