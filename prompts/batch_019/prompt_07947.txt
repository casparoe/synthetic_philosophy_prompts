Question: In "On Denoting," Russell raises three puzzles about denoting phrases that motivate his theory of descriptions. State the three puzzles, give Russell's quantificational analysis of "the F is G," and explain what he means by calling definite descriptions "incomplete symbols." Then use the primary/secondary occurrence distinction to give the two readings of "The present King of France is not bald," including their truth-values.

Student answer: Russell thinks ordinary talk about "the F" tricks us into treating descriptions like names, and that's where the three puzzles come from. First, the excluded middle puzzle: since "the present King of France" doesn't refer to anything, both "the present King of France is bald" and "the present King of France is not bald" seem to have no subject for either predicate to be true or false of, yet by excluded middle one of the two has to be true. Second, the puzzle of nonexistence: to meaningfully deny existence, like in "the round square does not exist," it looks like the round square has to be some kind of object (a la Meinong) just so we can be talking about it at all, which commits us to a bloated ontology of subsistent nonexistent things Russell finds distasteful. Third, the puzzle of informative identity: "Scott is the author of Waverley" is informative, and George IV could wonder whether Scott wrote Waverley without thereby wondering the trivial "is Scott Scott," even though the description denotes Scott.

Russell's fix is to deny that descriptions are names to begin with. "The F is G" unpacks into: there's an x that's F, only one thing is F, and that thing is G - existence, uniqueness, and predication bundled into one quantified sentence. Because the description isn't a name, it doesn't need a referent to be meaningful; it's an "incomplete symbol" that only means something in the context of a whole sentence, which is what lets Russell dodge Meinong's extra objects in puzzle two.

For puzzle three, this is basically Frege's move: "Scott" and "the author of Waverley" have the same referent but differ in sense, so swapping them can change the cognitive content of a belief report even though the referent is unchanged, which is why George IV isn't automatically credited with wondering about a self-identity.

Finally, "the present King of France is not bald" is ambiguous because the description can take either primary or secondary occurrence relative to the negation. On the primary occurrence the description has narrow scope: "it's not the case that there's a unique King of France who's bald," which is true. On the secondary occurrence it has wide scope: "there's a unique King of France, and he's not bald," which is false, since no such person exists. That's how Russell avoids a truth-value gap - the sentence isn't neither-true-nor-false, it's just ambiguous between a true reading and a false one.
