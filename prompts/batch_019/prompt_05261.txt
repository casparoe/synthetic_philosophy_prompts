For six years I have organized my company's "calibration club" -- engineers and product managers who make and score probabilistic forecasts about our own launches -- and this year I am giving the ten-minute remarks that open our annual workshop, which always includes a couple of academic epistemologists as guests. This year's session opens under a cloud: our most respected forecaster staked a high-confidence prediction on a launch even though two colleagues had raised a specific objection to her model that she admits she never rebutted; she left her probability unchanged, and the forecast turned out badly wrong. Half the club calls this admirable conviction under pressure; the other half, including our guest philosopher, calls it a textbook case of "epistemic akrasia" and treats it as proof that nobody -- including her -- really knows whether it is rational to hold a belief steady once you have granted that the evidence against your own reliability carries real force. Please write my opening remarks: they should be honest about the disagreement, useful as a frame for the day's sessions, and they cannot hand either camp a tidy resolution to a question the specialists in the room know is still unsettled.
