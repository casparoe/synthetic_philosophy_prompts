I've been down a rabbit hole this week rereading old LW posts on "coherence arguments" -- the Eliezer-since-the-Sequences line that if your preferences aren't transitive (no cycles, so a consistent utility function is possible) then you're money-pumpable, and money-pumpable agents get drained dry by a sufficiently patient bookie, so anything smart enough to stick around eventually acts like an expected-utility maximizer. The basic pump I've got no trouble with: if you like A more than B, B more than C, and C more than A, someone can walk you around that loop forever, charging a small fee every time you trade up, and you end up back where you started minus your wallet.

What I can never keep straight in my head is the standard objection to this and the reply to the objection. The objection is that a sufficiently foresighted agent would just see the loop coming and refuse to step into it, so the pump only really threatens naive agents, not cyclic-preference agents as such. And I recall the reply being something like: fine, but the only way the agent can refuse is by turning down a trade that, by its own lights right at that moment, is a strict improvement -- and there's nothing in the actual preferences that says which of the three trades in the loop is supposed to be the one it refuses, since the cycle looks exactly the same from any point you start at. So "just dodge it with foresight" doesn't rescue intransitivity after all, it just quietly imports an extra rule from outside the preference ranking telling the agent where to plant its feet.

Could you build me an actual worked example of this -- real objects, real trades, real small fees, not just letters A/B/C -- that walks a cyclic-preference agent through (a) getting pumped the naive way, and then (b) trying to be clever about it with foresight, right up to the exact moment where being "clever" forces the agent to turn down a trade it would otherwise call a straightforward improvement? And once that's on the table, I'd genuinely like your take on the bigger question lurking underneath: does that moment show that "can't be money-pumped" really does just collapse into "has transitive preferences," or do foresighted/resolute agents get some kind of genuine wiggle room that a flat transitivity requirement doesn't capture? My housemate (decision theory PhD, of course she has opinions) says I'm overcomplicating it, but I don't think she's actually engaged with the symmetric-cycle part of the problem.
