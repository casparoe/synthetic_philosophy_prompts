Ordering, Manner, and Reflexive Intention in "and"

Consider two descriptions of the same event:

(1) She handed him the wrench and he tightened the bolt.
(2) He tightened the bolt and she handed him the wrench.

If "and" contributes nothing beyond truth-functional conjunction, (1) and (2) have identical truth conditions. Yet no competent speaker treats them as interchangeable: each is understood as reporting events in the stated order, so that (2) would be a strange way to describe a wrench being handed over only after the bolt was tightened. Since this ordering is absent from the truth-functional content of "and," where does it come from?

One answer posits ambiguity: alongside a symmetric, truth-functional "and," there is a distinct, asymmetric "and-then" sense. The response more consonant with Grice's own methodology keeps "and" univocal and derives the ordering pragmatically, from the fourth sub-maxim of Manner ("be orderly") together with the Modified Occam's Razor - the principle that lexical senses should not be multiplied beyond what is strictly necessary. If a single sense of "and" plus a general conversational principle accounts for the data, a second, dedicated sense is explanatorily redundant.

The pragmatic story handles cancellability comfortably: "She handed him the wrench and he tightened the bolt, though not necessarily in that order" is perfectly coherent, as we should expect if the ordering is merely implicated. The ambiguity theory also faces a cost the pragmatic account avoids: the same default ordering appears under bare juxtaposition of two independent sentences ("She handed him the wrench. He tightened the bolt."), where there is no conjunction to carry a special sense at all. A theory locating the effect in a lexical property of "and" has nothing to say about this parallel case, while a maxim governing orderly presentation predicts it without further stipulation.

Detachability is less tidy. Grice treats Manner-based implicatures as the paradigm of a detachable implicature, since they depend on the specific words chosen rather than on what is said, and so should disappear under substitution of an alternative, content-preserving form of words. But I cannot find any way of truth-functionally conjoining two event-reporting clauses in English - with "and," with a semicolon, with plain sequence - that lacks the ordering suggestion. If the implicature really is this hard to detach, that sits awkwardly with classifying it as Manner-generated, since Manner implicatures are supposed to be exactly the ones tied to a particular form rather than surviving across all of them.

A further tension concerns calculability. The ordering inference looks like a generalized conversational implicature: it goes through by default, absent special context, and disappears only under explicit cancellation. But Grice's calculability requirement asks a hearer to reconstruct, case by case, a chain of reasoning from what is said and the Cooperative Principle to what is implicated, and this reasoning is supposed to track the speaker's actual reflexive intention, in the sense given by Grice's account of utterer's meaning: to mean that q, over and above saying that p, is roughly to intend an audience to think that q partly by recognizing that very intention. A speaker who utters (1) without ever forming any intention about event order will still be read as endorsing that order. The implicature seems to outrun the intention meant to ground it.

The obvious reply is that the relevant intention need only be tacit or dispositional - something the speaker would endorse on reflection - rather than occurrent. But this concession threatens to empty the reflexive-intention requirement of content: if any regularity a cooperative speaker would accept counts as intended, it is unclear what the requirement still excludes, as opposed to collapsing into the kind of default-inference model Levinson, and from a different direction the relevance theorists, have offered as a replacement for Grice's picture rather than a friendly amendment to it.
