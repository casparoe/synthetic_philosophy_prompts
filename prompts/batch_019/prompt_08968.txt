Okay so I'm outlining a short story that's basically a modern riff on that old "Appointment in Samarra" fable -- the one Somerset Maugham retells, where the merchant's servant bumps into Death in the marketplace, panics because she looks startled, and gallops off to the other city to escape her, not realizing she was startled because that's literally where they had an appointment that night. I want my version to put the choice in front of my protagonist in a self-aware way -- she knows the old story, she knows running is exactly what got the servant killed -- and then has to decide what to actually do about it. While digging around for research I found out decision theorists turned basically this exact scenario into a real puzzle called "Death in Damascus" (Gibbard and Harper, I think, late 70s), which conveniently even uses the cities I already wanted for the story, Damascus and Aleppo, instead of Baghdad and Samarra. Now I definately can't write the scene until I understand what the "smart" move is actually supposed to be, or whether there even is one.

Here's my rough understanding of the puzzle -- correct me where I'm butchering it. Death is a near-perfect predictor who has already committed to a city, Damascus or Aleppo, before the man decides anything, and he dies only if he ends up in the same city as her. Because her choice is already fixed, causal decision theory seems to say his current choice can't causally influence it, so by symmetry he should be indifferent between staying and fleeing. But the moment he actually leans toward "I'll flee," that very inclination seems like it should raise his confidence that Death predicted "flee" and is waiting in Aleppo -- so fleeing suddenly looks bad, at which point leaning back toward staying should make staying look just as bad, and so on forever, which feels less like a solution and more like a loop. I vaguely remember reading that this is supposed to be a worse problem for causal decision theory than Newcomb's problem, something about there being no "ratifiable" act at all in this case (I think it was Andy Egan who pushed hardest on this?), whereas ordinary Newcomb-style cases at least let you patch causal decision theory that way. Some of this might be slightly garbled, which is half of why I'm asking instead of just winging it in the story.

Could you write me an essay that actually works through this properly, instead of just gesturing at "well, philosophers disagree"? Lay out what causal and evidential decision theory each actually recommend in Death in Damascus, explain why the case is supposedly worse for CDT than plain Newcomb, and then commit to an actual verdict -- should the man stay, flee, or is the honest answer more conditional than that (depending on how reliable Death is, say, or some other detail of the setup)? I brought this up with my writing group and literally no one agreed on what the rational move even is, so at this point I just want one clear, defended position I can build the ending around, not a survey of opinions.
