Longtime readers of the rationalist blogosphere will be familiar with the debate over whether rational preferences must be complete, and with the standard rejoinder that some values are incommensurable, which makes completeness an unreasonable demand. That rejoinder sent me back to Ruth Chang's work on hard choices, where she argues that many hard choices, such as her well-known example of choosing between a career as a lawyer and a career as a clarinetist, are neither cases of one option being better than the other, nor of the two being incomparable, nor of their being exactly equal in value, but instances of a distinct fourth relation she calls being "on a par." What I haven't been able to reconstruct on my own is the mechanics of her small-improvement argument for why "on a par" can't simply collapse into rough or imprecise equality: why does the fact that a modest, uncontroversial sweetening of one option still fails to make it determinately better than the other show that the original relation could not have been equality after all? I'd like you to walk through the actual structure of that argument, rather than just asserting its conclusion.
