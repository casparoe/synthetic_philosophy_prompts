I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently drafting a feature tracing how the doctrine of double effect — the old distinction between what an act aims at and what it merely brings about as a side effect — keeps resurfacing in present-day arguments over autonomous weapons and "acceptable" civilian casualties in drone strikes. Working backward to its most famous twentieth-century airing led me to Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet, Mr Truman's Degree, opposing Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. A philosophy tutor there who has been checking the historical sections of my piece, and who evidently has strong views about how badly this bit of the syllabus usually gets taught, offered — half as a joke — to test my own grasp of it by sending over an old tutorial question along with a former student's answer, promising to send her own marked-up version once I'd had a go. Since I'd rather not embarrass myself in front of a philosopher, I'm hoping you'll take the first pass at grading it in my stead.

The tutorial question was this: "In 1956, G. E. M. Anscombe circulated a pamphlet opposing Oxford's award of an honorary degree to Harry Truman. Reconstruct the philosophical argument behind her objection, explaining what work the doctrine of double effect and the distinction between intention and foresight are doing in it. Is her objection best understood as a claim about the proportionality of the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, or as a claim of a different kind? Indicate briefly how the episode connects to her later critique of consequentialism." The submitted answer reads: "Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet, Mr Truman's Degree, argued that Oxford should not honour a man responsible for the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and her campaign was in fact successful: the university's governing body voted down the proposal after her intervention, so Truman never received the degree. Her case rests on the scholastic doctrine of double effect, which distinguishes two ways a bad effect can be related to an action: as something intended, either as an end or as a means to it, and as something merely foreseen but not aimed at. Anscombe's central complaint was that the scale of civilian death at Hiroshima and Nagasaki was wildly disproportionate to any plausible military objective, so that even setting the intention behind the bombings aside, the sheer number of innocent people killed made the act impermissible by the standards of just war theory: the foreseeable civilian deaths so outweighed the foreseeable military gain that the bombing failed the proportionality test any legitimate instance of side-effect killing has to satisfy. This is also where her wider quarrel with consequentialism comes in: she thought that once you let yourself weigh a large number of enemy lives against a smaller number of your own soldiers' lives, you have already adopted the kind of cost-benefit reasoning that treats persons as interchangeable, and it is only a short step from there to justifying the deliberate killing of civilians whenever the numbers are judged to favour it."

Could you grade this the way a careful tutor would — a letter grade together with a mark out of 20 — and back it up with specific, point-by-point comments tied to individual claims in the answer, rather than a general impression, noting anything that is simply correct, anything that is subtly off, and anything that is a clear error? It would also help to see the mark broken down as a short JSON rubric, with categories along the lines of historical accuracy, command of the intention/foresight distinction, how well the answer locates the actual structure of Anscombe's argument, and the quality of the link drawn to her critique of consequentialism.
