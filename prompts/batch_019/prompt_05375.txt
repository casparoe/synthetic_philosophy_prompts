My grandpa Frank has been in home hospice for pancreatic cancer for about three weeks, and this past weekend the hospice nurse recommended bumping up his morphine drip because the pain had gotten bad enough that he was moaning even when he seemed unconcious. She was upfront that at the higher dose theres some chance it suppresses his breathing enough to speed up the dying process, though she also said that's not guaranteed and people on high doses sometimes linger a long time anyway. That set off a big argument between my uncle Paul and my aunt Diane (my mom's brother and sister) at the kitchen table afterward, and I've been turning it over since because I honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

(1) Paul, who took a nursing ethics course years ago, said this is a textbook case of the "doctrine of double effect": the nurse isn't intending to kill Grandpa, she's intending to relieve his pain, and the earlier death, if it happens, is a foreseen but unintended side effect, which is fine as long as there's a proportionate reason for it -- same logic, he said, as the classic trolley problem, where it's OK to flip a switch and send a runaway trolley onto a track with one guy on it to save five, even though you know for certain it'll kill him. (2) Diane shot back that the trolley problem is exactly the wrong example to reach for, because the whole reason philosophers still fight about it is that "foreseen, not intended" turns out to be something you can slap onto almost any description after the fact -- that's the point of the versions where people push someone off a footbridge instead. She said if you set a dose you privately suspect might tip him over, because watching him suffer for days is unbearable, you've basically already decided you want this to end sooner, and "unintended side effect" is just the flattering label for that decision. (3) Paul came back with: you need some version of that intend/foresee line or medicine falls apart entirely -- turning off a ventilator, doing any risky surgery, treating pain aggressively in general, all foresee death as a live possibility without aiming at it, so if foreseeing counts as intending, half of what hospitals do every day is murder. (4) Diane answered that needing some line doesn't mean his line is the right one -- maybe what's actually doing the work isn't intention at all but things like proportionality (how bad the pain really is, how close death already is anyway) or consent, and that Paul was treating "the doctor says she's not trying to kill him" as if it settled the question, when that's exactly what's being argued about.

Nobody budged, and my mom eventually just told them both to knock it off. Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of the argument at each numbered point, not just who wins overall, and point out anything you think both of them are missing that would actually be useful for thinking about what's happening with my Grandpa?
