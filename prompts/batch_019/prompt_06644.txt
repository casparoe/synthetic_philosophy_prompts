Here's Marcus's answer, word for word:

"Leibniz's argument starts from the idea that God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and morally perfect, combined with the Principle of Sufficient Reason, which says that nothing is ever the case without there being a reason why it's that way and not some other way. Applied to creation, this means God must have had a sufficient reason for making this particular world instead of any of the infinitely many other possible ones, and the only reason that fits a perfect being is that this world is the best one available to create. Leibniz pictures all possible worlds as existing first as ideas in God's understanding, and God's will then actualizes the best one. Importantly, God isn't just picking the best individual things one at a time and mixing them together, because not all good things can coexist in the same world — Leibniz calls this compossibility. For instance, since Leibniz thinks every individual's complete concept already contains everything that will ever be true of them, a version of Julius Caesar who never crosses the Rubicon would technically count as a different possible individual belonging to a different possible world than our Caesar, so God can't just mix and match the 'best' version of every individual into one super-world. Instead, God has to compare whole self-consistent (compossible) worlds against each other, and picks whichever one has the best overall balance of the greatest variety and richness of phenomena together with the simplest, most orderly underlying laws. This is also the basis for Leibniz's answer to the problem of evil: some suffering and even sin has to show up in the best possible world, either as a side effect of having the most orderly and lawful system of nature, or because a world containing free creatures who are capable of sinning is better overall than a world of perfectly well-behaved automatons, even though any individual instance of evil is regrettable on its own.

As for why the world only counts as contingent rather than necessary: Leibniz separates two kinds of necessity. A truth is absolutely (or logically/metaphysically) necessary when denying it produces a contradiction, the way 'a triangle has four sides' is self-contradictory. A truth is only morally necessary when it's guaranteed by God's wisdom and goodness choosing the best, rather than by logic itself, since God's reasons incline him toward the best world without logically forcing him the way a proof forces a conclusion, so a lesser world stays logically conceivable even though God would never actually pick it. But once you grant that a perfectly good and wise being will always, without fail, identify and select whichever world is best, and that there's only one such best world, God's choice was never actually up for grabs — moral necessity still pins down one and only one outcome just as reliably as logical necessity does. So Leibniz's two categories end up being a difference in name only: he needs the label 'moral' instead of 'absolute' so he can keep calling God's act 'free,' but the actual outcome is exactly as fixed either way, which is exactly why he can say with total confidence that this is the best of all possible worlds instead of merely his best guess."
