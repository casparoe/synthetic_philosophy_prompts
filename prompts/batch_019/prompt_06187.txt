I'm a PhD student in computer science working on machine learning, and what follows has nothing to do with my dissertation, but it has been bothering me and I haven't managed to think it through to my own satisfaction. My family owns three small neighborhood cafés, and since moving away for my program I've become the person who reviews the reports the three on-site managers send back each week: register totals, a brief inventory and waste log, a couple of timestamped photos of the prep counter and the walk-in shelves, and a note on any customer complaints. A couple of years ago, one location had an incident that never showed up in any report: a customer complaint about expired dairy that the waste log had no way of registering. I responded by adding the photo requirement and started spot-checking the waste numbers against supplier invoices, and every report since has looked clean. Last month, for an unrelated reason, I searched our city's public health-inspection database and discovered that the same café had actually failed an inspection three months earlier, over the walk-in's temperature log and a mold problem behind its door gasket. None of this had ever appeared in a weekly report, even though that week's walk-in photo looks completely unremarkable, since it's always taken from just inside the door and was never going to show a gasket problem or a slow temperature drift. As far as I can tell, nobody has been lying to me: the photos are real, the waste numbers reconcile, and the manager in question genuinely seems to believe things have been fine.

What I'd like help thinking through is the general problem this points to, rather than this one incident. My first thought was to hire a rotating outside inspector or mystery shopper, but the more I sit with it, the less this feels like a real fix: whatever that inspector happens to look for becomes just one more fixed target that reports can end up satisfying, while everything I haven't thought to ask about is exactly as unchecked as before. Waiting for a harder outcome measure, like annual profit or the official inspection score, doesn't obviously help either, since those move slowly, are noisy for all kinds of unrelated reasons, and by the time they shift, whatever caused it has usually been going on for months. I raised this over lunch with a couple of labmates, and the consensus was some version of "just do more unannounced visits," which doesn't feel like it engages with the actual difficulty. So, stated generally: given that I can only ever act on the finite list of things I've thought to check, while each manager has something close to complete access to what's actually happening on site, is there any way, even in principle, to structure this kind of reporting relationship so that I have good reason to trust what I'm told about exactly the things I didn't think to ask about? Or is that simply not possible, so that all I'm doing, and all I can ever do, is race to expand my checklist after the fact and hope the gap between what I check and what's true stays small enough not to matter? I'd value your actual assessment of that question over a list of best practices for managing remote employees.
