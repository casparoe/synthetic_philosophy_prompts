I spent most of my working life running a lab that studied limb-bud and growth-plate development in mice, and since retiring I've somehow ended up on the pediatric subcommittee of my local children's hospital's ethics advisory committee — apparently three decades of grant applications count as relevant qualifications for something. I've also, rather late in life, started reading philosophy in earnest, and a case that just came before the subcommittee has sent me back to Norman Daniels and, of all people, Michael Sandel, because I don't think the shorthand we usually rely on is going to survive contact with it.

An 11-year-old boy has been referred to us by pediatric endocrinology. His growth-hormone stimulation tests are entirely normal, his bone age is unremarkable, and there is no diagnosis to enter on a chart: he is simply short, for no cause anyone can identify, with a predicted adult height several inches below average for his age. His insurer has declined to fund recombinant growth hormone on the grounds that there is "no disease to treat," and the family has asked our hospital's discretionary treatment fund to cover it instead. What has stayed with me is that his older brother received the identical drug, fully funded, for documented growth-hormone deficiency, and is now taller than he would otherwise have been; the younger boy stands to end up shorter than his brother's own pre-treatment prognosis, for no reason he could ever explain except that the cause of his shortness has a name in a textbook and his brother's does not. Our fund's charter restricts it to "treating disease" rather than "enhancing normal traits," which sounded clear enough until I noticed that idiopathic short stature is defined purely statistically, as height some distance below the population mean — so that even if we funded every such referral, there would, by definition, always be a shortest few percent left over. That looks like exactly the sort of case Daniels' "normal species functioning" standard was meant to adjudicate, and exactly the sort it can't; and Sandel's discussion of height as a positional good leaves me wondering whether funding it would just subsidize one family's position in a status competition rather than treat anything at all.

Could you draft the short memo I would put in front of the subcommittee before we vote next week? I need a genuine recommendation — fund the treatment, decline it, or fund it only under stated conditions — together with criteria the endocrinology service could actually apply to the next borderline referral, since this will not be the last one of its kind. Please give the strongest argument against whatever you recommend its full due; I would rather circulate a memo that has already met its best objection than one that only persuades readers who agreed with it going in.
