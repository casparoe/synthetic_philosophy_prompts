I am a novelist whose current manuscript turns on a reveal that has to survive some philosophical scrutiny: for two hundred pages, my protagonist believes that the stranger who has been anonymously returning her late husband's letters and the intelligence officer who recruited her for a mission she still resents are two different men; in the final chapters, she learns they are the same person. I want this discovery to feel like she has genuinely learned something new about the world, not merely that a mask has been removed from a face she already knew — the way "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative in a manner that "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not, despite both being about the same planet. Frege's own resolution of this puzzle about identity statements, and the strain that later cases such as Kripke's Paderewski example put on it — a rational person sincerely holding contradictory beliefs about a pianist and a statesman who turn out to be one man — seem directly relevant to what she can coherently believe before the reveal and what exactly must change in her afterward. Please describe the most important implications of this literature for how I construct her belief states across the book, and flag whichever of the usual novelistic shortcuts (a literal disguise, plain ignorance of a fact, mistaken identity in the everyday sense) would actually let me dodge the underlying philosophical problem rather than dramatize it.
