A few months ago I was walking home alone quite late, feeling on edge for no particular reason, and for a split second a plastic bag caught under a parked car looked to me exactly like a crouching animal; by the next morning, in daylight, it was obviously just a bag. That small experience got me hooked on reading about the psychology and philosophy of perception in my spare time, even though I have never studied philosophy or cognitive science formally. I keep running into an idea some philosophers call "cognitive penetration": the claim that what we believe, want, or fear can reach in and change how things actually look to us, rather than merely changing how we interpret or react to what we see. I find the idea fascinating but strangely hard to pin down. Whenever I try to construct a clean example of it, such as my own bag-versus-animal moment, I cannot tell whether my fear genuinely altered the raw visual experience itself, or whether I saw the same ambiguous shape that anyone would have seen and simply attended more closely to its animal-like features, or jumped to the "animal" interpretation a split second faster than I would have in daylight. Almost any example I invent seems like it could be redescribed by a skeptic as attention, memory, or after-the-fact judgment doing all the work, leaving the actual perceptual experience untouched.

What I would like from you is a single, carefully worked-out example, either drawn from real perceptual research or invented from scratch, that brings this problem into the sharpest possible focus: a case that a genuine defender of cognitive penetration would want to cite as strong evidence that belief or emotion alters perceptual experience itself, yet which a committed skeptic could still resist by redescribing entirely in terms of attention, memory, or post-perceptual judgment. Please walk me through exactly where, within your example, the two sides would locate their disagreement, since that is the part I always lose track of, and then give me your own honest assessment of whether there is any way, even in principle, to settle who is right, or whether this kind of dispute is simply bound to remain empirically underdetermined no matter how cleverly the case is designed.
