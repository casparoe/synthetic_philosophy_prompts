I've spent a lot of time reading LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten over the years, and one habit that scene has given me is a fondness for "double crux"-style analysis: trying to find the specific claim that, if one side changed their mind about it, would actually resolve a disagreement. I usually see this applied to AI risk or decision theory, but lately, partly prompted by all the debate about conscription and targeted killing coming out of recent wars, I've been reading through the just war literature. I think the split between Michael Walzer's defense of what's come to be called the "moral equality of combatants" (in Just and Unjust Wars) and the revisionist critique associated with Jeff McMahan's Killing in War is an unusually good candidate for this treatment. Both sides are answering the same underlying question — does a combatant's individual responsibility for the justice of his cause matter to what he's permitted to do, and have done to him, on the battlefield — yet McMahan builds his answer directly out of ordinary interpersonal self-defense theory, while Walzer treats the battlefield as a special institution that such theory doesn't straightforwardly reach.

Could you write me a dialogue between two contemporary philosophers holding these opposing views? Rather than putting words in Walzer's or McMahan's actual mouths, just call them "the Traditionalist" and "the Revisionist." The Traditionalist should hold that combatants on both sides have an equal right to kill enemy combatants and an equal liability to be killed, regardless of which side's cause is just, and should treat this symmetry as indispensable to a workable ethics and law of war. The Revisionist should hold that liability to lethal force tracks moral responsibility for contributing to an unjustified threat, so that (with exceptions) unjust combatants lack the right to kill and just combatants retain it, even where individual unjust combatants are excused. Please make neither one a pushover. I'd like to see the Revisionist pressed on the epistemic objection — that soldiers are typically in no position to judge the justice of their cause — and on the worry that rejecting combatant equality collapses the independence of jus in bello from jus ad bellum that the laws of war rely on. I'd like to see the Traditionalist pressed, in turn, on how that symmetry survives cases of conscripts who are coerced or deceived about what they're fighting for. Let the dialogue run for a dozen or so exchanges, and end it with each side trying to state their actual crux — the specific claim that, if they came to reject it, would change their verdict on the moral equality of combatants — rather than letting them settle for polite disagreement.
