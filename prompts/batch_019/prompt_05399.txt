A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing over a passage of Pascal's that I had brought up almost in passing: his observation, in the Pensées, that because human beings could never agree on how to make justice strong, they arranged instead for whatever is strong to be treated as just, so that justice without force is dismissed as powerless while force without justice is denounced as tyranny, and the two are reconciled in practice by simply redefining the winning side as the just one. My friend took this as more or less confirmed by history and waved away every counterexample I raised as special pleading. I kept pressing him with the American civil rights movement, the Indian independence movement, and the largely peaceful collapse of Communist rule across Central Europe in 1989 — cases in which a side with no army, and often no recognized legal standing, seemed to prevail over regimes holding every material advantage, apparently on the strength of the justice of its cause rather than any force it could muster. We never settled whether such movements refute Pascal's remark or merely confirm it under another description: mass noncooperation and strikes might themselves simply be force operating by other means, so that the stronger side wins regardless, just under a different name.

What I would actually like is to settle this the way Pascal himself might have, rather than the way my friend and I did. Elsewhere in the Pensées he argues that ordinary people obey established authority not because they have verified its justice but out of custom and imagination, that overturning an established order is a graver evil than tolerating the everyday injustices it contains, and that genuine justice belongs to an order — the order of charity — radically incommensurable with the orders of body and of mind in which worldly power operates. Taking these commitments together with the justice-and-force fragment, what do you think Pascal would actually have said about a large, disciplined campaign of nonviolent noncooperation aimed at bringing down an unjust but firmly established regime? Would he have had to treat it as just one more instance of the stronger side dressing itself up as the just one? Would his own horror of civil disorder commit him to condemning any such campaign regardless of its restraint from violence and the justice of its aims? Or is there a reading of his views on which this is precisely the one path to justice he could endorse without contradicting himself? I would like you to work through the tension in his own position rather than resolve it in whichever direction happens to be convenient.
