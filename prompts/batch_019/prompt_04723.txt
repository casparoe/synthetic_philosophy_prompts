I'm a philosophy PhD student working mostly in epistemology, and my reading group has spent the last two sessions stuck on the anti-luminosity argument in Timothy Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits*. I can follow the formal skeleton on the page. Williamson considers a subject standing outside continuously from well before dawn, when she clearly feels cold, into the late morning, when she clearly does not, and picks out a long series of moments spaced so closely together that any two neighboring moments are subjectively indiscriminable. He then invokes a margin-for-error principle for this sort of inexact self-knowledge -- roughly, that if you know at some moment that you feel cold, you must still feel cold at the very next, indiscriminably similar moment -- and chains this from the clearly-cold end of the series to the clearly-not-cold end, generating a contradiction meant to show that no non-trivial condition is luminous, where a condition is luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, one is thereby in a position to know that it obtains. I'm presenting this chapter to the group next week and can reproduce every step, but I don't yet feel that I understand why imprecise self-discrimination should force so sweeping a conclusion, rather than merely showing that introspection gets shaky right around the fuzzy boundary of a gradual change.

The group is also split on what the argument is supposed to show about the KK principle: the thesis that knowing P puts you in a position to know that you know P. A couple of people in the group think the whole argument is really just a Sorites paradox wearing epistemic clothing, so that it only threatens luminosity for conditions like "feels cold" that admit of vague, borderline cases, and has nothing much to say about KK as a general thesis. Others insist that the vagueness of "feels cold" is doing no essential work, and that the same margin-for-error structure applies to "knows that P" even though knowing need not be vague in that way -- which is exactly why Williamson takes himself to have shown that KK fails quite generally for creatures with our sort of imperfect self-access, and not merely as an artifact of this one example. Could you give me an intuitive, non-formalism-first account of why the margin-for-error setup forces the more general failure of luminosity (and hence of KK), and tell me which side of my reading group has the better of the dispute?
