For my formal epistemology seminar, we have spent the last two weeks on Aumann's agreement theorem and its reliance on a common prior. For the response paper due next week, I would like a dialogue that shows what the theorem actually forces us to say about real, persistent disagreement, rather than one that just restates the proof.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Priya and Tomas, who are both fully conversant with the theorem and who currently hold different posterior probabilities for some specific proposition they have been debating (you may invent the proposition and the numbers). They have just gone through several rounds of honestly announcing and updating on each other's posteriors, a process that should generate common knowledge of one another's beliefs, yet their numbers still disagree. Priya holds that this remaining disagreement can only be explained by the fact that they never really shared a common prior, and she presses Tomas to say what, short of some hidden asymmetry in their evidence, could justify their priors having differed in the first place. Tomas defends a permissive view on which two equally rational agents may simply start out with different, individually coherent priors that owe no further justification, so that their disagreement can survive full mutual transparency without either of them being at fault. Let the two of them genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves, and do not force the dialogue to a tidy resolution if the arguments do not actually earn one.
