Here's the question I gave them: "Explain Kripke's skeptical paradox about meaning using the plus/quus example. State the dispositional theory of meaning and Kripke's two main objections to it. Then explain what the 'skeptical solution' is supposed to do, and evaluate whether it actually solves the problem or just relocates it."

And here's the student's answer, in full: "Kripke's puzzle starts from a seemingly mundane fact: I have only performed finitely many addition problems in my life, and all of them involved numbers smaller than, say, 57. Suppose I'm now asked to compute 68 + 57, and I answer '125' because that's what 'plus' means. Kripke's skeptic asks what fact about my past made it the case that I meant plus, and not some deviant function like quus, where quus(x,y) = x + y for x, y < 57, and quus(x,y) = 5 otherwise. Since every past computation I've done is consistent with both plus and quus, nothing in my prior usage seems to settle which one I meant -- so, Kripke argues, we can never really know which function we meant by '+,' or by any word at all. This is the skeptical paradox about meaning: for any word, past usage radically underdetermines its meaning going forward.

The most natural response is dispositionalism: to mean plus by '+' just is to be disposed to answer with the sum, for any addition problem you're presented with. Kripke thinks this fails for two reasons. First, my actual dispositions are finite -- there are additions I'd never live to finish, or numbers too large for me to even process, so my disposition doesn't automatically range over the whole infinite function the way 'meaning plus' is supposed to. Second, dispositions are purely descriptive: they tell you what you would do, not what you should do. If I make an arithmetical slip, that's compatible with my disposition (I did what I was disposed to do), but not with what I meant -- meaning is normative, it's supposed to justify one answer as correct and rule the rest out, and a disposition can't do that on its own.

Since no fact about the individual will do the job, Kripke's Wittgenstein proposes the 'skeptical solution': what makes it true that I mean plus by '+' is that my use of the symbol agrees with how the rest of my linguistic community is disposed to use it. Meaning plus, on this view, just is being disposed to answer the way my community would answer, and it's this widely shared disposition -- rather than any private, individual one -- that grounds the meaning facts and licenses the practice of correcting anyone who deviates (a quus-user would eventually get caught out once the numbers got large enough). This is also why Wittgenstein rules out a private language: without a community to check your usage against, there's nothing to anchor what you mean, so meaning-statements about a solitary individual would be empty.

Is this a genuine solution, or does it just relocate the puzzle to the level of the community? I don't think it's a mere relocation. The finitude objection loses its force at the community level, because even if no single person could compute arbitrarily large sums, the community as an ongoing, open-ended practice isn't limited the way an individual's dispositions are, and that's enough to secure a fact about communal meaning where there was none for the individual. So the skeptical solution earns its name -- it actually answers the challenge instead of just kicking it down the road."
