My grandpa Walt died this past Tuesday, 89, pretty peacefully, and because I'm the one in the family doing a philosophy PhD (modal logic and philosophy of religion, mostly) I've been drafted to give the eulogy Saturday, which everyone else seems to think is a nice fit and which I think is genuinely terrifying. Walt taught high school calculus for something like thirty years, but his real hobby, the thing he'd bring up completely unprompted at Thanksgiving, was Kurt Godel — not the incompleteness theorems, which he also loved, but the argument for an afterlife that Godel laid out for his own mother in a series of letters in 1961, after she'd asked him point-blank whether he thought people saw their loved ones again after death. Grandpas version went something like: the world is demonstrably rational and ordered rather than, as he loved to put it, "a meaningless heap of atoms," and a genuinely rational world couldn't just let a person's development get cut off mid-way by death with nothing there to complete it — so there has to be some kind of continuation on the other side. He picked this up from a book decades ago and it basically became his entire personal theology, precisely because, as he put it, it wasn't "one of the sloppy religions," just a chain of reasoning he was ready to defend against anybody, including the two guys from his old logic reading group who will 100% be there Saturday quietly grading me on it.

The service is at the funeral home, not a church, which he would have insisted on. And here's the part I'm actually stuck on: his argument doesn't work by ignoring how unfinished his own life felt, it runs on that feeling. He wanted to do a PhD in logic himself once, before money and then kids got in the way, and it obviously ate at him for decades, especially after my grandma died eight years ago, when "Wiedersehen" (his word, straight out of the Godel letter) stopped being an abstract nice idea and turned into the thing he actually needed to be true. So I don't think I'm allowed to just smooth the regret over to comfort people — on his own logic, the regret is basically the premise the whole thing runs on.

Could you write me the actual eulogy? Something like 900-1100 words, eight minutes or so out loud. It has to work for my cousins and his bridge buddies, who have never heard of Godel in their lives and just want to feel okay about grandpa, without being so watered down that the reading-group guys wince at it — I don't want a logic lecture, I want something that's allowed to be sad and a little funny and actually about Walt, built out of his real argument instead of sliding into generic comfort-talk the moment things get hard.
