For the past several months I have been working through Aristotle's *On Interpretation* on my own, mostly out of a general interest in logic, and the argument in chapter 9 about the sea battle has genuinely unsettled me. As I understand it, Aristotle points out that a sentence such as "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" must, like any other statement, be either true or false today, even though nobody currently knows which. But if the sentence is already true, or already false, today, it seems to have been equally true or false a year ago, or a thousand years ago, since truth does not seem like the kind of property that switches on at some particular moment. Followed all the way back, this appears to mean that literally everything that will ever happen was already fixed before anyone existed to influence it, regardless of what any of us decide to do in the meantime.

What strikes me is that this is a purely logical route to more or less the same conclusion that Laplace later reached on physical grounds with his image of a demon who, knowing the exact position and momentum of every particle in the universe, could calculate every future state from the laws of mechanics. I have started describing the sea battle passage to friends as, in effect, the first working proof of universal determinism, arrived at through grammar and the law of excluded middle roughly two thousand years before classical physics made essentially the same point mathematically. I did come across a short summary noting that Aristotle's own way out of the puzzle was to deny that such future-tense sentences are "bivalent," but that struck me as a technical maneuver that changes what we are permitted to say about the sentence without changing whether the sea battle itself is already locked in, so I do not think it actually rescues anything. Am I right that Aristotle, whether he intended to or not, beat the physicists to determinism by roughly two thousand years, just using language instead of matter in motion?
