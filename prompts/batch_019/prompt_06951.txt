I'm a philosophy PhD student working mainly in philosophy of science, but this term I'm auditing a formal epistemology seminar, where we just spent two sessions on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle: the requirement that my current credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that my credence in A at some later time will take a specific value r, must itself equal r (formally, something like P_now(A | P_later(A) = r) = r). Everyone in the seminar talks about this as a substantive, sometimes-violated constraint on rational belief, one that can apparently fail even for agents who never commit any ordinary probabilistic error. I raised this objection in section and didn't get a fully satisfying answer: the principle looks to me like nothing more than ordinary Bayesian conditionalization wearing fancier notation. Of course my present opinion about A, given that I'll believe r later, should be r itself — I'm just trusting that whatever process gets me to r will have gotten there by legitimately incorporating whatever evidence turns up between now and then. Why does van Fraassen need a separate Dutch-book argument for this, rather than it simply falling out as a corollary of ordinary updating?

This isn't purely an abstract complaint for me. I have a strong hunch that once I finish coding my last batch of interview transcripts this spring, I'll end up substantially more confident in my dissertation's central claim than I am right now. If Reflection really is just conditionalization under another name, it seems to license raising my confidence today to roughly wherever I expect it will land in a few months, rather than waiting around for the coding to actually happen — after all, I'd just be deferring to my own future, presumably better-informed, self, which is exactly what the principle asks of me. A friend in the statistics department is convinced this move is obviously illegitimate, but neither of us can articulate what, specifically, in the Reflection Principle would rule it out. Is my "it's just conditionalization in disguise" reading of the principle correct, and if not, where precisely does it go wrong?
