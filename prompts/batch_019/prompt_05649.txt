I'm reporting a piece for our science section on why siblings raised in the same household so often end up with entirely different accounts of their own childhood, and the research keeps pulling me back to a version of this problem that hits much closer to home. My sister and I have never agreed about our father, who died two years ago, and a recent dispute over his estate has hardened things into a standoff neither of us can let go of. She describes him as a fundamentally generous man and can list a long, specific set of episodes from throughout our childhood to prove it: money quietly given, plans rearranged, sacrifices made without being asked. I describe him as a fundamentally controlling man, and from that same stretch of years I can list an equally specific set of episodes to prove it: decisions made for us rather than with us, affection that only arrived once we had fallen in line.

What makes this hard to dismiss as ordinary family bias is that neither of us disputes the other's facts. She doesn't deny the controlling episodes; she just treats them as minor exceptions to who he really was. I don't deny the generous episodes; I read them as a kind of currency he spent to maintain control. Nobody is lying, and it doesn't reduce to selective memory either, since the disagreement survives even once every fact is on the table. I'd be curious what you make of a disagreement shaped like this one — not who's actually right about our father, but the more general question of whether one of us is simply mistaken, whether the truth here is permanently out of reach, or whether there's some more rigorous way of holding two contradictory, equally well-supported characterizations together, short of declaring a winner, retreating to "it depends on your perspective," or just concluding that people contain multitudes.
