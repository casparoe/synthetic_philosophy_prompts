I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and a few of us in the department run a small interview series where we bring in researchers to discuss work that never quite makes it into the seminar syllabus. Our next guest works on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL): roughly, the idea that if the universe (or multiverse) is large enough to contain many causally disconnected agents with a wide range of values, an agent can reason that her own choice to cooperate with others' aims is evidence -- not a cause, but evidence -- that relevantly similar agents elsewhere are reasoning the same way. On this view, something like mutually beneficial cooperation across a vast population of value systems becomes possible even though no two parties ever communicate, bargain, or causally affect one another. The idea traces back to Caspar Oesterheld's work on multiverse-wide superrationality, and I've followed the more recent write-ups from longtermist research groups closely enough to know the standard exposition, but I don't want the interview to just walk through the setup again for our listeners.

Could you draft nine or ten questions for this conversation? I'd like at least one question on each of the following, since these are the points where my own reading group has gone back and forth without really resolving anything: (1) how much of the argument's force depends on evidential decision theory specifically being correct, and whether someone committed to causal decision theory, or to an updateless or functional decision theory, can secure the same practical upshot on their own terms; (2) how confident we can really be that agents "similar enough" to us in decision procedure actually exist to correlate with, given that by hypothesis we have no causal contact with them and thus no direct evidence about who or what is out there; (3) the worry that cooperating in this sense commits you to giving weight to value systems you would find monstrous if you encountered them directly, and whether there is a principled way to exclude some correlated agents from the bargain that doesn't just smuggle in your own values as the tiebreaker; and (4) whether ECL is doing any independent normative work, or whether it collapses into ordinary hedging under moral uncertainty once the decision-theoretic vocabulary is stripped away. For at least three of the questions, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give -- the kind of follow-up you'd need when a guest retreats to something like "that's a modeling detail we haven't worked out yet." I'd also like the final question to press on falsifiability: what, if anything, would have to be true of the world for her to abandon ECL as a guide to action? Please skip generic warm-up questions about how she first got interested in the topic.
