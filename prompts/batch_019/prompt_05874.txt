In my school's philosophy club, we have been working through Bertrand Russell's *Problems of Philosophy*, and following a citation trail through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, I keep encountering references to "Bradley's regress" in discussions of the unity of the proposition. I think I understand the basic setup: if a fact such as "Desdemona loves Cassio" were just Desdemona, the loving, and Cassio placed side by side, nothing would explain why these add up to one unified fact rather than a bare list of three items; and treating the relation "loves" as one more item that needs tying to the other two seems only to require a further relation to do the tying, and so on without end. What I have not been able to grasp intuitively is why this regress is supposed to be vicious rather than harmless, and why the standard response—that some relations "relate" by their very nature and so need no further relation to bind them to their terms—is supposed to genuinely stop the regress rather than simply asserting the problem away. Could you walk me through the underlying intuition here, ideally without leaning too heavily on jargon like "unsaturatedness"?
