After forty years spent measuring how condensed-matter systems relax toward equilibrium, I retired two years ago and have since been filling my mornings with philosophy, mostly through a small reading circle that meets at our local library. We recently spent a month on Aristotle's discussion, in Chapter 9 of *De Interpretatione*, of whether it is already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. The puzzle struck me immediately as a logician's version of a problem I had only ever met in a physical guise: if "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already either true or false today, then nothing that happens between now and tomorrow seems to be what makes it so, and the future starts to look just as fixed as the past — a conclusion that sits very badly with our sense that deliberation and action matter.

What has kept me occupied since is discovering how many serious attempts have been made to defuse this argument, each apparently trading one cost for another. Aristotle himself is usually read as denying that future contingent statements are determinately true or false while still keeping "p or not-p" itself true; Diodorus Cronus, by contrast, seems to have accepted the fatalist conclusion outright in his Master Argument, while the Stoics tried to keep both universal fate and ordinary bivalence without giving up on the propriety of deliberating. Centuries later, Boethius and then Aquinas tried to dissolve the theological version of the puzzle by placing God's knowledge outside time altogether, and in the twentieth century Arthur Prior's branching-time logic produced two rival descendants of Aristotle's own move: an "Ockhamist" semantics that assigns future contingents a determinate truth value by privileging one branch as the actual future (a so-called thin red line), and a "Peircean" semantics on which an unsettled future-tensed claim simply comes out false. Richmond Thomason later offered a supervaluationist alternative that keeps "p or not-p" true without needing any thin red line, and more recently John MacFarlane has argued that the truth of a future contingent should be relativized to the context in which it is later assessed, rather than fixed once and for all at the moment of utterance.

Having now read all of these at least once, I can follow each argument on its own terms but have no principled way of weighing them against one another, so I would value a more rigorous outside perspective than my reading notes provide.
