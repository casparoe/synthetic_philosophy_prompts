I'm drafting a novel set in Esterlin, an offshore charter city whose founding constitution dispensed with its legislature in favor of a futarchy: residents vote every few years on a single composite welfare index, and from then on every contested policy is settled by conditional prediction markets that pay out against that index depending on which option is adopted. Before I let two of my characters debate whether such a system could actually hold up, I wanted to hear the argument made by people who think about markets professionally, so I described the charter to two friends of mine over dinner: Anton, who ran a derivatives desk for a decade before becoming an angel investor, and Fatima, an economist who referees mechanism-design papers for a living. What followed went several rounds, and neither of them gave an inch.

(1) Anton opened by arguing that markets are simply the best tool we have for aggregating dispersed information, since they reward being right and punish being wrong with real money, so a conditional market that pays out on the welfare index depending on which policy is chosen just imports that discipline into lawmaking instead of leaving it to campaign promises. (2) Fatima said this borrows credibility that hasn't actually been earned: the markets he had in mind get to settle the same type of bet over and over, which weeds out bad predictors over time, whereas a policy market only ever pays out on the branch that is actually implemented — the contract on the rejected policy is simply voided — so no trader ever builds a track record on the specific counterfactual claim the whole system depends on. (3) Anton replied that liquidity doesn't require an identical contract to repeat, only a structurally similar one, and that market makers profit from the spread on plenty of genuinely one-off bets; more importantly, anyone who tries to push a conditional price away from its accurate level simply hands a profitable opportunity to better-informed traders willing to take the other side, so manipulation is self-limiting rather than free. (4) Fatima answered that 'self-limiting' presumes a deep pool of informed, well-capitalized traders willing to make that corrective bet, which may not exist for a narrow municipal question nobody has ever priced before, and that even granting the average case, there is no way to check whether manipulation succeeded on any one high-stakes decision, because the rejected policy's real-world payoff is exactly what we never get to observe — a gap no subsidized market maker can close, since subsidizing one only relocates the question to who sets the subsidy and how large it is. (5) Anton said that objection would disqualify every method of collective choice, since voting and expert panels are equally blind to the road not taken, so it seemed unfair to hold futarchy to a standard nothing else has to meet. (6) Fatima said that was true but beside the point: voting does not justify itself by promising superior accuracy, whereas futarchy's entire case for replacing voting is that it is more accurate, so it is the one method whose own advocates have invited exactly this test — and it is not unfair to apply a standard someone has volunteered for.

I don't have the economics background to know who actually has the better of this, and I would rather not hand either character a bad argument.
