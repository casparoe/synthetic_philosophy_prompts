My buddy Priya is doing a philosophy PhD and keeps telling me about "deflationary" theories of truth, and I think I've actually found a hole in it, but she won't admit it (or maybe I'm just missing something, which tbh is why I'm asking here instead of just assuming I'm right). Quick context: I'm a backend dev with zero formal philosophy training, so I'm probably going to butcher some terminology. Anyway, the basic idea, as she explained it to me, is that saying "snow is white" is true adds literally zero content beyond just saying snow is white -- its the disquotation thing, "P" is true iff P, truth isn't some extra property riding on top of the sentence like some invisible tag. Ok, fine, I can buy that part. But then it hit me that this is exactly the classic code smell where a junior dev writes if (isDone == true) instead of just if (isDone). Any linter will flag that as redundant, because == true never changes what the expression evaluates to, its just dead weight sitting there doing nothing. So if "is true" really and truly never adds anything beyond the plain sentence, shouldn't deflationists be able to just refactor the whole predicate out of the language, delete it completely, and lose zero expressive power?

When I pushed Priya on this she got kind of prickly and mumbled something about how it's not so simple once you dont have the specific sentence sitting right in front of you to swap in for "P," but she didn't really spell out why that would matter, and honestly it sounded like she was just being defensive about her thesis topic. So: is there an actual rigorous reason the truth predicate can't just be deleted as dead code, the way I'm describing, or have I basically caught deflationism in a genuine logical inconsistency here?
