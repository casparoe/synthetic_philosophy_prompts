So I've been down a rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm working on about physics and metaphysics, and I want to run something by you. Everyone knows the Newton's bucket bit — water climbs up the sides of a spinning bucket even though there's nothing around it for the water to be rotating relative to, which Newton took as evidence that space itself is a real, substantial thing rather than just a system of relations between objects. What I hadn't fully appreciated until now is that Einstein basically walked into his own private version of that exact same problem back in 1913-15, while he and Grossmann were wrestling with how generally covariant a gravitational theory could even be: the "hole argument" (Lochbetrachtung), where two solutions that agree everywhere outside some little patch of spacetime but disagree inside it seem to make the theory wildly indeterministic — but only if you think the points of the manifold are real individual things in thier own right. Einstein talked himself out of the worry with what's now called the point-coincidence argument: spacetime points have no physical identity apart from which events (worldlines crossing, etc) happen to coincide at them.

Then, decades after he was gone, Earman and Norton dug the exact same argument back up in 1987 and pointed it at anyone who thinks spacetime is a substance in its own right, which kicked off a whole "sophisticated substantivalism" scramble to save the view — Butterfield arguing that points don't have their own "thisness" apart from their properties and relations, Maudlin arguing points have their metrical properties essentially so the two "rival" solutions were never really describing different possibilities for the same points, and so on.

Here's what I can't stop chewing on: if Einstein had somehow lived to see his own youthful headache turned into this whole metaphysical cottage industry, what do you reckon he'd actually have said about it? Would he have recognized the sophisticated substantivalists as basically dressing his own point-coincidence move up in new clothes, or thought they'd missed what he was getting at? And given that he started out pretty sympathetic to Mach's relationism and was later openly annoyed that general relativity never fully cashed out Mach's principle, where do you think he'd have ended up landing on the whole substance-vs-relations question by, say, the 1990s — team substance, team relations, or "this is a pseudo-problem dressed up in tensor calculus"? I've already got the standard menu of positions off a couple of SEP articles, so don't just summarize those for me — I want your actual best-guess reconstruction of Einstein specifically, and why, tied to stuff he actually said or did.
