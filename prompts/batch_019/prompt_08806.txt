Working on a scene in my novel where two old grad school friends -- now both philosophy professors, and still needling each other after twenty years -- end up arguing at the wake over whether the deceased's estranged son had any reason to go visit him before he died. The son had said flat out that he felt nothing, no love, no guilt, nothing pulling him there. One friend takes the Bernard Williams internalist line: if theres nothing in the son's actual motivational set that sound deliberation could've gotten him to, then he just didnt have a reason to go, full stop, whatever we think of him morally for it. The other's more of a McDowell/Nagel externalist and thinks that's letting the son's psychology do way too much work -- the reason was there whether he could feel its pull or not. Can you write out their argument as an actual back-and-forth, four or five exchanges, where each one is genuinely responding to what the other just said and neither folds at the end? Both of them are sharp, this has been a running fight between them for years, so no strawmanning either side.
