At work I have been experimenting with representing uncertainty in a forecasting model as a range of probabilities rather than a single number, since the evidence is often not precise enough to support a sharper estimate. In reading about this 'imprecise credence' approach, I came across Adam Elga's argument that the position is self-undermining: he constructs two bets, each of which an imprecise agent may rationally decline when offered on its own, even though accepting both together yields a guaranteed profit regardless of outcome. The standard rescue — that imprecise agents should evaluate whole anticipated sequences of decisions as a single package rather than bet by bet — strikes me as relocating the problem rather than solving it. Could you write a dialogue, staged at the moment the two bets are actually offered, between a philosopher defending sharp credences who presses this argument and a philosopher defending imprecise credences who must decide whether to accept, decline, or invoke the package strategy — with each genuinely engaging the other's strongest move, to see whether the package strategy actually rescues the imprecise view or merely smuggles a sharp probability back in?
