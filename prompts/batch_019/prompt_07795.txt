My mother had a cardiac arrest six weeks ago; her neurologists now say she's in a minimally conscious state — she tracks faces and occasionally squeezes a hand on request, but little else. Since then I've been reading about disorders of consciousness, and the detail I can't shake is philosophical rather than medical. On higher-order theories — David Rosenthal's version is the one I keep returning to — a state isn't consciously felt merely because the relevant sensory or pain circuitry is active and doing its job; it's felt only if there is a further, numerically distinct state representing oneself as being in it, and that monitoring capacity is exactly the kind of thing diffuse cortical injury is liable to disrupt (a first-order theorist would count the cortical activity itself as sufficient; Rosenthal wouldn't). So a scan showing "pain-related activation" in someone like her wouldn't settle whether she's suffering: it could be first-order processing running with no one registering it. What unsettles me more is these theories' own admission that the monitoring state and the state it monitors are only loosely coupled, so it can misfire either way — registering distress that isn't there, or missing distress that is. Neither a positive nor a negative scan would really be conclusive.

Write me a short story, around 1,000–1,500 words, set at a bedside like this one, that dramatizes that specific bind rather than resolving it. You can choose the point of view, but bear in mind that narrating from inside her own mind would quietly settle, by fictional fiat, the very question the story is meant to leave open.
