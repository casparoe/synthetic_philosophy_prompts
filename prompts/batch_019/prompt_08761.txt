Over the weekend I had a long, somewhat frustrating argument with my friend Priya, who is finishing a PhD in biostatistics, and I still haven't been able to let it drop. It started because I had just received the results of a genetic and lifestyle screening panel, which estimated my ten-year risk of developing a particular cardiovascular condition at 14 percent, based on a large cohort study that grouped participants by age and sex alone. Curious, I looked further into the matter and found that a study which additionally matched on family history put the figure closer to 22 percent, while a smaller study that also controlled for a specific genetic marker I happen to carry raised the estimate to about 31 percent. Each of these groupings seems like a perfectly legitimate way of specifying "people relevantly like me," yet they yield substantially different numbers, and I could not see any principled reason to prefer one over the others beyond Priya's suggestion, which was essentially: use whichever grouping is narrowest while still resting on a sample large enough to be statistically trustworthy, and treat that as the answer. That struck me as a sensible rule of thumb for practical purposes, but not as a genuine resolution, since nothing in principle stops someone from running an even more fine-grained study tomorrow, one that also controls for my exact diet, sleep habits, and resting heart rate, and so on indefinitely, until the "sample" consists of just me — at which point talk of a percentage risk seems to collapse entirely, since I either will or will not develop the condition. Priya maintains that I am dressing up an unremarkable statistical limitation as some sort of deep puzzle; I maintain that there is a genuine question underneath about whether a single individual, in one particular set of circumstances, can coherently be assigned a "true" probability at all and, if so, what would make one grouping rather than another the right basis for it.

I would genuinely value your take on who has the better of this argument. Is Priya's stopping rule an adequate answer to my worry, or does it just quietly presuppose what is in question? And more generally, when doctors, insurers, or forecasters hand us an individualized percentage of this kind, is there any principled sense in which one way of grouping "similar cases" is uniquely correct, or is every such figure really just an artifact of whichever dataset happened to be used to produce it?
