I was recently appointed as one of three arbitrators on a panel hearing a commercial dispute, and something odd happened during our first round of deliberations that I have not been able to shake. Under the governing rule, the respondent is liable only if a valid contract existed and the respondent breached it. Voting issue by issue, a majority of us agreed a contract existed, and a separate majority agreed it had been breached, so, on the letter of the rule, liability seemed to follow — yet when our chair polled the panel directly on the ultimate question of liability, a majority actually favored the respondent. A co-arbitrator who used to teach contract law recognized this as the "doctrinal paradox," which they said is studied more generally, under the name "discursive dilemma," in the judgment-aggregation literature. Before we reconvene, I would like to understand what that literature actually implies: what are the most important implications of judgment aggregation theory for how a small panel like ours should decide between voting issue-by-issue and voting on the bottom line, and is there any principled sense in which one procedure is more defensible than the other — or is this simply the kind of structural impossibility that Arrow's theorem represents for preference aggregation?
