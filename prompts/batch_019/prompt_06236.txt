I'm taking a seminar this term called "Democracy and Its Critics," cross-listed between philosophy and political science, and this week's reading is Simone Weil's 1943 essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (I've been working from the NYRB Classics edition, translated by Simon Leys). We covered Rousseau's Social Contract a few weeks ago, so I'm reasonably comfortable with the basic idea of the general will, but there's a stretch where Weil adapts Rousseau's argument using an analogy about water, and I keep getting tangled up in exactly what work that analogy is doing. Paraphrasing as closely as I can from my notes, since the book is back at my apartment, the passage runs roughly like this:

"A people can be compared to a mass of water. Taken individually, its members are like countless drops in ceaseless, disordered motion; and yet the whole, precisely because these small motions point in every direction and so cancel out, comes to rest in a level surface that mirrors exactly whatever is set before it and registers the true weight of anything immersed in it. That is the condition under which a people's will can be trusted the way Rousseau trusts it. But let a single passion seize the people all at once, collectively, and the water is no longer still: it is driven by one great current, or by several at cross-purposes, and in neither case does it reflect anything but its own disturbance — nor is there any private virtue, short of sainthood, that can hold firm against a current of that size."

A few of us argued about this after seminar and didn't really get anywhere. What I can't work out is, first, what actually distinguishes a "collective" passion from the situation Rousseau already allows for, where lots of individuals separately have passions of their own — Weil seems to need collective passion to behave in a qualitatively different way (not just cancelling out more slowly, but genuinely never cancelling out at all), and I don't see where that difference is established rather than just asserted through the water image itself. Second, even granting her the analogy, I don't see what in this particular piece of argument is supposed to single out political parties as the source of collective passion, as opposed to, say, nationalism, mass media, or a referendum campaign — all of which seem equally able to whip up the kind of "current" she describes. Is there a premise I'm missing that does that work, or does she only earn that conclusion later, once she starts arguing that parties are specifically built to manufacture exactly this kind of passion? I'd appreciate help reconstructing the logic here, and please do tell me if I've simply misread what the water is supposed to represent.
