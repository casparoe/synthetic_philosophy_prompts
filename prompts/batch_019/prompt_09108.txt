I have long been puzzled by cases where someone's failure to act gets blamed for what follows, such as a neighbor who forgets to water my plants while I am away, so that they die. It feels natural to say the failure caused their death, yet an omission is not an event — nothing happened, so nothing seems available to do the causing. The puzzle sharpens once I notice that it is equally true of every other person on Earth who also did not water my plants that they would have survived had that person done so, and yet we do not call all of those non-waterers a cause, only my neighbor. The obvious reason is that she agreed to the job and they did not, but leaning on an agreement to settle a question of causation makes me suspect that what I have seen called "causation by omission" may not name a real relation at all, and is instead a moral verdict wearing causal language.

Write a dialogue between two people who have thought this through carefully and come down on opposite sides: one who holds that omissions can be genuine causes and can explain, without quietly relying on any notion of duty or expectation, why some omissions count as causes and others do not; and one who denies that any omission is ever a real cause, treating all such talk as blame dressed up as causal description. Have each character engage directly with the other's strongest points, and do not let either one simply concede.
