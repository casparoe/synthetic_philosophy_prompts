Last week, in a formal epistemology seminar I'm taking, a friend and I got stuck in a long argument about Richard Jeffrey's rule for updating on uncertain evidence — what he called "probability kinematics." The professor had motivated it with Jeffrey's own kind of case: sometimes evidence doesn't make you certain of some proposition, the way ordinary conditionalization assumes, but instead just shifts your confidence across a partition of possibilities, as when candlelight makes you more confident, without making you certain, that a piece of cloth is green rather than blue. Jeffrey's rule handles this by holding your conditional credences on each cell of the partition fixed and simply reweighting by your new, less-than-certain confidence in the cells themselves.

My friend argued that this move doesn't earn the same rational standing as ordinary conditionalization. First, she pointed out that the "rigidity" condition — the requirement that those conditional credences stay fixed through the update — is doing all the real work, and nothing in the framework tells you when it's actually satisfied; it's simply assumed whenever the rule is invoked. Second, and what she thought was more damaging, successive Jeffrey shifts on different partitions generally fail to commute: shifting toward E1 and then toward E2 can leave you with different final credences than shifting in the reverse order, even though nothing about ordinary conditionalization depends on the order in which you become certain of two things. She also brought up van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin" scenario — the soldier who receives a garbled radio report about the relative odds of being in one part of "Red" territory rather than another — as a related worry: the moment you try to make the framework handle even slightly richer uncertain evidence, it starts reassigning probabilities to propositions about "Blue" territory that the report never mentioned, which struck her as a symptom of the same underlying looseness. I pushed back that path-dependence isn't obviously a defect — learning two soft things really might be order-sensitive in a way learning two hard things isn't — and that Jeffrey's rule, rigidity assumed, admits Dutch-book- and accuracy-based defenses that are structurally no worse off than the classic argument for conditionalization. We went back and forth for a while and never converged.

I'd like a more careful adjudication than we managed. Could you write an essay addressing whether Jeffrey conditionalization deserves to be treated as a genuine rational requirement on updating in the face of uncertain evidence, on a par with strict conditionalization, or whether it is at best a permissible modeling convenience without real normative force? Please engage specifically with the rigidity assumption and the failure of commutativity (Judy-Benjamin-style cases are fair game too, if you think they bear on the verdict), weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, and close with a definite verdict rather than a survey of the positions.
