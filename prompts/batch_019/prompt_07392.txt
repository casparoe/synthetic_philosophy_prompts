Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about whether John Dewey's picture of democracy still holds up: not a fixed set of institutions to defend or mourn, but an experimental method of shared inquiry, testing policies and habits against their consequences and revising them the way a scientist revises a hypothesis. She thinks it was always naive and looks worse every year, given how tribal and impervious to evidence public argument has become; I think the fact that we spent two hours revising our own positions under pressure from each other's reasons, and still parted on good terms, is closer to evidence for Dewey than against him. I can't keep arguing about it, though, because my high school civics teacher, Ruth, who became something like a second mother to me over the twenty years since I graduated, died this week, and her family has asked me to speak at the funeral. She never let anyone call her an optimist about democracy — she insisted on "meliorist," a term she picked up from the pragmatists, meaning that things can be made somewhat better by specific, fallible effort, with no guarantee from history that they will be, and no license to assume they won't. Several of the mourners are former students of hers who became political scientists and journalists and will recognize exactly what she meant; most are just family and neighbors who remember her turning dinner conversation into an argument about the news. Write the eulogy, about five minutes long, so that it stays honest to that demanding, unsentimental piece of philosophy — no promise that democracy will be fine, no elegy for a lost golden age either — while still working as a eulogy for the people in the room who have never heard of Dewey.
