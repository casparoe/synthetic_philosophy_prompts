Kavka's toxin puzzle, quick recap in case: an eccentric billionaire will pay you $1M tomorrow morning if, by midnight tonight, you form a sincere intention to drink a nasty (but non-lethal, no lasting harm) toxin tomorrow afternoon. you get paid regardless of whether you actually drink it, and the money's in your account before the drinking would even happen. problem is, once you've got the money, you obviously have zero reason to go through with drinking it - so how can a rational agent sincerely intend, tonight, to do something they already know they'll have no reason to do once the time comes? and if they can't, they miss out on free money for just being the kind of agent who'd do it.

usual suspects that try to dissolve this (names from memory, correct me if i've mangled one):
- Gauthier-style constrained maximization - rational disposition-adoption instead of act-by-act maximizing
- Bratman's planning-theory response - intentions as stable attitudes with their own norms, not just predictions about yourself
- McClennen's resolute choice - you don't re-litigate the plan once you've adopted it
- the deflationary "you literally cannot form the intention, full stop, and that's the correct answer" line
- the LW-ish move of just switching to updateless/functional decision theory - evaluate the policy, not the individual act, and the paradox dissolves
