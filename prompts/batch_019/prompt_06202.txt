Leibniz's big dream was basically a "calculus ratiocinator" -- a universal symbolic method where instead of arguing philosophy or law out forever, two people could just sit down and say "calculemus" (lets calculate) and the calculation itself would settle whos right. Kind of funny that his actual dissertation as a young guy was on combinatorics -- the exact field that later turns out to be the source of combinatorial explosion and NP-hardness. So given what we now know from computational complexity theory (that a huge number of problems seem to be decidable in principle but need exponential time in practice -- the whole P vs NP situation), what do you think Leibniz would've said if someone sat him down and explained this to him? Would he just shrug and say in-principle computability was always all his rationalism needed, or does intractability actually undercut something deeper -- like his confidence that reason could, in practice and not just in principle, adjudicate between rival positions? Feel free to also bring in how this lands on stuff like the principle of sufficient reason or "best of all possible worlds" (which is basically God solving a giant optimization problem over all possible worlds) -- does God get to ignore complexity theory in a way we don't get to? Try to actually reason from Leibniz's own commitments rather than just giving whatever take sounds good.
