I've spent the last couple of weeks working back through old decision-theory threads on LessWrong and the EA Forum about how an agent should handle threats and blackmail, including the "acausal" case, where the threatening party never causally interacts with you at all but threatens you on the basis of predicting or simulating what you would do -- the kind of scenario that comes up in discussions of multiverse-wide cooperation and simulation-based threats. A friend of mine who works on AI alignment keeps insisting that two of the standard proposals for handling this are really the same idea in different guises, and I'm not convinced he's right. The first, associated with functional and updateless decision theory, says an agent should simply be the sort of agent that never gives in: if a sufficiently good predictor can see in advance that paying up would get it nothing, carrying out the threat is pure cost for no benefit, so it never bothers threatening you to begin with. The second, which I've mostly seen discussed under the label "surrogate goals" -- I want to say the idea traces back to people around the Center on Long-Term Risk, though I could be misremembering exactly where I first read it -- has the agent deliberately cultivate, and advertise, some substitute object of concern that it doesn't actually care about, so that if a threat is nonetheless carried out, the damage lands on the decoy rather than on anything the agent truly values.

What I'd like from you is an account of how these two strategies actually relate, rather than the hand-wavy "they're complementary" gloss the blogs tend to give. Is adopting a surrogate goal just a special case of threat-resistance -- a way of implementing the "never give in" policy for an agent that can't fully trust its own commitment, or can't always distinguish a genuine threat from a genuine offer -- or is it solving a distinct problem that would remain even for an agent whose threat-resistance is flawless? I'd also like your take on a worry that occurred to me: could being known to carry a surrogate goal change a rational threatener's incentive to test you in the first place, in a way that undermines the deterrence that a pure non-negotiation policy is supposed to provide? Please justify your answer by walking through the actual game-theoretic mechanics of both proposals, rather than resting on their usual one-paragraph gloss.
