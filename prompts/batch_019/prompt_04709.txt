I'm a novelist currently at work on a book set in seventeenth-century England, one strand of which follows the long-running feud between Thomas Hobbes and the Oxford mathematician John Wallis over Hobbes's repeated claims to have squared the circle. In the course of researching it, I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy of science, and the instructor, aware of the novel, recently asked me to help mark a small batch of short take-home responses to one of the seminar's questions. I agreed, but months of research have left me rather fond of Hobbes as a character, and I'd rather not let that sympathy color my sense of how good an answer actually is. So before I hand anything back, I'd like a second, disinterested pass on one response in particular.

The question the students were set was this: "Explain why Hobbes treated geometry as the model for all genuine science, including civil philosophy, and what he thought this required in practice. Then discuss the significance of Hobbes's dispute with Wallis over his claimed quadrature of the circle: does that episode amount to no more than a mathematical blunder on Hobbes's part, or does it expose something more basic about how Hobbes understood geometrical objects like points and lines?" And here is the response I'd like assessed:

"For Hobbes, geometry was the one science in which people did not endlessly quarrel, and he thought this was no accident: geometers stipulate their own definitions and then simply follow out what those definitions entail, so a correctly executed proof settles the matter for good, unlike questions of morals or politics where people's interests are at stake. He wanted civil philosophy rescued from perpetual dispute in the same way. If words like 'person,' 'right,' and 'commonwealth' were defined as exactly as 'triangle' and 'parallel,' then the duties of subjects and the rights of sovereigns could be demonstrated rather than merely argued about, which is one reason Leviathan spends its opening chapters building up definitions of sense, imagination, and speech before it ever turns to politics. Hobbes's own devotion to geometry supposedly began late: Aubrey tells the story of Hobbes, around forty, opening a copy of Euclid in a gentleman's library, landing on the Pythagorean theorem, refusing at first to believe it, and working backward through the chain of earlier propositions until he was won over for life.

That same self-image as a geometer is what drove the long fight with Wallis. Hobbes announced, in De Corpore and afterward, that he had squared the circle, and Wallis spent years publishing point-by-point rebuttals showing exactly which step in each construction failed. Because a straightedge-and-compass quadrature is impossible whenever the underlying ratio is transcendental, Wallis's rebuttals were in effect a formal proof that nothing Hobbes tried could ever have worked, no matter how many times he revised it. To his credit, Hobbes never simply gave up: he kept producing new attempts almost until he died, still convinced Wallis was missing the point. I'd say the whole affair is really a story about one very stubborn amateur mathematician, since the unorthodox way Hobbes defined a point — as a body whose magnitude we simply agree not to count, rather than as strictly dimensionless — was more a philosophical flourish, consistent with his materialism, than anything that actually drove the specific errors Wallis kept finding."

Could you grade this on its own terms? I'd like, first, a JSON object scoring each of the following five dimensions from 0 to 4, with a one-sentence justification tied to specific claims in the response for each score: (a) the explanation of why geometry was Hobbes's paradigm of certain knowledge; (b) the account of how that model was meant to extend to civil philosophy; (c) the accuracy of the biographical material about Hobbes and Euclid; (d) the accuracy of the basic facts of the Hobbes–Wallis dispute; and (e) the accuracy and sophistication of the claim about what the dispute does or doesn't reveal about Hobbes's conception of geometrical objects. After the JSON, please expand on your reasoning for each score in a bit more detail, and close with an overall grade expressed both as a total out of 20 and as a letter grade.
