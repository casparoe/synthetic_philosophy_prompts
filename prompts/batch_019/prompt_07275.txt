The following question and a student's answer to it are from a graduate seminar exam in metaphysics of science, worth 20 points (6 for part a, 7 for part b, 7 for part c).

Question: Lewis's Best System Analysis and Armstrong's theory of natural necessitation both try to give lawhood more content than a bare regularity theory does, but only one of them counts as Humean.
(a) State the Humean supervenience thesis and explain how the Best System Analysis, including the roles of simplicity, strength, and fit, is meant to satisfy it.
(b) Explain Armstrong's proposal that laws consist in a relation of natural necessitation, N(F,G), between universals, and say whether this makes his theory necessitarian in the same sense as dispositional essentialism (Bird, Ellis).
(c) Present the "inference problem" as an objection to Armstrong's theory, and explain one strategy that has been used to answer it.

Student's answer:

(a) Humean supervenience is Lewis's thesis that every contingent truth about the world — including truths about laws, causation, and chance — supervenes on the Humean mosaic: the total spatiotemporal distribution of local, categorical properties, with no fundamental relations of necessitation between distinct events or property-instances. The Best System Analysis cashes this out for laws specifically: a generalization counts as a law just in case it is a theorem of whichever true deductive systematization of the mosaic best combines simplicity (few, economical axioms) with strength (a great deal of informational content). Lewis later added fit as a third criterion, so that competing probabilistic systems could be ranked by how well their chance-theorems match the pattern of outcomes that actually occurs. Since lawhood is here just a matter of which regularities best systematize the mosaic, nothing beyond the mosaic is required to fix the laws, which is exactly what Humean supervenience demands.

(b) Armstrong denies that regularities, however well they systematize the world, are sufficient for lawhood. He holds instead that a law to the effect that all Fs are G consists in a distinct, irreducible state of affairs: a relation of natural necessitation, N(F,G), holding between the universals F-ness and G-ness, whose obtaining necessitates that any particular instantiating F also instantiates G. Because the necessitation relation is what grounds the regularity rather than being read off it, Armstrong's theory is necessitarian in the same sense as dispositional essentialism: on both views it is metaphysically necessary that Fs are G, so no world in which the relevant universals are instantiated could fail to exhibit the corresponding regularity.

(c) The inference problem, pressed by Lewis, objects that merely postulating N(F,G) does not explain why its obtaining entails that all Fs are in fact G: the second-order fact that F-ness and G-ness stand in N seems to need exactly the same kind of connection to the first-order regularity that Armstrong denied a bare Humean regularity could have on its own, and simply naming that connection "necessitation" does not supply one. Critics conclude that the theory does not so much solve the problem facing regularity theories as relocate it one level up. In response, Maudlin argues that N should be accepted as a primitive that admits of no further explanation: just as the Humean accepts the mosaic itself as a brute, unanalyzed starting point, the anti-Humean can accept primitive necessitation as a brute starting point and deny that anything more is owed.
