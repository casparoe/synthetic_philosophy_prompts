I'm teaching Nozick this week in my political philosophy seminar, and I had a back-and-forth with a sharp student after class that I want sanity check before I email her back. We'd gone through the Wilt Chamberlain argument -- you stipulate some patterned distribution D1 that your favorite egalitarian theory says is perfectly just, then a million people voluntarily hand over a quarter each to watch Wilt play, he ends up with way more than anyone else, and Nozick says the resulting D2 is exactly as just as D1 was, since nobody's entitlements were violated along the way ("liberty upsets patterns," as he puts it). She argued, and I'm inclined to agree, that this basically shows the historical origin of a distribution shouldnt matter to us at all -- all that should matter is whether the most recent leg of transfers was consensual. So on her reading, the fact that so much real-world property ultimately traces back to conquest, slavery, or outright theft several generations back shouldn't actually bother a committed Nozickian, since Chamberlain-style reasoning says we only need each recent transfer to be clean, not the whole causal chain running back to the year dot.

Is that basically the right way to take the argument, or is there something built into entitlement theory that keeps that older history from being irrelevant in the way we're assuming? I ask partly because next week's reading has Nozick spending a surprising amount of time worrying about "rectification" of old injustices, which seems like a strange thing to worry about if Wilt Chamberlain already tells us history washes out as long as the recent links in the chain were voluntary.
