Last night a friend and I spent nearly three hours arguing about whether "brain death" names a real biological event or is better understood as a convention we've adopted for the sake of transplantation and hospital resource limits. She kept returning to Alan Shewmon's old challenge to the whole-brain standard: brain-dead bodies routinely go on regulating their own temperature, healing wounds, fighting infection, and, in a handful of documented cases, sustaining a pregnancy long enough to end in a live birth, which she took as evidence that losing all brain function doesn't actually coincide with the loss of "the organism functioning as an integrated whole," as the standard rationale assumes. I argued the other side — that denying brain death is real death commits you to saying that a body with no brainstem reflexes and no possibility, ever again, of consciousness or unassisted breathing is still a living person in some meaningful sense, which seemed like the worse thing to be committed to. Neither of us gave ground, and I went home mildly furious.

I bring this up because it stopped being an abstract dispute about twelve hours later: my mother called this morning to say my grandmother died overnight, and I'm giving her eulogy at the funeral home on Saturday. My grandmother spent almost thirty years on her hospital's ethics committee, including several fights over organ-procurement policy, and she held — carefully, and in print — almost exactly the position I'd been arguing against the night before: that whole-brain criteria quietly import the kind of "integrated organism" talk her own physicalism couldn't actually license, that the higher-brain and strictly circulatory alternatives fail for their own reasons, and that we'd be more honest calling the current standard a useful legal fiction than a discovery about the moment a person ceases to exist. She died an entirely undramatic cardiac death in hospice, no ventilator involved, so there's no live dispute about whether she herself is dead — but she left written instructions that nobody was to describe her as watching over anyone or persisting in any form once integrated brain function was gone, on the grounds that she thought no coherent version of that survival was available to her, gradual or otherwise.

Saturday's room will be split roughly in half between colleagues from three decades of ethics-committee and organ-procurement work, who will notice immediately if I misstate her actual view, and family who have never heard of the dead donor rule and just want to grieve their mother and grandmother without a seminar. Could you write the eulogy? It should be recognizably faithful to her real, fairly demanding position on what death is and isn't — substantial enough that the colleagues don't feel she's been flattened into a truism, warm enough that the family isn't sitting through a lecture, and careful never to reach for the comforting language about presence or spirits that she spent her career treating as confused.
