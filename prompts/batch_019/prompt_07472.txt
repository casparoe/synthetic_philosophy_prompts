I joined the board of a small housing cooperative about six months ago, and lately we've been debating whether to amend our bylaws so that a handful of "foundational" provisions -- chiefly the rule barring any unit from being converted to a market-rate rental -- could only ever be changed by a 90% supermajority, rather than the ordinary 60% threshold we use for everything else. While reading around the topic, I kept running into the framing of such entrenched rules as a collective "Ulysses contract": the idea, associated with Jon Elster's work on precommitment, that a constitution (or a bylaw, or a treaty) is essentially a group binding itself to the mast the way Ulysses had his sailors bind him, so that he could hear the sirens' song without being able to steer the ship onto the rocks. I mentioned this at our last planning meeting, which set off a long exchange between two other board members -- both, unlike me, trained as lawyers -- that I found genuinely hard to referee.

Nadia opened by arguing that our situation is a clean instance of the Ulysses case: the current board, thinking clearly and under no immediate pressure, is protecting the co-op's founding purpose against a future board that might be pressured by a few members facing a real-estate windfall. Owen objected that the analogy fails exactly where it matters: Ulysses bound only himself, and specified in advance precisely when and how his future self was to be released; a supermajority clause instead binds people who aren't yet members and never agreed to it, which looks less like self-control and more like the present board legislating from beyond the grave. Nadia replied that this objection proves too much, since every rule we have binds people who joined after it was passed, and we don't think that makes ordinary bylaws illegitimate; the co-op persists as the same entity across membership turnover, much as a person persists through gradual psychological change, so there's no special problem about some "different" co-op being bound later. Owen came back that the trouble isn't the mere passage of time but revisability: an ordinary bylaw stays legitimate because whoever currently holds a majority could repeal it, so it remains the continuing expression of the co-op's present will, whereas the entire point of the supermajority clause is to survive against that will -- which is a much harder thing to justify than simply being an old rule. At that point Nadia shifted ground, arguing that entrenchment needn't be justified as protection from our future selves at all: a rule that's hard to repeal is also what makes our commitment credible to prospective members and to any lender assessing the co-op, quite apart from any worry about a future board going astray -- Ulysses's crew, after all, also needed a plan sturdy enough to trust. Owen's rejoinder was that this was itself a concession: Nadia had quietly swapped one justification for entrenchment (protecting a continuous agent from its own foreseeable weaknesses) for a completely different one (sending a credible signal to outsiders), and that once these are pulled apart, the tidy Ulysses story stops being a good fit for either.

Neither of them backed down, and the vote ended up getting postponed. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether Owen's closing charge -- that Nadia is running together two distinct justifications for entrenchment -- actually holds up, and whether there's a way of defending something like our supermajority clause that isn't vulnerable to it.
