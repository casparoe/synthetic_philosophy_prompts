About three weeks ago I tried a flotation-therapy session for the first time: an enclosed pod filled with a shallow layer of body-temperature, Epsom-salt water, sealed off from light and sound, where nothing touches your skin and there's no reliable way to sense where your own limbs are. I went in mostly out of curiosity, after a stretch of stressful months at work, and expected little more than an hour of quiet. Once the initial restlessness passed, though, something odd happened: I became completely certain that I existed, that there was a "me" undergoing the experience, even though I had no awareness whatsoever of having a body - no weight, no temperature, no sense of my limbs, nothing at all. It wasn't that my body went numb and I noticed its absence; it felt more like I had never needed it in the first place in order to know I was there.

Afterward, the woman running the studio told me this reaction is common, and that she reads it as evidence that the self is fundamentally separate from the body - proof, as she put it, that "you aren't your body, you just usually mistake the two for each other." I mentioned the whole thing to a friend who's partway through a philosophy PhD, half expecting her to roll her eyes, but she got quite animated instead and said it reminded her of some old argument from a seminar she'd taken years ago, though she couldn't remember the details or whose argument it was.

I've been turning this over ever since, and I can't decide how much weight it deserves. The certainty I felt about my own existence in there was completely real and unforced, not something I talked myself into afterward. But it still feels like a large leap from "I wasn't aware of my body in that moment" to "my self must therefore be the kind of thing that doesn't ultimately depend on a body at all." Is there an actual, defensible philosophical argument lurking here, or is the studio owner simply over-interpreting a fact about attention and sensory input that doesn't really support her stronger claim? I'd like to understand precisely what would need to be true for the experience to license the conclusion she's drawing, rather than a much weaker one.
