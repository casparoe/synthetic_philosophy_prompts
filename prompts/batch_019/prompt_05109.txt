And here's the student's answer, copied over exactly as they wrote it (typos and all):

"The simple conditional analysis (CA) says x is disposed to M when C just in case x would M if x were in C — e.g. a glass is fragile iff it would break if struck. Martin's electro-fink is a counterexample in both directions: take a dead wire hooked to a 'sorceror' device that senses an impending touch and makes the wire live just long enough to shock whoever touches it — now it's true that the wire would conduct if touched, even though the wire isn't actually live beforehand. Run the device the other way on a live wire, killing the current the instant contact happens, and you get a wire that IS live (disposed to conduct) even though it's false that it would conduct if touched. Either way, having the disposition and the conditional being true come apart, so simple CA fails as a biconditional.

Lewis's repair adds a causal basis: x is disposed to M when C iff x has some intrinsic property B such that, if x were in C and kept B for a little while, B and C together would cause M in the standard way. This blocks Martin's cases because a fink works by changing whether B itself is present right around the moment of the stimulus, and Lewis's clause rules that out by requiring B to persist through the process.

Masking is different from finking in an important way: an antidote doesn't touch the causal basis at all. A poison masked by an antidote is exactly as toxic at the molecular level as it always was; the antidote intervenes further downstream, after B and C are both in place. Since Lewis's whole fix is a persistence-of-basis requirement, and masking leaves the basis completely untouched, Lewis's requirement is trivially satisfied in masking cases too — which is why he extended the Reformed Conditional Analysis to explicitly cover Johnston's masking cases as well, closing the gap left by simple CA with the very same clause that handles finks."
