Been on a bit of a nostalgia kick lately rereading old MIRI-adjacent stuff from around 2013-2015 -- the Tiling Agents paper, a bunch of Eliezer's old LessWrong posts on the "Löbian obstacle," some archived Arbital pages from back before that site died. The whole thread is about why a proof-based agent apparently can't just adopt "if my successor proves X, then X is true" as a general trust schema, because plugging that into Löb's theorem blows the whole system up into proving everything (i.e., inconsistency). In one of the old comment sections somebody mentioned, almost as a throwaway line, that Gödel's second incompleteness theorem -- the one where a system can't prove its own consistency -- is actually just a special case of Löb's theorem, the case where you plug in "falsehood" for the proposition being trusted. That's been rattling around in my head for like a week now and I keep flip-flopping on how big a deal it actually is.

So here's what I want you to untangle for me: compare the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents (in the successor-agent / self-modification sense MIRI people mean it) with plain old Gödelian self-doubt about one's own consistency. Given that G2 apparently drops out of Löb's theorem as a corollary once you set P = falsum, is "the obstacle to self-trust" basically just Gödel's second theorem wearing a trenchcoat, or does the fully general version -- holding for any P, not just falsum -- make it a substantively different and harder problem for an agent trying to reason about or build a successor? I got into an argument about this over drinks with a friend who does ML, and he thought I was overstating how novel the self-trust framing really is, so now I need backup either way.

One more thing while you're at it: is this whole obstacle really an artifact of insisting the agent reason via formal proof in the first place, or does something recognizably Löbian still bite an agent that reasons probabilistically or heuristically instead? I vaguely recall "logical induction" being motivated partly by wanting an answer to that second question, but I'd like you to actually make the case rather then just take my word for it.
