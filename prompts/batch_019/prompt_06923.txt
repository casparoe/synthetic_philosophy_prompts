I'm running a graduate seminar on laws of nature this term, and this week we finally worked backwards from all the contemporary "Humean vs. anti-Humean" stuff (Lewis's Best System Analysis, Armstrong/Dretske/Tooley on necessitation, dispositional essentialism, etc.) to the actual Hume text that the label comes from, instead of just gesturing at "regularity theory" the way the textbooks do. My TA and I ended up arguing about the passage below yesterday and I want another set of eyes on it before Thursday's class. Its from Section VII of the first Enquiry, right near the end, just before he moves on to liberty and necessity:

"Similar objects are always conjoined with similar. Of this we have experience. Suitably to this experience, therefore, we may define a cause to be an object followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second. Or, in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed. The appearance of a cause always conveys the mind, by a customary transition, to the idea of the effect. Of this also we have experience. We may, therefore, suitably to this experience, form another definition of cause, and call it an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other. But though both these definitions be drawn from circumstances foreign to the cause, we cannot remedy this inconvenience, or attain a more perfect definition, which may point out that circumstance in the cause which gives it a connexion with its effect. We have no idea of this connexion, nor even any distinct notion what it is we desire to know, when we endeavour at a conception of it."

Here's what's bugging me. Those two definitions don't have to pick out the same cases: you can imagine a brute regularity nobody's mind has ever tracked (no "customary transition" involved), and you can imagine a mind forming that habitual transition off a run of coincidences that isnt a real regularity at all. So they can come apart pretty easily. And Hume himself seems to admit the whole exercise is a bit of a bust, since both definitions are "foreign to the cause" and leave us with "no idea of this connexion." Given that, I'm no longer sure this passage actually licenses the standard textbook move of crowning Hume the founding father of the regularity theory of laws - it almost reads like neither definition is supposed to capture what we're really after, which sounds closer to the "New Hume" sceptical-realist line (Strawson, Kail) than to the tidier regularity-theorist Hume you get in Mackie or in a lot of the laws-of-nature literature. Could you help me untangle this a bit? Specifically: is the gap between the two definitions an actual problem for reading Hume as a regularity theorist, or is there a standard way of dissolving it that I'm forgetting, and does the "foreign to the cause"/"no idea of this connexion" bit really support the sceptical-realist reading, or am I over-reading two throwaway lines?
