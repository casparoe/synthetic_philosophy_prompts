I spent thirty-five years as a condensed-matter physicist before retiring four years ago, and in retirement I have finally been working through the philosophy-of-physics literature I never had time for while the lab was running. Statistical mechanics was close enough to my own work that the question of what actually justifies the Second Law's asymmetry -- the fact that entropy overwhelmingly increases toward the future and essentially never toward the past -- seemed like a natural place to read closely, rather than simply invoke Boltzmann's H-theorem the way I did in lectures for three decades without much further thought.

What has genuinely unsettled me is David Albert's move in Time and Chance. He grants, as Boltzmann himself came to appreciate, that a fully time-symmetric statistical mechanics yields a one-directional entropy gradient only once you additionally assume that the universe (or at least our region of it) began in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrocondition. But rather than treating that assumption as a mere contingent initial condition -- something you simply posit and move past, as I would have as a working physicist -- Albert argues that this "Past Hypothesis" belongs among the fundamental laws of physics, on a par with the dynamical laws themselves, and that only this move, together with a uniform probability distribution over the compatible microstates, entitles us to the further asymmetries of records, memory, and causal order that we take for granted. That a boundary condition should be asked to carry this much weight struck me as strange. So it was something of a relief, and then a fresh puzzle, to come across what I believe is John Earman's reply to Albert: not merely that the Past Hypothesis is an unnecessary law, but that, given the well-known difficulties in defining anything like a Boltzmann entropy for the universe as a whole once gravity is included, it may not even be a well-formed enough claim to count as true or false, let alone as a law -- an objection whose title, if I am remembering it correctly, pointedly echoes Pauli's old put-down about a theory being "not even wrong."

I would like help thinking this through properly rather than just picking a side by temperament, since I no longer trust my physicist's instincts here. Please compare Albert's argument for elevating the Past Hypothesis to a fundamental law with Earman's objection that it is not even a candidate for truth or falsity, laying out precisely what a "law" is being asked to do in Albert's picture that an ordinary initial condition supposedly cannot do, and exactly where Earman thinks the very notion of entropy gives out when applied to the cosmos as a whole. Then tell me which position you find more defensible, and why: does Earman's difficulty show that Albert's proposal was misconceived from the start, or only that it needs a better-defined notion of cosmological entropy to stand on?
