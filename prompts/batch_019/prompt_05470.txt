Been down a rabbit hole on this game theory concept called "program equilibrium" (Tennenholtz, I think, is who's credited with it). The idea: instead of just picking an action in something like prisoner's dilemma, you submit a program that gets to read the other player's source code before it decides anything. Turns out a dumb little program like "cooperate if your code is identical to mine, else defect" makes mutual cooperation an actual Nash equilibrium, not just wishful thinking. Newer versions use Löb's theorem so two programs can each prove the other one will cooperate before committing (people call these things FairBots), and its basically the theoretical case for why smart contracts are supposed to let strangers trust each other.

Anyway, this got me wondering what von Neumann would've thought of it, since he's the one guy who's directly responsible for both halves of the idea: he co-invented game theory AND he's the reason "read the other program's source code" is even a coherent thing to ask a machine to do (stored-program architecture), plus he'd already worked on self-reproducing automata so self-reference wasn't new to him either. But I also read that when Nash showed him his equilibrium concept, von Neumann waved it off as basically trivial, "just a fixed point theorem" - and program equilibrium is arguably just Nash equilibrium wearing a trenchcoat. So would he have been won over by the computer science side of it, or dismissed the whole area for the same reason he dismissed Nash? What's your actual best guess and why, based on what he really believed about cooperation and equilibrium concepts, not just vibes.
