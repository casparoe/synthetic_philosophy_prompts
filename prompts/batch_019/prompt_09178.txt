so I've been sitting on this idea for a blog post and want a sanity check before I write it up properly. basically: at work we write postmortems after every big outage, and I've noticed people always slip into one of two "modes" when writing them.

Mode A is root-cause mode: "the outage happened because config flag X was left set to Y under condition Z" -- which kind of implicitly claims this is a repeatable law, i.e. same setup = same crash every time.

Mode B is narrative mode: "on tuesday alice shipped a change, then bob's cron job fired at ust the wrong moment, then the on-call missed the page because of a timezone bug in the alerting config..." -- this isn't claiming any general law at all, it's just walking through the specific chain of events that produced THIS particular outage, most of which will never recur in that exact combination again.

I went down a wikipedia hole a while back and it turns out this is basically a real fight in philosophy of history?? Something like: Hempel had this "covering law" (deductive-nomological) model where a proper historical explanation = a general law + initial conditions that logically entail the event, same structure as an explanation in physics. Then a bunch of people pushed back hard on this (Dray, Gallie, Danto, Mink, and I think Hayden White is specifically the "emplotment" guy?) and argued history doesn't actually run on laws at all -- it explains by building an intelligible narrative sequence, where an event counts as "explained" once it's followable / makes sense in hindsight, no general law required anywhere.

My rough hypothesis is that "root cause" postmortems are basically always secretly narrativist underneath -- the root-cause framing is a comforting fiction that makes a one-off, unlikely confluence of events feel lawlike and preventable, when really we're just doing Mink-style "configurational" storytelling and dressing it up in law language after the fact.

A few things I'd love your take on before I embarass myself in a blog post:
1. am I even representing Hempel and the narrativist crowd correctly, or is this a butchering
2. does the postmortem analogy actually hold up, or is there a real disanalogy I'm glossing over
3. is there a more defensible "third position" in this whole debate that I should know about first
