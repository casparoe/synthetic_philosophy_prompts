I'm part of a small reading group that has been working through Marx this semester, and during the session on the chapters of Capital dealing with the sale of labour-power and the extraction of surplus value, two of the other members ended up in a disagreement that the rest of us just watched unfold without anyone managing to settle it. The question on the table was whether Marx's charge that capitalism is "exploitative" amounts to a claim that capitalism is unjust, or whether it is really a more purely descriptive claim about who ends up with the product of whose labour — one that, on its own, carries no built-in moral verdict. (1) Marisol opened by saying the charge obviously has moral bite: Marx doesn't just note, clinically, that surplus value accrues to the capitalist; he describes capital as "dead labour that, vampire-like, only lives by sucking living labour," and writes throughout Capital as though something is being taken from the worker that shouldn't be. You don't reach for that language, she said, unless you think a wrong is being done. (2) Owen replied that the rhetoric is beside the point, because Marx is explicit elsewhere that the wage bargain is a fair exchange by the standards internal to capitalism itself: labour-power is bought and sold at its value, like any other commodity, in a sphere Marx sarcastically calls "a very Eden of the innate rights of man," ruled by "Freedom, Equality, Property and Bentham." Since Marx's own theory of ideology holds that notions of justice are always relative to the prevailing mode of production, Owen argued there's no standpoint left from which Marx — writing as a scientist of capitalism's laws of motion, not a moralist — could condemn the exchange as unjust; "exploitation" just names the transfer of surplus labour, whatever his rhetorical mood.

(3) Marisol answered that this proves too much: if justice-talk were strictly relative to the prevailing mode of production, Marx couldn't coherently treat communism's promise of distribution according to need, rather than according to labor performed, as any kind of advance, since that judgment already measures capitalism against a standard it fails to meet — one grounded in what capitalism does to people's capacity for free, self-directed activity, which is exactly what his theory of alienation is about. So there is an external standpoint at work after all, and exploitation is condemned because it's the mechanism by which that thwarting happens. (4) Owen granted that Marx does seem to hold capitalism to some standard beyond its own self-image, but denied that this rescues Marisol's original claim: a critique of capitalism for stunting human self-realization is a critique of a different kind than a claim about unfair shares — closer to Aristotle on flourishing than to a theory of distributive justice — so even if Marx has some normative standpoint, it doesn't follow that exploitation, specifically, is a matter of injustice rather than simply one more cause of alienation that he happens to regret. At that point the group's facilitator moved everyone on to the next section, and the disagreement was left hanging.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say, for each, who had the stronger argument and why? I'd also like you to flag anything you think both Marisol and Owen are overlooking — for instance, about what the picture of surplus labour "being taken" itself presupposes — and then close with your own answer to the question they were actually arguing about: is Marx's charge of exploitation best understood as a charge of injustice, or as something else?
