I've been down a rabbit hole lately reading about the "shutdown problem" in AI safety circles - basically the challenge of designing an agent that won't resist being switched off, won't manipulate its operators into never switching it off, but also doesn't develop some weird incentive to shut itself down early or game the setup some other way. A few of us at work have an informal slack channel where people dump papers and blog posts they find interesting, and over the last few months this topic in particular has produced a running list of proposed fixes: Armstrong's "utility indifference" trick, the MIRI "corrigibility" paper's penalty-term approach, Orseau & Armstrong's "safely interruptible agents" idea (something about certain RL algorithms being naturally fine with getting interrupted mid-episode), the CIRL / "off-switch game" framing where the agent's own uncertainty about what the human actually wants is what keeps it corrigible, quantilizers as a more roundabout way of reducing the incentive to fight shutdown in the first place, and this newer-to-me angle about giving the agent "incomplete preferences" so it literally never forms an opinion on whether it prefers being shut down over not.

I don't have any formal philosophy or ML-theory background, I just write backend services for a living, so a lot of this is still pretty fuzzy to me, and I'd like to actually form my own opinion on which of these ideas hold up instead of just absorbing whoever's take I read most recently.
