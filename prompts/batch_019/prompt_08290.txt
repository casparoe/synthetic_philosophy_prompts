Hey, so for the science podcast I run on the side, I've got a researcher lined up next week from one of those longtermist/EA-adjacent institutes who works on this concept called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL). The rough pitch, as I understand it: if the universe or multiverse is big enough, there are tons of agents out there who reason kind of like I do but who I can never causally contact — too far away, different Everett branch, whatever — and because our decisions are correlated (not caused, correlated) I should act as though I'd struck a deal with them: bake some compromise of everyone's values into my choices instead of just pursuing my own, and this is supposed to pay off even though no signal ever passes between us. I read Oesterheld's original report a while back (I think it was called "multiverse-wide cooperation via correlated decision making" before people started saying ECL, correct me if that's wrong) plus a few EA forum threads, so I basically get the gist, but every time I try to explain it out loud to my producer I end up hand-waving at Newcomb's problem and loosing the thread halfway through.

Can you draft me 8-10 interview questions that get past the "here's the concept" stage? I want at least one that presses on why this specifically needs evidential decision theory, and forces her to say what a committed causal decision theorist would say back; one on how anyone could actually estimate the correlation between their own decision procedure and some agent they can't observe, talk to, or ever get feedback from; one on whether the whole argument just collapses into a fancier, cosmology-flavored version of Kant's universalizability move (or the golden rule) once you strip the multiverse talk out; and one on how much weight the argument puts on contested premises about the universe or multiverse actually being large or branching — what happens to the whole edifice if that turns out false, or just permanently unknowable. For each of those, tack on a quick follow-up keyed to whatever dodge she's most likely to reach for. Bonus if one question gets at the Pascal's-mugging-shaped worry — tiny probability of correlation, times a huge number of correlated agents, times whatever payoff — since that smells a lot like the fanaticism problem that keeps popping up everywhere else in this corner of EA.
