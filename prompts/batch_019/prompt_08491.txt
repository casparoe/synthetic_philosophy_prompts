ok this has been bugging me since an argument at an ACX meetup a couple weekends ago. Everyone in the rationalist-adjacent world has internalized some version of "Good's theorem" -- I.J. Good's result from the 60s that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should always want to look at a free signal before acting, because worst case you just ignore it and do what you would've done anyway, so the expected value of information can never be negative. People use it as basically a proof that "I'd rather not know" is always some kind of confusion, and that curiosity is instrumentally rational full stop.

Then one of the econ people at the meetup brought up the (I think it's Hirshleifer?) result that information can actually destroy insurance markets -- the standard example is that if a perfectly accurate weather forecast becomes public before crop insurance contracts get written, the insurance market unravels, because insurance needs everyone to not already know whose harvest is going to fail. So there's a real sense in which free, accurate information makes people worse off from behind their own original veil of ignorance, which sounds like it flatly contradicts Good's theorem. I spent an embarrassing amount of time on the walk home trying to figure out whether these two results actually conflict or whether its some kind of equivocation -- private vs. social value of information, single-agent vs. multi-agent decision problems, anticipated vs. unanticipated arrival of the information -- and got about halfway to an answer I actually believed.

So: could you write me the dialogue that should have happened at that meetup, between a Bayesian decision theorist who thinks Good's theorem basically settles the matter and an economist who thinks the insurance case is a genuine counterexample? I don't want either of them to win by default -- the Bayesian needs to actually feel the weight of the insurance-unraveling case instead of dismissing it in one line, and the economist has to engage with the strongest version of "but that's not really a violation of the theorem" instead of just declaring victory. Real back and forth, where each one is responding to what the other just said, until they either land on a distinction that actually dissolves the disagreement or agree on exactly where the theorem's guarantee stops holding.
