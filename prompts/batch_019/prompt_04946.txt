So I've been down a research rabbit hole for a story collection I'm slowly putting together, and I keep getting stuck on Henry Sidgwick -- not the utilitarian of the ethics textbooks, but the version of him who spent the better part of two decades, on and off as president, running the Society for Psychical Research: testing children for telepathy, mailing surveys out to seventeen thousand strangers asking whether they'd ever had a vivid hallucination of someone who wasn't actually there. What gets me is that this wasn't a hobby for him, it was almost load-bearing -- his big ethics book ends by more or less admitting that without some kind of moral order to the universe, egoism and morality don't actually line up, and he couldn't let that go, so he went and tried to find the evidence himself, as rigorously as he could manage, and by his own account kept coming up short (the Creery sisters case especially, the SPR's star piece of evidence for thought-transference, until the girls turned out to be signalling to eachother with a code).

I want to turn that into a short story but keep circling a version that's just spooky-Victorian-séance pastiche, which isn't what I'm after. Could you write me one -- something in the 1000-1500 word range, doesn't have to use Sidgwick's actual name if it's easier to invent a stand-in, but I want the protagonist to be that specific type: a rigorous, self-doubting investigator in exactly that period, in the middle of running one of these experiments. Don't have anyone explain the philosophy out loud -- I want the dread of it, the sense that he needs this evidence to hold his own ethics together and is half-expecting to catch someone cheating again, sitting underneath the scene rather than spelled out. It doesn't need a tidy ending; I don't think the real history gave him one either.
