A friend and I spent much of last evening arguing about a colleague of mine, Renata, who has spent the past year publicly calling out coworkers for padding their expense reports, only for it to emerge that she did exactly this herself, undisclosed, several years ago. My friend maintained that this revelation is irrelevant to the merits of her complaints: the padding she flags is either wrong or it isn't, independent of her own history. I maintained that something about her moral position relative to her colleagues has genuinely changed, even though I could not pin down precisely what. Neither of us budged, and I would like a more rigorous adjudication than we managed to reach on our own.

Consider five distinct accounts of why, if at all, a hypocrite's past misconduct undermines their standing to blame others for the same fault:

(a) Equal-standing account: blaming presupposes a relation of mutual moral accountability between equals; hypocritical blame covertly claims for oneself an exemption it denies the target, breaching that equality and voiding the blamer's authority to address them on the matter.
(b) Unfair-advantage account: the hypocrite's core failing is enjoying the benefits of a norm's general enforcement while exempting themselves from its costs; the standing defect is simply this exploitation carried over into the act of blaming.
(c) Audience-relativity account: standing is not all-or-nothing but depends on the mode of address; hypocrisy defeats public condemnation, which trades implicitly on the speaker's own exemplary authority, while leaving intact private moral judgment or a complaint voiced only to a third party.
(d) Epistemic-unreliability account: hypocrisy is evidence of compromised moral perception or self-serving bias, which discounts the evidential weight of the blame without barring it outright.
(e) Eliminativist ("tu quoque") account: there is no distinct phenomenon of "standing" at stake; blame is fitting or not based on the facts of the case, and invoking the blamer's own history is a dialectically weak tu quoque that bears only on their tact or credibility, not on the propriety of the blame itself.
