And here is Maya's answer, word for word:

"Anatta is the teaching that there is no permanent, unchanging soul (atman) underneath our experience, unlike the Hindu idea of an eternal atman. Instead, what we call a person is just a convenient label for five aggregates — form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness — none of which is a fixed self, since they're all impermanent and constantly arising and passing away. So anatta doesn't deny that persons exist in some everyday sense; it denies that there's a permanent essence behind the aggregates. This is why the monk Nagasena, in his dialogue with King Milinda, compares a person to a chariot: a chariot is nothing more than its wheels, axle, and frame assembled together, and 'chariot,' like 'person,' is just a convenient name for the assemblage, not a separate thing on top of it.

Later Abhidharma philosophers pushed this further and argued that even the aggregates don't persist for more than an instant — each mental state arises and immediately ceases, replaced by a causally related successor moment to moment. This raises an obvious problem for karma: if the 'me' who acts is already gone a moment later, who inherits the consequences? Buddhism solves this by treating consciousness as the one aggregate that threads continuously through the whole causal series, carrying the imprint of past actions forward from moment to moment — and even from one life to the next — the way a single flame is passed from candle to candle without any wax or wick surviving the transfer. Because there's this continuous strand of consciousness, the tradition can talk about memory and karmic 'fruit' without smuggling back in a soul.

Parfit's reductionism says personal identity just consists in overlapping chains of psychological continuity and connectedness, so a person isn't a separately existing entity over and above these interrelated mental states, merely a convenient way of talking about them, exactly like the chariot. Parfit himself points out that this is essentially what the Buddha taught twenty-five centuries earlier, so his reductionism isn't just similar to anatta, it's the same thesis reached independently through two different routes, one through introspection and one through analytic metaphysics."
