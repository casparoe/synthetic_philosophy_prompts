I've been slowly working through Russell's Marriage and Morals for a seminar on the history of sexual ethics, and there's an argument in there that I can't stop turning over. He claims a big chunk of the traditional sexual double standard - why an unfaithful woman was treated as scandalous while an unfaithful man mostly got a pass - comes down to the plain fact that motherhood was always certain and fatherhood wasn't, so a lot of the patriarchal machinery around female chastity (virginity before marriage, chaperones, brutal punishment of adultery, etc) was really just a crude technology for giving men confidence that the kids inheriting their name and property were actually theirs. He even throws in that Malinowski material on the Trobriand islanders, who supposedly had a totally different, matrilineal way of assigning fatherhood, to argue that "fatherhood" as we know it isn't some biological given, its a social arrangement that tracks whatever people happen to believe about paternity at the time.

Here's what I keep getting stuck on though: that whole argument only works if paternity is actually uncertain. Nowadays you can swab your cheek and know with like 99.9% accuracy whether a kid is yours, cheaply and instantly, and courts use exactly this to settle custody and child support cases all the time. Russell died in 1970, well before anything like reliable DNA testing existed, so this obviously isn't a factor he could've built into his reasoning. If you had to guess, what do you think he'd say now, given the rest of what he argues in the book? Does cheap, certain paternity testing basically finish the job he thought was underway - i.e. does the "reason" he gives for the double standard just dissolve, leaving no honest grounds left for treating men's and women's infidelity differently? Or would he say that jealousy and possessiveness (which he also treats at length, and considers only partly a matter of custom) were never purely downstream of the paternity problem, so resolving it wouldn't actually change his practical prescriptions much? I'd like your best reconstruction of how he'd reason it through, not just a guess at the conclusion.
