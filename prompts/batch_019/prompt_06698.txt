I spent most of my working life as a condensed-matter physicist, thinking about how ordered macroscopic phases — magnetism, superconductivity, crystalline structure — emerge from enormous numbers of locally interacting particles, none of which has the faintest "awareness" of the global pattern it is helping to produce. Since retiring I have been reading a good deal of philosophy, and that reading has pulled me, somewhat unexpectedly, into the history of economic thought as well, partly on the lookout for other instances of the same trick: get enough uncoordinated micro-level interactions going under the right conditions, and macro-level order falls out without any designer. Carl Menger's account of the origin of money, laid out in his 1871 Principles of Economics and sharpened in his 1892 essay "On the Origin of Money," struck me as a beautiful specimen of exactly this pattern: money, on his telling, is not legislated into being, nor agreed upon by contract or convention, but crystallizes unintentionally as self-interested traders gradually gravitate toward accepting whichever goods happen to be most "saleable" — easiest to trade away again later — even when those goods are not what they themselves ultimately want, until eventually one or two commodities become universally accepted and an institution exists that nobody designed or decreed. This sits in real tension with accounts on which money is social all the way down — I have in mind John Searle's idea that something is money only because a community collectively and continuously imposes and recognizes a certain status function on it (roughly, "this counts as money here"), so that money is an institutional fact resting on collective intentionality rather than anything like a naturally emergent kind.

Menger died in 1921, decades before anyone had dreamed up anything resembling Bitcoin, and this is where my question lies. Suppose you could put Bitcoin in front of him and ask him to judge it by his own lights. Does it qualify, or could it ever qualify, as money on his account of saleableness? I am especially interested in how he would handle a complication that Ludwig von Mises, extending Menger's own theory, later made explicit in his regression theorem: that anything functioning as money today must trace its value back to some earlier point at which it was demanded for an ordinary, non-monetary use. Bitcoin, unlike Menger's own examples of cattle, salt, or precious metals, never had such a prior use. Does that sink its claim to being money on Menger's terms, reducing it to a merely conventional token that happens to circulate — or does his emphasis on saleableness leave room for a purely informational good to acquire "moneyness" through some comparable, decentralized process? I would like you to commit to a definite answer on Menger's behalf, rather than simply listing considerations on both sides, and to work through his actual reasoning about saleableness in some detail rather than gesturing at "spontaneous order" as a slogan. Finally, I would be curious what your answer suggests about the wider question that first got me started on this: whether money is at bottom something like a natural kind that happens to arise out of human transactions the way a crystal arises out of a solution, or a social kind that is constructed and sustained by collective belief and recognition, with nothing "natural" underneath it at all.
