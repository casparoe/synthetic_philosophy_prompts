The two camps that kept coming up were "epistemicism" and "supervaluationism" (I think the names Timothy Williamson and Kit Fine came up too, if that means anything to you). As far as I can tell, epistemicism says there really is one exact, sharp cutoff -- one specific day, one specific grain of sand -- where the property genuinely flips from false to true, but nobody can ever know exactly where that cutoff is, not because we lack data, but supposedly as a matter of principle, forever. Supervaluationism sounded different: a claim only counts as flat-out true if it comes out true under every reasonable way of precisely "filling in" the fuzzy word, and false only if it's false under all of them, and if different reasonable fillings-in disagree with each other the claim is just neither true nor false, stuck in some kind of limbo, rather than there being a hidden fact of the matter.

Specifically, I saw someone mention in passing that supervaluationism has a much harder time handling "higher order vagueness" -- the fact that the line between "clearly a heap," "borderline case," and "clearly not a heap" is itself fuzzy rather than sharp -- in a way that epistemicism supposedly dodges. Is that actually a real problem for supervaluationism, and if so, why does epistemicism get off easier?
