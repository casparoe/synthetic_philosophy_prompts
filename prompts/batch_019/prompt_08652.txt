Okay so this argument happened in my grade's group chat after our AP Physics teacher spent the last five minutes of class going off on a tangent about the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. My friend Noah latched onto the "quantum suicide" thing (I think it's originally a Max Tegmark thought experiment) where you rig up a machine that has a 50/50 quantum-random chance of instantly killing you, and you pull the trigger over and over — the claim being that you should never be scared to do this, because in every branch where "you" are still around to notice anything, you obviously survived, so subjectively it would feel like getting infinitely lucky forever. Noah thinks this basically proves that if many-worlds is true, your immortal in some real sense. My friend Maya thinks the whole argument is confused. They went back and forth about it for like an hour and neither one convinced the other, so I'm bringing it to you instead since I honestly can't referee this myself.

Here's roughly how it went: (1) Noah's opening point was that you will never experience being dead, period, so your credence that "I'll still be having experiences after I pull the trigger" should basically be 1 every single time, no matter how many rounds you play, because there's no experience of the branches where you don't make it. (2) Maya said that's a non-sequitur — never experiencing your own death doesn't mean you should expect to survive, same way "I've never experienced not existing before I was born" doesn't mean you should've expected to be born. She said the rational credence should track the actual quantum-mechanical "weight" of the surviving branches (the Born-rule measure), which shrinks every round, so going into it you should expect you're very likely about to die — the fact that you keep sticking around to ask the question again is just a selection effect, not evidence of anything. (3) Noah replied that's backwards: there is no "before the trigger" moment where some single continuous you is uncertain, because whichever version of you is asking the question again already knows the answer, so "measure" is bookkeeping about the multiverse as a whole, not something an actual person should anticipate for themselves. (4) Maya then brought up a nastier problem she said she found in an old philosophy paper (by a guy named David Lewis, not the Narnia one — she thinks the title was something like "How Many Lives Has Schrödinger's Cat," but wasn't totally sure): real killing devices don't have a clean "dead" vs. "perfectly fine" split, they have tons of ways to half-fail, so the branches where "you" keep surviving would increasingly be branches where the gun jammed and mangled you rather than clean escapes — so even granting Noah's whole argument, "immortality" would mean an eternity of getting more and more messed up, not a happy forever. (5) Noah said that's a problem with badly designed machines, not with his logic — if the death were somehow made perfectly binary and instant, the maiming issue disappears and his original point stands untouched. (6) Maya answered that's a cop-out, because getting a sharp all-or-nothing outcome out of one quantum event always requires some chain of physical steps to amplify it up to a macroscopic difference, and every extra step in that chain is one more place for a partial, ugly failure to sneak in — so "clean instant quantum death" isn't something you can just stipulate into existence, it's a physicist's idealization that real setups can't actually deliver.
