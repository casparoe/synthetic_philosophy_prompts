I'm a philosophy professor who works mainly on ethics, and for the past two years I've served as the academic ethicist on my hospital's bioethics committee, which also oversees the clinical ethics consultation service. We are currently revising the service's charter, and the sticking point is not a clinical question but an epistemic one: what weight a treating team owes to the consultant's written recommendation. Under current informal practice, when the on-call ethics consultant is paged for a hard case — a disputed withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, say, or a capacity determination complicated by family conflict — the consultant writes a note recommending a course of action, and the team almost always simply implements it, typically without working through the underlying moral reasoning itself.

One faction on the committee wants to formalize this practice by charter amendment, with language along the lines of "the consultant's documented recommendation shall guide the treatment team's course of action absent a documented clinical contraindication." Their reasoning: we don't ask an internal medicine team to independently re-derive a cardiology consult before acting on it, so there's no principled reason to expect more of them on the ethics side, and consistency and speed both favor formalizing what mostly happens already. A second faction, including our chaplaincy representative, is uneasy: they worry that implementing a conclusion one hasn't actually worked through is a different and lesser thing than acting rightly, that a policy rewarding compliance over comprehension will leave teams unable to explain or defend a decision later — to a family, to risk management, or to themselves — and that it will quietly erode whatever claim the consultation service has to moral, as opposed to merely administrative, authority. This strikes me as a live instance of the debate in moral epistemology over deference to moral testimony; I recall, for instance, that Alison Hills distinguishes moral knowledge from moral understanding and argues that only the latter resists straightforward testimonial transmission, though I'd want that distinction, or a better one, actually put to work here rather than just gestured at.

Could you draft the short opinion I'll circulate to the committee before next week's vote? It should recommend a specific charter provision rather than "it depends," give reasoning that a skeptical committee member could engage with point by point, and take the strongest form of whichever position it doesn't adopt seriously rather than dismissing it out of hand. If you think the right answer distinguishes among categories of case — by reversibility, by time pressure, or on some other principled basis — say so explicitly and build it into the proposed language.
