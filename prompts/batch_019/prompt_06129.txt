I just had a long argument with a friend over dinner about Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina -- specifically, whether his strategy for reconciling Copernicanism with Scripture is really doing something different from the modern "non-overlapping magisteria" idea, or whether that's just an anachronistic gloss we're imposing on him. Neither of us budged, so I'd rather not tell you which way I currently lean.

As it happens, I'm now grading finals for a course on early modern science and religion, and one essay question turns on exactly this issue. I'd like a disinterested second opinion on a student's answer before I settle the grade. Here is the question:

"In the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615), Galileo lays out a principle for handling apparent conflicts between Scripture and natural philosophy. Reconstruct that principle, explaining (a) why he thinks Scripture sometimes speaks in accommodated rather than literal terms, and (b) what work the notion of demonstration is doing in his argument -- i.e., why he thought the Church should not simply settle the question of the earth's motion by appeal to the literal text. Then explain briefly why this position is not simply an early version of the modern idea that science and religion occupy 'non-overlapping magisteria.'"

And here is the student's answer:

"Galileo's strategy rests on accommodation: Scripture routinely speaks in the language of everyday appearances rather than technical truth, which is why it can describe God as having hands or feeling anger without our taking this literally. The same holds, he argues, for passages that touch on the physical world, since Scripture's authors wrote for the sake of salvation, not astronomy -- hence his own famous line that the Bible teaches us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go. Because Scripture and nature share a single divine author, an apparent conflict between a demonstrated physical truth and the literal sense of a passage cannot be a real conflict; it only shows the passage has been misread, since the obscure should always yield to the clear. This is also why Galileo thought it reckless for the Church to declare Copernicanism false outright: if the earth's motion were later demonstrated, the Church would have tied biblical authority to the losing side of a settled question. In that sense, Galileo's view is essentially a forerunner of the 'non-overlapping magisteria' position later associated with Stephen Jay Gould -- astronomy and Scripture were never answering the same question in the first place, so once this is seen clearly, the appearance of conflict simply dissolves."

Could you grade this?
