I'm at work on a historical novel set in Cambridge during the 1680s and 90s in which Isaac Newton is a significant character, and I want a pivotal scene to turn on the moment a young, fictional protégé of his discovers a private theological manuscript and begins to grasp how far Newton's real religious convictions stood from what he professed in public. I already know the general stakes: that a royal dispensation from Charles II in 1675 had quietly excused Newton from the ordination that would otherwise have cost him his Trinity College fellowship, and that decades later his own successor in the Lucasian chair, William Whiston, was stripped of that professorship and expelled from Cambridge for openly professing views not unlike these, at a time when England's Blasphemy Act made antitrinitarianism a genuine legal danger. What I haven't managed to work out for myself is the actual texture of the theology, since I don't want my character's dawning realization to rest on something as vague as "he secretly rejected the Trinity."

While reading through the Newton Project's online transcriptions I found a short manuscript (Keynes MS. 8) usually catalogued as the "Twelve Articles on Religion," in which Newton sets out his private creed in a clipped, itemized form, almost like a counter-confession. Four of the articles read as follows (I've silently closed one parenthesis and added a few commas for readability; nothing else is altered):

"Artic. 1. There is one God the Father everliving, omnipresent, omniscient, almighty, the maker of heaven & earth, & one Mediator between God & Man the Man Christ Iesus.
Artic. 6. All the worship (whether of prayer, praise, or thanks giving) which was due to the father before the coming of Christ is still due to him. Christ came not to diminish the worship of his father.
Artic. 11. To give the name of God to Angels or Kings is not against the first commandment. To give the worship of the God of the Iews to Angels or Kings is against it. The meaning of the commandment is Thou shalt worship no other Gods but me.
Artic. 12. To us there is but one God the father of whom are all things, & we of him, & one Lord Iesus Christ by whom are all things & we by him: that is, we are to worship the father alone as God Almighty, & Iesus alone as the Lord, the Messiah, the great King, the Lamb of God who was slain & hath redeemed us with his blood & made us kings & Priests."

Could you help me work out exactly what argument Newton is making in Articles 11 and 12, and place it precisely on the theological map? He clearly isn't just flatly denying that Christ is divine in any sense; he seems to be constructing a fairly careful, almost lawyerly distinction between applying the title "God" to someone and directing to them the specific, exclusive worship owed to the God of Israel, so that Christ can be venerated as "Lord" and "Messiah" without being folded into the "one God" of the first commandment. I'd like to know how this compares with classical Arianism and with other subordinationist positions circulating at the time, and what New Testament "problem verses" a maneuver like Article 11 would have been designed to defuse. Article 12 also sounds to my ear like it's quietly echoing Paul; if that's right, I'd be glad to know how deliberate that sort of scriptural anchoring typically was in antitrinitarian argument of this period, since it would help me decide how self-consciously scholarly, as opposed to simply devout, to make my character's private reasoning feel on the page.
