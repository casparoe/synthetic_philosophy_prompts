Like many longtime readers of the rationalist blogosphere, I try to take Bayesian updating seriously as a personal epistemic practice, and I keep a running log of predictions that I revisit periodically to see which of my hypotheses have held up. This habit has left me newly uneasy about the problem of old evidence. If some fact E was already known with credence 1 before I ever formulated a hypothesis T, strict conditionalization seems to rule out E raising my credence in T once I notice that T entails E — yet this is precisely the situation Glymour highlighted with general relativity's retrodiction of Mercury's perihelion precession, which clearly struck physicists at the time as genuine confirmation, and it is exactly the feeling I get whenever an old dataset I've been sitting on suddenly "clicks" with a theory I've only just started to take seriously. I know Bayesians have tried to dissolve this puzzle in several ways: by relaxing the logical-omniscience assumption so that discovering "T entails E" itself counts as news (in the spirit of Garber's move), by reconstructing a counterfactual credence I would have assigned absent prior knowledge of E, and by appeal to the older predesignation tradition that ties evidential weight to whether E was predicted in advance rather than accommodated after the fact — but none of these has struck me as a fully satisfying heuristic for everyday use. Please lay out the most important implications of this debate for how someone doing informal Bayesian reasoning day to day should treat cases where a favored hypothesis is retroactively "confirmed" by evidence already on hand, and be explicit about which proposed solution, if any, actually licenses raising one's credence in such cases, versus where the honest verdict is that no legitimate update has occurred and what one is really tracking is something else, such as increased understanding or explanatory unification rather than confirmation.
