I'm part of a small retirement-community reading group (mostly ex-academics, plus me, thirty-five years in organic chemistry before I hung up my lab coat), and we've been slowly working through Moore's Principia Ethica this spring -- yes, the irony of an organic chemist getting hung up on "organic unities" was not lost on the group. Last meeting turned into a proper argument between two of the members, Dave and Fiona, about how the famous "two worlds" passage squares with what Moore says later about friendship. You probably know the passage: Moore asks you to imagine one maximally beautiful world and one maximally ugly, filthy world, stipulates that no mind will ever exist to perceive either one, and then asks whether it isn't still better for the beautiful one to exist.

Here's roughly how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it afterward over tea. (1) Dave opened by saying the thought experiment basically settles things: it shows beauty has value entirely independent of anyone's mind, which is exactly what Moore needs to defeat Sidgwick's claim that all good must be some state of consciousness. (2) Fiona shot back that Dave was overselling it, since Moore doesn't even stick to that conclusion himself -- later in the very same book he hedges and says beauty on its own has little or maybe no value at all, and by the time he wrote his later book Ethics he'd basically come around to Sidgwick's side. So citing the thought experiment as "Moores view," full stop, is misleading; even Moore didn't fully trust his own intuition pump. (3) Dave answered that this is a biographical fact about Moore getting cold feet later on, not a flaw in the argument itself -- an argument doesn't become unsound because its author later wobbled. Then he turned it back on Fiona: doesn't her own preferred reading, where value always needs a mind attached, sit awkwardly with Moore's other big idea, that personal affection is valuable as an "organic unity" whose worth exceeds the sum of its parts, including the admirable qualities of the other person as a kind of object with weight of its own? Isn't that the same basic move as the two-worlds case, locating real value partly outside anyone's private experience of pleasure? (4) Fiona said no, it's not parallel at all: in the friendship case the appreciating mind is never actually removed from the picture. If anything, she said, Moore's favorite version of personal affection is a kind of doubled-up consciousness -- appreciating someone else's appreciation of things -- so its consciousness twice over, not consciousness subtracted out. She said the friendship material actually explains why Moore got cold feet about the lonely beautiful world in the first place.

Nobody budged, and honestly I can't settle it in my own head either.
