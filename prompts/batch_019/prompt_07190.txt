I've been reporting a piece on how scientific journals handle misconduct allegations, and a source inside one journal's ethics committee sent me the internal vote breakdown from a recent case that's honestly messing with my head a bit. The committee's own guidelines say a paper only gets retracted for misconduct if the answer is yes to all three of: was the data fabricated, was there intent to deceive, and did it materially change the paper's conclusions. Three people sit on this particular committee, and for this case they voted on the three questions one at a time, and then each of them seperately gave their own personal yes/no verdict on whether to retract.

Here's the part I can't shake. On the fabrication question, two of the three said yes. On the intent question, two of three said yes too, but a different pair. On the materiality question, again two of three said yes, and again a different pair. So question by question, every single condition for retraction passed by majority. But when it came to each committee member's own personal, bottom-line verdict, all three of them said no — because each one had personally voted no on at least one of the three questions, and by their own logic that meant no grounds for retraction. So depending on whether you tally the votes question-by-question or person-by-person, you get completely opposite outcomes, from the exact same three people and the exact same underlying votes.

I ran this by a friend who does survey design for a living and she just kind of stared at me. Is there an actual "correct" way to count votes in a case like this, or is this basically a built-in flaw in how committees like this are structured? And more practically, if a journal asked you how to redesign this process, what would you actually tell them to do?
