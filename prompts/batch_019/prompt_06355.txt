My older sister is about to have her first child, and she has asked me — her brother, currently a few years into a philosophy PhD focused on decision theory and formal epistemology — to give the toast at the baby shower her partner is hosting next weekend. The guest list is a strange mix: her side of the room will be entirely prenatal-yoga friends, aunts, and coworkers, while my side, because she did her undergraduate degree alongside half of my current cohort, will include several people who read L.A. Paul's *Transformative Experience* in the same seminar I did and will notice exactly what I'm doing the moment I gesture at it.

I can't stop thinking about how cleanly her situation maps onto Paul's argument, which is the subject of my current dissertation chapter. Becoming a parent is her paradigm case: it is epistemically transformative, since no amount of description or secondhand testimony from other parents can substitute for the first-person acquaintance with what it's actually like to love and be responsible for a child in that specific way; and it is personally transformative, since the very preferences that would be needed to evaluate the choice are themselves reshaped by having the child. That combination is exactly why Paul reaches for the vampire case as an analogy, and exactly why she thinks ordinary expected-utility reasoning was never in a position to tell my sister, or anyone, to do this. The upshot I can't get past is that nobody in that room, including the most seasoned parents present, is actually entitled to promise her she'll be glad she did it — the honest, Paul-flavored answer to "will I love this?" is closer to "you can't know that yet, and strictly speaking, neither can we," which is not exactly toast material.

So here is what I would like help with: please write the toast itself. It should run two to three minutes read aloud, be genuinely warm and, where it can be, funny, and it needs to work equally well for the yoga friends, who should never notice the philosophical machinery underneath, and for my cohort, who will notice, and should feel that it was handled honestly rather than fudged. The one hard constraint is that it cannot quietly cash out as "you're going to love this," and it cannot lean on any parent's or friend's testimony as though that settled the matter, since that is precisely the kind of pre-transformation certainty the argument says none of us are entitled to. If there is a way to be genuinely reassuring that does not depend on predicting her future self's preferences for her — something closer to honoring the leap itself than forecasting its outcome — I would like the toast to find it.
