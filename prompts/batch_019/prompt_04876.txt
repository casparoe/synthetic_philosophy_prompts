I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've been working through Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā after a colleague brought it up during a discussion about paraconsistent logic, and one verse has me stuck. Nāgārjuna's signature move through most of the text is to take some question -- does a thing arise from itself, from something else, from both, or uncaused? -- and deny all four options in turn; that is literally how the whole treatise opens. But in chapter 18, verse 8 (at least in the editions I've compared), he seems to do the reverse. Paraphrasing across translations rather than quoting any one of them exactly, it says something very close to: "Everything is real; everything is not real; everything is both real and not real; everything is neither real nor not real -- this is the Buddha's teaching." Here he asserts all four legs, including two that look like flat negations of one another, about literally everything.

As someone who spends a lot of time thinking in truth-values, my first instinct was that this is just gesturing at some four-valued scheme -- true, false, both, neither, along the lines of Belnap's logic -- where affirming a contradiction doesn't automatically explode into triviality. But most of what I've read suggests that reaction is too quick and misses the actual point of the verse. Could you help me understand two things: why the "assert-all-four" move here doesn't simply contradict the "deny-all-four" method used everywhere else in the text, and what is specifically wrong (or right) about reading this verse through a four-valued-logic lens?
