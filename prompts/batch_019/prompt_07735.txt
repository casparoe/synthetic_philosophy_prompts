I've been falling down a philosophy-of-science rabbit hole this week for a piece on whether we should actually trust our current best theories, and I keep circling back to the pessimistic meta-induction — Larry Laudan's old move of listing off a graveyard of theories (phlogiston, caloric, the crystalline spheres, all the various "ether" theories of light and electromagnetism) that were once wildly successful and are now considered flat-out false, so why should we think todays theories are any different. The standard rescue move, from what I can tell, is John Worrall's structural realism: even when the "furniture" of a theory gets thrown out, the mathematical structure often survives into the next theory, so you can supposedly be a realist about the structure while staying agnostic about the underlying entities. And the case Worrall (and before him, apparently, Poincaré) always leans on is Fresnel's equations for light, which got carried over more or less intact into Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, even though Fresnel thought light was mechanical vibrations in an elastic-solid ether and Maxwell reinterpreted the whole picture in terms of fields.

Here's the thing though - a philosopher friend of mine mentioned, kind of offhandedly, that this example gets trotted out SO often as the poster child for structural realism that she's suspicious it's basically one convenient case being asked to carry the entire argument, and that there's real pushback in the literature on whether it even does the job Worrall wants it to do. I'd love for you to actually make that case rather than just gesture at it: please argue, as rigorously as you can, that the Fresnel-to-Maxwell transition does NOT rescue structural realism from the pessimistic meta-induction. I'm especially interested in arguments that dig into what "structure" is even supposed to mean here — is it doing real metaphysical work, or is it just picked out after the fact to make the history look continuous — and in whether leaning so heavily on one historical case commits structural realists to a kind of cherry-picking that a sufficiently mean-spirited critic could turn into its own pessimistic induction against structural realism itself.
