I've been reporting a story on how people make calls when they can't pin down the odds, and one conversation from my interviews keeps nagging at me. My source is a nurse choosing between two job offers before Friday. Hospital A has been through three rounds of layoffs already, so HR just told her straight up: "historically about one in five nurses in this unit get cut within eighteen months." Hospital B is a brand-new clinic backed by a health-tech startup, and literally nobody can give her a number — not the recruiter, not her friends already working in health-tech, not even the founders themselves. It could fold within a year, or it could take off and everyone gets promoted twice over. When she actually sat down and ran the math on pay, benefits, and the odds of getting promoted, she figured the expected value at the startup clinic was probably at least as good as staying at Hospital A, maybe better. But she still can't bring herself to pick it, and when I pushed her on why, all she said was "at least I know what im dealing with at A," which felt more like a feeling dressed up as a reason than an actual answer.

I brought this up with some friends over drinks and it turned into a whole argument. One friend insisted she's just risk-averse, full stop, end of story. Another said no, that's not it exactly — not knowing the odds at all seems to be doing something different in her head than just knowing the odds are mediocre, and he couldn't explain why that should matter but was adamant that it does. So now I'm stuck: is she being irrational here, or is there an actual case for treating "nobody can tell you the odds" as worse than "you know the odds and they're not great"? And practically speaking, what would you tell her to do?
