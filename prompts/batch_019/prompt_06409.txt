My grandma died on Sunday. She was 84 and ran a wildlife rehab out of her garage for something like 45 years, foxes, owls, fawns, whatever the neighbors found hurt on the side of the road. Somewhere in the middle of all that she lost the ability to watch a nature documentary like a normal person. She used to say the "circle of life" framing was a story people tell themselves so they don't have to think about it properly, that a fawn starving to death over three days, or a rabbit getting eaten alive from the inside by botfly larvae, isn't some beautiful balance, its just suffering, and the fact that its "natural" doesn't make it fine. She'd get into real fights with the other rehab volunteers about whether it was actually good to release a healthy-looking animal back into the woods to probably die within the month, versus keeping it in permanent care, and she was firmly on the "we didn't patch this thing up just to feed it back into the machine" side. I found out after she died that theres apparently a whole philosophical literature on exactly this, "wild animal suffering," which she'd arrived at completely on her own after decades of hands-on work. She left instructions that whoever spoke at her service should not say anything about her "returning to nature" or being "at peace among the animals now," because she considered that exact framing to be the comfortable lie she'd spent twenty years arguing against.

I'm the one giving the eulogy, and I got into a massive argument about this with my friend Dana last night. Dana's position is that most of the people showing up are going to be hunters, birdwatchers, and old family friends who like the idea of nature being basically harmonious, and that hitting them with real wild-animal-suffering content at a funeral is going to land as either depressing or completely out of nowhere. My position is that this is precisely the comfortable lie grandma built her later life around refusing to tell, and that the rehab-center crowd who actually knew her views will clock it immediately if we chicken out and go generic on them. Neither of us could actually figure out how you'd write the thing.

So could you write the eulogy? Nothing crazy long, maybe five minutes. It has to work for a room that's roughly half people who've never thought about any of this and just want to feel okay, and half people who knew exactly what she believed and will wince if its watered down. It should be warm, and it should genuinely comfort people, but it can't lean on any nature-is-benevolent imagery to get there, it has to earn the comfort some other way, the way she'd actually have wanted.
