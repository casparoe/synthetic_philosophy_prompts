I'm putting together a session for my early modern philosophy seminar on Hobbes's surprisingly idiosyncratic philosophy of mathematics, and I keep coming back to a short but knotty passage from Leviathan that I'd like another reader's eyes on before I teach it. In Chapter 4, "Of Speech," Hobbes writes: "Seeing then that truth consisteth in the right ordering of names in our affirmations, a man that seeketh precise truth had need to remember what every name he uses stands for, and to place it accordingly; or else he will find himself entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twigs; the more he struggles, the more belimed. And therefore in geometry, which is the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind, men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations they call definitions, and place them in the beginning of their reckoning."

Read quickly, this sounds like a fairly deflationary point about avoiding verbal confusion through stipulation. But elsewhere, in De Corpore, Hobbes insists that a proper geometrical definition does not merely fix what a word is to mean — it must state the generation of the thing defined, as when a circle is defined through the motion of a straight line about one of its fixed endpoints, so that the definition itself exhibits the efficient cause of the figure and everything later demonstrated of it. That looks like a far stronger, causal-constructive notion of definition, and it is what underwrites Hobbes's famous claim that geometry alone is fully certain, because we ourselves are the makers of the very objects it studies, whereas physics can never achieve the same certainty about a nature we did not construct. A colleague who works on Hobbes's natural philosophy tells me this is a well-known sore point in the secondary literature, but the treatments I've found so far talk past one another rather than resolving it.

So here is what I would like help sorting out: is the "settling of significations" Hobbes describes in the Leviathan passage the very same notion of definition as the generative, causal one from De Corpore, or are these two different things that Hobbes is running together under one word? If they are meant to be the same, how does a merely stipulative fixing of a word's meaning come to carry causal information about how the thing named is actually produced? And if the tension is real rather than merely apparent, does it undercut Hobbes's larger argument for geometry's superiority over physics, or is there a reading of "definition" here that dissolves the difficulty rather than papering over it? I would rather have a careful reconstruction of the live interpretive options than a one-line gloss, since this is exactly the point at which I expect my students to get stuck.
