Bit of context: I just started as an RA in a causal inference lab (health-econ-ish focus), and our PI has this thing where before anyone's allowed to touch a DAG in R they have to read some actual philosophy of causation first — some Lewis, some Woodward. I rolled my eyes at first but whatever, orders are orders, and now it's suddenly relevant to my actual life because it caused a whole argument at lab meeting last week that I can't stop thinking about.

Two of the senior PhD students, Jonah and Deepa, spent a solid twenty minutes going back and forth on whether Woodward's interventionist theory is actually a different (and better) theory of causation than Lewis's counterfactual one, or whether it's basically Lewis with extra steps. Reconstructing as best I can: (1) Jonah opened by saying interventionism is a real advance, since it swaps out Lewis's whole "closest possible world" similarity ranking — which he called hand-wavy and basically reverse-engineered to give the right answers — for something precise, i.e., structural equations and graph surgery, and that it also handles preemption cases more cleanly and matches how working scientists actually talk. (2) Deepa said not so fast, since "what would happen to Y if we intervened on X" is just a counterfactual conditional, Woodward says as much himself, so at best this is one specific, well-behaved subset of counterfactuals, not a different kind of theory at all. (3) Jonah conceded that but said the restriction IS the advance — you trade Lewis's need for a global similarity ordering over every possible antecedent for one clean, well-defined operation, and that's not nothing. (4) Deepa's comeback was a circularity worry: to even say which variables get held fixed, or which edges get "cut," in an intervention, you already have to know a bunch of the causal structure going in, so the theory tells you how to read off one causal fact from others you're assuming, but never explains where causal structure comes from, wheras Lewis was at least trying to ground the whole thing in something non-causal. (5) Jonah's response was that Woodward openly admits it isn't a fully reductive analysis and is totally fine with that, since the goal isn't a Humean reduction to the non-causal but an account of what a causal claim commits you to, and it still earns its keep by cleanly handling cases — feedback loops, certain preemption setups — that were genuinely awkward for Lewis. (6) Deepa's last word was that Jonah doesn't then get to also claim it "solves" the old problems in the sense people originally wanted them solved — it just swaps in a more tractable question, and a committed Lewisian could point out that the direction/asymmetry of causation is still unexplained, just relocated into the unanalyzed notion of "intervention."

Nobody backed down, and I'll be honest, by the end I was loosing track of whether they were even still disagreeing about the same thing. Could you go through it move by move, 1 through 6, and say who has the better of it at each step rather than just picking an overall winner? And then flag anything you think both of them are talking past each other on, or missing entirely. Trying to actually get my head around this properly before next week's meeting.
