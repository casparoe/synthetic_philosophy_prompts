Harsanyi's aggregation theorem gets cited a lot in rationalist and EA-adjacent discussions of moral uncertainty and interpersonal utility comparison, usually in the compressed form: individual and social preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, social preferences respect a Pareto condition, and therefore social utility must be an affine combination of individual utilities — utilitarianism falling out of rationality plus Pareto almost for free. I'd like the actual mechanics behind that claim, since I think the compressed version overstates it. My understanding is that the core argument, run on Pareto indifference alone (society is indifferent whenever every individual is), pins down social utility as some affine combination of individual utilities but leaves the sign of each person's weight wide open, so nothing yet rules out a social welfare function that's decreasing in someone's utility — you seem to need a strictly stronger Pareto condition to force every weight non-negative. And even once the signs are fixed, each weight can apparently still be rescaled arbitrarily, since a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function is only unique up to its own positive affine transformation. Could you walk through, at the level of the proof rather than the folk summary, (a) exactly where Pareto indifference alone fails to constrain the sign of the weights, (b) what the stronger Pareto condition adds to fix this, and (c) why the theorem still falls short of resolving interpersonal comparability even once the signs are settled? I recall Sen pressing roughly this last point against Harsanyi, but I'd like the mechanics rather than just the verdict.
