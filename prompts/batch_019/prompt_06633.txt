I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but a running argument with a coworker has had me reading on my own for a few months now, and I'd like some help thinking it through properly. He maintains that calling a chatbot's output a "belief" or a "want" is just a sloppy figure of speech, no more literal than saying a thermostat "wants" the room warmer. Trying to work out whether he's right, I found my way to Daniel Dennett's writing on what he calls the intentional stance: the strategy of predicting a system's behavior by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, rather than tracing its physical mechanism (the physical stance) or its blueprint (the design stance). In the essay where he first lays this out — "True Believers," if I have the title right — Dennett uses a chess-playing computer to make his case: you can't practically predict its next move by tracing circuits, and often not even by reading the code, but you can predict it very well by assuming it wants to protect its queen and believes some move sets up a fork. His conclusion seemed to be that being a genuine believer just consists in being reliably predictable this way, with no further fact needed about inner representations — which makes belief-talk sound almost deflationary, not so different in kind from the thermostat after all.

What complicates this for me is a later piece of his, "Real Patterns," where he doesn't want beliefs to be quite that cheap. There he compares them to something like a center of gravity: not a fiction we impose for convenience, but a real, if abstract, feature of a system, earned by the fact that describing things that way genuinely compresses and predicts the data better than any cheaper description does. That raises the bar again, and it's exactly where I get stuck trying to apply any of this to a large language model. Unlike a brain, we actually have the model's entire "design" sitting on a hard drive — every weight is right there — so the intentional stance isn't forced on us by missing information the way it is with other people, or even really with a chess program whose source code we could in principle read. It seems to be forced on us, if at all, purely by scale. And there's a competing low-level description available for an LLM that has no real analogue for a chess program or a brain: "it's just assigning probabilities to the next token," which sounds like it might be buying exactly the same predictive compression as the intentional description, minus the beliefs.

So: does Dennett's own two-part picture — predictive success first, real-pattern status second — give principled grounds for saying a large language model has beliefs in whatever sense he actually cared about, or does the availability of that "just predicting tokens" description show that intentional talk isn't earning its keep here the way it does for the chess program? Please write an essay that takes the strongest version of each side seriously and then actually settles the question. I'm not looking for a conclusion along the lines of "it depends what you mean by belief" — I want to know which way you think it comes out, and why.
