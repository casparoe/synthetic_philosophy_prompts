"Split-brain surgery, technically a corpus callosotomy, involves severing the corpus callosum, the main bundle of fibers connecting the brain's two hemispheres (sometimes along with the anterior commissure). It's used as a last-resort treatment for severe epilepsy, since it stops seizures from spreading from one hemisphere to the other. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga's experiments on these patients starting in the 1960s are the classic evidence base. In one well-known paradigm, an object is flashed briefly to the patient's left visual field, which projects only to the right hemisphere. Since language is housed in the left hemisphere in most people, the patient will verbally deny having seen anything. But if asked to reach under a screen with their left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere) and pick the object out from a group of objects by feel, they can do it correctly – showing that the right hemisphere registered information the "talking" left hemisphere has no access to. The left hemisphere will even confabulate reasons for the left hand's choice, which is what Gazzaniga meant by calling it an "interpreter" that's constantly constructing a story to explain behavior it didn't actually initiate. Because the dissociation is so sharp, split-brain patients are generally understood to be living with two independent streams of consciousness running side by side at all times, each hemisphere its own self-contained perceiver walled off from the other's experience.

The unity of consciousness refers to the way the many things we're aware of at a given moment – sights, sounds, thoughts – seem to belong together, as parts of one experience had by one subject, rather than being scattered across separate subjects. Split-brain cases matter philosophically because they look like a real-world case where this unity comes apart, forcing the question of whether the number of subjects of experience here is really one, or two, or something in between that our ordinary way of counting minds isn't built to handle.

Puccetti's answer is that the surgery is best understood as splitting one mind into two: before the operation the callosum lets a single subject draw on both hemispheres, and cutting it produces a second subject where before there was only one. I find this more plausible than Nagel's view, since it actually answers the question instead of declaring it unanswerable, and it fits the further finding that the two hemispheres can display diverging emotional reactions and preferences once disconnected, which is easier to explain if there are genuinely two subjects there to have diverging preferences in the first place, rather than one subject whose unity has merely become somewhat looser or harder to describe."
