I teach a graduate proseminar on formal/social epistemology, and last week we did the peer disagreement unit -- Elga's equal weight view, plus the classic restaurant-bill case where two friends independently work out their share of the check in their heads and land on different totals (like $43 vs $45), and the puzzle of whether they should each move all the way toward 50/50 confidence. Afterwards two of my students, Nadia and Owen, kept arguing about it out in the hallway for a solid twenty minutes, and the conversation drifted somewhere I wasn't planning to get to until later in the term. I jotted down the gist from memory afterward, so the wording below is mine, not theirs, but I tried to keep the moves faithful:

(1) Nadia defended the equal weight view: if she and a genuine epistemic peer independently work through the same problem and land on different answers, neither has any non-question-begging reason to think she's the one who got it right, so both should move a long way toward the middle. Generalized, whenever a real peer disagrees with you about anything, conciliating hard is just what rationality requires; anything less is arbitrarily favoring your own perspective for no reason.

(2) Owen said this is self-undermining: plenty of careful, serious epistemologists reject the equal weight view outright, and those people are obviously Nadia's peers on this exact question if anyone is -- more so than some rando is her peer on a bar tab. So by her own theory she should currently be suspending judgment on whether the equal weight view is even true, rather then confidently using it to beat him in an argument.

(3) Nadia's answer was that the view was never meant to be unrestricted -- it's a rule about first-order factual disputes, and a theory can coherently decline to cover disagreements about itself without any contradiction. Restrict the scope, and the self-undermining worry just evaporates.

(4) Owen wasn't having it: that's exactly the move he'd expect from someone who doesn't want to update -- like an uncle who says he defers to experts about everything except the one topic he's touchy about. Unless Nadia can say something principled about why disagreements about disagreement are different in kind, and not just inconvenient for her personally, this is special pleading wearing a technical costume, and it quietly drains the view of the bite that made it interesting in the first place.

(5) Nadia tried once more: some methodological commitments have to be held fixed for you to reason your way through anything at all -- you can't rationally revise modus ponens just because somebody disagrees with you about modus ponens -- and she wants to say conciliationism is that kind of fixed point, not an arbitrary carve-out. Owen granted the modus ponens point but denied conciliationism belongs anywhere near that category, since it's exactly the sort of contestable, substantive thesis that careful people reject for what look like genuinely good reasons. He also pointed out that even granting Nadia the exemption at the meta level, she still hasn't explained why she gets to be so confident applying the view at the object level (the bill-splitting cases and the like) while so many peers reject the very principle behind it.

Nobody budged (I eventually had to shoo them both out of the hallway so I could lock up), and I honestly couldn't referee it myself even though I'd rather stay neutral here. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each individual step, not just who comes out ahead overall? Then give me your overall verdict: does the self-undermining objection actually sink the equal weight view, does Nadia's restriction move save it, and is Owen's special-pleading charge fair? I'd also like to know if there's something important you think both of them are missing.
