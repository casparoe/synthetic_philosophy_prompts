A friend and I spent most of a long car ride arguing about betting strategy, and I'd like a neutral opinion on who was right. It started with sports betting but quickly turned into a debate about decision theory. I had always assumed that betting "rationally" simply meant maximizing your expected winnings on each wager. My friend, who had recently been reading about something called "geometric rationality" — the idea that in many decision problems one should maximize the geometric expectation of an outcome rather than the ordinary arithmetic expectation — insisted this was a mistake, and that the Kelly criterion is the only genuinely rational way to size a repeated bet. Her argument, as best I can reconstruct it, was this: a bettor who maximizes expected wealth at every step will, over a long enough sequence of favorable bets, end up ruined with probability approaching one, even though that bettor's expected wealth is technically climbing the entire time; meanwhile, a bettor using the Kelly fraction will almost surely end up wealthier than any such rival given enough time. By the end of the drive she'd convinced me that betting anything other than the Kelly fraction is simply a mistake, full stop, in more or less the same way that holding intransitive preferences is a mistake, and that appeals to "risk tolerance" or "time horizon" are really just dressed-up ways of refusing to follow the math where it leads.

Having had a few days to think it over, that last step feels too fast, though I can't quite pin down where it goes wrong — the individual pieces of her argument all seemed to check out on their own. Could you help me think through this properly? Specifically: (1) is it actually true that maximizing expected wealth and maximizing expected geometric growth can pull apart in the way she describes, ideally illustrated with a simple concrete bet; and (2) even if the Kelly bettor really does almost surely end up ahead of any fixed-fraction rival in the long run, does that fact actually establish that full Kelly betting is the objectively correct policy for any rational bettor, or is there a substantive assumption being smuggled in about what a bettor ought to care about — one that someone with a finite horizon or a strong aversion to large drawdowns could reasonably reject without thereby being irrational in any technical sense?
