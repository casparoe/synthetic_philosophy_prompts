I sit on the academic integrity board at my university, and we've got a case on the docket next month that's messier than the usual copy-paste plagiarism stuff, and it's kind of eating at me. A five-person senior capstone team submitted one joint final report under all five names, and it turns out the test data behind one section's conclusions was made up. Two of the five, Priya and Jordan, admit they fabricated it because the prototype kept failing bench tests and they'd run out of time before the deadline. Ana, the nominal team lead, didn't fabricate anything herself, but she signed the certification page attesting the results were accurate, and she's told us privately that she had a bad feeling about that section but didn't push it because she didn't want another fight with Priya and Jordan that week. Marcus joined the team three weeks before submission, replacing someone who dropped the course, and says he had no idea any of the data was fabricated. Sam actually flagged the weirdness in the team's group chat two months ago, was told to drop it, and did.

The board is split down the middle. Half want to sanction the team as a unit — five names went on one document, they held themselves out as a team, so it's the team's failure that gets sanctioned, with individual circumstances treated as mitigation at most. The other half thinks that's basically a fiction: only individuals actually know things and decide things, so you can only punish people for what they personally knew or chose, and "the team failed" is just a loose way of describing five seperate stories stapled together. (I mentioned all this to a friend who does philosophy of action as a hobby, and she said "oh, that's just Bratman vs. Gilbert" and went back to her crossword, so thanks for nothing there.) What I actually need is the memo I can put in front of the board before we meet: a real recommendation for what happens to each of the five, not a menu of options, reasoned in a way the individual-responsibility camp could read and feel genuinely answered rather than steamrolled — and it needs a timeline, since final grades are due to the registrar in eleven days.
