For thirty-odd years I ran a laboratory studying brainstem control of respiration, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy in any serious way -- mostly philosophy of mind, and lately, via a somewhat roundabout path through the AI safety literature, the discussions of "wireheading" and reward tampering in artificial agents. It struck me rather quickly that the thought experiments these authors construct -- an agent that learns to short-circuit the signal meant to track its goal, rather than pursuing the goal itself -- are not hypothetical at all in my own field: they describe, more or less exactly, intracranial self-stimulation, which Olds and Milner discovered in rats some seventy years ago and which remains, in updated form, a live experimental paradigm. I still serve as the senior scientist member of my former university's IACUC, and a protocol has just come before us that has made the connection feel considerably less academic.

The proposal, from a behavioral-neuroscience lab, seeks approval for an added experimental arm in which mice are implanted with an optogenetic construct targeting dopaminergic neurons of the ventral tegmental area and then given extended, near-continuous access to a nose-poke that triggers stimulation -- explicitly modeled on the classic "unlimited access" self-stimulation paradigms -- in order to generate a robust compulsive-seeking phenotype against which candidate anti-addiction compounds can be tested. Food and water remain freely available; the proposed humane endpoints are a body-weight-loss threshold and twice-daily welfare checks. One faction on the committee, including our veterinarian, regards this as routine and well-precedented: the endpoints are standard, the scientific payoff (a better mechanistic grip on compulsive reward-seeking, with obvious relevance to human addiction and anhedonia) is real, and the animals' own vigorous pursuit of the stimulation is, if anything, reassuring rather than troubling. A second, smaller faction disagrees sharply. They point to the "wanting" versus "liking" distinction from the Berridge and Robinson research tradition -- the finding that dopamine-driven incentive salience can be dissociated from hedonic pleasure proper -- and argue that a protocol expressly designed to hijack the incentive-salience system is precisely the case in which we cannot treat the animal's self-selected behavior as evidence about its own welfare, since that very behavior is the thing being engineered. On their view we need welfare evidence that does not pass back through the manipulated reward channel, and the protocol as written offers none.

Could you draft the short written opinion I will circulate to the full committee ahead of our vote? It should reach a concrete recommendation -- approve, approve with specified conditions, or reject and return for revision -- with a timeframe attached if conditions are involved, and it should take the second faction's argument seriously on its own terms, rather than treating it as an unfalsifiable worry about animal minds that can never be settled either way.
