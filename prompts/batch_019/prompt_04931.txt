My dad died on Tuesday and I'm giving the eulogy this weekend, and I need help getting the shape of it right, not just nice words. Backstory: for the last twenty-odd years he was completely fixated on this one essay by Moritz Schlick called "On the Meaning of Life," which he found in some secondhand philosophy anthology back in like 2001 and basically never shut up about since. His pitch, as best I absorbed it secondhand from a guy who fixed furnaces for a living, was that almost everything we do is just a means to some further end -- work for money, money for security, security for retirement, retirement for finally having time to do something that isn't a means to anything -- and that real meaning only ever shows up in activity that's its own point, which he'd just call "play." Kids get this automatically, he said, and most adults get talked out of it by a culture that worships work and seriousness, and only a few people (scientists, artists, and, he'd add with a straight face, himself) manage to keep their whole life organized around stuff done for its own sake instead of a payoff down the line. That's basically why he spent thirty years and every spare dollar restoring a lightship that will never sail anywhere, to my mother's lasting frustration.

Here's my actual problem: half the room Saturday is family who've spent decades quietly thinking dad wasted a good mind and a lot of money on a boat that goes nowhere, and the other half is his flotilla of hobbyist and workshop buddies who thought he was the most alive person they knew. My sister already warned me that if I get up there and say some version of "he never lost his sense of childlike wonder," our uncle -- who actually read the essay because dad made him -- will visibly wince, because that line flattens the whole thing into a greeting-card sentiment when what dad actually thought he had was an argument: a real distinction between activities that carry their meaning inside themselves and ones that are just IOUs you cash in later, if ever. Can you write the eulogy? It needs to hold together as something recognizably his, not just borrow the word "play" a few times, it can't dodge the tension with mom, and it still has to land for people who are never going to read a word of philosophy in their lives.
