I served on a jury a few months ago for a criminal trial in which the defendant's main defense was duress: he admitted to driving the getaway car during a robbery, but testified that one of the other men had threatened to hurt his family if he didn't cooperate. During deliberations, almost the entire argument came down to one phrase in the judge's instructions — that duress requires showing the defendant had no "reasonable opportunity to do otherwise." A couple of jurors argued that since he was never drugged, restrained, or literally incapable of refusing, he technically did have that opportunity and should be held responsible regardless of the threat; others felt this missed the point, since the threat made refusal so costly that it wasn't a live option in whatever sense should matter morally. We convicted, but I came away oddly unsettled, and I've spent the evenings since then reading around the philosophical literature on moral responsibility and "could have done otherwise."

That reading eventually led me to Harry Frankfurt's argument against what's called the "principle of alternative possibilities" (PAP) — the thesis, central to my jury's whole disagreement, that a person is responsible for an act only if they could have done otherwise. Frankfurt's counterexamples involve a hidden intervener who would force the outcome if the agent showed any sign of choosing differently, but who never actually has to intervene because the agent does the thing on their own anyway. What surprised me was a throwaway line in something I read, claiming that John Locke had essentially anticipated this move, nearly three centuries earlier, in his chapter "Of Power" (Book II, Chapter xxi) in the Essay Concerning Human Understanding. Locke asks the reader to imagine this:

"Suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in, i.e. prefers his stay to going away. I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone. So that liberty is not an idea belonging to volition, or preferring; but to the person having the power of doing, or forbearing to do, according as the mind shall choose or direct."

Locke's own point is just to pry apart "voluntary" from "free," but I can't tell whether this really is a Frankfurt-style case or only looks like one on the surface. The man in the room still seems to retain some alternative possibility — he could at least have tried the door, or willed to leave, even though that willing would have failed to produce the actual leaving — whereas a genuine Frankfurt case is supposed to seal off the alternative at the level of the will itself, not merely at the level of the outcome. Is that residual "flicker" a real disanalogy, of exactly the kind that flicker-of-freedom defenders of PAP would seize on, or is it beside the point that Locke and Frankfurt are each actually trying to make? And on the most charitable reading, does Locke's argument commit him to rejecting PAP outright, or is his point narrower than that and fully compatible with it? I'd like a fairly careful, step-by-step treatment rather than a general summary, partly because I'd still like to work out what I think my own jury should have made of that "reasonable opportunity to do otherwise" instruction.
