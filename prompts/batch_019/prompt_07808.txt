I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, so I've always had a soft spot for arguments that get dressed up as clean axiomatic proofs, which is probably why Harsanyi's aggregation theorem hooked me so hard when I ran into it a few months back (Im newly retired and have been filling my time with philosophy papers instead of journal referee reports, for better or worse). The result felt almost like a free lunch: assume each person's preferences over gambles satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, assume society's preferences do too, then add a Pareto condition -- if everyone in the the group ranks two gambles the same way, society has to rank them that way too -- and out pops the conclusion that the social utility function must be a weighted sum of everybody's individual utilities. It reminded me of combining independent measurements back in my old lab, weights and all. But then I went looking for objections and ran into Peter Diamond's old critique: take two equally deserving people and one good that can't be split, and compare (a) flipping a fair coin to decide who gets it, against (b) just handing it to one of them outright. If the expected-utility numbers come out the same either way, Harsanyi's rule says the planner has to be completely indifferent between the coin flip and the arbitrary handout -- which strikes a lot of people, me included depending on the day, as missing something real about fair process, not just fair outcomes.

What I can't get straight in my own head is whether the "ex ante" side (Pareto applied to preferences over the gamble itself, before anyone knows how it turns out) has a decent comeback to that coin-flip case, or whether the "ex post" side (which cares about the fairness of what people actually end up with, or at least about the chances they were actually given) can survive the obvious counter-charge that they're just overriding people's own attitudes to risk in the name of "fairness." Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp people holding these two views -- say, two old friends from a decision theory reading group who've ended up on opposite sides of this -- really arguing it out over several exchanges? I don't want either one to fold early or miss the other's best shot: I want the ex-ante utilitarian's strongest reply to the coin-flip objection, the ex-post advocate's strongest reply to the risk-preferences charge, and so on back and forth, so that by the end I'm still genuinely unsure who I'd side with.
