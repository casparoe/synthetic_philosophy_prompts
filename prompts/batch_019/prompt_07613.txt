I am several chapters into a novel narrated by a field linguist, working loosely in the tradition of the thought experiment Quine lays out in Word and Object, and I have been doing background reading so that the philosophy behind her interior monologue can withstand scrutiny. Over dinner last week, a friend finishing a philosophy PhD walked me through the "gavagai" case: the native who exclaims "Gavagai!" at the sight of a rabbit, whose utterance could be rendered equally well as "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit-stage," with no possible observation of behavior ever able to decide among these. I came away certain I had finally grasped the moral of the story: that Quine had shown there is no objective fact about what any expression in a language really refers to, and therefore that "faithful" or "accurate" translation, as distinct from merely stylish translation, is not a coherent target at all — just a comforting fiction that translators and critics tell themselves and each other.

I would like to give my narrator a scene in which she says something along these lines to unsettle a younger, more idealistic colleague who still speaks of getting a translation "right." Before I commit the line to the page, though, I would rather test the philosophy than my instincts: if Quine really did demonstrate decades ago that no translation manual can be objectively more correct than any of its rivals, why do professional translators, editors, and literary critics still spend so much energy disputing whether a particular translation is faithful to its original? Is that whole critical vocabulary simply a holdover that the philosophy of language has already quietly discredited, or does the indeterminacy thesis actually establish something narrower than the sweeping claim I have just put in my narrator's mouth? I would rather find that out now than hand an ostensibly expert character a piece of philosophy she gets wrong.
