So my dad had a health scare a few months back and I ended up taking over the day-to-day running of our family's restaurant pretty much overnight. I'm the oldest, I'd been working there on and off for years, and everyone just kind of assumed I'd step in, so I did. Now I'm actually trying to figure out how I'm supposed to run the place, and I keep getting stuck on the same thing. My business school friends all tell me the "correct" way to think about this is like any manager would: set clear, uniform standards for every employee, apply them the same regardless of who the person is, and if my cousin (who's a server) or my aunt (who does the books) can't meet them, thats that. Anything else is just favoritism dressed up as tradition.

But that's not really how my dad ran things, and it wasn't that he was too soft to enforce standards. He'd pick up extra shifts himself so my cousin could deal with family stuff, he ran big decisions by my aunt in a way he'd never do with some random bookkeeper, and when I asked him about it once he just said "she's not just an employee, she's your aunt," like that settled the whole question. He didn't seem confused about the difference between being fair and being family - it was more like he thought the "boss" of a family business is playing a genuinely different role than the boss of a regular business, one that comes with different obligations built in, not just the same job done with a softer heart.

I can't decide if he was onto something real or if he just never examined his own assumptions, and my friends' "same rules for everyone" approach is actually the more defensible default that anything else needs to earn its way out of. Is there an actual case for thinking your obligations to someone can change in kind, and not just in degree, depending on your relationship or role with them - or is my dad's whole approach basically just nepotism with a better story attached to it?
