Ive been chewing on something in epistemic game theory for a few days and the standard explanations aren't really scratching the itch. Aumann's got that famous result that common knowledge of rationality (CKR) in a perfect-information game forces the backward induction outcome -- centipede game is the usual poster child. But backward induction only works by having each player reason about what happens at nodes that would require someone to have already deviated from the "rational" path. If rationality is genuinely common knowledge, those nodes are supposed to be impossible, so what does it even mean for CKR to still hold there, and why doesn't the whole argument just short-circuit the moment you need to reason about an off-path node? Kind of reminds me of the discomfort FDT/UDT folks have with reasoning about counterfactuals where an agent "could have" done something its own decision procedure rules out. Can you give me the gut-level version of how game theorists actually deal with this (if they even agree that they've dealt with it), rather than just restating the epistemic logic formalism?
