Since retiring from a career in condensed-matter physics, I have been filling my mornings with the early modern rationalists, and this week I reached the correspondence between Leibniz and Samuel Clarke. I was struck by a passage in the fourth of Leibniz's letters, where he defends the principle that no two individual things can ever be exactly alike:

"There is no such thing as two individuals indiscernible from each other. An ingenious gentleman of my acquaintance, discoursing with me in the presence of Her Electoral Highness the Princess Sophia, in the garden of Herrenhausen, thought he could find two leaves perfectly alike. The Princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the garden a long time to look for some; but it was to no purpose. Two drops of water, or milk, viewed with a microscope, will appear distinguishable from each other. This is an argument against atoms, which are confuted, as well as a vacuum, by the principles of true metaphysics."

What I cannot work out is what kind of claim Leibniz thinks he is making here. Read one way, this is simply an empirical wager: as a matter of contingent fact, the world happens to be furnished richly enough that no two objects ever coincide in every property, and a sufficiently fine microscope will always turn up some difference. Read another way, the anecdote is only a homely illustration of something Leibniz takes himself to have already proved from the Principle of Sufficient Reason: that God could have had no reason to create two utterly indiscernible things and assign them different places rather than the reverse, so that strict indiscernibility is metaphysically impossible, and not merely unobserved. These seem to me very different kinds of claims, resting on very different sorts of evidence, and I cannot tell which one the leaves and the microscope are meant to support, or whether Leibniz himself is even alive to the difference.

This matters to me because of something that has nagged at me since I first met, in a philosophy of science anthology, Max Black's two spheres: a symmetrical universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron globes, alike in every intrinsic respect and differing, if at all, only in their spatial relations to one another. Black's example is usually presented as a decisive refutation of Leibniz's principle. Yet if the principle was always meant only as the modest empirical claim the garden anecdote suggests, Black's stipulated universe would hardly touch it, since nobody doubts that two qualitatively identical objects are logically possible once you are simply allowed to posit them; it would refute only the far stronger, necessary version that Leibniz thinks he has derived from the Principle of Sufficient Reason. I would be glad of your help sorting out which of these two Leibnizian claims is really at stake in the passage above, and which of them, if either, Black's example actually undermines. Having spent much of my career working with the statistics of identical particles, I would also value knowing whether the "indistinguishability" invoked there bears any real conceptual kinship to Leibniz's "indiscernibility," or whether the shared vocabulary is little more than a coincidence.
