As a retired physicist who has only taken up philosophy seriously since retiring, I spent much of my career thinking about whether space might be infinite and about the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, so I was intrigued to find those cosmological pictures resurfacing in a decision-theoretic argument called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL): the idea that, because a sufficiently large or infinite universe almost certainly contains many agents whose reasoning correlates with our own even though no causal channel connects us to them, we have reason to cooperate with those distant agents -- moderating our own values toward a broader coalition rather than pursuing them fanatically.

Write me a dialogue between two decision theorists who disagree sharply about whether this argument should move anyone. One should defend ECL as a serious extension of the evidential reasoning already at work in Newcomb-style cases, now applied at cosmic scale. The other should press the reference-class problem -- that there is no principled way to say which distant agents reason similarly enough to be evidentially correlated with you -- and should also question whether the argument depends on a contested choice of decision theory and whether its practical upshot is anything beyond the moral caution one would endorse anyway. Have the two respond to each other's specific objections rather than simply restating their opening positions, and let each concede real difficulties where they exist.
