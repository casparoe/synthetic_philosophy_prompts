Quick bit of context: I'm about two-thirds into a novel where one of my leads, Mara — an ex-physics postdoc who cant sleep anymore — spends a night going down a Derek Parfit rabbit hole and comes out the other side actually changed, not just intellectually rattled for a night. She reads the teleporter case from Reasons and Persons (the scanner on Earth destroys the original, an exact replica gets built on Mars) and takes on board what she's sure is Parfit's real point: that strict personal identity isn't "what matters," and that the psychological connection between you-now and you-later is just a matter of degree, fading the further out in time you go. From there she takes what feels to her like the only honest next move — if the "her" in twenty years is barely continuous with today's her, on Parfit's own terms, then commitments made now don't really bind that later person. So she cancels her retirement contributions, quits the cello she swore to her dying teacher she'd keep up, and tells her fiancé Theo that a promise made to a future self who'll barely still be her isn't a promise to anybody in particular.

Theo did a philosophy minor and refuses to just call this extreme and change the subject — he wants to actually beat her on Parfit's own turf, and honestly, so do I, except I'm stuck on what his best move even is. So here's my actual question: once you grant that identity isn't what matters and connectedness comes in degrees, is Mara's slide into "future-me is basically a stranger, so nothing's owed" a fair place for that argument to end up, or is she smuggling in some extra step Parfit himself didn't sign up for? Its been years since grad school and I don't trust my memory of the book enough to write Theo's side without it sounding like I invented "philosophy" for the occasion — would love the real shape of this disagreement, precise enough that I can actually dramatize it instead of hand-waving toward it.
