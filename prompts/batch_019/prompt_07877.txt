I serve on my university's Honor Council, the panel that hears alleged academic integrity cases. This semester I'm also taking a philosophy seminar on collective rationality, where we spent two weeks on List and Pettit's work on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma. Our bylaws state that a panel may only find a violation if it is satisfied on two separate elements: that the prohibited act took place (such as unauthorized collaboration or copying) and that the student had the requisite knowledge that the conduct was prohibited. Panels are otherwise left free to run their deliberations however they like, and last month that gap caught up with us. A three-member panel heard a case involving a shared code repository: one panelist thought both elements were clearly met and voted to find a violation; a second thought the copying occurred but doubted the student knew it was against the rules, and voted no; the third thought what happened didn't really amount to "copying," given how much the code had been altered, but agreed that if it had occurred, the student's knowledge was obvious, and also voted no. Tallied issue by issue, a majority found the act occurred and a separate majority found the requisite knowledge -- which, under the bylaws' own rule, should yield a violation -- yet only one of the three panelists actually voted to find one, so polling the bottom line directly gave the opposite result. The chair adjourned without deciding which result should stand and now wants the procedure fixed before the next hearing.

Since I'm the one who keeps bringing up the seminar readings, the chair has asked me to draft the recommendation memo the Council will vote on at Thursday's meeting. It should recommend whether panels should aggregate votes issue-by-issue and apply the rule mechanically, have panelists vote only on the ultimate verdict, or follow some other procedure, and it needs to justify that choice in terms the rest of the Council -- none of whom have a background in social choice theory -- will find genuinely persuasive rather than just asserting a preference. Please also engage seriously with the strongest objection to whichever procedure you end up recommending: if it's the issue-by-issue approach, several panelists already feel that requiring a panelist to sign off on a bottom-line verdict she personally believes is wrong is hard to square with what it means for the accused student to actually be heard by a panel of peers, and the memo needs a real answer to that, not a dismissal.
