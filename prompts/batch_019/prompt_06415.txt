I'm taking a seminar on Victorian moral philosophy this term, and something a coursemate said after our last class has been bothering me.

I was raised in a religious household but no longer believe in God or an afterlife, though I still think people have decisive reason to act justly even at serious cost to themselves. What I cannot shake is the worry that, if this life really is all there is, there may be no way to show that a perfectly rational, self-interested person who could get away with real wrongdoing is thereby making any kind of error, rather than simply failing to share my values. This worry has led me, half-seriously, to start reading about research into near-death experiences, apparitions, and mediums — phenomena sometimes cited as evidence for survival after death — on the thought that if any of it held up, it might close this gap in a way that moral argument alone has not managed to. A coursemate insists this is simply inconsistent: having given up religious belief for lack of evidence, it is incoherent to go looking for the same reassurance in ghost stories and séances. Is she right that I am being inconsistent, or is my original worry a legitimate one, and if it is, was I wrong to think that this sort of empirical investigation is a sensible way of addressing it?
