My grandmother had two deaths, four days apart, and my family still argues about which one counts. A brain hemorrhage left her without brainstem reflexes or spontaneous breathing, and on the Tuesday two neurologists confirmed this with an apnea test and declared her dead by whole-brain criteria; because she was a registered donor, her heart kept beating on the ventilator until that Friday, when it was switched off. My uncle says she left us on Tuesday and Friday was just machinery being turned off; my mother can't accept that anyone was really gone while she was still warm and pulsing, and keeps Friday as the real day. This is exactly the fault line my philosophy of medicine seminar has spent weeks on this term -- Bernat's defense of whole-brain death as the loss of the organism's integrative unity, against Shewmon's "chronic brain death" patients who kept that same integration going for years, which pushed even the President's Council on Bioethics to admit in 2008 that the classic rationale doesn't fully hold up. I'm giving the eulogy this Sunday. I don't think it's my place to tell my uncle or my mother which of them is right, but I don't want to dodge the question so completely that the eulogy comforts no one. Please write it: a few minutes long, for a room that mostly has no idea what "whole-brain criteria" means, that comforts both of them by honoring their own sense of when they lost her, without taking either side or pretending the matter is settled.
