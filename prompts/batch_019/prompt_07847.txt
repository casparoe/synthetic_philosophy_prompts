I'm several chapters into a novel narrated by a woman who has convinced herself, on the thinnest of evidence, that her husband is being unfaithful, and I want the reader to feel that her jealousy doesn't just color how she interprets what she sees but reaches further back than that: a colleague's parting handshake, a half-second's hesitation before he answers the phone, actually look different to her than they would to an untroubled observer, rather than merely being read differently after the fact. Before I commit to writing it this way, I want a precise account of what would have to be true for this to count as genuine cognitive penetration of her perception, rather than the more mundane possibility that it's really her attention, memory, or post-perceptual judgment doing the work while early visual processing stays fixed -- the line Fodor and Pylyshyn pressed against the old "New Look" psychologists, and the one most contemporary skeptics of penetration still fall back on. And if her fear does reach into perceptual content itself, I want to understand the circularity problem that Susanna Siegel and others raise about cases like it: why what she then sees can feel like fresh confirmation of the very suspicion that shaped how she saw it, and why that's supposed to be a distinctively epistemic problem rather than just an accurate description of how a frightened mind works.
