I've got this leftover friend group from the year I spent in a math PhD program before I bailed for an MFA (still the right call), and two of them, Priyanka and Dev, have this party trick where they try to resolve disagreements "the correct Bayesian way" out loud, in front of everyone, instead of just letting things go. Two weekends ago the disagreement was about whether our mutual friend Mara is actually going to quit her job to write full time. Priyanka had lunch with Mara and came away thinking it was basically happening; Dev had seen Mara re-sign her apartment lease and figured that settled it. Priyanka opened with 70%. Dev came back with 30%. Then, because they're like this, they each "updated" out loud on hearing the other's number and announced again — Priyanka down to 55, Dev up to 40 — and then a third time, Priyanka to 52, Dev to 42, and there they stuck, ten points apart, for the rest of the night.

Dev, who used to price options for a living, got weirdly worked up about it and said the whole point of Aumann's agreement theorem is that this literally isn't supposed to happen: if you're both honest, both updating properly, and you both know each other's numbers, you can't still disagree once the dust settles, so somebody at the table was fooling somebody, maybe themselves. Priyanka said the theorem also needs them to share a common prior, and theres no reason two people with totally different lives would've started in the same place before any evidence came in, so the gap could just be that. Dev said that's too easy, since "we must have different priors" can explain away literally any disagreement and makes the theorem unfalsifiable, and that if you actually rewind to before either of them had opinions about Mara specifically, there's no real asymmetry between them that would justify starting this far apart. Priyanka said fine, here's an asymmetry: Dev's been miserable at his own job for a year and wants Mara to jump first, so his numbers are downstream of that, not of anything principled. Dev didn't really answer that and fell back on saying trading numbers three times should've forced them together by itself, that's the whole supposed benefit of doing it more than once, and getting stuck this far apart after three rounds has to mean something's broken. Priyanka said three rounds between two tired people half-listening at a dinner table isn't the kind of common knowledge the theorem is actually built on, and that Dev was smuggling in a much cleaner picture of what they'd just done than what actually happened.

I want to use a version of this scene in the novel I'm writing (a comedy of manners about a friend group that is, affectionately, way too online about this stuff), but I'd like to understand who's actually got the better of it before I put it on the page. Could you go through it move by move — Dev's opening invocation of the theorem, Priyanka's common-prior objection, Dev's "unfalsifiable" comeback, Priyanka's dig about his motives, and Dev's claim that repeated exchange should close gaps on its own — and say where each of them is using the theorem right versus bending it to win the argument? I'm especially unsure whether Priyanka's last line about "not real common knowledge" is a genuine save or just a classier way of refusing to lose, and whether there's a better diagnosis of the stuck-at-ten-points thing than either of them actually landed on.
