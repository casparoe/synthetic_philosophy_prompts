Write a dialogue between two epistemologists sitting on a bench as the evening air slowly cools — a nod to the sort of gradually shifting case Timothy Williamson uses to run his anti-luminosity argument. One defends the KK principle (knowing that P always puts you in a position to know that you know it); the other, a Williamsonian, presses the argument against it, walking their companion through the sorites-style series on which it depends, where each moment feels indistinguishable from the next in temperature even though the endpoints obviously differ. When the KK-defender objects that knowledge is too sharp and all-or-nothing a state to admit of the same kind of borderline case as a fading sensation of warmth, the Williamsonian must explain why exactly the same margin-for-error reasoning still threatens KK. Neither speaker should simply restate an opening position; each must respond to the specific difficulty the other has just raised.
