I'm at work on a novel whose chapters are each headed by a short epigraph, written as though it were a genuine fragment from some historical thinker, commenting obliquely on events that thinker never lived to see. The chapter now in front of me follows a sovereign-bond trader through the years after 2008, watching central banks across the developed world hold policy rates at or below zero for years at a stretch -- in Japan and the eurozone, the better part of a decade -- while simultaneously multiplying the money supply through quantitative easing: on its face, just the crude "more money, lower interest" story that Hume's own contemporaries argued fiercely about. The epigraph has to be Hume's, and it has to be earned: reasoned from what he actually argued about money, interest, and public credit in the Political Discourses, not from a modern economist's opinion dressed up in period spelling. Write it as a few sentences in his voice, reaching a considered verdict rather than a hedge, on whether this decade of near-free money vindicates his own account of what really governs the price of credit, or quietly embarrasses it.
