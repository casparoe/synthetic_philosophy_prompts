A close friend from my philosophy graduate program recently asked me to officiate her wedding this spring. She married young, at twenty-three, in a ceremony full of certainty that dissolved within three years, and has told me privately that she does not want vows this time that simply repeat the old promises in a more hopeful key. Two years ago, in a seminar on the philosophy of promising, I worked through the main rival accounts of why a promise obligates at all -- Hume's claim that the bond is an artificial convention we have all tacitly bought into, Scanlon's attempt to ground fidelity in what we owe to each other as reasoners, and Shiffrin's argument that treating the marriage vow as mere convention gets the underlying moral relationship backwards -- and never settled which view I actually believe. I refuse to stand in front of her family and pretend the question is simple, but I also refuse to turn her wedding into a defense of a term paper. Please write the remarks I will deliver as officiant: they must stake out one genuine, defensible position on why this promise can bind in a way the first one did not, expressed so that her family feels its force without ever having sat through that seminar themselves.
