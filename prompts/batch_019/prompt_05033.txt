The lottery paradox (Kyburg) and the preface paradox (Makinson) are often presented as two instances of the same underlying puzzle: a set of propositions that are each individually justified, even though their conjunction is justifiably denied. Please argue against treating them as structurally the same. Specifically, defend the claim that there is a genuine epistemic asymmetry between the two cases: an author can know, or be justified in believing, each claim in a book, even while being justified in believing that the book contains at least one error, whereas no comparable justification attaches to the belief that a particular lottery ticket will lose, however favorable the odds. Your argument should identify what grounds this asymmetry, and should explain why merely rejecting the closure of justified belief (or knowledge) under conjunction does not, on its own, vindicate giving the two paradoxes a unified solution.
