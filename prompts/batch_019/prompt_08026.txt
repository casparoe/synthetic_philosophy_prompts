Consider the following argument.

(2) Cruelty to animals is wrong.

(a) (8 points) Explain why this argument generates a problem for expressivist metaethical theories in particular, and why the same argument is unproblematic for descriptivist theories.

Below is the student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted apart from two typographical corrections:

"Expressivism denies that first-order moral sentences such as 'Cruelty to animals is wrong' function to describe or represent moral facts. Instead, following the emotivist tradition of Ayer and Stevenson and its more sophisticated descendants, the expressivist holds that to sincerely utter such a sentence is to express a certain non-cognitive attitude — for instance, disapproval of cruelty to animals — rather than to state a belief that is true or false in the ordinary sense.

The trouble is that (1)–(3) is a straightforwardly valid argument, valid in virtue of its logical form as an instance of modus ponens, and its validity seems to require that the sentence 'Cruelty to animals is wrong' mean exactly the same thing wherever it occurs: as the antecedent of the conditional in (1), and as the freestanding assertion in (2). This is essentially Frege's point, later pressed against non-cognitivist theories of ethics by Peter Geach in his 1965 paper 'Assertion': the content of a sentence is independent of whether it is asserted, and an adequate semantic theory must assign that content compositionally, in the same way, whether the sentence stands alone or is embedded in a larger unasserted context. For a descriptivist this is unproblematic, since 'cruelty to animals is wrong' expresses the same proposition, with the same truth-condition, in both places, and modus ponens is valid because it is truth-preserving. The expressivist, by contrast, seems committed to saying that in (2) the speaker expresses disapproval of cruelty to animals, while in the antecedent of (1) the speaker plainly expresses no such disapproval: one can assert a conditional without disapproving of, or even having any view about, its antecedent. On the expressivist's picture, moreover, moral sentences lack truth-conditions in the first place, so validity cannot simply be explained as the preservation of truth from premises to conclusion. The expressivist therefore owes an account of what 'cruelty to animals is wrong' means in (1) that is, at once, the very meaning it has in (2), compatible with the fact that asserting (1) does not commit the speaker to disapproving of cruelty, and capable of explaining why (1)–(3) is valid at all.

The most influential response is Simon Blackburn's, developed under the label of quasi-realism. Rather than making do with simple attitudes like disapproval, Blackburn constructs a whole logic of attitudes, on which complex sentences express higher-order attitudes directed at combinations of more basic ones. Using the Hooray!/Boo! notation that Gibbard introduces to formalize this logic of attitudes, asserting the conditional in (1) expresses not disapproval of cruelty as such, but something like disapproval of the combination of tolerating cruelty to animals while not tolerating teaching a child to be cruel to animals. Since this same higher-order attitude is in play whether or not one goes on to accept the antecedent, it can be held fixed across both premises, and the inconsistency of accepting (1) and (2) while rejecting (3) is explained as a practical or attitudinal inconsistency — a combination of attitudes that nobody could coherently hold — rather than as a matter of the premises and the negation of the conclusion being jointly untrue.

A serious problem facing this strategy, however, is close kin to the very problem it was designed to solve. As Mark Schroeder shows in setting out what he calls the Frege-Geach problem, expressivists cannot easily explain why 'cruelty to animals is not wrong' is inconsistent with 'cruelty to animals is wrong', since not being for an act is not the same attitude as being for not doing that act. The quasi-realist has no principled way to manufacture a distinct negative attitude for every logically complex sentence without simply stipulating a new sui generis attitude on each occasion, which threatens to turn the logic of attitudes into an unprincipled ad hoc construction rather than a genuine compositional semantics."
