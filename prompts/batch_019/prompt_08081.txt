In my formal epistemology seminar, two students, Mara and Deshawn, have been going back and forth over Jeffrey conditionalization's failure to commute, and I would value an outside verdict on their exchange.

The exercise that set them off was this: take propositions E and F under a shared prior on which they are positively correlated (P(E&F) = 0.4, P(E&¬F) = P(¬E&F) = 0.1, P(¬E&¬F) = 0.4, so each starts at credence 0.5). Jeffrey-conditionalize first on soft evidence that shifts credence in E to 0.9, then on soft evidence that shifts credence in F to 0.9, and you land at (E, F) ≈ (0.945, 0.9): F sits right at the value it was directly set to, but E has drifted above the 0.9 it was assigned earlier, because the later, correlated update on F bleeds back into it. Do the same two updates in the reverse order and you get (E, F) ≈ (0.9, 0.945) instead.

(1) Mara says this shows Jeffrey conditionalization is defective in a way that matters even short of a Dutch book: strict conditionalization on certain evidence never depends on the order in which two items of evidence arrive, so a rule under which an agent's final opinion can be steered just by reordering "equally strong" testimony is exhibiting a kind of incoherence.

(2) Deshawn answers that this rests on a confusion about individuating evidence: the 0.9 fed in on the second update is not the same evidential input as the 0.9 fed in on the first, since it is now conditional on whatever else has been absorbed in between; there is nothing incoherent about any single updating sequence, only in demanding that two different sequences agree.

(3) Mara replies that this concedes her point rather than answering it: if the rule cannot say what a fixed pair of testimonies should add up to independently of the order in which they arrive, then Jeffrey conditionalization is simply silent on a question strict conditionalization answers determinately, and "there's no fact of the matter here" is a cost dressed up as an insight.

(4) Deshawn responds that strict conditionalization's order-independence is bought cheaply: learning something with certainty screens off exactly the context-sensitivity at issue, so demanding that same order-independence once evidence is merely uncertain smuggles back in a picture of evidence as discrete, context-free packets — the very picture Jeffrey's framework was built to reject.

Please assess the exchange move by move rather than declaring an overall winner, saying who has the better of it at each stage, and close by flagging what, if anything, you think both of them are still missing.
