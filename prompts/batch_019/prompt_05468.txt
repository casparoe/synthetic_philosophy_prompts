In the graduate seminar on philosophy of mathematics I am currently teaching, we have reached the point in the syllabus where students tend to assume that, having learned that intuitionists reject the law of excluded middle (LEM) and read the logical connectives along Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov lines, they now understand the limits of what a constructivist can make use of. I would like to disabuse them of this using a genuinely surprising result, due independently to Diaconescu and to Goodman and Myhill: in an otherwise constructive set theory such as IZF, the full, unrestricted axiom of choice is on its own sufficient to derive LEM. Most students find this bizarre on first hearing it, since choice looks like the most constructively flavored axiom on offer — it merely asserts that one can make an explicit selection from a family of nonempty sets, which sounds like exactly the sort of "produce the object" demand a constructivist ought to welcome, rather than a covert way of reintroducing classical logic.

For next week's session, I would like your help drafting a handout that does two things. First, give a fully explicit example of the underlying construction: for an arbitrary, undecided proposition P, specify the actual sets or family involved, state exactly where and how the choice principle is invoked, and spell out, step by step, the case analysis by which one extracts a proof of P ∨ ¬P from the resulting choice function. I want the actual objects and the actual case split laid out clearly enough that a first-year graduate student could follow every step, not a gesture at the existence of "some slick argument." Second, address a question on which two of my colleagues in the mathematics department currently disagree: is the axiom of choice itself really the source of the non-constructivity here, or is it more accurately diagnosed as a symptom of allowing unrestricted separation (forming a set from an arbitrary, possibly undecidable predicate) in the background theory? Bring in how a predicative constructive set theory such as CZF, which restricts separation to bounded formulas while still validating choice-like principles without collapsing into LEM, bears on this diagnosis — and then tell me whether, in light of all this, you regard the slogan "the axiom of choice is intuitionistically suspect" as fair, or as somewhat misleading.
