My philosophy elective this term is taught by a teacher who also teaches AP Computer Science, and she built a unit around Scott Aaronson's essay arguing that computational complexity theory — not just computability — has real philosophical stakes. As homework, she assigned this question: "Explain, in your own words, what the P versus NP question asks. Then describe two specific ways this distinction has been argued to matter beyond computer science itself — for instance, for the nature of mathematical creativity, for theories of rational belief, or for debates about the mind — giving a concrete example for each." Before she grades our essays herself, she has us peer-grade a partner's answer and hand in written comments by tomorrow.

Here is my partner's answer in full:

"The P versus NP problem asks whether every problem whose solution can be checked quickly (in polynomial time) can also be solved quickly (in polynomial time) — in other words, whether the class P of efficiently solvable problems is really the same as the class NP of efficiently checkable ones. Almost all computer scientists believe P and NP are different, meaning that at least some problems are inherently harder to solve than to check.

One place this matters is the philosophy of mathematics. If P turned out to equal NP, finding a proof would be no harder than checking one someone else already wrote out, since checking a proof is a polynomial-time task and, by hypothesis, so would finding it be. Gödel raised almost exactly this worry in a 1956 letter to von Neumann, asking whether mathematicians could someday be replaced by machines. Mathematical creativity would then look less like a special kind of insight and more like a search process that simply happens to be fast.

A second place is epistemology. Ideal rational agents are usually modeled as believing every logical consequence of what they already believe, but no realistic, resource-limited agent can do this, since even deciding whether a formula in ordinary propositional logic is satisfiable is already NP-complete in the worst case. Because no finite mind can do this instantly, complexity theory shows that bounded rationality, and not logical omniscience, is the correct normative standard for judging real believers.

A third, related example is cryptography: RSA encryption is only secure because factoring the large numbers involved is NP-complete, so ordinary computers cannot crack it in a reasonable amount of time — though a sufficiently large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve this NP-complete problem efficiently and break RSA."
