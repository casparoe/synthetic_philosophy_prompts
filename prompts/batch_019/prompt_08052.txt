I've been asked to give this year's commencement address at Ashworth, a small conservatory that trains musicians, actors, painters, and sculptors together, and I'd like to build the whole thing around a text I've been living inside for a magazine piece on late Tolstoy: his 1897–98 treatise "What Is Art?" Most people who invoke Tolstoy at a graduation, if they invoke him at all, reach for the moral crisis, the beard, and the giving away of the estate; I'd rather use the actual argument. Tolstoy insists that the value of art has nothing to do with beauty or technical skill and everything to do with "infection" — whether the artist genuinely felt something and managed, through line, sound, or word, to make an audience feel it too, in a way that binds people together rather than flattering one class's private tastes. Applied without mercy, that standard forces him to rank most of his own novels, including War and Peace and Anna Karenina, below a couple of his own plain moral tales, and to treat a full rehearsal of Wagner's Siegfried that he sat through, and Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, as prime specimens of fashionable fraud.

The graduating class is about two hundred students across every discipline the school teaches, and the audience will also include parents who have never heard of the essay, sitting alongside faculty and a few visiting critics who will recognize every move I make and know exactly how punishing Tolstoy's own criteria are. I don't want a version of this speech that quietly files him under "make art from the heart" and moves on, since that erases the part that actually matters: by his own lights, a great deal of what is about to go up in these students' senior shows and recitals — virtuosity, irony, deliberate difficulty — would count as a symptom of bad, class-bound art rather than an achievement. At the same time, I don't want two hundred new graduates walking out convinced their training was wasted. Could you draft roughly ten minutes of remarks that actually let Tolstoy's argument decide what can and cannot honestly be said here, rather than gesturing at him and then defaulting to something safer, and that finds room, somewhere in there, to sit with the sheer strangeness of a novelist using his own indisputable masterpieces as evidence against himself?
