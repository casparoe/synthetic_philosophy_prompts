I spent my career as an experimental physicist and only took up philosophy seriously in retirement, which is how I've ended up as the resident scientist on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, currently stuck on a case. A patient in his sixties, himself a retired theoretical physicist, survived a cardiac arrest and is refusing the implantable defibrillator his cardiologist recommends. He has spent years arguing in print that the many-worlds interpretation, via the quantum suicide thought experiment, makes 'quantum immortality' rational, and says that because he will find himself alive regardless of what happens in this branch, declining the device costs him nothing. He is calm, coherent, and shows no sign of depression on any measure we've used. Half the committee wants to find him incapacitated, since his appreciation of the consequences seems distorted by a false physical belief; the other half answers that we would never reason this way about a patient who invoked an afterlife instead of a wavefunction, since capacity is supposed to turn on the coherence of a patient's reasoning, not the truth of its premises. Please draft the memo I will bring to Thursday's meeting: a specific recommendation on his capacity and ongoing care, reasoning that genuinely engages what quantum immortality does and does not imply, and a fair statement of the strongest case for overriding him.
