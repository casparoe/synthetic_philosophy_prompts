So I fell down a legal philosophy rabbit hole this week after a friend in law school sent me the whole Hart vs Fuller thing, specifically that "grudge informer" case: a woman in Nazi Germany reports her husband to the authorities over some anti-Hitler remark he made while home on leave, he gets sentenced to death for it (I think that got commuted to being sent to the front, but don't hold me to the details, I read this fast), and then after the war a German court turns around and convicts HER for what she did. The puzzle is basically whether the Nazi statute she used against him counted as valid law at the time or not. Hart's move, from what I can tell, is to bite the bullet and say yes, it was valid law back then, so we're punishing her retroactively for something that was technically legal, and we should just be upfront about that instead of pretending the statute was never really "law" to begin with. Fullers response is that a legal system rotten enough -- secret rules, retroactive changes, trials that are basically theater -- eventually stops being a legal system in any real sense, so calling that statute "not law" isn't a dodge, it's just correct, no retroactivity problem needed at all.

Doesn't Hart's old objection just win here -- that a well run protection racket could satisfy every single one of Fuller's eight principles of the "inner morality of law" too (clear rules, no retroactive changes, consistently enforced, etc.) and that tells you nothing about whether the content of the rules is remotely decent?
