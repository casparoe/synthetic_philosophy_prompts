"Skolem's Paradox arises because ZFC, as a first-order theory in a countable language, falls under the scope of the Compactness Theorem, which entails that if ZFC has an infinite model at all, it has models of every infinite cardinality, including countable ones. Let M be such a countable model. Since ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, M satisfies the sentence 'P(ω) is uncountable' for its own internal power set of ω, call it P(ω)^M. Yet M itself, considered as a set from the standpoint of the background theory in which we are working, has only countably many elements. This looks contradictory: how can a countable structure contain a set that is uncountable?

The resolution turns on the fact that M is a faithful model: because ZFC proves Cantor's theorem and M satisfies ZFC, whatever M calls its power set of ω genuinely fails to admit a bijection with ω, both internally and from the standpoint of the ambient set theory. What M's own countability shows is only that M's domain as a whole can be enumerated; it does not show that any particular set belonging to M, such as P(ω)^M, can be. So 'M is countable' and 'P(ω)^M is uncountable' simply concern different things — the whole domain versus one item within it — and there is no real tension between them; Cantor's theorem holds intact at every level, inside the model and out.

Skolem himself did not think any of this pointed to a technical flaw in ZFC, but he did draw a strongly relativistic moral from it: he argued that notions like 'uncountable' and 'well-ordered' have no absolute meaning apart from a choice of model, and that no axiomatization could ever pin down a single, intended universe of sets. Most later commentators have agreed with him on this point, which is why contemporary set theorists generally treat talk of 'the' universe of sets as elliptical for talk about some model or other, rather than as referring to a unique absolute totality. I think this is the right conclusion to draw: since the paradox shows that cardinality is never more than model-relative, insisting on an absolute, non-relative universe of sets would be to insist on something that outstrips anything our axioms could ever express."
