I spent thirty-five years as a field biologist studying sexual selection in birds (the evolution of costly ornaments and displays), and only since retiring have I had time to read the philosophical literature that grew up around the handicap principle, including the later debates over what exactly has to be true of a cost for it to keep a signal honest. That reading has left me uneasy about a long-standing practice of the committee I now sit on, which selects recipients of a small foundation's early-career fellowship in field biology: finalists must complete an unpaid six-week field placement, entirely at their own expense, before an offer is made. Long-serving members defend the placement in exactly the terms Zahavi used for costly signals: only candidates who are genuinely serious and able would pay that cost, so it reliably screens out the weaker ones. Newer members see nothing but a filter that rewards family money and free time, and want it scrapped. We vote in three weeks. Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee beforehand: it should recommend one specific option (keep, modify, or drop the placement) and engage seriously with the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't take, rather than the version that is easiest to dismiss.
