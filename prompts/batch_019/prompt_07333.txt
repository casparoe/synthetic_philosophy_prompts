So I spent my career as a comparative physiologist -- mostly crustacean and fish nervous systems -- and now that I'm retired I've ended up on my university's IACUC, which has turned out to be a bigger philosophy lesson than I expected. Next week's meeting has a protocol proposing comparative nociception-and-learning tests on octopuses and fruit flies, including noxious-stimulus trials with no anaesthetic, since anaesthetic would wreck the very learning effect being tested. Neither species is covered by the Animal Welfare Act the way a rabbit would be, so on paper we could just wave it through with minimal review. But since retiring I've read up on what actually counts as evidence of sentience, and its a genuine mixed bag: the octopus case for integrated nociception and motivational trade-offs looks solid, the fly's associative learning is arguably just as sophisticated, but the pain-affect evidence for the fly is much murkier. Two committee members want to hold both species to our full vertebrate standard 'to be safe'; one calls that absurd overreach for an insect and says it'll just push the work off-campus, unregulated. Could you draft the memo I'll bring Thursday recommending what we should actually require for each species and why, in a way that answers the overreach worry rather than just asserting precaution?
