I'm midway through a legal thriller whose plot turns on two cases reaching the same appellate court in the same term. In the first, a woman is convicted of attempted murder after the rifle she'd carefully sighted on her husband jams at the trigger — sabotage by a third party she never learns about — while every other fact of the case (premeditation, motive, the steps she took to ensure success) is identical to what it would have been had the gun fired. In the second, an unrelated defendant is convicted of completed murder under materially the same circumstances of planning and malice. My presiding judge character has spent the novel building a reputation as an unapologetic retributivist, someone who insists that punishment is justified by desert and by desert alone, and she now has to write the opinion explaining why the two defendants before her received such different sentences. I want her reasoning to be properly rigorous rather than something I hand-wave for the plot's convenience, so I'd like help thinking the philosophy through before I put words in her mouth.

Specifically, I'd like you to argue that a consistent retributivist — one who holds that deserved punishment tracks the quality of the agent's will and choice rather than the harm that happens to follow from it — is thereby committed to punishing the failed attempt exactly as severely as the completed killing, and must treat any gap between the two sentences as a concession to luck rather than to justice. I've read enough of the moral-luck literature descending from Nagel and Williams to know the objection in outline, especially what Nagel calls resultant luck — luck in how things turn out — but most treatments I've come across stop at toy cases (the reckless driver who does or doesn't hit a pedestrian) rather than engaging with how a retributivist might actually resist the conclusion inside a real sentencing scheme: for instance, by insisting that the harm actually done is itself partly constitutive of what the agent deserves, not merely evidence of her culpability, or that the completed act individuates the crime in a way the failed one cannot. I'd like the argument to take that rejoinder seriously and show exactly where it fails, or where it only succeeds by quietly importing consequentialist considerations the retributivist was supposed to have foresworn, rather than just asserting that retributivism "has a problem with luck" and leaving it there.
