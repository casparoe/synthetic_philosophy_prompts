During my working life I was a condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been trying to fill some gaps in my education by reading philosophy of science -- at the moment, on the question of reduction and emergence as it arises for phase transitions. I mentioned some of this to an old colleague over coffee last week, and we found we disagreed about how much weight to put on a point that recurs throughout the literature following Batterman's *The Devil in the Details*: that a phase transition, understood strictly as a genuine non-analyticity in the free energy (a jump in an order parameter, a divergence in a susceptibility, and so on), only ever appears in the thermodynamic limit N → ∞, since for any finite N the partition function is a finite sum of analytic terms and is therefore analytic everywhere itself. Writers in this tradition seem to want to conclude that phase transitions are therefore not merely practically but in some deeper sense irreducible to the statistical mechanics of finite assemblies of particles -- that the passage to infinity is doing indispensable explanatory work, rather than serving as a mathematically convenient stand-in for "very large but finite."

What I cannot get past, as someone trained to treat infinite limits as bookkeeping devices rather than metaphysically loaded moves, is why the singular character of this particular limit is supposed to establish something stronger than the rather mundane fact that some derivations are only tractable asymptotically. This bears directly, I think, on how one should read the renormalization group's account of universality: if RG methods let us explain, from quite general considerations about fixed points and the flow of relevant and irrelevant couplings, why wildly different microscopic Hamiltonians end up sharing the same critical exponents, doesn't that count as exactly the sort of systematic derivation that should count in reduction's favour rather than emergence's? I understand that some philosophers -- Butterfield among them, if I have this right -- have tried to split the difference by arguing that finite systems already display a weaker, but still perfectly genuine, precursor of the transition well before the limit is reached, so that reduction and emergence needn't be rivals here at all. I would be grateful for an explanation, pitched below the level of slogans like "more is different," of exactly where the substantive disagreement lies: what, precisely, would have to be true for the singular limit to license real ontological emergence rather than mere epistemic or computational difficulty, and whether the renormalization group's explanatory success should be read as settling the matter in reduction's favour, or as somehow orthogonal to the dispute.
