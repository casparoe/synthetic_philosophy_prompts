I have been asked to give the commencement address at a journalism school whose ethics sequence centers on two of Hannah Arendt's essays, "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics: Reflections on the Pentagon Papers" — specifically her claim that reporters, like historians and judges, matter to a democracy because they stand outside the arena of partisan political action rather than take part in it. Half the audience will be faculty and alumni who know those essays well; the rest will be the graduates' families, who mainly want to hear that their children are headed off to do something brave and important. My instinct is to tell the graduates that they are about to become warriors for truth who will fight power and change the world, but that is close to the self-image Arendt's argument says they cannot honestly claim. Write the speech so that it moves the families without misrepresenting her position to the specialists in the room.
