I am several chapters into a historical novel set during Spinoza's final winter in The Hague, and I have reached a scene I cannot yet write with real conviction. In it, Spinoza dictates last revisions to the Tractatus Politicus (his Political Treatise) to the small circle of friends who would later see it into print, and I want to dramatize the exact point, in early 1677, at which the real manuscript breaks off: only a few paragraphs into the chapter on democracy, and only a sentence or two after Spinoza has argued — on the grounds of nature rather than mere custom or law — that women are unfit for full citizenship. The work, and his life, end there, with whatever else he meant to say on the matter never written down.

The conceit of my novel is that a few further sentences were in fact spoken and then lost, and I would like your help drafting them: two or three paragraphs, written in the first person as Spinoza, that continue the argument using his own actual conceptual resources — conatus and potentia, his identification of natural right with power, and the imperium/multitudo relationship he develops across the rest of the Political Treatise and the Ethics — rather than simply having him recant in a way a modern reader would find comfortable. I want a passage that a careful Spinoza scholar would recognize as genuinely consistent with his system, whether it ends up reinforcing the exclusion or quietly working against it.

Once you have drafted that passage, I would like you to step outside the fiction entirely and answer a harder question in your own voice. Every state Spinoza would today recognize as a functioning democracy grants full political standing to women, to wage laborers, and to people who own no property of their own — precisely the categories his surviving political writings treat as naturally dependent or otherwise disqualified. Taking his mature theory on its own terms, rather than the more general defense of democracy as the most natural form of government that he offers earlier in the Theological-Political Treatise, do you think the logic of potentia and natural right he develops in the Political Treatise ultimately supports this later expansion of the franchise, or does it structurally resist it in ways that his modern admirers tend to quietly pass over? I would rather have your best considered judgment, argued from the texts, than an even-handed summary of both sides.
