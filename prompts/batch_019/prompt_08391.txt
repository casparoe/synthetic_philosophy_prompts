Question. Let P be an arbitrary proposition.
(a) [10 pts] State the BHK (Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov) clauses for ∧, ∨, →, and ¬, and use them to explain why intuitionistic logic does not accept P ∨ ¬P as a theorem for arbitrary P.
(b) [12 pts] Despite (a), ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P) is a theorem of intuitionistic propositional logic. Give a natural-deduction proof of this fact, and comment on how it relates to Glivenko's theorem.
(c) [8 pts] A classmate claims: "Intuitionists don't just withhold judgment on P ∨ ¬P — for suitable P they go further and assert its negation, ¬(P ∨ ¬P)." Is the classmate right? Defend your answer, referring to a standard example of an undecided proposition.

Submitted answer.

(a) On the BHK reading, a proof of A∧B is a pair of proofs, one of A and one of B. A proof of A∨B is a proof of A tagged as the left disjunct, or a proof of B tagged as the right disjunct — to prove a disjunction you must actually possess a proof of one side, not merely know that at least one side is provable in principle. A proof of A→B is a method that converts any proof of A into a proof of B. Negation is defined via ⊥: ¬A abbreviates A→⊥, so a proof of ¬A is a method turning any hypothetical proof of A into a proof of the absurd. Given this, asserting P∨¬P for arbitrary P would require, for every P, either a proof of P or a refutation of P in hand. For open conjectures we currently have neither, and there is no general algorithm that decides arbitrary mathematical statements, so intuitionistic logic refuses to include P∨¬P as a schematic theorem — doing so would presuppose a universal decision procedure that plainly doesn't exist.

(b) Proof. Assume ¬(P∨¬P) (call this H). Assume further P; then P∨¬P by ∨-introduction, contradicting H, so ⊥. Discharging the inner assumption gives ¬P. But then P∨¬P again, via the right disjunct, again contradicting H, so ⊥. Discharging H itself, we obtain ¬¬(P∨¬P), as required. This is exactly what Glivenko's theorem leads us to expect: a formula A is a classical theorem iff ¬¬A is an intuitionistic theorem, and since P∨¬P is a classical tautology, Glivenko hands us ¬¬(P∨¬P) intuitionistically for free. Glivenko's result generalizes cleanly from propositional to full first-order logic, so the same double-negation trick embeds all of classical predicate logic inside intuitionistic predicate logic: a first-order sentence is classically provable exactly when its double negation is intuitionistically provable. That's actually the underlying reason a system like Coq, whose core logic is intuitionistic, can still be used to verify ordinary classical mathematics — you double-negate your way in.

(c) I think the classmate is basically right. Since intuitionistic logic is obtained from classical logic by dropping P∨¬P as a blanket axiom, whenever we lack a decision procedure for a particular P, the intuitionist isn't merely agnostic about it — she treats P∨¬P as false, i.e., she's entitled to assert ¬(P∨¬P). Brouwer's own examples make the point vividly: he considered properties of the decimal expansion of π that were undecided in his day, and used them as weak counterexamples precisely by asserting the negation of excluded middle for that instance. So for a genuinely open P, excluded middle isn't just something intuitionistic logic fails to prove — it's something intuitionistic logic actively refutes, which is what makes it a real rival to classical logic rather than merely a fragment of it.
