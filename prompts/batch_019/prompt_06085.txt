I work as a quantitative trader at a small firm that runs automated strategies across several prediction-market exchanges, and about a month ago one of our algorithms was quietly turned into a genuine money pump. A rival desk noticed that our pricing across three correlated event contracts was very slightly cyclical — effectively contract A was priced above B, B above C, and C above A once fees were netted out — and for several days they extracted a small, steady profit by cycling us through all three before anyone caught it. Since then I've been reading around the decision-theory literature on money pumps to figure out what to recommend to the team, and I've gotten pulled into a debate that goes well beyond what I expected to find when I started.

The textbook fix is to force the algorithm's priced valuations to be complete and transitive so the pump can never get started in the first place. But a couple of our researchers want to preserve certain deliberate incompletenesses in the model — cases where it genuinely has no considered view about which of two contracts is more attractive, rather than manufacturing an artificial tie-break, on the grounds that forcing a complete ranking out of genuine uncertainty is itself a source of exploitable error. Digging into this, I found that Edward McClennen's notion of a "resolute" chooser — one who commits in advance to a plan and sticks to it even when a later step looks locally tempting — is supposed to let an agent avoid this kind of exploitation without ever needing fully transitive or complete preferences. But I also ran across references (I think tracing back to Wlodek Rabinowicz, and picked up again in a recent book-length treatment by Johan Gustafsson) to more elaborate "money pump with foresight" constructions that are supposed to show that even a resolute, fully foresighted chooser with incomplete or locally cyclical preferences can still be pumped — which, if right, would mean our researchers' proposal is a dead end no matter how cleverly the commitment policy is designed.

Before I bring a recommendation to the team, I want to see the strongest possible case for the researchers' side. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, for the position that diachronic rationality — immunity to exploitation across a sequence of choices — does not require an agent's preferences to be complete and transitive at every point in time: that a resolute chooser can maintain genuine incompleteness (and even certain local cycles) indefinitely without ever being maneuvered into a sure loss, and that foresight-based money-pump constructions don't actually succeed in closing this loophole. I'd like you to engage directly with how a foresight-based pump is supposed to work, rather than simply asserting that it fails, and to close by flagging what you take to be the single weakest link in your own argument, since that's exactly where I expect our skeptics to push back.
