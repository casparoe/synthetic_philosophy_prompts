question: "Explain Aquinas's account of the different kinds of tyranny and his view on whether private individuals may licitly kill a tyrant. Your answer should address why, despite calling tyranny the worst form of government, Aquinas is notably cautious about endorsing tyrannicide in the De Regno."

sample answer: "For Aquinas, a government is tyrannical when the ruler governs for his own private advantage rather than the common good, which is why he ranks tyranny as the worst regime: an oligarchy still serves the good of several people, and even a corrupt democracy serves the advantage of many, but in a tyranny only one man's interest is served, so it's the most complete departure from the common good possible. He also distinguishes two kinds of tyrant: a tyrant by title (a usurper with no legitimate claim at all) and a tyrant by exercise (someone legitimately installed who then governs unjustly). Subjects owe a usurper no obedience whatsoever since he never had any authority to begin with, while a legitimate-but-unjust ruler should be disobeyed only in his specific unjust commands, and removed, if at all, by the public authority that installed him rather than by private citizens acting alone.

What's interesting is that in the De Regno Aquinas is way more cautious than you'd expect. He warns that letting private individuals decide for themselves who counts as a tyrant is dangerous, since ambitious men could just use 'tyrant' as an excuse to grab power themselves, and he backs this up with Aristotle's Politics, which already showed that killing a tyrant usually just clears the way for a worse one to take over. His actual solution ends up being pretty close to Locke's in the Second Treatise: ultimate authority rests with the people, so a well-built regime should have constitutional checks that let the community restrain or remove a bad ruler through orderly, public means instead of assassination."
