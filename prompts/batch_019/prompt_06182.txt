I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and for a graduate seminar on the intellectual prehistory of existential risk thinking I've been assigned von Neumann as my "founding figure," working mainly from his 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" together with Stanislaw Ulam's later tribute, which recounts von Neumann remarking that technological change seemed to be approaching some "essential singularity" beyond which human affairs could not continue in their present form. Reading the two side by side, I've come to think that von Neumann's singularity idea was fundamentally an optimistic wager: because technological change is self-accelerating, it will eventually produce machines and automated systems sophisticated enough to manage the very dangers -- nuclear weapons, unchecked industrial growth, deliberate climate modification -- that cruder, earlier technology had created. On this reading, "surviving technology" mostly amounts to getting to the singularity intact, since the singularity is the point at which the risks essentially take care of themselves.

I'd like to build my seminar paper around this framing, presenting von Neumann as an ancestor of today's more optimistic voices in AI safety debates rather than of the gloomier ones. Before I commit to it, I'd appreciate help thinking through the textual support: which specific arguments in the Fortune essay show von Neumann treating more advanced technology itself, rather than political judgment or institutional restraint, as what ultimately gets humanity through the predicament? And do you know of any point where he squares this optimism with the noticeably more fatalistic tone of the essay's concluding pages, where he seems to deny that any once-and-for-all solution is available at all?
