I have been thinking about a puzzle that arises in recent work on acausal reasoning and threats, and I would like your help staging it as a dialogue. The scenario: an agent that fears it might be threatened acausally—blackmailed, say, by a predictor, a simulator, or some distant agent whose reasoning is correlated with its own—over an outcome it deeply disvalues (widespread suffering, for instance) can try to protect itself by adopting a "surrogate goal." It commits in advance to concede whenever this otherwise-trivial substitute is threatened, and never to concede when the real target is threatened. If the arrangement is credible, a rational threatener gains nothing by targeting what the agent actually cares about and everything by targeting the surrogate, so threats migrate onto something harmless.

Write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated decision theorists, set just after a workshop talk that proposed equipping advanced AI systems with surrogate goals of this kind. Both reject simply capitulating to threats, but they disagree sharply about whether the strategy is coherent. One should defend it as a stable extension of precommitment reasoning already considered rational in Newcomb-style cases. The other should press the objection that manufacturing and defending a goal one knows to be fake is disanalogous to ordinary precommitment, that the strategy is unstable once the agent reflects on the goal's arbitrariness, and that it invites a regress in which a sufficiently sophisticated threatener simply targets whatever the agent currently treats as response-worthy. Have each side engage the other's strongest move directly, and do not let either side win too easily.
