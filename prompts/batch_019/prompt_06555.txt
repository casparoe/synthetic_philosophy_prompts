For my school's political philosophy elective, we each have to write a short paper that closely engages one primary-source argument and then pushes on it ourselves rather than just summarizing it, and I picked the section of Aristotle's Politics (Book I) where he separates household management from what he calls "the art of acquisition" (chrematistike). I landed on this partly because our teacher brought it up almost as an aside during a class debate about whether payday lenders are doing anything morally different from ordinary banks. I think I have a solid reconstruction of what Aristotle actually argues, but the essay's whole payoff depends on a connection to a different text that I'm honestly not sure is legitimate, and I'd like a second pair of eyes before I turn it in on Friday.

Here is the core of my draft so far. Aristotle treats acquiring wealth within a household as natural and self-limiting: you only need so much grain, so many sandals, so much wool, and once the household has enough, the activity has done its job. Trade between households, once it moves past simple barter and starts running through money, changes character, because money, unlike grain, doesn't spoil and there's no natural ceiling on how much of it is enough, so buying and selling for profit loses the built-in stopping point that farming or herding has. Aristotle treats usury as the purest, most "unnatural" version of this, and even gets a small pun out of it: the Greek word for interest, tokos, also means offspring or birth, and his complaint seems to be that interest is money bred directly into more money, with no actual good — a house, a coat — changing hands in between, the way there would be in ordinary trade. What I want to add is that this isn't only an economic argument: early in Book II of the Ethics, right where he's laying out the doctrine of the mean, Aristotle explicitly borrows, and seems to endorse, a Pythagorean idea that badness in general belongs to the class of "the unlimited" while goodness belongs to "the limited," with virtue itself being a kind of mean between vicious extremes that have no natural stopping point. My thesis is that the Politics passage is quietly leaning on this: chrematistike gets condemned mainly for being apeiron, without limit, which is really an application of a prior ethical commitment rather than an argument that starts from anything specific to money. If that's right, though, it seems to prove too much, since plenty of things we don't consider vicious are open-ended in exactly the same sense — a family reinvesting all its profits back into a small business every year, say, or a retirement account left to compound for forty years. Neither one ever hits a natural ceiling either, but neither looks like usury to me.

Could you help me pressure-test this before I hand it in? Specifically, I'd love comments on: (1) whether my summary of the household-management/chrematistike/usury argument actually tracks what Aristotle says, or whether I've flattened or misread some part of it; (2) whether the link I'm drawing to the doctrine-of-the-mean passage is a defensible reading or whether I'm forcing together two texts that aren't really doing the same kind of work; and (3) whether the reinvestment/compound-interest case actually causes trouble for Aristotle's position, or whether there's an obvious response available to him that I'm missing. General notes on whether the argument is actually interesting to read, rather than just a chain of claims, would help too.
