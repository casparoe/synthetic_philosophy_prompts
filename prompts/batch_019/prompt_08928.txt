I am about two-thirds of the way through a novel whose protagonist, Marisol, is a theoretical physicist who made her name in her twenties by deriving, from pure symmetry considerations, a prediction that was only borne out experimentally years afterward. I have loosely modeled the beat on Gell-Mann's route to the omega-minus baryon by way of the Eightfold Way, though the actual discovery in the book is invented. Marisol is now in her sixties, has just delivered a eulogy-adjacent lecture for a colleague who recently died, and gets pulled afterward into a long-running argument with Teodor, an old friend from graduate school who left physics for philosophy of science. I keep drafting this scene myself and watching it collapse into two people taking turns restating Wigner's "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" puzzle at each other, so before I try again I would like a model version of the conversation to work from.

Marisol should hold something close to Wigner's own position: the fact that a symmetry group she wrote down years before anyone observed the particle it implied is, for her, evidence that the mathematics was there to be found rather than assembled after the fact to fit the data. Teodor should mount the deflationary case against her with real force rather than token resistance -- that this sense of miracle is a selection effect, since we remember the Gell-Manns and quietly forget the mathematical structures that led nowhere; that the space of possible formal systems is so vast that some corner of it fitting a given pattern is nearly guaranteed; and that the word "unreasonable" smuggles in an inflated standard for what physics owes us by way of explanation. Have him also raise Tegmark's mathematical-universe hypothesis, and then refuse it himself as a way of dissolving the puzzle by definitional fiat rather than answering it -- I don't want him reaching for an easy escape hatch any more than I want Marisol simply asserting mystery and stopping there. In turn, have Marisol press him on why the selection-effect story sits so awkwardly with cases where the mathematics sat on the shelf for decades before physics needed it -- non-Euclidean geometry waiting for general relativity is the example she always reaches for -- and make Teodor answer that directly rather than change the subject. These two have been having versions of this argument for thirty years, so neither should concede a point without a fight. Please write it as a continuous dialogue of eight to twelve exchanges, with no narration beyond minimal attribution, ending on a line that leaves the disagreement sharper rather than resolved.
