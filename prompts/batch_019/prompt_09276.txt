Over the holidays I read the second part of Franklin's Autobiography for the first time and, half as a private joke and half in earnest, adopted his method for this year's resolutions: a little ledger with a column for each day and a row for each of his thirteen virtues, one virtue given a full week of close attention while the other twelve are left to their "ordinary chance," a mark entered against any of the thirteen whenever I catch myself failing it, the whole thirteen-week course meant to repeat four times before the year is out. I mentioned this at a dinner with two old friends from graduate school, Nadia and Marcus, expecting to be teased for five minutes. Instead the two of them spent the rest of the evening arguing about Franklin himself, and I've been turning their exchange over ever since -- not least because I'm still filling in the ledger and would like to know whether I should feel silly.

Marcus opened by denying the project deserves the name moral self-improvement at all: Franklin's own framing is unapologetically about the usefulness of virtue for getting on in the world, and the chart itself only ever scores observable lapses, never motives, so it reads less like anything Aristotle would call character formation than like a productivity system avant la lettre. Nadia answered that this shows less than Marcus thinks, since Aristotle's own account of habituation also starts from the outside: on his view we become just by performing just acts before we are actually just, and the settled disposition catches up later, so scoring outward compliance first is exactly how that tradition says virtue gets built, not a departure from it. Marcus came back with a sharper version of the same worry: Franklin didn't cultivate the thirteen together but rationed them, giving each a solitary week on a fixed rotation while the rest went unwatched, which looks less like habituation than like compartmentalized self-monitoring, since the unity-of-virtue idea Nadia is implicitly relying on denies you can work on justice in isolation from temperance, courage, and the rest. Nadia replied that the rotation is scaffolding, not a claim about separability: Franklin says outright that he ordered the thirteen so that the earlier ones -- temperance, for a clear head; order, for the time to run the scheme at all -- would make the harder, later ones easier to acquire, which pictures the virtues as supporting one another rather than as detachable one at a time for good. Marcus then pressed the point he clearly thought decisive: Franklin admits he made little headway on the reality of humility, only its appearance, and half-jokes that even total success would just leave him proud of how humble he'd become -- which Marcus read as accidental proof that a ledger of observable lapses cannot, even in principle, detect the very state it is supposed to cultivate, at least for any virtue that lives in the motive rather than the act, and probably not only humility but sincerity and justice as well. Nadia's closing move was that this proves too much: the marks were never an outsider's tally but the record of a nightly introspective review, so the method was never purely behavioral to begin with, and Franklin saw its limits clearly enough to joke about them and to plan, even if he never finished it, a further book -- to be called something like The Art of Virtue -- meant to supply the actual means of acquiring the virtues rather than just a scorecard.

Neither of them budged, and I'd rather not just split the difference between two people who were both arguing in good faith. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- Marcus's opening claim, Nadia's Aristotelian reply, Marcus's compartmentalization point, Nadia's ordering-of-virtues reply, Marcus's argument from the humility passage, and Nadia's closing reply -- and say who has the better of it at each step, including anything a move concedes or overlooks that its author didn't seem to notice? Please begin with a JSON object scoring all six moves from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), keyed by move number, then give your reasoning in prose, and end by telling me whether Franklin's project amounts to real moral self-cultivation or something categorically thinner -- and whether there's a better answer to that question than either Nadia's or Marcus's.
