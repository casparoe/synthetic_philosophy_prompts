Me and my friend Priya got into a whole argument last night about Leibniz that we never actually resolved. She thinks his moral philosophy is kind of a con: if every monad's entire life story is already baked into its "complete concept" from the moment of creation, and God just picked this world because its the best one available among all the ones that could coexist, then nobody could really have done otherwise, so all his talk of sin, justice, and defining justice as "the charity of the wise" is just predestination with nicer branding. I countered with his idea that freedom doesn't mean uncaused, just that the act flows from your own nature rather than being forced on you from outside (he calls it spontaneity), and that its only morally, not logically, necessary that you sin — but honestly I'm not sure that actually answers her or just relabels the same problem. Write an essay that actually settles it: does Leibniz's system leave real room for the kind of moral responsibility his ethics needs — genuine praise, blame, desert, a God who justly punishes and rewards — or is it determinism wearing a nicer coat? Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, but land on an actual verdict, don't cop out with "it depends on your definition of freedom."
