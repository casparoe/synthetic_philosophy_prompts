I'm taking a seminar on classical Islamic philosophy, and this week's unit pairs al-Ghazālī's critique of causal necessity in the Seventeenth Discussion of *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* with Ibn Rushd's rebuttal in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*. In lecture, our professor mentioned in passing that survey treatments often flatten al-Ghazālī into "a medieval Hume," since both deny that we ever perceive a necessary connection between cause and effect, and both appeal to habit or custom to explain our expectations. For my response paper, I want to test that comparison rather than just repeat it. Please argue that this assimilation is misleading, focusing on what happens to necessary connection itself in each account: unlike Hume, al-Ghazālī does not dissolve necessary connection into mere psychological expectation, but instead relocates it from fire and cotton to God's power and will, so that it remains a real feature of the world. In building the case, address whether al-Ghazālī's notion of "habit" (ʿāda) is doing the same explanatory work as Hume's "custom," and whether Ibn Rushd's objection that occasionalism destroys the possibility of natural science creates a problem for al-Ghazālī with no real counterpart in Hume's own philosophy.
