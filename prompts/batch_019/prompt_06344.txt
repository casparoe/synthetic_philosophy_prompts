I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but I've spent enough evenings reading old Effective Altruism Forum and LessWrong threads that I keep running into an idea called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) that I can't quite let go of. As I understand it, the argument runs: because the universe (or multiverse) is probably vast or infinite, it almost certainly contains other decision-making agents who reason enough like I do that our choices are correlated even though we can never causally interact with each other -- essentially Hofstadter-style superrationality in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, scaled up to cosmic distances. If I act as though I'm cooperating with those agents -- say, by giving real weight to value systems other than my own -- that choice is itself evidence that similar agents elsewhere are doing the same for mine, so we all do better in expectation than if everyone optimized selfishly for just their own values.

What I want to know is whether this actually gives anyone a reason to act differently, or whether it's moral-uncertainty hedging dressed up in decision-theoretic language. Write an essay that states the strongest version of the argument -- be precise about what "relevantly similar" has to mean for the correlation to be real, and why this requires evidential rather than causal decision theory -- and then presses on its weakest points: whether that correlation can survive an agent explicitly reasoning about exploiting it, and the aggregation problems that surface once "vast or infinite" is taken literally. Don't just lay out both sides: tell me whether a consequentialist who is otherwise selfish about values should actually let ECL change what they do with their time and money, and defend that verdict.
