I am teaching a graduate seminar this term on the ethics of borders and migration, and for the past two weeks we have been working through Michael Dummett's short 2001 book *On Immigration and Refugees*, a text I assign precisely because it treats the immigration debate with the same premise-by-premise, fallacy-hunting rigor Dummett brought to disputes in philosophy of logic and language, rather than resting on free-floating cosmopolitan sentiment. One of my students pointed out, quite rightly, that Dummett died in December 2011, so the book predates the 2015 European refugee crisis, the Brexit referendum and the subsequent end of EU free movement, the Windrush scandal, and, above all, the more recent British policy of deeming asylum claims "inadmissible" when the claimant arrives via an "irregular route" such as a small boat across the Channel, paired for a couple of years with a since-abandoned plan to remove such claimants to Rwanda for processing.

I would like your help reconstructing, as carefully as you can, what Dummett himself — not a generic left-liberal academic, but specifically the author of that book, with its impatience toward the way the "genuine refugee" versus "economic migrant" distinction gets deployed in practice, and its diagnosis of the rhetorical devices by which restrictionist policy gets dressed up as principled border control — would most plausibly have said about the small-boats "inadmissibility" rule and the Rwanda scheme. Please reason from the actual structure of the arguments in the book rather than simply his general political sympathies, and be honest about any point at which his own framework makes the question harder, or cuts against the answer a sympathetic reader might expect, rather than smoothing this over.
