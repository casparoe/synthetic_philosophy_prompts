I'm writing a novel whose central figure is a sinologist in the final stages of a terminal illness, spending her last months completing a translation of the Zhuangzi. I want the philosophical scaffolding beneath her chapters to be sound rather than merely atmospheric, so I'd like help thinking through a comparison she keeps circling back to. She returns constantly to three passages: the scene in the "Perfect Happiness" chapter where Zhuangzi is found drumming on a basin after his wife's death, explaining that her death follows her life the way spring gives way to summer, summer to autumn, and autumn to winter; his subsequent conversation, in that same chapter, with a skull on the road, who describes death as freedom from any ruler above and from the business of the four seasons below; and, in "The Great and Venerable Teacher," the deathbed scene where a dying man's friend warns off the weeping family so as not to disturb the process of transformation, while the dying man himself likens heaven and earth to a great furnace and whatever is reshaping him to a skilled smith. Her Western-trained physician keeps countering with Epicurus's argument that death cannot harm us because we never coincide with it, and eventually reaches for Lucretius's point that we are untroubled by the eons before our birth and so should be equally untroubled by those after our death.

Could you compare these two positions in some depth, beyond the usual gloss that both thinkers simply tell us not to fear death? I'm especially interested in whether Zhuangzi's picture of death as one more transformation actually dissolves the fear of death or merely redescribes it without answering it, and whether it runs into some version of the problem Thomas Nagel raised against Epicurus: that the absence of a suffering subject at the moment of death doesn't obviously show that death is not a misfortune. Which position, if either, would you say stands on firmer philosophical ground, and why?
