I've been putting together my seminar on social epistemology and we finally got to List and Pettit's discursive dilemma this week -- the thing where a group votes on a couple of premises plus a conclusion that's supposed to follow from them, and even though every single member is perfectly consistent on their own, the majority view on the premises can end up flatly contradicting the majority view on the conclusion. Every textbook treatment I've ever assigned uses basically the same three-judge court case (something like: was there a valid contract, was it breached, is the defendant liable), and I'm pretty sure half my students have seen some version of it in another class already, because they're just pattern-matching rather than actually working through why the paradox happens.

So this year I want to hand them a short story instead of another dry legal hypothetical -- something like 800-1000 words -- and I was hoping you could write it for me. Pick any setting you like: a search-and-rescue crew debating whether to call off a search, a panel of astronomers deciding whether a blip counts as a confirmed detection, a small monastic council voting on a heresy charge, whatever you think would actually be fun to write. The one non-negotiable is that it has the real logical bones of the dilemma underneath the plot: two premises that are logically independant of each other, a conclusion that's only true if both premises are, and a vote in which the "premise-based" procedure and the "conclusion-based" procedure genuinely come apart, each internally coherent in its own way. I don't want any character to just stop and lecture the room on judgment aggregation theory -- I'd rather someone dimly sense that something isn't adding up without being able to say exactly what. And please don't let the ending neatly settle which procedure was the "right" one to use, since that's exactly what I want my students arguing about in the next class.
