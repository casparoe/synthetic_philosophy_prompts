I am several chapters into a historical novel set in The Hague in the autumn of 1672, shortly after the lynching of the de Witt brothers and the collapse of the republican "True Freedom" regime that Johan de Witt had led -- an event that, by the account of his earliest biographers, left the real Spinoza, who appears in my book as a supporting character, badly shaken. I want to write a scene in which he argues late into the night with a fictional acquaintance: a Dutch physician who has read his Hobbes closely, the De Cive in the original Latin and the Leviathan in the Dutch translation that had been circulating for some years, and who wants to know whether the summer's violence proves that rule by the multitude is simply too fragile to survive, or proves the opposite.

What I would like from you is the dialogue itself, not a summary of it. So far as I understand the two men, they start from strikingly similar ground: that natural right runs exactly as far as power does, and that a commonwealth is built out of some transfer or pooling of that right among its members. From there they diverge sharply. The physician defends the Hobbesian case that peace requires handing sovereignty over completely and undividedly, ideally to a single person, precisely because dividing it among many simply reproduces, inside the walls of the state, the condition of war the state was meant to escape. Spinoza, without disputing the premise about power and right, denies that such a transfer could ever really be complete, and maintains that a "democratic" state -- one in which each person's right passes only to the collective body of which he remains a part -- is not a sentimental preference but the most natural regime and, properly constituted, the most stable one, since it is harder to corrupt or frighten the judgment of a large body than the will of one man, and since it alone secures the freedom to think and to speak that he takes to be the whole point of political life.

I would like each man to engage the other's strongest move rather than talk past him, and neither should come off as a strawman for the other to knock down. Have the physician press Spinoza on whether "the judgment of the many" is anything more than a polite name for the mob that tore two statesmen apart in this city's streets only months before; and have Spinoza answer in his own terms, rather than simply asserting that the people know best. A page or two, ending with the disagreement still very much alive rather than neatly resolved, would suit the chapter perfectly.
