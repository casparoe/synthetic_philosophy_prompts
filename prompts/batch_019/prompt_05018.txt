I sit on the compensation and governance committee of a small nonprofit legal aid clinic. Our bylaws, drafted a decade ago by a founder with a philosophy background, include a clause I only recognized as Rawlsian after a friend in my reading group pointed it out: staff compensation is to be set the way principles chosen behind a veil of ignorance — as to whether one will turn out to be an attorney or a client here — would set them, and any resulting inequality in pay must satisfy Rawls's difference principle by working to the benefit of the clinic's least-advantaged stakeholders, understood in practice as both our lowest-paid staff and the low-income clients we exist to serve. The clause has never mattered until now. Our only attorney with the trial experience to handle contested asylum appeals has an outside offer at nearly three times her current salary, and says she will take it unless the board approves a comparable retention raise. Our executive director argues this is exactly the situation the difference principle was designed to license: losing this attorney would leave asylum clients, who have essentially no other path to representation, without appellate coverage for the better part of a year, so paying her more — even though it stretches our internal pay scale — leaves our worst-off clients better off than flat equality would, which is just what parties behind the veil of ignorance would have chosen. Two other committee members, both of whom studied philosophy, disagree, pressing a version of G.A. Cohen's objection to this style of reasoning: if the attorney is genuinely committed to the clinic's mission, she should not need to be paid three times as much to keep doing work she says she believes in, and rewarding a credible exit threat only teaches staff to develop one, hollowing out the ethos the bylaws were meant to protect rather than honoring the principle they invoke.

Please draft the short memo I will circulate to the full committee ahead of our vote. It should recommend one specific resolution — approving the raise, rejecting it, or approving some conditional or time-limited version — rather than dodge the disagreement by simply presenting both sides. Before landing on that recommendation, it should state the Cohen-style objection in its strongest form, including the natural Rawlsian reply that a principle meant to govern the basic structure of institutions was never intended to presuppose that individuals will renounce ordinary self-interested bargaining within the rules of an already-just scheme, and it should close by specifying what evidence or review point, if any, would count as vindicating the losing side later.
