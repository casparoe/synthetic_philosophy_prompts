I'm writing my IB Extended Essay in philosophy on the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism, and I want to press on one specific move in it rather than the argument as a whole. Most discussions I've found stop at the classic reply to John Lucas's original 1961 version: that we can never know our own reasoning is consistent, so we aren't entitled to assert the relevant Gödel sentence, and the whole argument collapses. Roger Penrose's revised version in *Shadows of the Mind* is built specifically to survive that reply, since it argues from soundness rather than mere consistency, concluding that we could never knowably certify any formal system as the sound basis of our own mathematics without contradiction. I want to argue that this revised version still fails to establish anti-mechanism, because a proof that we can't knowably identify our own underlying algorithm only shows a limit on self-certification, not that no such algorithm exists. Please construct this argument as rigorously as you can, and then address the strongest reply available to Penrose: that an algorithm which could never in principle be recognized as such, even by the very mathematician who is that algorithm, isn't doing the explanatory work a scientific theory of mind needs it to do.
