So we were four miles into a trail when we passed a solo hiker heading down who told us the footbridge over the creek another two miles up had washed out overnight and there was no safe way across. We turned around, lost the whole afternoon. That evening at camp it turned into a whole debate between two people from my cog sci program, Robbie and Faye (I'm doing my PhD in cog sci but I've been sitting in on a social epistemology reading group this semester since my advisor thinks it's relevant to my actual work on kids' selective trust in informants), about whether turning back was even rational, and it went on way longer than I expected. I can't tell who actually won and it's been bugging me for days.

(1) Robbie said obviously it was rational — you should trust a stranger's report like that by default unless you have some specific reason not to, because if you had to independently verify everything anyone tells you before acting on it, ordinary life (asking for directions, reading a trail sign, whatever) would just grind to a halt.
(2) Faye said no, it was rational only because of the specific evidence backing up this particular guy: he was coming from the right direction, his boots and pack were soaked and muddy in a way that matched actually having been up there, and there'd been a storm that night. Swap in a clean, dry stranger who'd wandered up from the parking lot saying the exact same sentence, and trusting him would've been way less reasonable — so it's not testimony as such doing the work, it's the specific backing.
(3) Robbie replied that the "specific evidence" she's describing — muddy boots, right direction — is only evidence of anything because they were already reading his whole presentation as basically sincere. That's not an independent check, he said, it's just crediting the guy with honesty via context clues instead of via his say-so directly, which is the same trust wearing a disguise.
(4) Faye said that's backwards: the fact that she would've discounted the claim if the cues had pointed the other way (say, if the trailhead log showed a dozen people had crossed fine that morning) shows the trust was never unconditional. It was earned turn by turn from evidence, which is exactly what makes it not a default.
(5) Robbie shot back that they had zero independent check on basically everything else the guy's sentence relied on — that there really is a creek up there, that a storm actually happened, that "washed out" means what they think it means. They took all of that on his word alone, cues or not. So if real warrant requires independent evidence for every load-bearing part of a claim, they shouldn't have turned back at all, muddy boots or no muddy boots.
(6) Faye answered that this proves too much: you asses whether the source is reliable enough via whatever cues you've got, and then that assessment carries over the rest of the claim — that's just how trusting any source works (a mechanic, a textbook, whatever), and it's still totally different from Robbie's view where trust is simply owed by default unless a red flag shows up.

At that point Robbie just said her "holistic, context-based" requirement is so cheap and so automatically satisfied in basically every human encounter that calling it a real requirement is kind of empty — it's default trust with extra steps — and they gave up and went to go set up tents.
