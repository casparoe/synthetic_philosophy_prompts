I'm giving the toast at my sister's wedding next month. She and her fiancé have a running joke that neither of them can say exactly when they got engaged: on a long drive home from a trip, they talked for hours about their future, and at some point they simply started saying "we're engaged," with no moment at which one of them proposed and the other accepted. My family finds this charming and leaves it there. But several guests are from my philosophy program, where the nature of shared intention has been argued over for years between Bratman-style planning theorists, for whom a "we" is just a structure of interlocking individual intentions and mutual responsiveness, and Gilbert-style joint-commitment theorists, for whom a real "we" requires a further joint commitment that gives each side standing to call out the other for reneging -- guests who will hear the couple's story as a live test case rather than a cute anecdote. Could you write me a five-minute toast that is warm and funny for my relatives, but that also does justice, for the philosophers present, to the puzzle of how two people end up genuinely bound to one another with no identifiable moment at which the binding took hold?
