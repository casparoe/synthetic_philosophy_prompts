Two members of my Indian philosophy reading group disagreed while working through the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas (the recognized sources of valid knowledge), specifically the status of upamāna, usually translated "comparison" or "analogy." The textbook case: someone who has never seen a gavaya (a wild-ox-like animal) is told by a forest-dweller that "a gavaya resembles a cow." Later, seeing an animal resembling a cow in the forest, they think, "so this must be a gavaya." Nyāya treats the resulting cognition as a pramāṇa in its own right, irreducible to perception, inference, or testimony. The disagreement went roughly like this:

(1) Marcus said this is just inference in disguise: given a general premise ("gavayas resemble cows," however acquired) plus a perceived resemblance, concluding "this is a gavaya" is simply applying a rule to a case, which is all that inference is.

(2) Nora said genuine inference requires an invariable concomitance (vyāpti) between reason and conclusion, established by observing many correlated instances, as with smoke and fire. No such track record exists here: the knower is applying a single, one-off testimonial claim to a new case for the first time. That is a different cognitive act from induction, so calling it "inference" stretches the word to cover anything.

(3) Marcus replied that if it is not inference, it should just be testimony, since every bit of content — "gavayas resemble cows" — came from the forest-dweller's say-so. All that is added is applying an already-taught term to a new case, and there is not normally thought to be a separate pramāṇa just for applying a learned word to new instances.

(4) Nora answered that ordinary word-application rests on the knower's own prior, repeated experience of whatever the word names: someone who has seen many dogs can correctly call a new animal a dog. Here, the knower has never perceived a gavaya; the connection between word and object is being fixed for the first time, by noticing a resemblance to something the word is not even about. That, she said, is why the Naiyāyikas treat the resulting cognition as a distinct achievement, produced by neither perception, testimony, nor inference alone.

(5) Marcus granted that the act might be structurally distinctive but said that this does not settle whether it deserves its own pramāṇa rather than being filed as a hybrid case. Other schools manage without it — the Buddhist epistemologists recognize only two pramāṇas — and Nyāya itself elsewhere invokes a principle of parsimony (lāghava) against multiplying categories. So perhaps, he said, the disagreement is not really about the cognitive facts, but about how liberal one should be in minting new pramāṇas.

Go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each step — not just who wins overall. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing about how the Naiyāyikas themselves justified individuating the pramāṇas.
