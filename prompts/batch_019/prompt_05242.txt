I run a small interview podcast as a side project, unrelated to my day job as a software engineer, where I talk to academics about arguments that never quite make it into the mainstream conversation. Next month's guest is a political philosopher whose new book revisits and defends a text I'd genuinely never heard of before I started prepping for the episode: Michael Dummett's short 2001 book On Immigration and Refugees. As best I can piece together from the book itself and the few discussions of it I've tracked down, Dummett's position is that a state's right to control its borders is real but conditional rather than absolute, that much of the British immigration debate in his lifetime dressed up racial hostility as concern about capacity and cultural cohesion, and that the colonial histories of states like Britain generate special, asymmetric obligations toward migrants from their former colonies. I don't have any formal training in philosophy, so I'm worried that if I just ask her to lay out the view, we'll end up with a restatement of the book jacket and nothing more.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push on the structure of the argument rather than its headline claims? I'd like at least one question that asks her for an actual, usable test for telling apart a border restriction that is racism in respectable dress from one that is a genuine, race-neutral response to capacity limits, given that no policymaker ever announces the racist version out loud; one question that checks whether grounding stronger admission claims in colonial history can be made consistent with treating a refugee's raw need as the more basic humanitarian consideration -- what does the view say about someone fleeing a state the host country never colonized?; and one question that puts her diagnosis of restrictionist sentiment as fundamentally about racism in tension with the more mundane objections critics raise about wages, public services, and social trust, since simply attributing bad faith to the other side looks like a way of avoiding their strongest arguments. For each of these three, and for whichever other questions you come up with, add a short follow-up that anticipates the reply she's most likely to give and doesn't let her rest there. Please skip anything generic like what first drew her to the topic -- I want this to read like two people who have actually sat with the argument.
