For a philosophy of science seminar I'm taking this semester, we've spent several weeks on debates about the reliability and validity of psychiatric diagnosis, but the case that has actually been occupying me comes from my own friend group rather than the syllabus, and I would like an outside opinion on it.

A friend of mine, Naomi, spent the better part of a year in online spaces where autism self-screening quizzes and "signs you were an undiagnosed autistic kid" content circulate constantly. She became convinced the label fit her, found a tight-knit community built around exactly that label, and about a year later received a formal adult diagnosis from a clinician who mentioned, in passing, that the clinical picture for "high-masking" adult women has itself been shifting recently, largely because so many patients now arrive already self-identified from communities like the one Naomi joined. Before any of this, Naomi struck me as, at most, a bit intense and sensorially picky — not obviously different from several other people I know. Since the diagnosis, she has reinterpreted years of unrelated habits as symptoms, adopted mannerisms (rehearsed openers for phone calls, more visible stimming) that she says she only allows herself now that she has "permission," and is, by her own account, a noticeably different person to be around than she was three years ago. Her mother finds none of this remotely puzzling: doctors refine their criteria as evidence accumulates, patients behave differently once they understand themselves better, and that is simply how knowledge about people improves over time. I am not convinced that response is adequate, but I also cannot quite articulate what it leaves out, especially since I do not want to imply that Naomi is not "really" autistic, or that she is faking anything.

The trouble is that this does not seem fully explained by ordinary confirmation bias on Naomi's part, since that would not obviously account for clinicians saying the criteria themselves are shifting; and it does not seem to be mere social contagion either, since the specific behaviors she picked up are exactly the ones the category is defined by, rather than generic community norms. So I would like help with two things: first, whether there is a genuine conceptual problem in a case like this — where the diagnosis helps bring about the very pattern of self-understanding and behavior that then feeds back into how clinicians define and recognize the category for the next cohort of patients — that is different in kind from the mundane fact that medical categories get revised as evidence comes in; and second, whether, if something distinctive really is going on, it puts any pressure on how confident we should be that Naomi "has" the condition in whatever sense we usually intend, or whether it is fully compatible with the diagnosis being straightforwardly true of her.
