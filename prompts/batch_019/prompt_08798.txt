After a session of my graduate seminar on decision theory, two of my sharpest students, Ilana and Marcus, kept arguing in the hallway about the old "Death in Damascus" puzzle, and since I was the one who put the case on the board in the first place, I'd rather have an outside party adjudicate than referee my own students. The setup, as I presented it: an agent currently in Damascus must decide whether to stay there or flee to Aleppo, and Death -- a highly reliable predictor of where the agent will end up -- will turn up in whichever of the two cities the agent is ultimately in; the agent wants above all not to be in Death's city, and starts out with no antecedent reason to prefer one destination over the other. The puzzle, which traces back to Allan Gibbard and William Harper's work on causal decision theory, is that neither pure option seems stable under reflection: firmly committing to "stay" is itself evidence that Death predicted "stay" and is waiting in Damascus, which makes fleeing look better, but firmly committing to "flee" generates the identical problem in reverse.

Their exchange went roughly like this. (1) Ilana argued that the rational response is to flip a coin: since committing to either pure act undermines itself once you factor in what the commitment reveals about Death's whereabouts, neither staying nor fleeing is "ratifiable" in Jeffrey's sense -- an act you'd still endorse on learning you were about to perform it -- whereas the 50/50 mixture is ratifiable, for exactly the reason a matching-pennies game has no pure-strategy equilibrium, only a mixed one. (2) Marcus objected that this "solution" cheats: if Death predicts your eventual city reliably enough to generate the paradox in the first place, there is no principled reason to assume Death cannot also predict how your coin will land, in which case randomizing buys you nothing, and calling it "rational" just dresses up wishful thinking as decision theory. (3) Ilana replied that Marcus was running together two different questions: ratifiability is a formal constraint on which recommendations are self-undermining, not an empirical claim that the recommended act deceives Death, so the coin flip can be the theory's correct output whether or not it actually improves the odds against this particular predictor. (4) Marcus's rejoinder was that this concedes his complaint rather than answering it: a notion of "stability" that a sufficiently good predictor can see coming and match anyway is not one an agent facing Death has any reason to care about, so rescuing ratifiability this way preserves the letter of the constraint while abandoning whatever made it worth imposing in the first place.
