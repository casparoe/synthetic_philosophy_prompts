So my friend Jake and I got into it pretty hard on Friday night about Bernard Williams's internal vs. external reasons stuff, in a conversation that went way later than it should have (we've both got some philosophy background — he did an MA years ago, I'm currently TAing a metaethics seminar — so neither of us was backing down, and we'd also had a couple drinks, which feels a bit on-the-nose given what the argument ended up being about). It started because I said my uncle, who genuinely could not care less about factory farming — no squeamishness, no guilt, nothing in his day-to-day that connects to it — doesn't actually have a reason to stop eating factory-farmed meat on Williams's picture, there's just a reason "for us" to wish he'd change, but no reason for him unless something in his actual motivational set (S) could get him there. Jake wouldn't let it go. His point was that Williams's whole apparatus leans on this "sound deliberative route from S" clause, and that once you let sound deliberation include stuff like vividly imagining what an animal goes through — which Williams needs if he wants to explain how anyone ever internally comes to care about things they don't already care about — you can always retroactively cook up some story where the agent "would" be moved if he deliberated correctly. So internalism ends up unfalsifiable and just quietly absorbs whatever the externalist wanted to say anyway. I said no, Williams blocks that, because he's explicit that you can correct someone's S for facts and logic but you can't just tack on extra moral or prudential premises the agent doesn't already accept. Jake's comeback was that this "no tacking on" rule is completely made up the moment you already let imaginative vividness count as legit deliberation, since that's basically a backdoor for adding new evaluative content anyway.

I went home annoyed and typed up my side of it properly instead of sleeping, so here's roughly where I landed — this is notes, not a polished essay, sorry for the bullet-point vibe:

1. Williams's core claim: A has a reason to φ only if there's a sound deliberative route from A's actual subjective motivational set S to φ-ing, where S is way broader than "desires" narrowly conceived — supposed to include (his own words, if I'm remembering the list right) "dispositions of evaluation, patterns of emotional reaction, personal loyalties, and various projects."
2. The unfalsifiability worry (basically Jake's move, though I think some version is in Parfit too): if "sound deliberation" is left totally open-ended, "A has reason to φ" collapses into "A would be caused to want to φ by whatever process we're calling deliberation," which can be gerrymandered after the fact to fit any answer — so internalism stops ruling anything out.
3. But Williams's actual constraint has two seperate moving parts, not one: correcting false beliefs (that's the gin/petrol case), and, independently, an imaginative requirement — apparently the relevant counterfactual only holds if the agent would still want the thing after both getting the facts right and really, vividly picturing what it'd be like for it to happen. So the imagination bit is official machinery, not something critics are smuggling in.
4. This is exactly what McDowell goes after in "Might There Be External Reasons?" — his move is that some motivational shifts are just conversions, a changed way of seeing your situation, not the unfolding of anything already latent in S. Williams's reply is basically that this isn't what he means by rational deliberation, wich always felt to me like restating the disagreement rather than resolving it.
5. My actual position: I think Williams has a non-question-begging line available, but it's about whether the new motivation is answerable to some independently attributable, standing, general capacity already in S (generic sympathy, a taste for consistency, whatever) versus installing content with no antecedent anchor at all — not about mechanism (inference vs. imagination). That's why Owen Wingrave (the Henry James kid whose dad guilt-trips him about the family's military tradition) still comes out right even once you allow imaginative deliberation: there's no dormant family loyalty or tradition-mindedness anywhere in his psychology to vividly awaken. He's just not that kind of person.
6. The catch: this saves internalism from strict logical collapse, but hands you a brutal epistemic problem instead, since from the outside we usually can't distinguish "dormant general capacity getting activated" from "brand-new content getting installed." So in practice, internal reason ascriptions end up nearly as unverifiable as the external reason claims they were supposed to replace.
