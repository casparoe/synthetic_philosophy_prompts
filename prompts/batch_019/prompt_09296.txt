I'm developing a magazine feature on how courts and regulators assign blame when serious harm follows from someone's failure to act rather than from anything they actively did — a landlord who never fixed a smoke detector, a regulator who sat on a safety warning, a bystander who could have intervened but didn't. While interviewing a philosopher of law for background, I learned that this legal question rests on an unresolved metaphysical dispute: whether omissions can be causes at all, as opposed to merely being things we treat as causes for practical, moral, or legal convenience. Counterfactual theorists of causation say yes, without much hesitation: if the harm counterfactually depended on the missing action — had the agent done X, the harm would not have occurred — then that dependence is really all causation ever consists in, so the omission counts as a cause just like any event would. Theorists who instead ground causation in some physical process or the transfer of a conserved quantity between cause and effect deny this: since no such process runs from a non-event to the harm, they conclude that omissions are at best what some philosophers call "quasi-causes" — that our everyday and legal habit of calling them causes is convenient shorthand rather than a description of how the world is actually built.

I'd like you to dramatize this disagreement as a dialogue between two philosophers — one a committed counterfactual theorist, the other a committed process theorist — who run into each other after a conference panel and end up arguing about a single concrete case: a lifeguard, absorbed in their phone, who fails to notice a swimmer in distress; the swimmer drowns. Make each side confront the strongest objection to their own view rather than simply restate it. The counterfactual theorist should have to answer the charge that their account is far too permissive, since it seems to make a cause of everyone whose different conduct would also have saved the swimmer — the other bystanders who never looked up, the instructor who never taught the swimmer to signal for help, the beach authority that never funded a second lifeguard for the shift. The process theorist should have to explain why ordinary and legal judgment so confidently singles out the lifeguard's inaction, and not all these others, as responsible for the death, if inaction is never strictly speaking a cause of anything. I'd like the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's moves — conceding points when they're pressed, revising their formulations, raising follow-up objections — rather than delivering parallel monologues, and there's no need to force the exchange to a tidy resolution if you think the disagreement runs deeper than that.
