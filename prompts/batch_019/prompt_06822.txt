I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading about Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument on my own time. Below are some notes in which I try to connect it to something more familiar from my own field: layers of abstraction in computing. I'd value your candid feedback before I show this to anyone else — particularly on whether the central analogy holds up, whether it quietly reinvents a move that's already well known (and already refuted) in the literature, and where the reasoning is weakest.

Physical Sufficiency and the Trouble with Levels

As I understand it, Kim's argument runs roughly as follows. Suppose a mental event M causes a physical effect P. Given physicalism, P also has a physically sufficient cause P*, the neural state that realizes M. Unless we are willing to say that M and P* routinely overdetermine P — a costly and ad hoc move to make for every instance of mental causation — M's contribution looks screened off by P*. Real mental causation then seems to require either reducing M to P*, or accepting a strange, systematic multiplication of causes behind every action.

This looked familiar from my own field. When a program crashes because of a null-pointer dereference, I say the bug caused the crash. A hardware engineer could just as truthfully describe the same event as the product of a physically sufficient configuration of transistor states on the chip. Nobody infers from this that 'the bug caused the crash' is thereby exposed as a fiction, or that the two descriptions are competing for the same causal credit.

My tentative diagnosis is that the exclusion argument gets its force from treating causation as a single, scarce resource that rival candidates must compete over. Software already gives us cases where we rely on several simultaneous levels of causal description without ever feeling that the lower level exhausts the causation and leaves nothing over for the higher one. If that intuition is correct in engineering, the exclusion worry starts to look like an artifact of how the problem is framed, rather than a genuine discovery about the mental.

The obvious objection to my own move: in the software case, we already think the higher-level description is dispensable — a convenient compression of the physical facts, suited to creatures who cannot track transistors one by one. Most physicalists want considerably more than that for the mental; they want mental causation to be real in a sense that outruns mere predictive convenience, which is precisely what the exclusion argument puts under pressure. So my analogy may simply presuppose, for the mental case, the very instrumentalism that non-reductive physicalists are trying hard to avoid.

I'd appreciate comments on three things: whether the abstraction-layers analogy actually clarifies anything or is a well-known dead end, whether the objection in my final paragraph is fatal to the whole idea, and which standard positions in the mental causation literature already occupy this territory and are worth my reading up on.
