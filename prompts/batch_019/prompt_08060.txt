I work as a fact-checker for a regional news outlet, and over the past year a growing share of my caseload has been viral videos purporting to show miraculous healings, apparitions, or other religious wonders—several of which our forensic review found to be wholly or partly AI-generated rather than authentic footage. Handling these cases has sent me back to Hume's "Of Miracles," where he argues that we should credit a miracle only when the falsehood of the testimony for it would be more improbable than the event itself, and where he treats testimony throughout as a kind of proof grounded in our general experience of how reliably witnesses report what they've seen.

Hume never had to reckon with synthetic video that can manufacture the appearance of direct eyewitness experience for millions of viewers at once, quite apart from anyone's honesty or credulity. On his own terms, would the mere existence of such technology make miracle reports even harder to ever credit, since there's now a standing, mundane rival explanation to weigh against them—or does it actually threaten the coherence of his whole framework, which seems to presuppose that testimony is a legible human act rather than something that can be fabricated wholesale and distributed at scale? I'd value your take on how far his eighteenth-century argument can really be stretched to cover this problem.
