I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel whose closing section turns on a retired physicist who, sorting through her late husband's notebooks, discovers that he spent his final years convinced he had lived the same October night over and over, in slightly different variations that eventually loop back into one another. A friend in my reading group, something of a Borges devotee, pointed me toward a handful of pieces to give the conceit some genuine intellectual ballast, and after sitting with "The Garden of Forking Paths," "A New Refutation of Time," and "The Doctrine of Cycles," I think I've finally got the shape of his actual position: that our sense of a single, linear, non-repeating time is an illusion generated by the false assumption of a continuous self, and that once that assumption is dropped, time turns out to be a branching structure in which every variant of an event occurs and which eventually closes back on itself into cycles — a conclusion he reached by argument alone, more than fifteen years before Hugh Everett's many-worlds interpretation arrived at something similar by an entirely different route.

I want my physicist's climactic scene to read as though she is genuinely re-deriving this argument under the pressure of grief rather than reciting Borges as decoration, so the logical steps need to be tight rather than merely atmospheric: starting from his denial of a continuous self, how did Borges actually get to a picture of time that is simultaneously branching and cyclical? And should I have her treat this, on the page, as a conclusion Borges regarded as settled, or does his own writing leave the matter considerably more open than that? I would rather my most philosophically literate readers find the scene rigorous than find it three separate pieces stitched together to resemble one argument.
