I cover climate science, and over the past few years I have written more than a few stories translating "attribution" studies for general readers -- the ones that let a scientist say, on the record, that a specific heatwave or flood was made some number of times more likely by climate change, or was even "virtually impossible" without it. The method behind those lines is fairly mechanical: run a large ensemble of climate model simulations under the actual historical forcing, run a matching ensemble under a counterfactual world with pre-industrial greenhouse-gas levels, count how often an event as extreme as the one that just happened turns up in each ensemble, and report the ratio as a probability (sometimes called a "risk") ratio. When I asked a researcher at one of the attribution groups what kind of probability that ratio was supposed to express, she told me it was just frequentism -- counting across runs, no different in kind from counting how often a die turns up six.

That answer has nagged at me ever since. The heatwave itself happened exactly once, in the one climate trajectory we actually have, which will never be rerun to generate a genuine long-run frequency; the "ensemble" is a set of simulations built on a shared, human-chosen model structure and a human-chosen definition of "an event this severe," which sounds like it smuggles in exactly the sort of judgment a subjectivist about probability would cheerfully own up to rather than disguise as counting. Yet the field's own papers also talk as though the models are estimating something objective about the physical climate system itself -- a real disposition of the current atmosphere-ocean state to generate extremes of a given magnitude -- which sounds less like a frequency or a credence than a propensity. I keep going back and forth, and I would like a sharper way of describing, in print, what these numbers actually claim.

Could you write me an essay -- something I could adapt for a piece on how the press ought to talk about attribution science -- that takes this specific case, rather than probability talk in general, and works out what a committed frequentist, a committed subjectivist, and a committed propensity theorist would each say a climate-attribution probability ratio means, and how each would have to handle the fact that the event itself is singular and, by the time anyone reads the number, has already either happened or not. Weigh the three accounts against one another specifically on how well they survive contact with this case, and then reach an actual verdict about which interpretation these numbers are best understood as instances of -- or, if you conclude the honest answer is some hybrid that doesn't reduce to a single interpretation, say so, but be precise about where the seams are and why that hybrid is stable rather than just a diplomatic fudge. Please don't let it end on "reasonable people disagree": I need something with enough of a spine that I could actually put a version of it in front of readers.
