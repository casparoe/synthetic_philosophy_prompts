I'm part of a small circle of friends who collect and occasionally trade vintage guitars, and a real disagreement broke out between two of them, Ravi and Dana, over a third friend's trading habits.

Owen owns three guitars — a Strat, a Tele, and a Jazzmaster — and ranks them in a circle: he prefers the Strat to the Tele on feel, the Tele to the Jazzmaster on weight, and the Jazzmaster to the Strat on tone. Over the past year he has repeatedly swapped with the other two owners, each time throwing in a little cash to sweeten the trade toward whichever guitar he currently ranked higher, and by now he's back with his original guitar and about $150 poorer.

(1) Ravi said this shows Owen's preferences are simply irrational: turning a set of preferences into what he called a "money pump" is exactly why philosophers treat a circular ranking like Owen's as a mistake rather than a harmless quirk. (2) Dana replied that all this really shows is that trading on whichever preference looks best in the moment is a bad policy: if Owen had looked ahead and refused the final swap back to the Strat, he'd have kept every dollar, and the very same three preferences would never have cost him anything, so the myopic trading is the problem, not the preferences behind it. (3) Ravi answered that Dana's fix doesn't remove the irrationality, it just relocates it: to dodge the pump, Owen has to, at some point, turn down a trade for the guitar he actually prefers more at that point, so either he acts on his ranking and loses money, or he keeps his money only by refusing to act on his own ranking, and neither option speaks well of the ranking. (4) Dana responded that this is just ordinary foresight, the same thing behind Ulysses tying himself to the mast or anyone making a New Year's resolution: refusing a trade you'd otherwise want, because you can see where it leads, is prudent self-management, not evidence of irrationality. (5) Ravi's closing point was that the analogy breaks down, because Ulysses is overriding a temptation his own sober judgment disowns, whereas Owen's three comparisons are all equally considered — none of them is a passing temptation to be corrected by a wiser self — and for Owen to even know in advance which trade to refuse, he has to be able to rank entire trading sequences against each other, which just brings back, one level up, the kind of consistent ranking his guitar preferences were supposedly allowed to lack.

I couldn't work out who was actually ahead at each stage, or whether they were both quietly right about different things. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
