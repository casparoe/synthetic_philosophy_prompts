Over the past year I've been trying to rebuild a set of personal habits after a difficult stretch, and at some point I designed myself a small paper tracker to help, half out of desperation and half because I've always liked systems and spreadsheets: a grid with about a dozen traits down the left-hand side -- things like staying calm under pressure, not interrupting people, being punctual, not gossiping, and keeping my desk tidy -- with a column for each day, where I mark a small dot whenever I notice myself slipping on one of them. I initially tried to monitor all dozen traits at once, found it completely unmanageable, and made almost no visible progress on any single one, so on a friend's suggestion I switched strategies: I now focus intensively on cultivating just one trait for a couple of weeks at a time, then rotate to the next, cycling back around to the first once I've worked through the whole list.

This has helped a great deal with whichever trait is currently "in focus," but I've noticed that the others quietly backslide while I'm not actively watching them, which bothers me more than I expected it to. Something odder happened as well: once I had built a long, unbroken streak on the trait I had labeled "humility," I caught myself feeling rather pleased with how humble and disciplined I had apparently become -- which seems to undercut the whole point, since feeling quietly smug about your humility streak is not itself much of an instance of humility.

My friend and I argued about all this for the better part of an evening this past weekend. She thinks I'm overanalyzing what is fundamentally just a productivity system, and that both problems are minor design flaws with easy fixes: track every trait simultaneously instead of sequentially, and simply don't put a virtue-sounding label on the column. I can't quite shake the feeling that something more structural is going on instead -- that a scorecard of this kind might be constitutionally incapable of producing the very trait it's meant to track, and produces instead a well-documented performance of that trait rather than the trait itself. Who do you think has the better of this argument, and is there actually a defensible way to design a self-improvement system like this one, or does the whole approach rest on some kind of mistake?
