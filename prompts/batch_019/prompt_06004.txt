I'm taking a graduate seminar on early modern political philosophy this term, and we recently finished Spinoza's unfinished Tractatus Politicus, including its strange final chapter on democracy. The argument has stuck with me more than I expected, partly because I'm drafting my qualifying-exam paper on Spinoza's theory of natural right and can't just shrug off a passage that sits right at the center of it. For most of the book, Spinoza grounds political right in power: a person or a body has exactly as much right as it has the power to maintain, and he argues democracy is the most "natural" form of state precisely because sovereignty stays, in principle, with the whole community rather than being handed over to one person or a few, so it departs least from the freedom and equality everyone had in the state of nature. Then, in the last chapter, when he specifies who actually gets to vote and sit in the governing council, he excludes foreigners, servants, and minors as not being fully their own masters -- and then pauses to argue, at more length, that women should be excluded too, appealing to their supposedly lesser strength and independence and to the fact that no society has ever been governed well with men and women ruling jointly. The manuscript breaks off, unfinished, not long after, because Spinoza died while writing it.

After class, two people in my cohort, Marcus and Dana, ended up arguing about this for a good half hour while the rest of us mostly listened. Marcus's position was that this is a contingent mistake rather than a flaw in the theory itself: Spinoza's own framework already entitles anyone genuinely capable of self-governance to full citizenship, his claim about women is just an empirical error by his own lights, and a Spinozist who corrects that one false premise gets full inclusion for free, with the rest of the theory intact. Dana pushed back that this lets Spinoza off too easily: a theory that simply defines right as coextensive with power has no independent standpoint from which to criticize an existing distribution of power, so an appeal to "no society has ever done this" functions as evidence of natural incapacity rather than as what it actually is, evidence of exclusion -- and that's not an accident befalling the theory from outside, it's what you'd expect from identifying right with power in the first place. Marcus answered that this proves too much, since Spinoza's "power" is explicitly not brute force but includes the cooperative power of reason, and by his own psychology the mind's power just is its capacity to think well, which he never claims is sex-specific -- so, again, the theory itself should include women once you get the facts right. Dana granted the point but replied that it only pushes the problem back a step: someone still has to certify whose "power" counts as the rational kind, and Spinoza settles exactly that question, for women, by an appeal to unexamined custom -- precisely the move a power-based theory makes easy in a way that, say, a theory grounding rights in a bare threshold capacity for rational agency would not. Marcus's last move was to say we should read the passage charitably, as a draft cut short by death rather than a finished position; Dana's was that we have to adjudicate the argument Spinoza actually left on the page, not the one we imagine he would have written given more time.

I couldn't tell who was actually getting the better of this, so I'd like an outside read. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the stronger position at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and separately, tell me whether there is a real, structural problem with power-based accounts of natural right that Marcus's responses never actually answer, or whether Dana is reading more into the theory's form than the text warrants?
