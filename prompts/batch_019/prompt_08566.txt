"The nonidentity problem, first raised by Derek Parfit, is a puzzle about whether a person can be wronged by the very choice that is also the reason that person exists. For example, imagine a woman is told that if she conceives this month she'll have a child with a mild but manageable disability, because of a virus she currently has, but if she waits three months to conceive, she'll have a different, healthy child. If she conceives now anyway, we want to say she's wronged her child. But the child she actually has couldn't have existed if she'd waited — a totally different child, from a different egg and sperm, would exist instead. So her child isn't made worse off by her choice, since for that specific child the only alternative was never existing at all, and (assuming the disability isn't severe enough to make the child's life not worth living) existing with a disability is better than not existing. This is basically the same move as Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion, where he shows that a huge population of people with lives barely worth living is better than a smaller population of very happy people, which is why he thinks total utilitarianism has to be rejected as a moral theory. Since the child isn't harmed on any standard person-affecting definition of harm, Parfit's own conclusion is that the mother hasn't actually done anything wrong, which is a useful corrective to our overly moralistic intuitions about reproductive choices."
