so i've been down a wikipedia rabbit hole on the axiom of choice lately and it's kind of blowing my mind. back in 1905 there was this whole fight between french mathematicians about Zermelo's proof that AC means literally any set, even the real numbers, can be well-ordered. Borel seemed like the most annoyed of the bunch - his big argument was that a "choice" isn't really mathematics unless you can actually specify a rule for making it, so picking one element from each of infinitely many sets with no rule for how to do it just felt like cheating to him, even though he (like basically everyone else in that debate) leaned on countable choice in his own proofs without seeming to notice or care.

he actually lived to see Gödel show, back in 1938, that AC can't be disproven from the rest of the axioms of set theory, which if anything seems like it should've been a little deflating for him. but Borel died in 1956, seven years before Cohen finished the job in 1963 by showing you can't prove AC either, meaning the whole thing is totally independent of ZF. and he definately never saw Solovay's 1970 result, where if you just assume some inaccessible cardinal exists somewhere, you can build a whole model of set theory that keeps dependent choice but drops full AC, and suddenly every single set of real numbers turns out to be Lebesgue measurable - no Vitali sets, no Banach-Tarski, none of the "monsters" AC produces.

if Borel had lived to see the Solovay result, do you think he'd take it as basically vindication of what he'd been saying all along, or would trading "AC gives you monsters" for "you need to assume some giant unprovable cardinal exists just to keep the monsters away" strike him as an even shadier deal? genuinely want your best guess at how his actual reasoning would react to this, not just a history lecture back at me.
