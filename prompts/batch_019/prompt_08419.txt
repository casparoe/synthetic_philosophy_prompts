Question: Explain the puzzle Aristotle raises with the "sea battle" example in Chapter 9 of On Interpretation. Why does the Principle of Bivalence seem to lead to fatalism here? Explain Aristotle's own way of avoiding this -- be precise about exactly which logical principle he keeps and which one he gives up. Then explain how the Stoic-era logician Diodorus Cronus's "Master Argument" relates to this same problem, and briefly describe how 20th-century logicians tried to formalize Aristotle's solution.

Her answer: Aristotle uses the sea battle to bring out a problem with assuming that literally every statement is either true or false right now (the Principle of Bivalence). Take the sentence "there will be a sea battle tomorrow." If that sentence is already true today, it looks like the battle is unavoidable no matter what anybody does, since the truth was already fixed in advance -- and if it's already false today, the same problem happens in reverse. Statements like this, about things that genuinely aren't decided yet, are called future contingents, and bivalence seems to force all of them to already have a fixed truth value, which would mean the future is never actually open and everything that happens was already necessary.

The standard reading of Aristotle's fix is that he gives up the Law of Excluded Middle specifically for these future contingent statements, while still holding onto bivalence at the level of the whole disjunction -- "either there's a sea battle tomorrow or there isn't" is still guaranteed to be true overall, even though neither half of it is true or false by itself yet. That way he can block the fatalist conclusion without having to say anything false.

This connects to the Megarian philosopher Diodorus Cronus, whose "Master Argument" gets to a similarly fatalistic place but from different starting premises -- roughly, that whatever is true about the past is now necessary, and that something impossible can never follow from something possible, which together push toward the conclusion that nothing counts as genuinely "possible" except what actually happens.

Later on, Arthur Prior formalized Aristotle's basic idea using what's called supervaluationism: a statement about the future is true if it turns out true along every way the world could still go from here, false if it's false along all of them, and neither true nor false if it depends on which way things go.
