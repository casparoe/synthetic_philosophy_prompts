For the past two years I've worked in a lab that grows small assemblies of human cortical neurons on multi-electrode arrays and studies how they respond to patterned electrical stimulation; part of what we do now falls under a label some people in the field have started using, "organoid intelligence." This kind of work got a lot of outside attention a while back when neurons cultured in a dish were shown to adapt their firing patterns, through closed-loop electrical feedback, well enough to control a paddle in a simplified game of Pong. At a joint seminar last month, a philosopher of mind in the audience remarked, half-joking, that what we've built in the incubator is "literally a brain in a vat," and that our neurons are exactly the kind of system Hilary Putnam had in mind when he argued, in the opening chapter of Reason, Truth and History, that the sentence "I am a brain in a vat" is self-refuting.

That comment sent me back to Putnam's argument, and I think I follow the basic move: if reference is fixed causally, a brain that has only ever interacted with a vat's electrode array cannot have a word "vat" that refers to real external vats — at best it refers to whatever pattern of stimulation produces vat-experiences — so the sentence cannot be true when asserted by the very brain it is meant to describe. What I do not follow is why this is supposed to hinge so heavily on the brain having been envatted "from the word go." Everything I have read since insists the argument does not work for someone who lived an ordinary embodied life and was only recently and secretly envatted, Matrix-style, since in that case "vat" and "brain" would still refer, via the person's pre-envatment history, to the real things. But the electrical signals currently producing that person's experience are, by hypothesis, exactly as disconnected from real vats and trees as in the lifelong case.

Could you walk me through why the history of a word's acquisition, rather than whatever is presently causing its tokening, is supposed to do all the reference-fixing work here, and why that difference is enough to let the recently envatted victim think a true thought that the lifelong victim cannot? And since I find myself relating all of this back to my own dish of neurons, which — unlike either of Putnam's characters — never had any causal contact with an outside world to begin with, not even a fake one, I would also like to know whether Putnam's argument has any purchase on a system like that at all, or whether it is simply the wrong shape of case for the argument to apply to: one with no reference at all, and hence no self-refutation, rather than reference to the wrong thing.
