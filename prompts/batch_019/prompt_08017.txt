I'm putting together a discussion exercise for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and I'd like a dialogue that moves past the standard "systematicity argument" presentation of the language of thought hypothesis and into one of its more startling implications.

Fodor argues that acquiring a new primitive (undefined) concept can only happen through forming and confirming a hypothesis about its extension, and that doing so already presupposes a representational system expressive enough to state that very hypothesis. Since this rules out learning primitive concepts in any interesting sense, he concludes that concepts like DOORKNOB and CARBURETOR must, in some sense, be innate.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of comparable sophistication: one defending this argument for radical concept nativism on its own terms, the other resisting the conclusion by appeal to a genuine alternative story of concept acquisition (for instance, Susan Carey's account of conceptual change via "Quinian bootstrapping," or a prototype- or exemplar-based learning story). Each interlocutor should target the specific weak point in the other's position rather than simply restating their own, and the exchange should end without either side conceding outright.
