I'm working on a novel where the sci-fi hook is a machine that lets a handful of characters look into — and eventually step briefly through to — neighboring branches of reality. About a third of the way in, my two leads, a married couple who are both burned-out philosophy dropouts, end up standing in front of the viewport watching a branch where Priya took the Berlin fellowship instead of staying, which means there's a version of her over there who never met Dev at all. Priya is half-wrecked and half-desperate for this to mean something comforting; Dev can't just let her have that.

I want to write their fight as an actual dialogue rather than summarize it, and I'd like help getting the philosophy solid before I do. Dev is basically a Lewis-style counterpart theorist: worlds are sealed, non-overlapping wholes, so strictly speaking nothing of Priya is over there at all, just a counterpart — someone similar enough, in whatever respects turn out to matter, to make it true that Priya "could have" gone to Berlin. Priya, a little surprised at herself, argues the other way, for old-fashioned transworld identity: it's really her, tracked rather than qualitatively matched, the same way Kripke says we get to just stipulate we're talking about Humphrey winning instead of needing some telescope to go check. Don't let them land on the Humphrey "couldn't care less" line and call it a day — these two have had versions of this argument since their PhD years and would treat that as an opening jab, not a finisher. I want Dev to press her on whether "tracked, not matched" is just smuggling in some primitive, unexplained thisness (a haecceity? I think that's the word) that does no actual work, and I want Priya to press him on the fact that his own counterpart relation runs on similarity, which is vague and context-dependent enough to hand you two equally good counterparts in the same branch, or none — and make him say why that's a smaller cost then the one he's pinning on her.

Keep them evenly matched, no strawmanning either side, and have them respond to each other's actual last point rather than each delivering a monologue — something like 700-900 words would slot right into the chapter.
