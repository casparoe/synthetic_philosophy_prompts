So I've been down a bit of a free will rabbit hole lately (started by re-reading some old LessWrong stuff on "could-ness" and determinism, then wandered off into the SEP, as one does). I kept running into references to Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility -- the one where he says that to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for the way you are, which means being responsible for the way you were before that, and so on back forever, and since nobody can be the ultimate cause of themselves, nobody is ever "truly" or "ultimately" morally responsible for anything. Apparently Strawson traces the core intuition behind this all the way back to Nietzsche, specifically to a passage in Beyond Good and Evil (part one, section 21) that keeps getting quoted in the secondary literature on this. I dug up an old public-domain translation and copied out the relevant bit:

"The desire for 'freedom of the will' in the superlative, metaphysical sense, which still holds sway, unfortunately, in the minds of the half-educated; the desire to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions oneself, and to absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society therefrom, involves nothing less than to be precisely this causa sui, and, with more than Munchausen's daring, to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the swamps of nothingness... The causa sui is the best self-contradiction that has been conceived so far, a sort of rape and perversion of logic; but the extravagant pride of man has managed to entangle itself profoundly and frightfully with just this piece of nonsense... in real life it is only a question of strong and weak wills."

I basically get the vibe -- wanting to be "truly" self-made so you can deserve credit or blame is like wanting to have pulled yourself out of a swamp by your own hair, which is nonsense -- but I cant quite reconstruct the actual step-by-step arguement hiding under the 19th century purple prose. Could you unpack the logical structure of what Nietzsche is claiming here, and then explain how it lines up (or doesn't) with Strawson's more careful, analytic-philosophy version of the regress? I'm also curious what you make of that last line about "strong and weak wills" -- it almost sounds like Nietzsche wants to chuck out the whole responsibility framework and replace it with something else entirely, which seems like a pretty different project from Strawson's, even though Strawson is clearly borrowing his best line. Does the regress argument actually hold up as a knockdown case against ultimate responsibility, or is there a move somewhere in here that's a bit too quick?
