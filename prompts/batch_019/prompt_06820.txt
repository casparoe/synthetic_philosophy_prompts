I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on the afterlife of the Vienna Circle, and this week's topic is Otto Neurath's Isotype project — the "Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics" that he developed with Gerd Arntz and Marie Reidemeister at the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in the 1920s. Working through Neurath's own writings on picture statistics, I keep running into a tension I would like your help thinking through. He clearly wanted his pictograms to do real cognitive work: to stand in for verbal claims about housing, unemployment, or production in a way that would be legible across languages and levels of literacy, free of the connotative residue that clings to ordinary words, and therefore better suited to his physicalist vision of a unified science than prose could ever be. Yet he was just as insistent that pictures could not simply replace word-language, and that captions, legends, and other conventional devices became indispensable the moment a claim involved negation, generality, conditionals, or tense.

What I would like from you is an intuitive, rather than merely historical, account of why Neurath thought pictorial representation hits a wall precisely at those logical operations, rather than at vagueness or complexity more generally, and how this connects to his understanding of the relationship between language and observation, given his rejection, in the protocol-sentence dispute with Carnap and Schlick, of any foundational and theory-neutral layer of description (the dispute that produced the well-known image of a boat being rebuilt plank by plank at sea). It would help to work through a concrete case or two: how, if at all, could an Isotype chart represent a claim like "unemployment fell in most, but not all, districts," or a genuinely conditional claim like "if exports rise, imports will fall"? Finally, since students in my seminar keep running the two together, I would be grateful for a short passage distinguishing Neurath's "picture language" from Wittgenstein's very differently motivated "picture theory of meaning" in the Tractatus, given that the two projects seem to share little beyond a single word.
