I have no formal training in philosophy or theology — my day job is writing backend infrastructure code — but I ran into David Lewis a while back through modal logic and possible-worlds semantics, which occasionally come up when I'm thinking about counterfactual reasoning in code, and I got curious enough to browse the rest of his bibliography. That's how I found the short, rather startling paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?," in which he applies his usual analytic style to the Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in the place of sinners. As best I can reconstruct it, his central move is to separate two things that ordinary talk of "guilt" tends to run together: actually having committed the wrong, which obviously can't be handed off to anyone else, and being liable to punishment for it, which he treats as closer to a debt — a status that might, at least in principle, be reassigned to a consenting substitute, the way a third party can pay off someone else's financial obligation. That struck me as a clever way of trying to blunt the standard objection that it is simply unjust to punish an innocent party in place of the guilty one, though I wasn't fully convinced it actually works.

Poking around further — mostly an encyclopedia entry on the atonement and a couple of conversations with a colleague who studied religion as an undergraduate — I learned that this exact objection has a long history: it was pressed by Socinus, a sixteenth-century critic of orthodox atonement doctrine, and Hugo Grotius tried to answer it in his own way long before anyone was talking about transferable liabilities. Grotius's "governmental theory," from what I gather, essentially gives up on strict retribution rather than trying to rescue it: on his view, God's purpose in punishing sin is fundamentally about upholding the moral order and deterring further wrongdoing, not about exacting an exact, desert-based penalty from one particular person, so Christ's suffering can count as an adequate public substitute for punishment without needing to be a literal, equivalent transfer of what each sinner individually deserved.

I'd like you to compare these two solutions to what is, as far as I can tell, the same underlying puzzle. Specifically, please: (1) reconstruct, as precisely as you can, how each account handles the core worry that a substitute's suffering cannot do the moral work that the guilty party's own punishment is supposed to do; (2) assess whether Lewis's guilt/liability distinction genuinely preserves a retributive conception of divine justice, or whether it quietly smuggles in governmental, more consequentialist assumptions much like Grotius's; and (3) tell me which of the two accounts offers the more successful, or at least the more intellectually honest, resolution of the puzzle, giving your reasons rather than a bare verdict. Please briefly correct me first wherever I've mischaracterized either view, but don't let that stand in for actually answering the comparative question.
