I've been down a rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing on how people in the effective altruism/longtermism world argue about "fanaticism" -- the worry that plain expected-value reasoning commits you to chasing vanishingly small probabilities of enormous payoffs. A couple of the papers I've been reading (I've gone through so many PDFs this month I've lost track of which one it was) bring up Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory as one possible way out, and I think I get the basic setup but not really why its supposed to be a big conceptual departure rather than just... expected utility theory with extra risk-aversion baked in.

From what I understand, REU has you rank outcomes worst-to-best and then run a function r over the *cumulative* probability of getting at least that outcome, instead of just weighting each outcome by its own raw probability like standard EU does. So you can end up giving disproportionate weight to the bad tail of a gamble. But when I asked a source how this is different from simply having a concave utility function (which also makes you averse to spread-out, risky outcomes), he said something like "no no, it's a global attitude toward risk, not a local one about the outcomes themselves" and then had to hop on a call, so I never got the actual mechanics.

Could you walk me through this properly? Specifically: what is the r-function doing, structurally, that a concave utility function over outcomes can't already do -- and which axiom of ordinary expected utility theory does REU have to weaken or drop to make room for it? I'm also curious whether the cumulative/rank-dependent trick is specifically what lets Buchak dodge problems like stochastic-dominance violations or Allais-style inconsistencies that you'd run into if you just crudely reweighted probabilities without paying attention to rank. Happy for this to get technical, that's kind of the point.
