I've noticed that whenever causation comes up on LessWrong or in Judea Pearl's popular writing, the interventionist theory — causation understood via hypothetical interventions on a causal graph, formalized in Pearl's "do" operator and developed philosophically by James Woodward — gets treated as the empirically sober modern alternative to any spooky talk of "necessary connection" between cause and effect, while David Lewis's rival counterfactual theory, which analyzes causation through similarity relations between possible worlds, gets discussed far less outside academic metaphysics. What's rarely mentioned in these discussions is that David Hume, in Section VII of the Enquiry ("Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion"), anticipates both lineages within a single passage: he first defines a cause as a matter of constant conjunction, then immediately glosses this "in other words" as the counterfactual claim that "if the first object had not been, the second never had existed," before going on, separately, to ground the whole idea in nothing but the customary transition of the imagination from cause to effect. Given Hume's Copy Principle, which admits only ideas traceable to some actual impression, and given that the express point of that section is to show we have no warrant for anything beyond this psychological habit, which of these two twentieth-century heirs do you think Hume would have recognized as the more faithful extension of his own definition — Lewis's possible-worlds semantics, or Pearl and Woodward's talk of interventions, most of which have never actually been performed? Don't just weigh considerations on each side: tell me which theory, if either, Hume himself would have endorsed, and defend the verdict by engaging with the specifics of his empiricism rather than just his reputation as a regularity theorist.
