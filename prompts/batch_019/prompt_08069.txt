I've spent years reading LessWrong and the EA Forum, and last year that finally turned into something concrete: I joined the small committee that allocates a pooled donation fund that four of us run out of a shared spreadsheet each giving season. We take our cost-effectiveness estimates seriously, and this cycle we've hit a snag that I don't think our usual "expected value per dollar" heuristic can resolve.

One applicant, a small biosecurity research collective, submitted an impact model that isn't a single point estimate but an open-ended list of mutually exclusive future scenarios, each roughly half as likely as the one before it, in which the estimated welfare effect of funding them alternates between very large gains (from a class of incident being averted) and very large harms (from information hazards and dual-use spillover), with the size of each swing growing even as its probability shrinks. When one member added the terms in the order the applicant listed them, she got a solidly positive figure; when another paired every "harm" scenario with the very next "benefit" scenario before running the total, he got a much smaller and less flattering one; a third member's grouping made the running total climb without bound. Nobody did anything illegitimate, and nobody disputes the applicant's underlying probabilities or payoffs -- this isn't a case of hidden overconfidence. I recognize the shape of this now: it's Nover and Hájek's "Pasadena game." Because the sum is only conditionally convergent, the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees that reordering the scenarios -- and no reordering is any more official than the applicant's own -- can drive the total to whatever value you like, or to none at all. No further biosecurity due diligence is going to fix that.

Could you draft the roughly one-page decision memo I'll circulate to the other three members before we reconvene next Tuesday? I need a specific recommendation -- fund, decline, or fund conditional on the applicant restructuring the model into something that converges regardless of ordering -- with a firm deadline attached if you go the conditional route, plus a short general policy the four of us can reuse whenever a future proposal's expected value turns out to hinge on how terms are grouped rather than on any further fact about the world. One member will feel steamrolled if the memo simply treats order-sensitivity as automatically disqualifying, so please state the strongest honest version of her objection first -- that refusing to fund anything whose expected value isn't rearrangement-invariant is itself a substantive, unexamined decision rule rather than a neutral default -- before explaining where you actually come down and why.
