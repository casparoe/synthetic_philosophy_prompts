I've spent the past few months reporting on citizenship and electoral-reform debates for a magazine feature, which has meant sitting through more conference panels than I'd like to admit on what political theorists call "the boundary problem" (a term usually traced to the political theorist Frederick Whelan) — the puzzle that almost any principle you might use to decide who should get a vote on some question already presupposes an answer to a prior question: who counts as a member of the group entitled to settle that principle in the first place. After one such panel, I brought two friends along to dinner, and the conversation turned into a real argument, still unresolved as far as I can tell, about the franchise rules for the UK's 2016 referendum on EU membership.

The facts, which neither of them disputed, are these: the referendum used essentially the franchise for UK parliamentary elections, so British and Irish citizens resident in the UK could vote, as could Commonwealth citizens resident there — a category that, because Malta and Cyprus both belong to the Commonwealth, happened to include Maltese and Cypriot nationals even though they were also EU citizens, while nationals of every other EU member state resident in the UK, however long they had lived there, could not vote at all. British citizens who had lived abroad for more than fifteen years were excluded too, a restriction Parliament has since scrapped, even though many of them — pensioners in Spain and France, for instance — had their right to go on living where they lived directly decided by the result. (1) Alina's opening argument was that this made the outcome illegitimate in a specific way: the people with the largest, most permanent stake in the result — long-settled EU nationals in the UK, long-settled Britons in the EU — were shut out, while someone who had moved from Australia to London eight months earlier could vote; if the point of a franchise is to give a say to the people whose lives are actually going to be rearranged by the answer, she said, this one failed badly. (2) Ben agreed on the facts but not the conclusion: "whose life gets rearranged" can't be the real standard, he said, since by that logic half of Europe should have had a vote too, from Irish exporters to anyone with a British in-law; you need a bright line somewhere, and citizenship plus a handful of long-standing carve-outs is at least a workable one, even if it does not track stakes perfectly. (3) Alina replied that Ben was defending the wrong thing, since this is not even a clean citizenship line: a Maltese barista who had arrived in Manchester eight months earlier could vote while a Polish nurse settled there for twenty years could not, for reasons that have nothing to do with the referendum question and everything to do with a leftover Commonwealth-era rule; that is not a principled boundary, she said, it is a historical accident that happened to land somewhere. (4) Ben's response was that every citizenship regime is full of accidents like that — birthright rules, old treaties, colonial hangovers — and that if mere path-dependence disqualified a line, no country's franchise would ever count as legitimate; what should matter, he said, is whether the rule is stable and generally reliable, not whether it can be derived from first principles, and citizenship generally is a reasonable proxy for long-term stake. (5) Alina's last word, before we were interrupted by the check, was that "generally reliable" is the wrong test: the rule did not fail at random, it failed specifically on the two groups with the most durable stake in the outcome, while working fine for everyone comfortably in the middle; a proxy whose errors cluster exactly at the highest-stakes cases, she said, is not a minor imperfection but evidence that it was never measuring the right thing to begin with.

I would like your help with what we could not sort out over dinner: please go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of the argument at each numbered point rather than just declaring an overall winner, and note anything you think both Alina and Ben are missing that would actually help settle the underlying question of where the boundaries of a demos should be drawn.
