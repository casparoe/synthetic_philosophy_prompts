Two friends of mine, Elena and Marcus, were prepping together for a graduate entrance exam and got into a disagreement after a timed practice section of multi-step logic puzzles, where the most common way to go wrong is overlooking a hidden case rather than making an outright error in any single step. Elena had pulled an all-nighter the night before, worked through the hardest puzzle with total confidence, and on review afterward still found her solution airtight. Marcus pointed out that sleep researchers have repeatedly found that significant sleep deprivation degrades exactly this kind of reasoning by impairing people's ability to notice a missed case, while leaving their felt confidence in the conclusion untouched, so an impaired and an unimpaired solver feel identical from the inside, and Elena has no independent way of knowing which she was that night. He called this a textbook case of higher-order evidence and said she was refusing to take it seriously.

(1) Marcus said Elena should lower her confidence in the solution itself, not just in her general reliability that night, because the sleep evidence specifically undercuts the one thing she was relying on to defend it: that it still looks right to her.
(2) Elena replied that the sleep statistic says nothing about her solution in particular, and that if a bare population-level base rate can override a chain of reasoning she has personally checked step by step, then her beliefs are hostage to whatever correlate of error someone happens to mention next, since one can always be found.
(3) Marcus granted that this would be an absurd policy for arbitrary statistics, but said this one is different because it specifically targets the reliability of the very capacity — error detection under fatigue — that Elena is relying on when she says the solution still looks fine, so deferring to it isn't arbitrary at all.
(4) Elena answered that even granting that, there is a difference between doubting she is well calibrated in general that night and revising this particular belief while it still seems correct; demanding the latter just means she must believe the solution, believe that her evidence doesn't support believing it, and call that combination rational, which is the epistemic akrasia the demand to respect higher-order evidence was supposed to rule out, not commit her to.

Neither budged.
