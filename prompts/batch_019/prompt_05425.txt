I spent almost thirty years running clinical trials before I hung up my lab coat, and Simpson's paradox bit me more than once - pool the data and drug A looks better, split it by site or severity and drug B wins in every subgroup. Back then we just had a rule of thumb: trust the split-up numbers, since they "control for" whatever's lurking. Now that I've been reading up on causal inference in retirement (Pearl's book, mostly), I gather that rule is wrong alot of the time - it apparently matters a great deal whether the lurking variable sits upstream of everything (a confounder), is a thing that both the treatment and the outcome cause (a collider), or sits in between the two as a mediator, like department choice in the old Berkeley admissions case. So dont just tell me "it depends" - write me an essay that gives an actual usable rule for when to trust the pooled numbers versus the stratified ones, defend it properly using that confounder/collider/mediator business, and say what it implies for a messy real case like Berkeley, where adjusting for the mediator is exactly what people still fight about.
