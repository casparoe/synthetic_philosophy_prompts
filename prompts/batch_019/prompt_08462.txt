I'm working on a feature about the porous boundary between early modern science and what we'd now call pseudoscience, and Johannes Kepler keeps complicating the tidy story I originally wanted to tell. Everyone knows, in a trivia-night sort of way, that the discoverer of the laws of planetary motion earned much of his living casting horoscopes and writing yearly astrological prognostications; what interests me is the very deliberate shape of the astrology he actually defended in tracts like "De Fundamentis Astrologiae Certioribus" (around 1601) and "Tertius Interveniens" (1610). In both, Kepler is scathing about the traditional zodiac signs, the system of astrological houses, and the standard planetary "significations" (Mars for war, Venus for love), dismissing all of it as arbitrary human convention with no real foundation in the heavens. Yet he insisted just as firmly on retaining, and even expanding, the doctrine of aspects: he held that specific angles formed between planets (trine, sextile, quintile, and other divisions tied to constructible regular polygons like the pentagon) really do act on earthly and psychological life, because the soul — both our own and a duller, instinctive soul he attributed to the earth itself — recognizes certain geometric proportions as harmonious in much the same pre-rational way the ear recognizes a consonant chord rather than a dissonant one.

A couple of historians of science I've interviewed for the piece treat this as evidence that Kepler managed to put astrology on a more defensible, quasi-physical footing than the tradition he inherited. That claim has started to nag at me, because it seems to assume rather than show that Kepler's chosen angles — and his particular theory of which polygons a non-rational soul can "construct," and therefore respond to — are any less arbitrary, in the relevant sense, than the twelve-fold zodiac he rejected. So I'd like you to make the strongest case you can for the opposite view: that Kepler's own criterion for separating defensible astrology from superstition doesn't actually succeed on its own terms, and that his retained theory of aspects smuggles back in, one level down, exactly the kind of unmotivated stipulation he criticized in the tradition he was reforming. Please engage with his actual reasoning — the appeal to constructible polygons, the analogy with musical consonance, the idea of a soul that acts geometrically without calculating — rather than resting on the general thought that anything called "astrology" must be pseudoscience. You're welcome to note what you take to be Kepler's best available rejoinder along the way, but don't hedge at the end: tell me whether his reform survives the challenge or not, and why.
