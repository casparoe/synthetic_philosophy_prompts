As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I've been handed a story I'm not equipped to report responsibly. A research group claims to have sketched a physical procedure—exploiting a spacetime with what relativists call Malament-Hogarth structure—that would let an observer resolve instances of the halting problem that no ordinary computer ever could; their critics dismiss the whole idea as a category error rather than a discovery. Before I write a word, I need the underlying issue explained properly, not at the encyclopedia level: what does the Church-Turing thesis actually assert, and why is its epistemic status so contested—a disguised definition, a genuine empirical claim about what the physical world permits, or something already as secure as a proven theorem, given that Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and general recursive functions all independently turned out to define the same class of functions? Tellingly, that convergence only fully persuaded Gödel once Turing, rather than Church, supplied it, and I'd like to understand what that episode reveals about the thesis's real standing. Given all that, is an exotic-spacetime device even the kind of thing that could count as evidence against the thesis, or does invoking one just change the subject to a different, physical thesis altogether?
