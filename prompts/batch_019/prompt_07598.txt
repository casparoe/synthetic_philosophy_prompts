So this happened at dinner last week and I still can't decide who won. Two friends of mine, Priya (she's basically ABD in a philosophy PhD, social epistemology) and Marcus (a med resident), ended up arguing for like 40 minutes over whether a mutual friend of ours was a victim of "testimonial injustice" in the specific Miranda Fricker sense, not just "treated badly." Backstory: our friend had joint pain and crushing fatigue for almost three years and got told by a bunch of diffrent doctors that it was "probably anxiety," go see a therapist, before someone finally ran the right panel and it turned out to be lupus.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Priya said this is close to a textbook case: the doctors kept discounting her account of her own body specifically because she read as a young, slightly-anxious-seeming woman, and that's exactly the identity-linked credibility discount Fricker calls testimonial injustice. (2) Marcus said no, that's not prejudice, that's base rates — in patients presenting that way, anxiety really is way more common than lupus, so a doctor shifting credence toward the likelier diagnosis is just doing correct Bayesian reasoning, and you shouldn't call good statistical inference an "injustice" just because it's sometimes wrong. (3) Priya said the base rate isn't a view from nowhere, it's partly a product of decades of under-researching and under-diagnosing autoimmune and pain conditions in women, so treating it as a clean, prejudice-free input just launders the same bias through a number — and anyway Fricker's "systematic" testimonial injustice doesn't require anyone to be consciously biased, it runs through background stereotypes that just feel like common sense. (4) Marcus said that move proves too much: if "this base rate has an ugly history" is enough to make relying on it unjust, basically every diagnostic heuristic in medicine becomes testimonial injustice whenever it turns out wrong for a given patient, and the concept stops picking out anything specific. (5) Priya said even granting the doctors' prior was locally reasonable, there's still a distinctive harm Marcus's version leaves out: our friend was made to doubt her own standing as a reporter of her own experience, and that's the actual wrong Fricker's after. (6) Marcus said that shows nothing either, since anyone misdiagnosed with a rare disease feels doubted and dismissed, prejudiced doctors or not — so unless Priya can show the doctors' confidence in "anxiety" overshot what the real base rate warranted because of the stereotype, rather than just tracking it, she hasn't shown prejudice did any work, only that the diagnosis was wrong in hindsight.

Neither of them gave an inch, and I was too busy eating to referee. Can you go through this move by move, (1) through (6), and say who's actually right at each step rather than just picking an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Priya and Marcus are even disagreeing about the same thing by the end — like, has "testimonial injustice" quietly shifted from being about the doctors' reasoning to being about the harm our friend suffered — and whether there's a version of Marcus's base-rate challenge in (6) that Priya hasn't actually answered.
