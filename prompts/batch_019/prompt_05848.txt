I'm working on a long-form piece about the philosophical scaffolding underneath modern game theory, and I keep circling back to Frank Ramsey, whose 1926 paper "Truth and Probability" relocated probability from Keynes's austere logical relations into something measurable in a person's actual betting behavior. I've just finished Cheryl Misak's biography of him, and what stays with me is how deliberately Ramsey's subjectivism was built to dismantle Keynes's central claim in the Treatise on Probability -- that evidence fixes, via something like direct rational insight, a uniquely correct degree of belief that any sufficiently rational person would converge on given the same facts -- a claim so effectively undermined that Keynes himself, in his obituary notice for Ramsey, seemed to concede the point.

That concession is what makes me want your help with a question Ramsey never lived to ask himself. Contemporary game theory, following John Harsanyi's work on games of incomplete information and Robert Aumann's later "agreeing to disagree" result, generally builds in what is called the common prior assumption: the idea that if two Bayesian agents currently hold different beliefs, this can only be because they have received different evidence, since absent that they would necessarily have started out from the very same prior probability distribution over the relevant states of the world. It is a load-bearing assumption across huge stretches of economics -- rational-expectations models, no-trade theorems, mechanism design -- and it has also been attacked, by economists such as Faruk Gul, as smuggling in an extra, distinctly un-Bayesian requirement that mere coherence, in Ramsey's own sense of just avoiding a sure loss, does nothing to justify.

So: given how allergic Ramsey was to exactly this kind of claim when Keynes made it, how do you think he would have reacted to the common prior assumption? Would he have recognized it as Keynesian logicism sneaking back into a framework built largely to keep it out, or is there something in his own writing -- on induction, say, or on the gap he pressed between mere logical consistency and the messier business of actually forming reasonable beliefs -- that could actually be read as friendly to it? I would like to build a couple of paragraphs of the piece around your answer, so please work through the tension rather than just gesturing at it.
