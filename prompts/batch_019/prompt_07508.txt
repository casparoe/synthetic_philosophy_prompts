I'm taking a democractic theory seminar this semester and we spent a whole class on what my professor called "the boundary problem" - basically the puzzle that a democracy is supposed to let "the people" decide things, but there's no non-circular democratic way to decide who counts as "the people" in the first place, since any vote on that question already needs an electorate to run it. We went through a couple of rival answers (something like the all-affected-interests principle vs. an all-subjected or coercion-based principle - Goodin and Abizadeh maybe? my notes are a mess) but it all stayed pretty abstract, focused on nation-states and border cases.

Afterwards a few of us started arguing about whether any of this actually cashes out for smaller cases, using my apartment building's tenants committee as the test case. Right now voting is one-per-lease, but we've got a landlord who owns three units here but lives across town, several adult kids/partners who live here full time but aren't on any lease, a rotating cast of month-to-month sublet and Airbnb-ish guests, and a superintendent whose whole job is shaped by whatever we vote through even though he doesn't live here. Could you lay out the most important implications of the boundary problem debate for a small, low-stakes case like this? I don't just want "this is a famous unsolved problem," I want your actual take on which considerations should carry the most weight here and what that implies for the landlord, the non-lease residents, and the sublet guests specifically.
