Write a dialogue between William Lloyd Garrison and Frederick Douglass, set shortly after the Dred Scott decision, in which they debate how the U.S. Constitution should be read on the question of slavery — the very dispute that had split them several years before. Garrison should argue that the three-fifths clause, the fugitive-slave clause, the clause barring any federal restriction on the slave trade before 1808, and now Taney's opinion in Dred Scott together fix the Constitution's actual meaning as proslavery, whatever the preamble says, and that continuing to work within such a system only lends it a legitimacy it does not deserve; his conclusion is that the Union must be dissolved. Douglass should take up these same clauses one by one and argue that, read on their own words rather than through the framers' private understandings or the courts' later gloss, none of them requires a proslavery construction, and that the ordinary rules of legal interpretation require reading each part in light of the whole and of the purposes announced in the preamble. Have each character directly rebut the other's strongest point — Garrison's charge that this is willful blindness to what the Constitution has always actually licensed in practice, and Douglass's charge that Garrison's position simply hands slaveholders, on no better evidence than their own self-interest, exactly the interpretive victory they want — so that the dialogue ends, not in agreement, but in a disagreement sharper and better defined than the one they started with.
