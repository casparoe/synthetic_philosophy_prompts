A friend and I spent an evening arguing about the metaphysics of modality and never really settled anything. She defends transworld identity: the view that one and the same individual can literally exist in more than one possible world. I was defending Lewis's counterpart theory, on which no individual exists in more than one world, so that de re modal claims about a thing are made true instead by its counterparts -- sufficiently similar individuals elsewhere.

We got stuck on a version of what's often called Chisholm's Paradox, applied to origin essentialism. An actual wooden table could plausibly have been built from a very slightly different plank; a table built from that plank could itself plausibly have been built from a plank differing from it by the same small margin; and so on. Chain enough such steps together and, by transitivity, the original table apparently could have been built from an entirely different material -- which almost nobody wants to accept. My friend insisted this is worse for me than for her, since mere resemblance needn't be transitive even though identity provably is; I insisted her view has no principled place to break the chain once every individual link is granted. Neither of us budged.

Could you write a dialogue between a convinced counterpart theorist and a convinced transworld-identity theorist working through exactly this puzzle? I'd like both sides to be equally sharp, genuinely responding to each other's strongest moves rather than talking past one another, and I'd like the exchange to reach some real clarity on whether either view is actually better placed to handle the chain, or whether they merely relocate the same difficulty.
