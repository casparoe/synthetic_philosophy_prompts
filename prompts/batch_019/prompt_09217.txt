My grandmother would have said this is exactly the sort of thing I overthink, but here it is. I'm a novelist, and my first book, *Low Ground*, has just won the Hartleigh Prize, a small but well-regarded award for a debut novel. It isn't the kind of thing that makes national news, but it comes with a dinner, and in three weeks I'll be expected to stand up and give a five-minute acceptance speech to about a hundred and fifty people. The room will be a strange mixture: a handful of literary journalists and two of the academics who reviewed the book seriously in print, my agent and editor, and then everyone else — parents, old roommates, half the town I grew up in — who came because they love me and have, at most, glanced at the jacket copy.

I mentioned my nerves about this to a friend who teaches philosophy, and she pointed me toward Kierkegaard's writing on the press and what he calls "the public" — the argument, from *Two Ages*, that a newspaper-saturated age conjures a phantom readership that can praise or savage a writer while answering to no one, and that this "leveling" flattens the very individuality it pretends to celebrate. She also told me about the Corsair affair: how Kierkegaard more or less provoked the fight that got him caricatured in the Copenhagen papers for his uneven trouser legs and his stooped walk, and how he seems never to have gotten over being looked at, even though — or because — he never once stopped wanting to be read. Something smaller but recognizable has happened to me this year: a few serious, careful reviews; one vicious pseudonymous pile-on in a book newsletter's comment section from someone who plainly hadn't finished chapter one; and now a room of people I am supposed to thank for a prize that exists, in the end, because "the public," in exactly Kierkegaard's deflating sense, decided to notice me.

I'd like you to draft the speech. It has to do real thanking — my editor, the judges, the friends who read six drafts — without turning saccharine, and it has to let me say something honest about the strange, compromised gratitude a writer owes "the public" without curdling into either a grievance or a philosophy seminar that leaves my aunt stranded in the third row. I want the Kierkegaard material — the leveling, the phantom public, the Corsair story as a kind of parable — doing real work underneath the speech: audible to the critics in the room, never lectured at anyone. Please keep it to about five minutes read aloud, and let it end in genuine warmth rather than irony.
