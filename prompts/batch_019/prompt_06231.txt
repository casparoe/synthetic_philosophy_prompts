I'm putting together a lecture for my "Enlightenment and Its Discontents" seminar and keep getting stuck on something about Goethe that I've never seen properly explained, so I'm hoping you can help me think it through. He starts off as this Sturm und Drang firebrand—Werther romanticizing suicide over stifling bourgeois convention, Götz von Berlichingen basically telling the local princes where they can shove their authority—and this is the Goethe everyone loves to cite as an avatar of individual feeling and genius against a hidebound establishment. And yet the very same guy spends his whole later career as a court minister for a tiny German duchy, writes stuff like Der Bürgergeneral that basically mocks the French Revolution as rabble getting ideas above their station, and by the time he meets Napoleon at Erfurt he's positively giddy about the whole encounter (Napoleon apparently looked him over and said something like "here, at last, is a man," or however that quote actually goes). That looks to me like about as complete a political reversal as you could ask for: proto-liberal rebel curdling into a throne-and-altar conservative.

My students are definitly going to want a tidier story than "he just mellowed with age," so before I stand up in front of them: is there an actual principled account in the scholarship of why someone with Goethes early sympathies ended up so firmly defending the old aristocratic order, or is it basically fair to say he got comfortable with his sinecure and rationalized backwards from there? Also, as a side note, Schiller was his closest collaborator for the whole Weimar Classicism project, and Schiller's plays (Tell, that Posa speech in Don Carlos) stayed so much more overtly pro-liberty. Were the two of them just quietly agreeing to disagree about politics the entire time, or am I misreading Schiller too?
