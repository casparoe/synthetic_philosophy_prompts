This term I'm taking a decision theory seminar that's cross-listed between the philosophy and economics departments, and this week's material has left me stuck on the "absent-minded driver problem" from Piccione and Rubinstein's 1997 paper in Games and Economic Behavior. As I understand the setup: a driver will pass two intersections on a highway that look identical to her, and because her recall is imperfect she cannot tell, upon arriving at either one, whether it's the first or the second. Exiting at the first is a mistake worth 0, exiting at the second (her actual destination) is worth 4, and driving past both without exiting is worth an intermediate payoff of 1. Treated purely as a planning problem — fixing, once and for all before setting out, a single probability of continuing to be applied at whichever intersection she happens to reach — the expected-payoff-maximizing probability comes out to 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3. Our professor then put a second calculation on the board, attributed to a companion paper by Aumann, Hart, and Perry in the same issue, in which the driver instead reasons "in the moment" from whatever credence she assigns to being at the first intersection versus the second, and arrives at a different, worse-performing policy. She then moved on to the next topic without really explaining why the two approaches come apart.

What I'd like is an explanation that gets at the intuition here, not just the arithmetic. Why is it so hard to pin down "the probability that I am at this intersection right now," given that the driver knows the entire setup, including her own strategy, in advance, and nothing informationally new happens to her at either intersection? Is one of the two ways of forming that in-the-moment credence simply making a mistake, and if so, what kind of mistake — or does the example show something more unsettling: that a rational plan and a rational "in the moment" policy can come apart even for a fully informed agent with no competing interests across time, purely because perfect recall has been given up? I'd also be curious, if it's not too much of a tangent, how you'd situate this next to the Sleeping Beauty problem, since a classmate insisted over lunch that the two puzzles are really the same disagreement wearing different clothes, and I wasn't sure whether to believe her.
