I'm working on a feature about the intellectual ancestry of market-based governance, and I want to examine how Nicolas de Condorcet's own arguments would hold up against futarchy — Robin Hanson's proposal that societies should "vote on values but bet on beliefs." Under futarchy, citizens would democratically choose a measurable welfare metric (GDP or some broader index), then let speculative markets, via conditional bets on rival policies, decide which policy is expected to maximize it, on the premise that market prices aggregate dispersed information more reliably than deliberation or majority voting ever could. Condorcet is the obvious precedent to test this against: his 1785 jury theorem and his broader program of "social mathematics" tried to demonstrate, with actual probability calculus, when and why collective judgment outperforms any individual's, and he risked — and ultimately lost — his life drafting a constitution built on that same rationalist confidence. Working strictly from his own writings and commitments rather than generic Enlightenment-rationalist impressions, tell me whether he would have recognized futarchy as the fulfillment of his project or rejected it, and why. Please surface the point where his own principles pull against each other: his insistence on political equality and his wariness of concentrated factional power sit uneasily beside a system in which influence over policy scales with capital at risk, even as his faith in mathematized collective judgment could just as easily make him the mechanism's most natural champion. Give me his single most plausible verdict, not a survey of possibilities.
