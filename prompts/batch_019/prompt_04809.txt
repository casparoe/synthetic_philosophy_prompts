Okay so this is bugging me from my TOK class (IB theory of knowledge). We did a whole unit on epistemology and got to the Gettier problem, and my teacher used the classic case where Smith has really strong evidence that Jones will get some job and that Jones has 10 coins in his pocket, so Smith ends up believing "the man who gets the job has 10 coins in his pocket" — except Smith himself gets the job instead, and completely coincidentally he ALSO has 10 coins in his pocket, so his belief was true and totally justified but it feels wrong to call it "knowledge," since it was basically luck. Fine, i get that part.

But then I went down a rabbit hole reading about it after class, and it turns out philosophers supposedly patched this pretty quickly right after Gettier's paper came out, just by adding a rule that a belief doesn't count as knowledge if you got to it through any false step along the way (i think it's called the "no false lemmas" thing, or something like that). And that seems like a clean fix to me, because Smith's whole reasoning depended on the false belief that Jones would get the job — so if you just rule out any reasoning that runs through a false premise, doesn't the Gettier problem basically disappear? My friend insists epistemologists are still fighting about this decades later so it cant be that simple, but when I asked her why, she couldn't actually explain it. So is "no false lemmas" basically the accepted fix and my friend's wrong, or is there some other flavor of Gettier case that this rule doesn't actually catch?
