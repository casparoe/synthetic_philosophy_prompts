I spent close to thirty-five years as a field herpetologist, much of that time studying the Ensatina salamanders that ring California's Central Valley, and until I retired I never had much occasion to worry about what a "species" actually is: we had our subspecies designations, our range maps, and Mayr's biological species concept sitting quietly in the background as the obvious right answer. Now that I have the leisure to read philosophy of biology properly, I find myself returning again and again to how uncomfortably that case sits with the textbook definition. Around the ring, each neighboring population interbreeds freely with its immediate neighbors, and yet the two terminal populations, which meet again in the south, behave for all practical purposes as fully distinct species. It is the kind of case that seems to break not merely one particular criterion but the very assumption that "can interbreed with" is a transitive, well-behaved relation in the first place — a point I have been mulling over in occasional correspondence with a former colleague who shares my new enthusiasm for philosophy but remains convinced I am making too much of what he calls a mere "boundary case."

From my reading, philosophers have not converged on a single way of absorbing cases like this. Some, following Ghiselin and Hull, hold that species are not kinds at all but historical individuals, so that a lineage's continuity is more like the spatiotemporal connectedness of an organism's body than like membership in a class defined by shared properties. Others, following Kitcher, are pluralists who argue that several legitimate species concepts can simply coexist, each tracking a different scientific purpose, with no need to crown any one of them as uniquely correct. Still others take the persistence of this disagreement as grounds for eliminativism, denying that "species" picks out any single natural category worth preserving at all. What I would like from you is not another survey of the various species concepts, which I can assemble well enough on my own, but an explanation of what is really at stake philosophically in choosing between these three responses — the individuality thesis, pluralism, and eliminativism — and of why a ring species is diagnostic of that deeper disagreement rather than merely an awkward edge case that a sufficiently clever definition could eventually patch over.
