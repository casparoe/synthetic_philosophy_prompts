I TA for a graduate course that sits at the intersection of philosophy and political economy, and this term's unit on alternatives to majority-rule democracy used Robin Hanson's futarchy proposal as the central case study of an "epistemic" institution: one that tries to hand factual questions off to markets while leaving value questions to ordinary politics. I've followed the arguments about it on and off since I first ran into Hanson's blog as an undergraduate, so it's a topic I care about, but I'd like an independent read on this particular submission before I settle on a grading key for the rest of the stack.

This was the fourth of five questions on the take-home midterm, worth 20 of the exam's 100 points, split 8/8/4 across its three parts:

Explain the two-stage structure of Hanson's futarchy proposal, often summarized by the slogan "vote on values, but bet on beliefs." Your answer should (a) describe how a specific policy is actually selected once the value metric has been fixed [8 points]; (b) explain Hanson's argument for why the scheme is comparatively resistant to price manipulation [8 points]; and (c) state what you take to be the single strongest objection to using futarchy for real-world policymaking, briefly assessing whether the argument in (b) does anything to blunt it [4 points].

Here is the student's answer in full:

Under futarchy, the legislature (or the electorate, at whatever scale the scheme is applied) first votes on a single welfare measure, such as an index weighted heavily toward GDP — this is the "values" stage, and it is settled in the ordinary democratic way. Once the measure is fixed, any proposed policy is evaluated by opening two conditional speculative markets: one that pays out according to the welfare measure if the policy is adopted, and one that pays out according to the measure if it is not. Whichever of the two markets attracts the greater volume of trading is taken to represent the stronger consensus among speculators, and its associated policy is the one enacted. This is the "beliefs" stage, meant to replace legislative argument about means (though not about ends) with a mechanism that rewards accurate forecasting rather than rhetoric or interest-group pressure.

Hanson is aware that such a scheme invites manipulation: a wealthy actor could try to move the price of a policy they favor by trading against its true expected value. He argues that this is not a serious problem, because in a sufficiently liquid market any attempt to push a price away from the collective estimate is immediately self-defeating — informed traders simply take the other side of the manipulator's position until the price snaps back, which is why Hanson thinks manipulation essentially cannot succeed once enough traders are watching.

The strongest objection I can think of is that any welfare metric simple enough for a market to price will leave out things we plainly care about — distributive fairness, rights, liberties, anything not easily monetized — so a policy could win the conditional-market comparison while making the country worse off along dimensions the index never captured. This is really a Goodhart's Law worry: press hard enough on a proxy and it comes apart from the target it was meant to track. The manipulation-resistance argument in (b) does not touch this problem at all, since it only concerns the integrity of prices within a market, not whether the market is pricing the right thing in the first place; a perfectly manipulation-proof futarchy would remain just as exposed to this objection.
