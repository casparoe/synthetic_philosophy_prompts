I am currently taking an upper-level seminar cross-listed between the philosophy and economics departments on rational choice and social epistemology, and this week we worked through Harsanyi's type-space framework for games of incomplete information alongside Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result. From the lectures, I came away with the impression that the common prior assumption is essentially the requirement that two rational agents begin their reasoning from the same total stock of background information about the world, and that Aumann's theorem builds on this by showing that any two such agents who each report their fully updated, honest probability estimate of some event to one another cannot end up disagreeing, since a shared informational starting point plus a truthful exchange of final credences is supposed to be jointly sufficient to force their numbers into line.

A few of us in my study group tried to apply this outside the seminar room by considering two well-regarded climate economists on a conference panel, each working from a different underlying model and different data sources, who state a fully updated, honest bottom-line probability that a given emissions target will be met after hearing the other's number, yet still land on noticeably different figures. Most of us concluded that, given the common prior assumption, this sort of persistent expert disagreement can only be explained by a lapse in rationality on the part of at least one of the two economists, since two agents who share a common prior and have openly exchanged their honest final estimates are not supposed to be able to keep disagreeing. Before I lean on this argument in my response paper, I wanted to check: is that really what the common prior assumption is doing in Aumann's result, and does persistent disagreement between two apparently careful reasoners actually amount to the kind of thing the assumption rules out?
