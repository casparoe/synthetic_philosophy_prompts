I'm a retired physicist who has spent the last couple of years working through the classics of analytic philosophy with a small reading group of old friends. We recently covered Derek Parfit's discussion in Reasons and Persons of what he calls rational irrationality, built around the hitchhiker case: dying of thirst in the desert, you can only get a ride if the driver is confident you will pay her $1,000 once safely in town; she is an excellent judge of character, so bluffing will not work. Someone who, once safe, sees no further reason to hand over money that can no longer buy back the rescue will be detected and left to die; someone disposed to pay regardless gets driven in. Since paying, once safe, cannot cause the earlier rescue, a purely self-interested calculator seemingly has no reason to do it -- yet exactly this incapacity is what strands such a person in the desert.

Two of the group, Renata and Oskar, got into a disagreement that the rest of us could not settle. Renata argued that the case simply refutes the idea that rationality means doing whatever is locally in your interest at each moment, since that policy is lethal here; the real lesson is that you should want to become a genuine payer, not a calculator. Oskar granted that you should want the disposition to pay, but denied this makes the resulting payment itself rational: Parfit's point, on his reading, is precisely that it can be rational to make yourself disposed to act irrationally later, and the disposition and the act it produces need not share a verdict. Renata replied that this split cannot hold: either you notice, at the moment of paying, that it serves no purpose, and renege -- in which case the disposition was never real, and a judge that good would have stranded you regardless -- or you pay anyway, in which case whatever moved you functioned in you exactly like a reason, and refusing it that name is empty stipulation. Oskar answered that plenty of cases share this shape without contradiction: a country's deterrence policy requires it to actually retaliate if attacked, even though retaliating serves no further purpose once deterrence has already succeeded or failed; we do not infer that retaliating is itself rational just because holding the policy was, and the hitchhiker's payment is no different.

I would value your assessment. Please go through the four moves in order, saying who has the better of it at each step and why, and then tell me whether there really is a stable gap between its being rational to become disposed to do X and its being rational to do X once the moment arrives, whether Renata's collapse argument defeats it, or whether both of them are overlooking some further distinction.
