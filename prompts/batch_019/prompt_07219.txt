In my seminar on formal epistemology and AI safety, we covered the "tiling agents" program and the obstacle it runs into via Löb's theorem: no consistent proof system can affirm its own "reflection schema"—roughly, "whatever I can prove is true"—in full generality, since a system that could prove this for every sentence could thereby prove anything at all, including outright falsehoods. This blocks an agent from ever fully certifying, from the inside, that a successor reasoning with an equally strong proof system can be trusted. I'm still working out how much weight this "Löbian obstacle" should carry for anyone trying to design self-modifying systems. Write a short dialogue between two agent-foundations researchers who disagree about it: one holds that it marks a genuine, irreducible limit that safe self-modification must be engineered around (through devices like hierarchies of successively weaker proof systems, or "Vingean reflection," where a smarter future self's conclusions are trusted without being re-derived); the other holds that it is an artifact of demanding deductive certainty, one that dissolves once trust is recast probabilistically, as in logical induction. Have them press on each other's strongest points—including whether the probabilistic route genuinely escapes the obstacle or just relocates it—so that neither comes off as the obvious winner.
