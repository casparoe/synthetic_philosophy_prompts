And here is the student's answer, copied over exactly as they wrote it:

"Gödel's argument starts from the idea of a 'positive' property - not positive in the sense of ordinary moral goodness, but a purely formal notion satisfying two axioms: (1) for any property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive, and (2) any property entailed by a positive property is itself positive. From these two axioms alone you can already show that the positive properties are jointly consistent, i.e. possibly co-instantiated. Gödel then defines an individual as 'God-like' if it has all the positive properties, and adds as a further axiom that being God-like is itself a positive property, which guarantees that a God-like being is possible. To get from possible to necessary, Gödel introduces the notion of an essence: a property is an essence of an individual if it entails every other property that individual has. He shows that if something is God-like, God-likeness is its essence. He then defines 'necessary existence' as the property of having an essence that is necessarily instantiated, and adds a final axiom that necessary existence is itself positive. Since God-likeness entails necessary existence, and a God-like being is possible, it follows that a God-like being necessarily exists - but only given a modal logic strong enough to let us infer, from 'possibly necessarily p', that 'necessarily p'. This is exactly the inference pattern licensed by the S5 axiom, which is why every commentator stresses that the argument is not neutral on the choice of modal logic.

The most famous objection is Plantinga's: he showed that Gödel's own axioms entail a 'modal collapse', meaning that if the axioms are true then every truth is a necessary truth and there is no genuine contingency in the world at all, which strikes almost everyone as an unacceptable price to pay even for a successful proof of God's existence. Anderson's well-known 1990 response to this was to weaken the axiom that necessary existence is positive, and this emendation is generally judged to remove the collapse while preserving a recognisable version of the proof, though at the cost of making the resulting notion of a 'positive property' considerably less intuitive than Gödel's original."
