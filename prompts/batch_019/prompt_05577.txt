I'm a PhD student in linguistics working on the semantics and pragmatics of conditional sentences, and I keep running into Lewis's triviality results as the reason so many philosophers of language deny that indicative conditionals express ordinary, truth-evaluable propositions. I can follow the algebraic proof that a proposition "if A, then B" cannot, in general, have its probability equal the conditional probability P(B|A) — the so-called Equation — across a class of credence functions closed under conditionalizing on new evidence, except in some degenerate special case. What the derivation hasn't given me is any real feel for why this has to be true. Could you explain, at a conceptual rather than a formal level, what actually goes wrong when one tries to make a single fixed proposition's probability track a conditional probability in this way?
