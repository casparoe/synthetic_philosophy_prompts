So a couple weeks ago I killed off a pretty major character in the family drama I'm drafting, sent the chapter to my writers group, and two people actually cried — one of them, Meg, told me at brunch the next day she was still a little sad about it, over someone who (a) never existed and (b) she knew never existed the entire time she was reading. My friend Priya, who did a philosophy MA before jumping ship to library science, said this is basically "the paradox of fiction" — she mentioned an old paper by a guy named Radford about crying over Anna Karenina, and then went off on how there are two totally different camps about what's actually happening in Meg's head. One camp (she called it something like Walton's view, with the "Charles and the slime monster" example) says Meg didn't feel real grief at all, just a kind of make-believe stand-in emotion generated by playing along with the fiction — like how someone watching a horror movie feels "quasi-fear," not real fear, because they don't actually bolt for the exit. The other camp (the "thought theory," I think she called it) says no, it's completely ordinary, garden-variety grief, the exact same stuff you'd feel for a real person, just triggered by entertaining a thought instead of believing something true, so theres nothing fake or "quasi" about it at all.

I'd like to actually get this properly instead of half-remembering Priya's brunch monologue, so could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers holding these two views, arguing specifically about Meg and my dead character? I want real back-and-forth — each one should have to respond to the other's strongest point, not just restate their own position past each other, and I especially want them to hash out what would even count as evidence for one side (is a lump in the throat or an actual tear "real" grief, and who gets to adjudicate that?). Don't give me a tidy resolution at the end — I don't think this one has one — and keep it to roughly a page, more a sharp exchange than a full essay.
