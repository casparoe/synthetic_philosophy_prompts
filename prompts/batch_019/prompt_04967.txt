I'm taking a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and this week's session on adaptationism left me wanting to see the debate actually argued out rather than just summarized in lecture. We read Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper, "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm," alongside the chapter in Dennett's *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* where he pushes back, and what struck me most wasn't the biology so much as how much weight the whole argument rests on an architectural metaphor that turns out to be shakier than it first appears. Apparently art historians have objected that the relevant features of the basilica are technically pendentives rather than spandrels at all, and the example Gould and Lewontin lean on most heavily -- the mosaics that fill those spaces -- were quite deliberately designed, which seems to cut uncomfortably close to the adaptationist point they are trying to refute. I also can't quite decide whether "exaptation" is doing independent conceptual work or whether it just quietly readmits selection through the back door the moment a structural byproduct turns out to be useful.

For our final assignment we have been given free rein on format, and I would rather write a dialogue than another essay. Could you write one between two evolutionary biologists -- one a committed adaptationist, the other a defender of the Gould-Lewontin critique -- staged as though they are literally standing beneath the dome of San Marco, arguing while they look up at the mosaics? I would like them to be evenly matched, genuinely responding to what the other has just said rather than restating their own position, and I would like the exchange to work through at least the pendentive objection, the worry about exaptation collapsing back into adaptationism, and what a genuinely non-adaptationist "null hypothesis" for a trait would even look like in practice, since that last part is what I most struggle to picture concretely. It does not need to end with either side conceding -- I would honestly find a tidy resolution a bit suspicious, given how unresolved this argument still seems to be among biologists and philosophers of biology alike.
