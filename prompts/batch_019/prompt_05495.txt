I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent most of my career in low-temperature physics, cryostats, calorimetry, that sort of thing), and for the last year or so I've been filling the science-shaped hole in my retirement with philosophy books, completely undisciplined, whatever grabs me off the library shelf next. Right now that's Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits, and his anti-luminosity argument against the KK principle has properly gotten under my skin, more than any actual physics problem managed to in forty years of work. My book club (three other retired engineers, god help us) had a go at it over coffee last week and we mostly just went in circles, so I wrote up my own take below and would like a harder-nosed read than they gave me.

As I understand it, Williamson wants to show that no interesting condition is "luminous" - there's no non-trivial state such that whenever your in it, you're automatically in a position to know you're in it. He runs this with someone who feels cold at dawn while the temperature creeps up all day until their clearly hot by evening, sliced into a long series of moments close enough together that you can't feel the difference between neighbours. Because your discrimination is that inexact, knowing you feel cold at any one moment requires a "margin for error", you must also actually feel cold at the very next moment, since otherwise your belief could too easily have been wrong. Chain that margin forward from a moment when you're clearly cold to one when you're clearly hot and you get a straight contradiction, so somewhere in between you must feel cold without knowing it. Run the same trick on knowledge itself, treating "knowing that p" as the target condition, and you get the general result: you can know p without being in any position to know that you know it, which sinks the KK principle.

Here's my trouble with it, and it's a very physicist-shaped trouble: this reads to me like error propagation wearing a costume, and in four decades of taking measurements I never once concluded that an instrument stops "knowing" its own reading past some sharp threshold, you just report a shrinking error bar. The whole induction seems to need the margin for error to be one fixed size across the entire cold-to-hot range, but every real instrument I've calibrated has uneven resolution depending on where in its range you are, so why can't the required margin shrink to nothing exactly as you approach the crossover, blocking the chain right there? And more basically, doesn't the whole "paradox" only bite because knowledge is being treated as all-or-nothing, when anyone who spends their career on measurement would just treat it as graded or interval-valued from the outset? I'd guess both of these are well-worn dead ends that any epistemology grad student could shoot down in a sentence, so don't be gentle about it - tell me first whether I've even got Williamson's argument right, and then exactly where each objection goes wrong (or, if by some miracle one doesn't, tell me that too).
