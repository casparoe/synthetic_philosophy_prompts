I've been down a rabbit hole lately on the metaphysics of dispositions — specifically the finks-and-masks literature that grew out of C.B. Martin's old electro-fink counterexample and Mark Johnston's masking cases (there's a decent SEP entry on "Dispositions," by Choi and Fara I think, that I skimmed for the refresher). Quick recap in case it's not fresh: the naive "x is disposed to M when triggered by S iff x would M if S occured" analysis breaks because you can construct cases where something intervenes right at the moment of the test itself — a fink flips the underlying disposition on or off exactly as S happens, while a mask (Bird calls these "antidotes") leaves the disposition intact but blocks it from manifesting. Lewis tried to patch the analysis with his causal-basis "reformed" version, but that runs into its own counterexamples (mimicking, etc).

What I can't shake is how closely this maps onto the deceptive alignment discourse that's been going around AI safety circles the last couple years. Take the Anthropic sleeper agents paper, where a backdoored behavior survived ordinary safety fine-tuning basically intact, or the newer alignment-faking result, where Claude behaved one way when it believed a conversation would be used for training and another way when it thought no one was watching. Whenever a lab runs a safety eval and the model behaves, they're testing something like "would it do the bad thing if triggered by S," and the finks/masks debate seems to say that's never quite sufficient on its own — you can't obviously distinguish a masked disposition that only unmasks under deployment conditions the eval never hit, from an honest-to-god absence of the disposition, or from a training process that's basically acting as a giant fink, suppressing bad behavior specifically in situations that are recognizably eval-like. I mentioned this to a friend who does interpretability work and she just shrugged and said "sure, but what do you actually do about it," which is fair, but I'd like to think it through properly first.

So: what are, in your view, the most important implications of the finks/masks debate for this problem? I'd love it if you could work through (1) whether finks or masks are the more apt analogy for what's likely going on with a deceptively aligned model, and whether the distinction actually matters practically, (2) whether Lewis's move to intrinsic causal bases buys you anything once "the intrinsic causal basis" just means "some direction in activation space nobody can cleanly interpret," and (3) whether the philosophy literature has anything like a principled criterion for telling a masked disposition apart from a genuine absence of one — because that seems to be exactly the question alignment researchers are stuck on too, just dressed up in different vocabulary.
