I've been reading on my own about Leibniz's claim that this is "the best of all possible worlds," and in particular about his so-called principle of plenitude: the idea that God chooses the world containing the greatest possible variety and abundance of creatures that can still be held together by a simple, harmonious set of laws. What strikes me is that Leibniz also ranks his monads in a hierarchy, from the dim, confused perceptions of the simplest substances up to the rational "spirits" capable of self-aware thought and virtue, who alone belong to what he calls the City of God. Separately, I've come across the "repugnant conclusion" from modern population ethics: the disturbing result that, if you simply add up total well-being, an enormous population of people whose lives are barely worth living can outscore a much smaller population living excellent lives, purely because there are so many more of them. Putting these together, I can't decide whether Leibniz's own principles would actually commit him to something like the repugnant conclusion, since he does want reality to contain as much being and variety as possible, or whether his emphasis on rank, harmony, and the special worth of rational spirits would give him solid grounds to reject it. Working strictly from what Leibniz himself argued, rather than from his popular reputation as a starry-eyed optimist, what do you think his verdict would have been, and why?
