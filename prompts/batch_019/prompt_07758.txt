I write a science-and-ideas column, and while chasing an unrelated story about cochlear implants and cross-modal plasticity I came across the work of a vision scientist whose lab performs cataract-removal surgery on congenitally blind children in a low-resource setting and then studies, almost day by day, how their newly functioning eyes learn to make sense of the world. Reading through her group's papers, I realized the program amounts to a running, real-world version of Molyneux's question: the puzzle Locke's correspondent William Molyneux posed to him in the early 1690s, asking whether a person blind from birth, having learned to tell a cube from a globe by touch, could distinguish the two by sight alone the instant vision is restored, before ever touching them again. Locke and Molyneux both said no; Berkeley agreed, but for the more radical reason that he took visual and tactile ideas of shape to be fundamentally heterogeneous, connected only by learned association; Leibniz thought the answer could be yes, but only for a newly sighted person clever enough to reason his way there from the abstract geometry of edges and curves, rather than simply seeing the answer at a glance. For the interview, I want to press on what her data can and cannot actually settle about all this, rather than have her simply narrate the history.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for her? I'd like at least one question, plus a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, on each of the following: first, the fact that her subjects' cross-modal matching is typically close to chance for the first several days after surgery and then improves quickly, rather than being simply present or absent from the outset, and what that gradual-but-fast trajectory should lead us to conclude about the original yes/no question; second, the standard objection that newly sighted children are also just bad at parsing visual shape for reasons that have nothing to do with touch -- motion and coarse color perception typically return well before fine form perception does -- and how she rules out that her cross-modal failures are simply low-level shape-perception failures being misread as evidence about cross-modal transfer; and third, whether anything in the children's behavior resembles Leibniz's proposed route to a "yes," working out the match by reasoning about surfaces and edges, as opposed to fast associative perceptual learning, and how she would even distinguish those two possibilities in subjects too young to articulate what they're doing. Please end the list with one question asking what single result, if it turned up in her data next year, would make her abandon her current reading of what these children's experience shows about Molyneux's problem.
