I spent most of my working life as a field geologist and palaeontologist, and I've only really been reading philosophy properly since I retired a few years back, so go easy on me if I mangle some of the terminology (and sorry in advance for typos, my typing hasnt improved with age). A good chunk of my career overlapped with the argument over what actually killed off the dinosaurs at the K-Pg boundary - the asteroid-impact camp versus the Deccan Traps volcanism camp, everyone arguing about which explanation best accounted for the iridium spike, the shocked quartz, the crater, the timing of it all. You obviously can't rerun the late Cretaceous, so all anyone could really do was ask which story best explained the trace evidence and argue from there. When I stumbled into the philosophy of science literature I was pleased to discover there's a proper name for that: "inference to the best explanation," which I gather Gilbert Harman put into circulation sometime in the 60s, and which Peter Lipton later refined with his distinction between the "likeliest" explanation and the "loveliest" one.

Here's what's bugging me though. Digging further back I found that Charles Peirce had already been writing about something he called "abduction" decades before Harman, with a little pattern that goes something like: a surprising fact C is observed; but if A were true, C would be a matter of course; so there's reason to suspect A is true. I brought this up with an old colleague from my department and he just said "sounds like the same idea with a fancier hat on," which is exactly the kind of comment that annoys me, because if it really were the same idea there'd presumably be no need for two separate literatures to grow up around it, each with its own textbook chapter and its own defenders.

So could you actually compare Peirce's abduction with the more general modern idea of inference to the best explanation for me? Specifically: in what ways is Peirce's original notion narrower, or different in kind, from IBE as Harman and Lipton later set it out - is one more about generating hypotheses and the other about justifying belief in them, say - and is there a real, substantive difference between the two or is it mostly a difference of packaging and emphasis? And since you'll know the primary material better than I do at this point, which of the two frameworks, if either, comes closer to actually describing what a working scientist is doing when they favour one historical explanation over a rival on the strength of the physical traces it left behind?
