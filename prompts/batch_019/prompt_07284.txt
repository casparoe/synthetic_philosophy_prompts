My school's student council is rewriting its election bylaws this year, and a few of us on the rules committee have been arguing about whether to replace our plain plurality vote with something "harder to game." While researching for the proposal, I ran across Arrow's impossibility theorem and then the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, which as far as I can tell says that once there are three or more candidates, every reasonable non-dictatorial voting rule can be strategically manipulated by some voter under some set of true preferences. My AP Economics teacher confirmed the theorem is legitimate but admitted that the only manipulation examples she had ever worked through by hand were for plain plurality or the simple Borda count, where insincere voting is fairly intuitive (compromise toward your second choice under plurality, or bury your favorite's biggest rival at the bottom of your Borda ranking).

I would like to see this happen somewhere less obvious than that. Could you construct a full, concrete example under either the Coombs method (repeatedly eliminate whichever candidate is ranked last by the most voters, until some candidate has a majority of first-place votes) or ranked pairs / Tideman's method (tally every pairwise majority comparison, then lock them in from the largest margin to the smallest, skipping any comparison that would create a cycle with what's already locked in) — whichever lets you build the cleaner case? Please specify a full set of voters with fully specified true preference rankings over three or four candidates, show the winner when everyone votes sincerely, and then show a single voter's insincere ranking that changes the outcome to something that voter strictly prefers according to their real preferences, while everyone else's ballot stays exactly the same.

Finally, walk me through why this really is an instance of what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem guarantees must exist somewhere, rather than a coincidence of the specific numbers you chose, and give me your honest take on whether the information the manipulator needs here — essentially, correctly anticipating everyone else's ballots — is realistic for a roughly two-hundred-student election, or whether that assumption is doing most of the work in making the manipulation feel like a real threat.
