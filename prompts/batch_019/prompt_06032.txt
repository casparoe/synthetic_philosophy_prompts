I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, currently drafting a dissertation section on how the verification principle constrains religious language, and I've settled on A. J. Ayer as my central case study. On my reading of Language, Truth and Logic, any statement admitting no possible empirical confirmation or disconfirmation -- talk of God, of Bradley's Absolute, of a soul that outlasts the body -- comes out not merely false but strictly meaningless, a pseudo-proposition rather than a genuine one. That is what makes Ayer's famous 1988 episode so useful for my argument: after his heart stopped in hospital, he described, in an essay he wrote about the experience, a vivid episode involving a red light and some sense of beings responsible for administering the universe, and he said afterward that it had genuinely shaken his lifelong confidence that death is simply annihilation.

The claim I raised with my advisor last week is that Ayer's own willingness to revise his credence about survival on the strength of a private, unrepeatable experience amounts to a tacit admission that the question was meaningful all along -- which would make his deathbed reasoning a live counterexample to the very criterion he spent his career defending, since a consistent verificationist has no business letting an unshareable hallucination move him on a question he had officially classified as nonsense. My advisor looked skeptical but didn't have time to explain why before our meeting ended. Could you help me work out whether this "self-refutation" reading actually holds up and, if it doesn't, sketch a more accurate way of relating the biographical episode to the verification principle for my draft?
