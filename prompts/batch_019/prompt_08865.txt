I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist keeps doing the right thing without ever acting from one clean motive — duty, self-interest, and plain affection for the people involved all seem to pull in the same direction at once — and I'd like the philosophical scaffolding behind him to be sound rather than merely impressionistic. Kant held that only the motive of duty confers moral worth on an action, which seems to imply that mixed motives dilute or even cancel that worth outright, but this "overdetermination problem" has drawn serious responses: Barbara Herman's suggestion that duty can operate as a background "limiting condition" rather than the motive actually operative in the moment, and Julia Markovits's proposal that what matters is whether an agent's real motivating reasons coincide in content with the reasons that justify the act. Nomy Arpaly's discussion of Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim escape slavery despite his own explicit, mistaken conviction that doing so is wrong, complicates the picture even further. Could you walk me through how these accounts actually disagree with one another about what confers moral worth, rather than simply restating Kant's shopkeeper example?
