Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through Hume's first Enquiry, and Section X, "Of Miracles," has given me more trouble than the rest of the essay combined. Everyone quotes the famous conclusion about testimony whose falsehood would have to be "more miraculous" than the fact attested, but I am stuck on an earlier paragraph where Hume tries to spell out, in more quantitative terms, how a "wise man" actually weighs evidence:

"A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence. In such conclusions as are founded on an infallible experience, he expects the event with the last degree of assurance, and regards his past experience as a full proof of the future existence of that event. In other cases, he proceeds with more caution: He weighs the opposite experiments: He considers which side is supported by the greater number of experiments: To that side he inclines, with doubt and hesitation; and when at last he fixes his judgment, the evidence exceeds not what we properly call probability. All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

What I cannot work out is what sort of calculation Hume actually has in mind. Taken literally, "deduct the smaller number from the greater" sounds like plain subtraction of counts, not a ratio, a frequency, or anything like a modern likelihood ratio; and his "experiments" seem to mean individual instances of experience rather than designed trials. Is Hume committed to a genuinely additive, non-probabilistic notion of evidential weight here, and if so, does it sit comfortably with the more multiplicative-sounding language of "proof against proof" that he uses later, when the miracles of rival religions are said to cancel one another out? I would welcome help reconstructing exactly what model of evidence is operating in this paragraph, and whether it holds up.
