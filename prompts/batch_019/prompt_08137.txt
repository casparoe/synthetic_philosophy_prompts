I'm a retired analytical chemist, and after forty-odd years thinking about combustion chemistry for a living, I've somehow landed in a little philosophy discussion group that meets at our local library, mostly other retirees. We just spent a couple of sessions on al-Ghazali's *Incoherence of the Philosophers*, and the chapter on causation genuinely stopped me in my tracks — the bit where he argues that when cotton touches fire and burns, it isn't really the fire doing the burning, since all we ever actually observe is the two events occuring together, never any necessary connection between them; on his view it's God producing the burning directly each time, and the fire is just "customarily" present alongside it.

One of the other regulars, a retired mechanical engineer, is now going around telling everyone that Ghazali basically scooped David Hume by six or seven hundred years, since Hume's whole argument against necessary connection between cause and effect — and his appeal to "custom" as the real source of our expectation that fire will keep burning cotton — is, in his words, "the same move minus the theology." A couple of the others in the group seem to have latched onto that too, and I'll admit that on the surface it's hard to argue with: they're both denying we can observe or rationally derive any necessity linking cause and effect, and they even both reach for the exact same word, "custom," to explain why we go on expecting the pattern to hold. So, honestly: is it fair to say Hume essentially just secularized an argument al-Ghazali had already worked out, and that the only reason our intro philosophy courses treat Hume as the towering figure on causation, rather than a medieval Muslim theologian, is basically Eurocentrism baked into the canon? I'd like to have a proper answer ready before our next meeting, because at this rate the whole group is going to walk in already agreeing with the engineer.
