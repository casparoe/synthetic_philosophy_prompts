I'm working on a long-form piece on Isaac Newton's private theological writings for a science-and-ideas publication, which sent me down a rabbit hole into the manuscripts the Newton Project has digitized. One thing that strikes me is how methodologically serious Newton was as an unorthodox theologian: in the unpublished "An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture," which he sent to Locke around 1690 intending anonymous publication in Holland before losing his nerve and withdrawing it, he argued on manuscript and patristic grounds that 1 John 5:7 (the "three heavenly witnesses") and 1 Timothy 3:16 were later insertions fabricated to shore up Trinitarian doctrine — essentially anticipating the modern textual-critical verdict on the so-called Comma Johanneum. Yet in his "Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel, and the Apocalypse of St. John," he treated the book of Daniel as straightforwardly authentic sixth-century-BC prophecy, reliable enough that he used its numerology to calculate that the world could not end before AD 2060.

Here is the wrinkle Newton never had to confront: the mainstream of historical-critical scholarship now dates the visions that dominate the second half of Daniel (roughly chapters 7 through 12) to the Maccabean crisis of the 160s BC, on grounds that look a lot like Newton's own — late linguistic forms, garbled Persian and Babylonian court history, and a suspicious pivot from pinpoint accuracy about Antiochus IV Epiphanes to vague, unfulfilled generalities immediately after his reign, the classic fingerprint of prophecy written after the fact. In short, the same species of philological argument Newton was willing to level against the New Testament, in service of his anti-Trinitarian theology, is essentially what modern scholarship levels against Daniel as predictive prophecy.

So, reasoning from Newton's actual documented habits of argument rather than simply calling him a hypocrite: how do you think he would have responded had someone put the Maccabean-dating case to him directly? Would his own evidentiary standards have pushed him toward some grudging accommodation, or can you reconstruct a principled distinction, available from within his own thought and not just special pleading, between exposing scribal corruption in a text's transmission and admitting that a "prophetic" book's core visions were composed after their supposed fulfillment? I am less interested in a verdict on whether Newton would have been right than in your best reconstruction of the actual argument he would have made.
