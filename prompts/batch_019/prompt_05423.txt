I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of metaphysics and philosophy of physics, and for the past few weeks a reading group I'm part of has been working through the literature that grew out of Max Black's 1952 paper on the identity of indiscernibles. One thing that strikes me is that Black's own piece is written as a dialogue between two speakers debating whether a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, a few miles apart, is conceivable — yet almost every discussion of it I come across stops at that opening exchange and never follows the debate into its more technical later developments. In particular, I keep getting stuck on Simon Saunders' notion of "weak discernibility": the claim that even if two objects share every monadic (non-relational) property, they can still be discerned by an irreflexive relation that holds between them but that neither bears to itself — each sphere being exactly two miles from the other, say — and that this is enough to rescue a suitably weakened version of Leibniz's principle. What I can't settle for myself is whether this genuinely rescues anything Leibniz would recognize as his own principle, or whether it just redefines "indiscernible" so that the principle comes out true by stipulation.

Could you write me a dialogue, picking up roughly where Black's two speakers left off, between a metaphysician who thinks weak discernibility is a real vindication of the identity of indiscernibles and one who thinks it is a face-saving retreat that changes the subject rather than winning the original argument? I want both of them to be genuinely sharp and to engage with each other's actual moves rather than talk past one another: the defender should have to answer the objection that weak discernibility is cheap — that almost any two distinct things will stand in some irreflexive relation or other, so the principle risks being trivially true of everything — and the skeptic should have to answer the charge that restricting the principle to monadic properties in the first place is already a substantive and question-begging choice. Somewhere in the exchange, I'd also like them to take up whether quantum statistics helps either side: whether two fermions in a singlet state, standardly said to be weakly discernible via their opposite spins thanks to the Pauli exclusion principle, and two bosons in a fully symmetric joint state, which arguably cannot be weakly discerned by any physical relation at all, show that Black's original spheres were either too easy or too hard a case to settle anything general. The dialogue doesn't need to end with either side winning outright, but it should make clear exactly where, at bottom, the two of them locate their disagreement.
