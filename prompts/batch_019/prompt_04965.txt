Here's the question as I posed it: "Explain Bertrand's paradox -- the three classical constructions for picking a 'random' chord of a circle, and the probability each one assigns to the chord being longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle -- and explain why they disagree. Then take a stand: does the paradox show that the principle of indifference is simply incoherent as a rule for assigning probabilities, or can it be rescued in some form (e.g., by imposing invariance requirements, as Jaynes tries to)? Defend a verdict, 3-4 paragraphs, max one page."

And here is the student's answer, copied verbatim:

"Bertrand's paradox concerns the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle. There are three classic ways of making 'random chord' precise. First, pick two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and connect them; by symmetry we can fix one endpoint and ask where the second falls relative to the arc between the triangle's other two vertices, which is a 120° arc out of 360°, giving probability 1/3. Second, pick a radius of the circle at random and then a point uniformly along that radius, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius; the chord is longer than the triangle's side exactly when the point is within R/2 of the center, which happens with probability 1/2. Third, pick a point uniformly inside the disk and let it be the chord's midpoint; the chord exceeds the triangle's side iff the point lands inside the concentric circle of radius R/2, and since area scales with the square of the radius, that inner region is 1/4 of the total area, giving probability 1/4. So depending on which procedure cashes out 'at random,' we get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 for the very same event.

These disagree because each procedure stipulates uniformity over a different parameter -- the angle of the second endpoint, the distance from the center, the position of the midpoint in the disk -- and a distribution that's uniform in one of these parameters generally isn't uniform in another, since the map between them is nonlinear. 'Choose a chord at random' just doesn't pick out a unique measure on the space of chords; there are infinitely many equally natural-sounding randomizing procedures, and the principle of indifference gives no way to choose among them, since it presupposes we already have a privileged partition into equipossible cases, and here we have several rival partitions that each look privileged from inside their own construction.

This looks like bad news for the principle: a rule that outputs different answers depending on an arbitrary choice of parametrization isn't really outputting anything, so as stated the principle can't be doing the epistemic work Laplace and Keynes wanted from it. Jaynes tried to save something in this vicinity by adding invariance requirements the bare principle doesn't include: since nothing in the problem fixes the circle's size, location, or orientation, he required the solution to be invariant under translations, rotations, and rescalings of the figure, and showed that this pins down a unique distribution over chords. That distribution matches the random-midpoint construction above, vindicating 1/2 over the other two candidates, and Jaynes even backed this up experimentally by tossing straws onto a grid of circles and tallying where they landed.

On balance, I don't think the bare principle of indifference survives Bertrand's paradox: 'assign equal probability to each alternative absent a reason to favor one over another' isn't a well-formed instruction once the alternatives can be sliced up in inequivalent ways, and the principle itself contains nothing that tells you which slicing to use. Jaynes's move shows you can sometimes recover a unique, non-arbitrary distribution, but only by importing extra information -- the symmetries of the physical setup -- that the naive principle never had on its own. Since almost no real case of ignorance comes with that kind of rich symmetry, the paradox is less a solved problem than a demonstration of how little the unadorned principle can do; more generally, it shows that the Kolmogorov axioms alone are not sufficient to fix determinate probabilities even in principle, since all three constructions above are perfectly legitimate measures satisfying those axioms and yet they can't be reconciled with one another."
