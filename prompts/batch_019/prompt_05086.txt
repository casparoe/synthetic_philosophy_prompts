So in my free will and moral responsibility seminar this week we read Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," and it basically ate the rest of my evening because two people in the class, Theo and Leah, would not let it go over coffee after our three hours of seminar on it. I'm only a few weeks into this course so I might be garbling some of the finer terminology, but I followed enough of it to want a second opinion, because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning at any given point and I'm not signalling which of them I think is right.

(1) Theo opened by saying Strawson basically dissolves the whole free will debate: demanding that our practice of resentment, gratitude, blame etc. be justified "from outside" -- by libertarian free will, or by some purely forward-looking consequentialist story about deterrence -- is confused, because there's no standpoint outside the reactive attitudes from which that demand even makes sense. We didn't choose this framework based on evidence, we're stuck with it as social creatures, so a general thesis like determinism can't get a grip on it. (2) Leah said that can't be right as stated, because then any sufficiently ingrained attitude would count as beyond criticism just for being ingrained -- she brought up in-group hostility as something arguably just as natural and just as hard to shake, and said "can't help having it" obviously doesn't equal "justified." (3) Theo's comeback was that the analogy misfires: Strawson isn't running a "natural therefore justified" argument, his actual point is that the reactive attitudes aren't one hypothesis among others about how to treat people, competing with alternatives on the evidence -- they're part of what it even is to regard someone as a fellow person rather than an object of policy. So stepping back to ask "should we have this framework at all, compared to some other one" just is stepping into what Strawson calls the objective attitude, which isn't a competing moral view, its the abandonment of having one at all toward that person. (4) Leah wasn't satisfied -- she pointed out Strawson himself carves out plenty of local suspensions of the reactive attitudes: excusing someone because they were coerced or it was an accident, or exempting someone entirely because they're a child or "not responsible for what they do." So he already thinks particular facts about an agent can defeat resentment. Her question was why a genuinely global fact, determinism being true of everyone, couldn't be a reason of exactly that same kind, just applied everywhere at once -- and she didn't think Theo had shown that question was confused rather than just unanswered. (5) Theo said Strawson does answer it: excuses and exemptions only make sense against a backdrop of normally holding people responsible -- calling something "an accident" presupposes that usually people do mean what they do -- so if you tried to generalize the excuse to literally everyone, you wouldn't get "everyone is excused," you'd get the concepts of excuse and exemption collapsing entirely, since there'd be no normal case left for them to be defined against. That's why determinism can't be modeled as one giant excuse. (6) Leah's last word was that this is exactly the conclusion a hard determinist would be happy to accept -- that the concepts lose their footing -- so Theo has just re-described the radical result rather then blocked it, and that Strawson's fallback move, that we're psychologically incapable of sustaining the objective attitude toward everyone all the time, is a fact about human limitations, not an argument that the practice is justified. She thinks that's the gap Theo keeps sliding past.
