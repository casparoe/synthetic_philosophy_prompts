For an independent project in my philosophy elective, I've been working through the puzzle of material constitution using the classic case of a statue and the lump of clay it is made from. The standard argument for their numerical distinctness runs through Leibniz's Law: since the lump could survive being squashed into a ball while the statue could not, the two differ in their modal properties and so cannot be one and the same thing. I've also come across Allan Gibbard's rival proposal in his paper "Contingent Identity," where he concedes that the statue and the lump are, in the actual world, identical, but denies that this identity need hold of necessity, thereby blocking the squashing argument from forcing distinctness. Could you compare Gibbard's contingent-identity solution with the more standard "coincident objects" view, on which the statue and the lump are simply distinct objects that happen to share all their matter at a given time? I would especially like you to address whether contingent identity is coherent at all, given Kripke's argument that true identity statements involving rigid designators hold of necessity, and to say which of the two solutions offers the more defensible account overall.
