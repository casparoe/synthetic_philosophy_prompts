I work on the clinical policy team at a mid-size health insurer (not gonna name it) and there's a coverage question that keeps getting punted up the chain instead of actually resolved. Ever since the WHO folded "gaming disorder" into the ICD-11 a few years back, we've had a steady trickle of prior-auth requests and appeals for treatment of what DSM-5 more cautiously calls "Internet Gaming Disorder" -- its still just a "condition for further study" there, not an official diagnosis, last I checked. Our behavioral health consultants are split down the middle: half think this is a real disorder, basically a behavioral addiction with the same hijacked-reward-circuitry story you'd tell about substance use disorders, and the other half think we're one committee vote away from medicalizing "kid plays too much Fortnite" the way an earlier generation medicalized things that were really just moral disapproval in a lab coat. My director wants an actual written recommendation before next year's behavioral health policy gets finalized, not more ad hoc case-by-case rulings.

The thing making this harder than a normal coverage call is that one of our consulting ethicists keeps insisting it all turns on a theoretical question nobody wants to actually sit with: does "disorder" require a genuine dysfunction in some evolved mechanism -- something like Boorse's biostatistical theory, or Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis, where you need both a broken mechanism and resulting harm -- or is it enough that a behavior pattern seriously wrecks someone's ability to pursue their own goals, whether or not anything is actually malfunctioning? She keeps throwing wrenches into the naturalist side too: gaming might just be exploiting a perfectly healthy reward system with a "supernormal stimulus," the same way junk food does, which would make it a bad fit for the substance-addiction analogy even though it looks similar from outside. And she says Boorse's own theory judges "subnormal" against a reference class like age and sex, so if heavy gaming is basically typical for teenage boys, it might not even count as statistically subnormal functioning for that group, no matter how much harm it's doing.

Can you write the actual memo I'd bring to the committee? I need a real recommendation -- cover it, don't cover it, or cover it only under specific conditions -- with the reasoning laid out for smart people who aren't philosophers, and since I'll have to defend this when someone pushes back, I want an honest, specific version of the strongest argument against whatever you end up recommending, not just a "there are considerations on both sides" shrug.
