Since retiring after thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist, I have joined a small reading group that works through classic papers in welfare economics and social choice theory, partly because the axiomatic style feels reassuringly familiar. John Harsanyi's aggregation theorem struck me, on first encounter, as a beautiful specimen of that style: if each individual's preferences and the social preference ordering both satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social ordering respects the Pareto principle, then the social utility function is forced to be an affine combination of the individual utility functions with non-negative weights. My first reading was that this amounts to something close to a derivation of utilitarianism from premises that a great many people would accept on independent grounds. (I gather this is a distinct argument from Harsanyi's separate "impartial observer" construction, which I would like to set aside here.)

Working further through the secondary literature, though, I found that several serious critics — Amartya Sen, John Weymark, and Peter Diamond among them — regard the theorem's utilitarian upshot as considerably less secure than my first reading suggested, and not merely because one might reject expected-utility theory outright. One line of objection, developed by Sen and sharpened by Weymark, holds that the unanimity doing the real work in the theorem is in some sense spurious, since the relative weights attached to each person's utility function are artifacts of an arbitrary choice of scale rather than anything the axioms themselves fix. A second line, associated with Diamond, points to cases where satisfying the Pareto principle at the level of ex ante expected utility seems to come apart from fairness intuitions about how the resulting risks or goods are actually distributed ex post. I would be grateful for an explanation, pitched at the level of intuition rather than a re-derivation of the representation theorem itself (which I have already worked through), of exactly where the inference from "the axioms are satisfied" to "utilitarianism has thereby been vindicated" is supposed to break down — and whether, in your assessment, these objections are decisive or merely counsel a more modest reading of what Harsanyi's theorem actually establishes.
