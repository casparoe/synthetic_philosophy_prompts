I spent close to forty years as a field biologist studying foraging behavior in birds, and a good deal of that work leaned on optimal foraging theory: the idea that patch-departure times, diet breadth, and the like could be derived from models that maximize net energy intake subject to constraints such as travel time and handling time (Charnov's marginal value theorem was close to gospel in my corner of the field). What always nagged at a few of us was that the animals we actually watched seemed to follow much cruder rules of thumb — giving-up densities, fixed giving-up times — that only approximated the theorem's prediction, and there was a standing, never-quite-settled dispute in the field about whether those rules of thumb were genuine departures from optimization or simply the optimal policy once you priced in the cost and unreliability of assessing intake rates on the fly. It always struck me as a close cousin of Gould and Lewontin's complaint about the adaptationist program: once a modeler is permitted to invent whatever trade-off is needed after the fact, "optimal" stops being a falsifiable claim about the organism and becomes a tribute to the model-builder's ingenuity.

Since retiring I have been reading my way through Herbert Simon on bounded rationality, and I keep running into what looks like the very same argument wearing different clothes. Simon offers satisficing — searching only until some aspiration level is cleared, rather than exhaustively optimizing — as a genuinely distinct, non-optimizing mode of choice suited to agents with limited time and computation. Yet almost any such stopping rule can apparently be redescribed, in the manner of Stigler's economics of search or the reservation-wage job-search models associated with McCall that grew out of it, as the exact solution to a constrained optimization problem in which the marginal cost of further search is itself folded into the objective function. If that redescription always goes through, satisficing is not a rival to optimizing at all, just optimizing one level up, and "bounded rationality" risks exactly the charge of unfalsifiability that was leveled at the adaptationists. My understanding is that Simon tried to head this off by distinguishing "substantive" from "procedural" rationality, judging a choice by the appropriateness of the deliberative process rather than by the optimality of what it outputs. What I would like help thinking through is whether that move actually blocks the redescription argument or merely relocates it one level further up, since we can now simply ask whether the procedure itself is the cognitively optimal one given the reasoner's constraints — and, more generally, whether there is any principled place to plant a flag between "satisficing" and "optimizing subject to a fuller accounting of costs," or whether the distinction ultimately trades on modeling convenience rather than on any substantive fact about the chooser.
