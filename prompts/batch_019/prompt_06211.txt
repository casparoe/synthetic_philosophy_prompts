So this term I'm TAing a seminar on practical reason, and this week's readings were Ruth Chang on "parity" plus a chunk of Joseph Raz's Morality of Freedom on incommensurable values, and its been stuck in my head since. The bit I keep chewing on is the small-improvement argument: if two options are genuinely equally good, sweetening one of them just a little should make it come out ahead, but in an actual hard choice (career, partner, whatever) a tiny improvement to one side doesn't obviously break the tie -- which is supposed to show the options aren't equal, but they're also clearly not incomparable in the way that, say, the number 7 and the taste of cinnamon are incomparable. Chang wants a fourth relation, "on a par," to cover cases like this, and her further claim is that this is exactly where our agency shows up: a hard choice isn't a puzzle with a hidden right answer we've failed to find, it's a moment where choosing is what settles the matter, rather than discovering it.

I keep telling my cohort that the stock examples for this (lawyer vs. clarinetist, philosopher vs. banker, etc.) are kind of dry, and that a piece of fiction should be able to make the phenomenon land in a way a journal article can't. So: could you write me a short story, maybe 800-1200 words, with an actual character and plot, built around a hard choice of exactly this kind? Please don't have anyone stand around monologuing about parity or incommensurability -- I want the structure of the choice itself, through plot and small concrete details, to make it viscerally clear why "just pick whichever's better" and "they're equal, flip a coin" both feel wrong here, and ideally to gesture at the agency point too, so that whichever way the character ends up choosing feels like an act of self-definition rather than a correct answer they landed on. Set it wherever and whenever you like, honestly the only real request is: no career-choice cliché, we've all read that one already.
