I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term and we just spent two weeks on the Lucas-Penrose argument, and I feel like I only half get it. Everyone always brings up the "obvious" reply to Lucas's original 1961 version -- that he needs the formal system F that's supposedly equivalent to his own mind to be consistent in order to assert that its Gödel sentence is true, and by Gödel's second theorem F can never prove its own consistency, so Lucas is smuggling in an assumption he has no right to. Fine, got that part, its in every intro summary of the debate.

Where I get lost is what Penrose does about this in Shadows of the Mind. My professor said Penrose tries to route around the objection by switching the argument from talking about F's consistency to F's soundness -- something like, if we believe our own mathematical reasoning is sound rather than merely consistent, we can still derive a contradiction even without knowing exactly what F is. I don't really see how just swapping "consistent" for "sound" fixes the "we don't know which machine we even are" problem, since either way you seemingly need to have F in hand to construct its actual Gödel sentence and run the diagonal argument on it. I also have this hazy memory from somewhere (maybe a footnote in a reading?) that "soundness" isn't even the kind of property a system can state about itself the way consistency can, but I can't reconstruct why that would be true or why it would matter here. Apparently there was a whole pile of journal responses to Penrose's book from actual logicians hashing this out at the time, though I don't remember where.

Could you walk me through, step by step: (1) what the actual logical structure of Penrose's soundness-based version of the argument is and why he thought it was an improvement over Lucas, and (2) what you'd say is the single strongest technical reason it still doesn't escape the "which formal system are we even talking about" problem? I'd like something more precise than the usual one-line "well, some people think Gödel refutes mechanism and some people don't" -- I want to actually see the moving parts and where exactly the argument breaks, if it does.
