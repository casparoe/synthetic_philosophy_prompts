I'm building a case study on classical pragmatism for my graduate seminar, and I want something sharper than the "truth is whatever works" gloss most students arrive with. Please write a dialogue between Charles Sanders Peirce and William James, set sometime after James's 1898 Berkeley address ("Philosophical Conceptions and Practical Results"), in which James publicly credited Peirce with originating the pragmatist principle; have Peirce explain why he later felt driven to coin the uglier term "pragmaticism" — by his own description a word "ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers" — to keep his account of truth apart from James's. Peirce should press James on whether defining truth as what is "good in the way of belief" collapses the difference between a belief's being true and its merely being satisfying to hold, so that a comforting falsehood and a hard-won truth become indistinguishable; James should press back that Peirce's identification of truth with the "final opinion" at the ideal limit of an indefinitely prolonged inquiry is either an empty promissory note no finite inquirer can ever cash, or itself just a further, covert article of faith about what would satisfy the community in the long run. Each should answer the specific challenge just raised rather than restate his own view, and neither should be allowed to settle things by declaring the dispute merely verbal.
