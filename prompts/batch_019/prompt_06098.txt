I don't have any background in philosophy or economics, but I've spent a good part of the last year reading about Bayesian reasoning on my own, including Robert Aumann's result that two honest, rational people who share the same prior beliefs can't remain in genuine disagreement once their probability estimates are common knowledge between them. I help run a small forecasting group at work, where about fifteen of us submit percentage estimates on things like whether a project will ship on time, and we track everyone's calibration on a leaderboard. Our long-standing house rule is that when our two most active forecasters land far apart on a question even after a recorded call where each walks the other through their reasoning, we simply average their two numbers and move on. After a recent case where the gap didn't close at all, I brought up Aumann's result, and the group is now split: some think it exposes our averaging rule as a dodge, since a gap that survives a real conversation must mean somebody is still withholding information or reasoning badly, and averaging just splits the difference on that error; others think expecting real convergence between busy, fallible colleagues is unrealistic, and that averaging is simply the fair response to honest disagreement. As chair of our three-person rules committee, I need to circulate a short memo before the next cycle settling what we actually do when two forecasters still disagree after talking it through. Please draft that memo: it should recommend one concrete rule, and it should engage seriously with the Aumann-based objection -- explaining where the theorem does and doesn't actually apply to a group like ours -- rather than just asserting that reasonable people can disagree.
