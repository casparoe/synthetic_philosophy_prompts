Last night a friend and I got into a long argument that started, of all things, over my inability to begin revising for an exam I keep insisting I care about. They are taking a course in behavioural economics and maintained that my procrastination is perfectly explicable once hyperbolic discounting is taken into account: my preference between revising now and watching one more episode simply reverses as the smaller, sooner reward draws close, so there is no real weakness of will involved, only a predictable change in what I actually want from one moment to the next. I objected that this cannot be the whole story, since at the very moment I reach for the remote I still judge, all things considered, that I should be revising instead — the "worse" option never actually becomes my genuine all-things-considered favourite, as their account seems to require.

Neither of us could land a knock-down move, and once I looked into it afterwards I realised we had stumbled into a very old dispute. Socrates holds in the Protagoras that no one does wrong knowingly; Aristotle spends much of Nicomachean Ethics VII trying to rescue the reality of akrasia from that claim; and Davidson mounts a modern defense of weakness of will as action against an unconditional, all-things-considered judgment the agent has not actually renounced. My friend's position, it turns out, has real teeth against all three: on their view, Ainslie-style hyperbolic discounting models show that every apparent case of akrasia can be redescribed as a preference reversal in which no standing judgment is ever actually violated, so the whole tradition from Socrates onward has been arguing about a phenomenon that doesn't exist.

I wasn't convinced at the time, but I couldn't find the flaw either. Please argue their side as rigorously as you can: that hyperbolic-discounting accounts of intertemporal choice, whatever their original purpose in describing impulsiveness and addiction, show that akrasia in the strong, philosophically loaded sense never actually occurs, and that such models therefore end up vindicating Socrates rather than merely changing the subject on him. I would especially like you to engage with my own objection above — that the tempting option never feels like it has become one's genuine all-things-considered favourite — and to explain why that piece of phenomenology is, or is not, good evidence against the discounting story. Please commit fully to the thesis rather than settling into an even-handed survey.
