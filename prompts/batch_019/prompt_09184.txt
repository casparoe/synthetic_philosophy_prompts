Last spring my grandmother made me promise that I would keep her rose garden alive after she died, and I have kept that promise ever since. In my philosophy class we read T. M. Scanlon's expectation-based account of promissory obligation from *What We Owe to Each Other*, on which breaking a promise is wrong principally because it defeats an expectation that one has intentionally and voluntarily led another person to form. This account seems to face a serious difficulty in my case: my grandmother no longer exists to have her trust betrayed or her expectations disappointed, yet it seems exactly as wrong for me to let the roses die now as it would have been while she was alive. Please argue, on Scanlon's behalf, that his expectation-based framework can fully accommodate the continuing bindingness of promises made to people who have since died — engaging with the strongest form of this objection rather than dismissing it too quickly, and without quietly importing independent rule-based or virtue-based considerations that have nothing to do with defeated expectations.
