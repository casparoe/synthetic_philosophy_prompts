I have been working for the past year on a novel that follows the final eighteen months of a monarchy's collapse, narrated partly through the letters of a minor court official and partly through the retrospective voice of his granddaughter, who is trying to work out why the regime fell when it did rather than a decade earlier or later. A friend of mine who teaches historiography read an early draft and told me, not unkindly, that what I had written was a story rather than an explanation: that a genuine historical account would need to show the collapse as an instance of something more general — the kind of thing that happens to any government facing fiscal exhaustion and elite defection — rather than a sequence of events that merely makes narrative sense in hindsight. Her comment sent me back to a philosophy of history course I took years ago, and I have not been able to settle the question since, either as a matter of craft or as a matter of what is actually true of historical explanation.

I would like you to compare, in some depth, Hempel's covering-law model of historical explanation with the narrativist alternative developed by philosophers such as Arthur Danto and Hayden White. I am especially interested in whether narrative explanation is, at bottom, just a covering-law explanation whose general laws have been left unstated — what Hempel himself called an "explanation sketch" — such that a sufficiently thorough history could always cash it out in nomological terms, or whether the narrativists have identified something a covering-law explanation cannot capture even in principle: Danto's point, for instance, that the historical significance of an event is often only describable in terms of later events that had not yet occurred, or White's claim that one and the same sequence of events can be truthfully emplotted as tragedy or as comedy with no further fact deciding between them. Please work through exactly where the two camps disagree about what an explanation is, and then tell me which, if either, comes closer to what a historian — or a novelist trying to do a historian's job honestly — actually owes the reader.
