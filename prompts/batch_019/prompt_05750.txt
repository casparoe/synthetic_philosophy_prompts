I spent about thirty-five years as a field ornithologist before I retired, mostly working on nest predation and a bit of sexual selection stuff, and philosophy was never really my department. But a couple of years ago I joined a little "philosophy for retirees" reading group at my local library, and lately we've been chewing on Gould and Lewontin's old spandrels paper, along with Dennett's chapter going after it in Darwin's Dangerous Idea and a chunk of Sober's philosophy of biology book covering the same ground. I think I've finally got the headline claim: adaptationists can slip into telling plausible "just-so" stories about why a trait must have been selected for, when really it might just be a structural or developmental byproduct — a spandrel — that was never selected for at all, and only later got co-opted for some other use (Gould and Vrba's "exaptation," if I'm remembering the term right). It rang a bell from my own career too — I had more than one grant reviewer who wanted a tidy adaptive story for absolutely everything, and heaven help you if you suggested a trait might just be an accident of development. Amused me no end, by the way, when I found out that art historians apparently think Gould and Lewontin got the architecture wrong to begin with, and that the San Marco features in question are pendentives rather then true spandrels — feels like a nice little parable in its own right.

What I haven't been able to shake is how you'd actually catch this kind of thing happening on a live research question, rather than in hindsight, using the same tidy textbook examples everybody always reaches for (the cathedral itself, the human chin, male nipples, the panda's thumb — please steer well clear of those, I've chewed on them plenty already, and if I'm honest a couple of them always felt a bit too neat). So could you give me an example of one actual trait, in whatever organism you like (birds would be fun given my background, but I really don't mind), where working biologists still genuinly disagree about whether it's a straightforward adaptation or a spandrel/byproduct that got exapted for its current use later on? I'd like you to walk me through exactly why the normal toolkit — comparative method, optimality modelling, developmental genetics, whatever's relevant here — has trouble settling the question one way or the other in that particular case. I'm less after a rehash of the general adaptationism-versus-pluralism debate than after seeing precisely where, in this one case, the evidence runs out and the interpretation becomes genuinely underdetermined, because that's the bit that never quite made it into any of the reading we've done so far.
