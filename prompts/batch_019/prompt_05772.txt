My younger brother ships out for his first deployment in a few weeks, and beneath being proud of him I've felt a dread I can't quite justify to myself. Trying to think it through rather than just feel it, I went back to Hegel's *Elements of the Philosophy of Right*, where he makes an argument more uncompromising than I remembered: that a state left too long in comfortable peace doesn't simply get fortunate but rots ethically, as its citizens sink into private property, family, and business, and that war performs the positive function of recalling them to something universal beyond those finite interests, at the cost of their own safety and even their lives. He's explicit that this isn't a defect of bad rulers, or an evil that a Kantian league of peace-loving states could simply legislate away, but a permanent and even healthy feature of how a people stays ethically alive. I don't want an essay on whether he's right. Could you write me a short story, set in an invented society rather than any real war, that dramatizes this claim from the inside — letting it feel both true and monstrous — without resolving too easily into either propaganda for war or a tidy rebuttal of Hegel?
