I've covered experimental philosophy before (the whole "x-phi" beat) but never expected to watch the Knobe effect play out live at my sisters dinner table this weekend. She runs a small bakery and switched flour suppliers to cut costs, which she knew would shrink the amount of day-old bread she donates to the local food pantry -- she wasn't trying to cut the donation, she just wanted cheaper flour and didn't care one way or the other about the pantry. Two of her friends who were there ended up arguing about whether she "intentionally" reduced the donation.

Colin said obviously yes -- she knew it would happen and did it anyway, that's just what intentional means. Naomi said no way: intentional requires aiming at the outcome, and she was aiming at cost, totally indifferent to the pantry, so at most that's a foreseen side effect, not an intended one. Colin came back with: if the switch had increased the donation instead, everyone at the table (Naomi included) would've called that a nice, deliberate thing she did, not a lucky accident, so "indifference" can't be doing all the work since her actual attitude never changed, only the outcome's moral color did. Naomi's reply was that this just shows people get careless with "intentional" when they're handing out blame or praise, and that it's a performance error rather than a real discovery about the concept -- slow people down and they'd take it back. Colin said that's a suspiciously convenient way to dismiss inconvenient data, especially since the same asymmetry turns up in little kids, across pretty different cultures, and even in cases that have nothing to do with morality, like breaking an arbitrary rule in a game -- which looks less like sloppiness and more like "intentional action" genuinely tracking whether you were for, against, or neutral on the side effect. Nobody budged and then we had to go help clear plates.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got the better argument at each step, not just who wins overall? Also curious where you personally land on the bigger question underneath it -- is the Knobe effect a bias we should correct for, or does it show something true about how the ordinary concept of "intentional action" actually works?
