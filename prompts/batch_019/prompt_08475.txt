So over the weekend I was reading my kid one of those illustrated versions of the Odyssey and we got to the bit where Odysseus has his crew stuff their ears with wax so they can't hear the Sirens, but he gets himself tied to the mast instead and tells them flat out: no matter how much I beg or order you to untie me, tie me tighter, don't listen to a word I say. That scene stuck in my head and I went down a bit of a rabbit hole afterward and found out there's an actual term people use, "Ulysses contract," for basically any setup where you bind your future self in advance because you don't trust your future judgement. Apparently some people apply the exact same logic to constitutions - the idea being that "we the people," in a calm moment, tie our own hands (a bill of rights, rules that are hard to change) so some future panicked majority can't do something everyone will regret. I even read that Germanys constitution has a clause, I think its Article 79, saying a few core things - human dignity, the basic democratic setup - can never be amended, full stop, no matter how big the majority or how many generations later.

I brought all this up with two friends of mine, Nadia and Colin, and it turned into a proper hour-long argument that never got resolved. Colin's opening move was that the comparison is basically right and that's a good thing: a country protecting its own basic structure from getting voted away in a panic is doing exactly what Odysseus did, being smart in your calm moments about what your panicked moments might do to you. Nadia pushed back that the analogy falls apart at the important point: Odysseus tied his own hands, and it's still him the whole time, and presumably if the ship had actually started sinking the crew would've cut him loose - it's reversible, same guy, real emergency exception. A clause that can never be undone, ever, no matter how large the majority or how many generations pass, isn't a guy binding his future self, it's binding a bunch of people who weren't even born yet and never agreed to any of it. Colin came back with: total agreement across generations was never on the table anyway, nobody gets literal unanimous consent for anything, that's exactly why supermajority rules exist, and ordinary amendment procedures already let the vast majority of any constitution be changed - the permanently locked bits are just the handful of things we have most reason to be suspicious of any generation, panicked or not, wanting to tear up. Nadia's comeback was that this actually gives the game away: the whole point of a precommitment device is supposed to be that it holds regardless of whether the future self's reasons for wanting out are any good - the ropes hold Odysseus even if he's got a perfectly good reason to want off the mast - so the moment Colin starts arguing "but this particular content deserves protection," he's stopped defending a neutral rationality mechanism and started just stating his own opinion about which rights matter, dressed up as procedure.

Neither of them gave any ground and I honestly don't know enough political philosophy to say who's actually landing punches here.
