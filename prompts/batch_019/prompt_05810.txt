I'm about a third of the way into a historical novel set in 1824 London, and there's one chapter I keep rewriting because I can't get the voice right. My narrator is a young woman who's talked her way into her first real assignment for the Westminster Review (which, conveniently, Bentham himself helped bankroll that very year) — officially she's there to interview the old man about law reform, but she's gotten hold of fragments of the stuff he never let anyone print in his lifetime: the manuscripts on sexual morality that didn't actually surface until decades, in some cases over a century, after he died. The whole chapter is structured as the interview itself, so I need it to read like she's genuinely pressing on the mechanics of his reasoning rather than doing a costume-drama voiceover about how "surprisingly progressive" he was for his time. (My critique partner thinks a chapter-length Regency interview about Bentham's sex writing is a hard sell. I think its the best thing in the book so far, so I'm doing it anyway.)

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions she could put to him, each paired with a follow-up keyed to whatever answer a careful, slightly cagey Bentham is most likely to give — I'd rather the friction come from the next question than have her spell out the objections herself. I want at least one question that goes after his move of ruling "asceticism" and mere antipathy or disgust out as legitimate inputs to the felicific calculus, pressing on why the disgust respectable people feel doesn't just count as another pain to be weighed on his own terms; at least one on how far he's actually willing to run the calculus once it's applied to acts that would've scandalized even his own radical circle (I gather he bites the bullet in more than one place, not just the obvious one); and at least one on the sheer cheek of setting Jesus against Paul on a subject he privately thinks neither of them had real authority over. Then I'd like the set to build to a last question about the irony of a man who built his whole politics on publicity as the great check on power choosing total silence, for decades, on this one subject specifically — I want that to be the question he can't just answer by restating the calculus one more time. If there's room left before that, a question on whether the thirty-odd years between his first draft on this (practically a young man) and the later manuscripts changed the argument itself or just his nerve would be a nice bonus.
