My advisor died a couple weeks ago -- she was the classical Chinese philosophy person in our department, and I, one of her PhD students, got asked to say a few words at the memorial this weekend. The room's going to be split: half Daoism/early-China specialists who can recite the Zhuangzi passages themselves, half her family who've never read a word of it. She was a real Zhuangzi person about this, not just professionally -- she used to say that if anyone stood up at her funeral and said she was "in a better place" or "watching over everyone," she'd sit up just to argue about it, because that's exactly the picture of a self persisting past death that she thought his whole account of transformation (hua) is supposed to dissolve, not relocate to heaven. She taught the wife-and-basin story every single year and always leaned hard on the line that dying is no different in kind from the seasons turning, and she loved the bit near the end where, dying himself, he tells his students not to bother burying him properly since he's just as happy feeding crows and kites as worms underground. Can you write the eulogy I have to give? It has to actually comfort her family and not just perform cleverness for the philosophers in the room, but it can't smuggle in anything about souls or persistence that she'd have flagged as a mistake -- bonus points if there's something in there the specialists catch without loosing everyone else.
