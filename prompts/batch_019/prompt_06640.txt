Fell down a bit of a rabbit hole this week reading up on Aquinas and tyrannicide, partly spurred by yet another round of internet arguing about when (if ever) it's okay to violently remove an authoritarian leader. Turns out the pop-summary version that gets thrown around ("Aquinas said tyrants can be killed, actually") is way more hedged than that once you look at the actual texts. From what I can piece together across his commentary on the Sentences and the De Regimine Principum (De Regno, in some editions), the load-bearing distinction is between a guy who seized power with no legitimate title at all — sometimes glossed with the scholastic label "tyrannus ex defectu tituli," though don't quote me on the precise Latin — versus a guy with a fully legitimate claim to rule who then governs tyrannically ("ex parte exercitii" or something like that). For the first type he's basically fine with anybody resisting or deposing him, since there was never any real authority there to betray in the first place. For the second type he gets a lot more cautious: especially in the De Regimine he spends real time warning that private individuals taking it on themselves to assassinate a tyrant tends to go badly (ambition dressed up as zeal for justice, the tyrant getting more paranoid and repressive in response, etc), and says correction of a legitimate-but-tyrannical ruler should come from the community acting collectively, or from whatever higher authority hes answerable to, not some guy acting on his own judgement with a knife. (I gather John of Salisbury gets read as more gung-ho about tyrannicide than this, but I want to stick to Thomas specifically. Classic rationalist-brain move, I realize, turning a live political question into a scholastic taxonomy exercise, but bear with me.)

Here's the part I can't work out and would love your take on. How would this framework handle a case that doesn't map cleanly onto "usurper vs legitimate ruler" at all — namely the standard modern-autocrat playbook, where a guy wins a genuinely free election (so nobody disputes his title), and then over two terms guts the courts, neuters the legislature, jails independent journalists, and rewrites the constitution to cancel term limits. The gradual "competitive authoritarianism" backslide, basically, rather than a dramatic coup. Does Aquinas's own logic force you to say this guy stays a "tyrant by conduct" the whole way through, meaning only "public authority" can legitimately remove him, even once he's captured more or less every institution that would've counted as the relevant public authority (which seems like a weird dead end, since destroying the legitimacy of process is exactly what he's done)? Or is there a principled point, using Aquinas's own categories, where the erosion of constitutional constraints effectively converts him into something more like a usurper, such that private resistance becomes licit again on his own terms?

And a bonus question, if you're up for it: is his whole case against private tyrannicide (ambition masquerading as justice, provoking worse repression) really an argument against lethal violence specifically, or would it apply just as well to modern non-lethal "tyrannicide-adjacent" moves — leaking classified material, coordinated sabotage of state infrastructure, hacking government systems to cripple a regime? I keep flip-flopping on whether his analysis cares about the means at all, or only about who has the standing to act.
