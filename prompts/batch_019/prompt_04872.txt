I'm drafting a novel in which two evolutionary biologists, who trained together under the same advisor twenty years ago and have circled one another warily at conferences ever since, end up seated side by side at a colleague's retirement dinner. I want their polite small talk to curdle, within a page or two, into a real argument about whether the human female orgasm is an adaptation shaped directly by natural selection, or whether it is instead a developmental byproduct — a "spandrel," in the sense Gould and Lewontin gave that word (borrowed, as I understand it, from the roughly triangular spaces left over where the rounded arches supporting a dome meet, as in the Basilica di San Marco) when they warned against what they called the adaptationist programme: the habit of assuming that every trait must have been individually shaped by selection for some function, and of treating any alternative explanation as the thing that needs to be argued for, rather than as a live default.

Could you write me that dialogue? I'd like one character to make the case, in the spirit of Gould, Lewontin, and later Elisabeth Lloyd, that female orgasm is best understood as a side effect of the shared embryological development of male and female genitalia — the same kind of story usually told about male nipples — and to argue that none of the standing functional accounts (orgasm as a mechanism for pair-bonding, as an "upsuck" aid to retaining sperm, as a signal of mate quality tied to bodily symmetry) has actually been nailed down by evidence in the way a genuine adaptive hypothesis should be. I'd like the other character to be a serious, committed adaptationist and not a pushover: someone who can concede that any single one of those functional stories may be underdetermined by the data while still pressing hard on the byproduct account itself — for instance, by asking why a shared developmental origin should count as evidence against a trait having been separately fine-tuned by selection once it existed, or by turning the "just-so story" charge back around, and asking whether "it's merely a spandrel" isn't just as convenient and untested a story as the ones it's meant to replace. Please don't let either side simply win by the end: I want the sort of exchange in which each of them has to actually revise or sharpen a claim in response to something the other just said, the way it would go between two people who respect each other's minds and have been having some version of this argument, on and off, for twenty years. Something in the neighborhood of a page and a half would suit the scene well.
