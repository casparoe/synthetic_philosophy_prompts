I spent most of my working life as a behavioral ecologist, studying kin-biased alarm-calling in ground squirrels and, later, cooperative breeding in fairy-wrens, and since retiring I have finally found time to sit in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. We recently worked through Urmson's classic essay "Saints and Heroes," which introduces the notion of supererogation: acts that are praiseworthy to perform yet not obligatory, going beyond anything duty strictly requires -- a soldier smothering a grenade to save comrades, a stranger anonymously donating a kidney, and so on.

Listening to my fellow group members treat this as an open philosophical problem, I found myself thinking that evolutionary biology had already settled the underlying question. Hamilton's rule, Trivers's theory of reciprocal altruism, and Zahavi's handicap principle were all developed to explain how self-sacrificing behavior toward kin, group members, or reputation-tracking strangers can nonetheless increase an individual's inclusive fitness, and Dawkins built an entire popular book around the claim that it is the gene, rather than the organism, that behaves "selfish," with organism-level generosity simply being one of the ways a "selfish" gene can propagate itself. If the disposition to act heroically or like a saint is itself a strategy that was favored by selection because it pays off, in the currency of genes or reputation, over some longer or less obvious timescale, doesn't that show that so-called supererogatory acts are not genuinely "beyond" self-interest at all? It increasingly seems to me that moral philosophers have introduced an elaborate deontic category, complete with its own Latin name, for a phenomenon that population genetics already explains without remainder. Is there something the evolutionary story is missing, or does it, in fact, make the philosophical concept of supererogation redundant?
