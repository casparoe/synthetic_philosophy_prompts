I'm on my school's Ethics Bowl team, and this week our coach assigned a packet of background readings on moral realism before we start drafting our cases. One excerpt made a claim I hadn't run into before: dispositions like favoring our own kin, feeling loyalty to our in-group, or resenting free-riders are exactly what natural selection would have installed in us whether or not any of them track something objectively true, since simply having the feeling is what helped our ancestors survive, regardless of whatever moral reality it was supposedly picking up on. The suggestion was that this should make us doubt whether our moral convictions track real facts at all, rather than being side-effects of selection pressures that never had anything to do with truth.

Two of my teammates, Kai and Sofia, ended up spending most of practice arguing about this instead of drilling our actual case. Kai thought the claim was sound, and pushed it further: he said it should make us suspicious even of intuitions we treat as bedrock, like "suffering is bad," since feeling that way would have paid off for our ancestors regardless of whether it's true. Sofia objected that the same logic applies to arithmetic and basic logic, since ancestors who counted reliably or reasoned validly out-survived those who didn't, yet nobody concludes from this that 2+2=4 is any less objectively true — so why single out morality? Kai replied that the two cases aren't actually parallel: with counting, selection favored the trait because it was accurate, since miscounting predators really did cost you, so truth-tracking explains why the trait spread; with kin-favoritism, the identical survival payoff would follow whether or not "kin deserve more" is true in any deeper sense, so accuracy never had to do any work in that story. Sofia's comeback was that this just assumes there's no fact that makes caring for kin genuinely good and that would, for that very reason, have been favored by selection too — if it really is true that caring for kin is good, we'd expect evolution to have pushed us toward feeling that way precisely because it's true, in which case Kai's claim that the payoff comes "either way" hasn't actually been established, only assumed.

Practice ended before either of them budged, and I haven't been able to stop turning the exchange over since. Could you go through their disagreement move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's some consideration neither of them raised that would help settle how much of a threat evolutionary explanations of morality really pose.
