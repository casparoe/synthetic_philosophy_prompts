I've spent a long time following the "modest epistemology" debates on LessWrong and Overcoming Bias — roughly, disputes over how much deference is owed to expert consensus when you have no way to audit the underlying reasoning yourself. It strikes me that this practical question has a much older analogue in mainstream epistemology: the dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimonial justification. Reductionists, following Hume, hold that whatever warrant testimony confers ultimately reduces to the hearer's own inductive evidence that testimony — in general, or from this speaker in particular — is reliable; anti-reductionists, following Reid and defended in different forms by Coady (Testimony: A Philosophical Study, 1992) and Burge ("Content Preservation," 1993), hold instead that testimony is a basic, non-inferential source of justification, on a par with perception or memory, such that a hearer is by default entitled to accept it absent specific defeaters. Please describe the most important implications of this debate for how much credence I should place in a specialist's unsupported say-so within a technical field I have no independent way of checking — say, a claimed result in a subfield of theoretical physics or pure mathematics — and note where a moderate position, such as Fricker's local reductionism or Lackey's dualist account, would change the practical upshot.
