I am a PhD student in philosophy of law, currently drafting a dissertation chapter that tests the Hart–Fuller exchange against a case neither man lived to comment on: the 1990s trials of former East German border guards (the so-called Mauerschützen) for shootings at the Berlin Wall, in which German courts, after reunification, revived a version of Gustav Radbruch's formula to strip the GDR's shoot-to-kill border regulations of legal validity. Could you write a dialogue, running over several exchanges, between a present-day Hartian legal positivist and a present-day Fullerian natural lawyer debating how their respective frameworks ought to handle this case? The Hartian should maintain that convicting the guards required either a frankly retroactive statute (Hart's own preferred fix for the grudge-informer case) or a strained reinterpretation of GDR law, and should press Hart's old objection that satisfying Fuller's eight principles of the inner morality of law is quite compatible with great substantive wickedness, so that one cannot simply read the injustice of the shoot-to-kill orders off those procedural criteria. The Fullerian should try to answer on Fuller's own terms — for instance via the congruence or constancy principles, or via the gap between the GDR's public border statutes and its secret shooting orders — rather than quietly importing Radbruch's substantive test. Neither side should concede a point too readily; each turn should engage with the specific objection just raised rather than restate a general position.
