the question was: "Reconstruct Bradley's regress against relations, and explain how the same underlying difficulty resurfaces as the problem of the unity of the proposition. Then take ONE strategy meant to block the regress -- either Russell's account of relations in the Principles of Mathematics, or Armstrong's treatment of relations via states of affairs -- and assess whether it actually succeeds."

here's the students answer:

"Bradley's regress starts from the thought that if a relation R holds between two terms a and b, R has to be related to a and to b -- otherwise R just sits alongside a and b without doing any relating, and 'aRb' collapses into the mere list 'a, R, b.' But then the relation tying R to a and b needs a further relation tying it to R, a, and b, and so on forever. Since the relating never actually gets finished at any stage, Bradley concludes that relations in this ordinary sense are unintelligible.

The same shape of problem shows up when we ask what makes 'Caesar dies' a genuine unity and not just the list 'Caesar, dying.' Listing the ingredients isn't yet asserting anything -- something has to weld them into a single truth or falsehood, and whatever does the welding can't be one more ingredient sitting next to the others, on pain of needing its own further weld. Same regress.

Russell's response in the Principles of Mathematics is that a relation has a twofold nature: the very same relation can occur "as relating" -- actually doing its relating work, the way "loves" functions in "Desdemona loves Cassio" -- or as a mere logical subject, the way we talk about loving as an abstract entity. Because a relation-in-its-relating-role isn't just another term waiting to be hooked up, no further relation is needed to hook it up, and the regress never gets going. This is basically the same idea Russell relied on a decade or so later in the multiple relation theory of judgment, where believing is an unmediated many-term relation between the believer and the several objects, with no separate proposition-entity needed to unify them -- so Russell had, in effect, put Bradley's puzzle to rest for good.

Bradley, for his part, takes the regress to show that the relational form of thought is ultimately unreal and mind-dependent -- relations hold only relative to a mind that does the relating, which is one of the premises behind his idealism and his claim that only the undifferentiated Absolute is fully real.

Overall Russell's distinction seems like the more promising move, since it locates the unity in the relation's own dual nature rather than in an outside thinking subject."
