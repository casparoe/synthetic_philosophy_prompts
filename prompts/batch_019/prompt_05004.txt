I am currently in mediated negotiations to dissolve a six-year software partnership with my co-founder, and the process has turned into an unexpected crash course in bargaining theory. We are dividing an ongoing royalty stream from a patent we hold jointly, and our mediator originally modeled the split using what she described as "basically the Nash bargaining solution." Partway through the process, a new licensing opportunity emerged that expanded the total pool of royalties available to divide; when the mediator reran her Nash-based formula on the enlarged pool, my co-founder's allocation actually decreased relative to the previous round, even though nothing about the new opportunity was in any way disadvantageous to him specifically. Everyone in the room found this result counterintuitive, and the mediator ultimately switched to what she called the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution instead, on the grounds that it does not permit this kind of reversal.

Reading around the topic since then, I have learned that this is essentially the textbook motivation for the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: Kalai and Smorodinsky proposed it in 1975 as an alternative to Nash's solution that trades away Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives for a monotonicity property Nash's solution lacks, apparently building on an arbitration scheme Howard Raiffa had already sketched in the early 1950s. A friend who works in political philosophy also mentioned, to my surprise, that Gauthier's "minimax relative concession" principle from Morals by Agreement is essentially a rediscovery of the same solution concept in contractarian dress. None of this is purely academic for me at this point, since whichever solution concept the mediator ultimately settles on will shape my income for years to come, and I would like to understand which of these formalizations of "fair compromise" actually deserves to be treated as normatively authoritative rather than merely mathematically convenient.
