Over a forty-year career in observational astrophysics I relied on Bayesian methods as a matter of course: computing posterior odds between competing models of stellar structure, updating parameter estimates whenever new survey data came in. I confess I had always taken for granted that the basic schema — evidence E confirms hypothesis H just when the posterior P(H|E) exceeds the prior P(H) — was doing genuine epistemological work rather than mere bookkeeping. Since retiring I've joined a small reading group that works through philosophy of science, and we recently spent a session on Clark Glymour's "problem of old evidence." It left me considerably more unsettled than I would have expected a decades-old objection to so well-established a formalism to manage.

The example that keeps recurring in what I've read is the one closest to my own field: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, some 43 arcseconds per century beyond what Newtonian mechanics predicted, a discrepancy known and precisely measured decades before Einstein arrived at General Relativity, and still cited as one of that theory's most compelling early confirmations once Einstein showed GR entailed exactly that figure. The difficulty, as I understand it, is that if the datum was already known with certainty when the theory was proposed — P(E) was already 1 for any astronomer assessing GR in 1915 — then conditionalizing on E cannot move one's credence in H at all, since conditionalizing on a proposition one already assigns probability 1 leaves that credence exactly where it was. Taken at face value this seems to show that the Mercury result could not have confirmed General Relativity, which is about as direct a contradiction of settled scientific judgment as a piece of formal epistemology could produce.

I gather Daniel Garber and Richard Jeffrey proposed a way out by denying that real reasoners are logically omniscient, so that what actually confirms the theory is not the old datum E itself but the newly acquired awareness that H entails E — a logical fact not previously worked out, and so not already certain. That move has a definite appeal, but it also strikes me as suspiciously convenient, and I believe I have seen at least a gesture in the literature toward the worry that the same trick should be replayable one level up: once a scientist has actually derived and absorbed the fact that H entails E, haven't they thereby become certain of that entailment too, so that the very proposition now doing the confirming work is itself "old evidence," threatening to go epistemically inert by the same argument that felled the naive account? I would be grateful for a philosopher's hand talking me through this: why exactly does old evidence pose a problem for naive Bayesian conditionalization in the first place, and does the Garber–Jeffrey move genuinely dissolve that puzzle, or does it just push the difficulty back to the level of logical learning, one step removed from where Glymour originally located it?
