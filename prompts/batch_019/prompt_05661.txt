Three months ago I moved from a master's program in philosophy into a machine-learning research fellowship, and I have ended up in the middle of an argument I did not expect to find waiting for me on the interpretability team I was assigned to. Half of my collaborators talk about the features we recover with sparse autoencoders, and the circuits we trace between them, as discoveries — as though the network is built around this decomposition and we have found joints that were already there, the way a neuroscientist discovers that a biological neuron encodes edge orientation. The other half treats "feature" and "represents" as convenient shorthand for whatever regularities let us predict and intervene on the model's behavior, with no further commitment to the idea that the network is, in some deeper sense, right to have these particular features rather than some other equally serviceable decomposition of the same activations.

This has stopped feeling like a merely verbal dispute to me, because our safety case leans on the answer. If a circuit "represents" a harmful goal only in the deflationary sense of correlating with one under our chosen basis, then a clean-looking circuit might not be tracking anything the model is doing when it actually matters, and I do not yet know how worried that should make me.

I would like you to write an essay that takes a side on this rather than leaving it open. Weigh the strongest grounds for skepticism about representational realism here — the underdetermination created by superposition and polysemanticity, and the worry, familiar from the probing-classifier literature, that an autoencoder can impose structure on activations rather than merely detect structure that was already there — against the strongest grounds for thinking a working notion of representation survives all this, drawing on whatever the philosophy of mind's existing treatments of content and representation (Dennett's "real patterns," teleosemantic and causal theories of content, deflationary positions like Frances Egan's on computational explanation) have to offer on whether "represents" needs a metaphysically demanding reading in the first place. Please do not split the difference: tell me whether, and under what conditions, current interpretability practice is entitled to realist talk about neural network representations, and say plainly what that verdict implies for how much my team should trust what our tools report.
