A few of us who did our philosophy MA together still run a monthly reading group, mostly as an excuse to keep arguing the way we used to as students. This month we read Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree," written to oppose Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary degree, in which she argues that he was guilty of murder for ordering the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. It produced the longest discussion the group has had in two years, and toward the end it narrowed into a back-and-forth between two members, Marta and Denis, that the rest of us mostly just sat and watched. I've tried to reconstruct their exchange below as faithfully as I can, because at the time I genuinely couldn't tell who was getting the better of it at each stage, and I'd like a considered, move-by-move verdict rather than just an overall winner.

(1) Marta opened with what she took to be Anscombe's central claim: Truman intentionally used the killing of a huge number of civilians as the very means of forcing Japan's surrender, and for Anscombe it simply doesn't matter that this arguably shortened the war or spared lives an invasion would otherwise have cost, since choosing to kill the innocent as a means to a good end is murder regardless of the balance of consequences. (2) Denis granted the description but said this sounds like it could still be, at most, what the double-effect literature calls a "strategic bomber" case rather than a "terror bomber" one: perhaps what the plan really targeted was Japan's capacity and will to keep fighting, and the civilian deaths, however massive and certain, were a foreseen but unintended side effect of achieving that rather than the mechanism itself. (3) Marta replied that the historical particulars settle this in favor of the terror-bomber reading: the targets were chosen as population and morale centers in the first place, not incidentally destroyed en route to some other objective, so on the means-end account of intention Anscombe works out in "Intention," the killing of civilians was constitutive of the plan rather than a side effect of it. (4) Denis countered that this rescues Anscombe's principle only by making it hostage to a contested empirical claim about what Truman's planners were actually optimizing for, and that a moral verdict which pivots entirely on archival reconstruction of wartime targeting rationale isn't the clean conceptual result Anscombe presents it as. (5) Marta answered that every application of the intended/foreseen distinction depends on facts about the plan in exactly this way, including in the stylized cases philosophers use to motivate double effect generally, so this isn't a special problem for the Truman case; she added that Anscombe doesn't rest everything on that distinction anyway, since she also denies that any amount of averted harm elsewhere could license deliberately killing this many innocents as a means, which is a further, independent premise. (6) Denis replied that this further premise is exactly what's in dispute, and that dismissing it as beyond argument — in the spirit of Anscombe's later remark, in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (the essay where she coined the term "consequentialism"), that anyone treating such questions as open shows a "corrupt mind" — isn't itself an argument against those who think something like a genuine supreme emergency can override even very weighty constraints on harming civilians; to him, Marta's position was starting to look less like a refutation of Truman than an eloquent restatement of an absolutism that plenty of careful just-war thinkers simply don't share.
