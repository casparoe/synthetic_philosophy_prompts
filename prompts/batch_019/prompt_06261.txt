Over the past few weeks I've been agonizing over whether to leave a secure, well-paid position as in-house counsel at a mid-sized company for a much riskier, lower-paying role helping to build a small nonprofit legal clinic, and no pro-and-con list I draw up ever tips decisively in either direction. At dinner, a friend finishing her philosophy PhD told me, only half-joking, that I was describing a textbook "hard choice" in something close to the technical sense, and pointed me toward Ruth Chang's work on value incommensurability. I have been reading around the topic ever since, and I keep getting stuck on a disagreement I don't know how to resolve myself.

Chang rejects what she calls the trichotomy thesis: the assumption that whenever two options are comparable at all, exactly one of three relations must hold between them -- better, worse, or equally good. Her small-improvement argument is supposed to show this is false: sweeten one option slightly (say, a modest raise attached to the corporate job) and the sweetened option still isn't better than the other, the two still aren't equal, and it obviously isn't worse than it was before -- which she takes as evidence that a fourth relation, "parity," was governing the comparison all along, one that a small improvement isn't enough to force into any of the other three boxes. But other philosophers, most notably John Broome, have argued that the whole phenomenon is just the vagueness of "better than" wearing a new metaphysical costume: being unable to rank two options is no different in kind from being unable to say exactly when a collection of grains becomes a heap, and licenses no special new relation at all.

What I'd like from you is an essay that actually adjudicates between these two readings rather than laying them out side by side. Please engage with Chang's best reason for denying that parity reduces to vagueness -- roughly, that in a hard choice we aren't uncertain which option is better, we're confident that none of the three standard relations hold, which looks like a different situation from an ordinary borderline case -- and tell me whether that reason survives scrutiny. I'd also like the practical stakes spelled out: if Chang is right that I can resolve a case of parity by "creating a reason" through the sheer act of committing to one option, that's a very different account of what I'd be doing in making this decision than if the vagueness theorists are right and I'm simply choosing however I please within an indeterminate zone. Please end with an actual verdict on whether parity is a genuine, sui generis value relation, rather than a summary of considerations on both sides.
