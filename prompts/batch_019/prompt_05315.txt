I teach a graduate seminar on twentieth-century political thought, and this term two of our sessions were devoted to Simone Weil's short 1943 essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," composed in London not long before her death and published only afterward. Weil argues that a political party, regardless of its platform, is constituted by three features: it functions as a machine for manufacturing collective opinion; it exerts a standing psychological pressure on each member that makes a certain line obligatory for their thinking even on questions far removed from the party's original purpose; and its own indefinite growth becomes, in practice, an end that takes precedence over truth and over justice. On this basis she concludes that organized parties are incipiently totalitarian even within a competitive, pluralist system, and that a healthier democracy would abolish them outright while retaining elections and free deliberation among unaffiliated individuals, rather than merely multiplying or regulating them.

I have found myself turning this essay over in connection with a much smaller and more mundane setting than the one Weil had in mind: the nine-person steering committee of a small scholarly society in my subfield that I help run. We have no parties in any formal sense, yet on questions as different as which papers to accept for our annual meeting, how to spend a small bequest, and whether to keep our meetings permanently online, the same two informal camps recur and vote together, and I have more than once caught myself predicting a colleague's vote from their camp before hearing a word of their reasoning.

Please describe the most important implications of Weil's argument for how I should understand and respond to this situation. I am especially interested in whether the three features she treats as constitutive of a "party" actually transfer down to a body this small and informal, or whether her diagnosis tacitly depends on conditions of scale, professionalization, or permanence that a nine-person committee simply lacks; and, since I have none of the legislative power that Weil's own proposal presupposes, what her argument implies about how I ought to change my own habits of attention, speech, and voting if I mean to take her diagnosis seriously.
