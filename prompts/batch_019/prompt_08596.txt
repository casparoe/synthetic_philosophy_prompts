A friend and I had a long argument about whether epistemic contextualism (in Keith DeRose's sense) and pragmatic encroachment (also known as subject-sensitive invariantism, and associated with John Hawthorne and Jason Stanley) are genuinely different positions, or just two vocabularies for the same underlying facts. We agreed that both frameworks handle the standard low-stakes/high-stakes bank case identically: on the very same evidence, someone with nothing riding on it can 'know' the bank is open on Saturday, while someone who would be ruined by a bounced payment intuitively cannot. My friend's position was that, since both theories were built to accommodate exactly this kind of case, no example involving a single subject in a single conversation could ever pull them apart, and that anyone who thinks otherwise has misunderstood one of the two views. I couldn't produce a counterexample on the spot, but I remain unconvinced. Please construct one scenario, with a fully specified evidential situation, in which contextualism and pragmatic encroachment deliver different verdicts about the truth of one particular utterance of a knowledge-attributing sentence. Then explain precisely which structural feature of your scenario does the work of separating the two theories, and why the ordinary bank cases lack that feature.
