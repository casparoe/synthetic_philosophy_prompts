In my government class, we spent a week on a mock trial unit built around a negligence lawsuit that our teacher adapted from a real case. Under the judge's instructions, the defendant could only be held liable for the plaintiff's injury if two separate factual questions both came out a certain way: first, whether she owed the plaintiff a duty of care and breached it, and second, whether that specific breach was actually what caused the injury, as opposed to some unrelated cause. Once our class of eighteen had deliberated, our teacher had us vote on the verdict in two different ways just to see whether the method itself would matter. In the first round, we voted on the two factual questions separately: a two-thirds majority said there was a breach, and a two-thirds majority (not quite the same group of people) said the breach caused the injury, so by the judge's own rule, the defendant should have come out liable. In the second round, our teacher instead asked each of us to just give a single up-or-down verdict based on our overall impression of the case, and this time only a third of the class said "liable," with two-thirds saying "not liable," because plenty of people who were convinced on one factual question weren't convinced on the other.

What's stuck with me is that nobody involved seemed to be reasoning badly or being inconsistent, since each student's answers to the two factual questions lined up logically with their own overall verdict when you looked at that person in isolation. It was only once we combined everyone's votes that the two ways of tallying the class pulled apart. My teacher mentioned in passing that this sort of thing is a recognized issue with group decision-making in general and not some fluke of our particular class, but the bell rang before she could explain further, and a few friends and I have been arguing about it ever since without really resolving anything. It has stayed on my mind partly because I also sit on my school's Honor Council, which decides academic integrity cases through a similar two-stage process: first the council rules on specific factual questions about what a student did, and only then on whether that adds up to a violation. Knowing that two seemingly fair ways of counting the very same set of votes can point to opposite outcomes makes me nervous both about verdicts we have already handed down and about how much weight to put on the specific procedure we use going forward.

Could you help me make sense of what's actually going on in a situation like this? I would like to understand whether one of the two voting methods can be considered the genuinely correct one and the other a kind of mistake, or whether there's some deeper reason both can be entirely reasonable on their own terms and still contradict each other. And if it's the latter, I'd be curious whether there is anything a group like our Honor Council could realistically do to keep this from producing verdicts that end up feeling arbitrary or unfair.
