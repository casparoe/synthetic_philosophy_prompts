I sit on the small interdisciplinary panel that awards our university's seed grants at the intersection of formal philosophy and AI safety, serving as the resident logician among the computer scientists. This cycle's most contested proposal argues that the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents should be dissolved rather than solved: Löb's theorem prevents a proof-based agent from trusting that whatever its successor proves "good" really is good, unless the agent could already prove this directly, which defeats the point of delegating the decision at all. The applicant's proposed fix replaces proof with logical-induction-style calibrated credence in a successor's future reliability, on the grounds that no embedded, resource-bounded agent ever had proof-level guarantees about anything else. Two proof theorists on the panel object that this secures only calibration in the limit of indefinitely many trading rounds, never the guarantee needed before a single irreversible decision — precisely the case the tiling-agents literature was meant to handle — so the proposal redefines "self-trust" rather than earning it. Our funding cycle closes in ten days. Draft the panel's decision memo: fund, decline, or fund contingent on named revisions, with a timeline and reasoning the applicant could actually act on, and a fair hearing for the proof theorists' objection.
