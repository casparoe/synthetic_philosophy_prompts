In my graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, we recently spent two sessions on a question I still find genuinely unresolved: can omissions—failures to act, absences, non-events—be causes in their own right, or is "causation by omission" really just causal explanation wearing causal language it hasn't earned? The trouble is that our two most influential general theories of causation pull in opposite directions. A broadly Lewisian counterfactual account seems to entail, almost for free, that if my office philodendron would have survived had I watered it, then my failure to water it is among the causes of its death, since the death counterfactually depends on the omission in exactly the way the theory requires. A conserved-quantity or process theorist in the spirit of Salmon or Dowe denies this outright, since no physical process links my inaction to the plant's wilting, and offers at best a deflated "quasi-causal" relation to capture whatever truth there is in the claim without diluting causation proper.

Matters get worse once you notice how promiscuous the counterfactual test is: my neighbor, the mail carrier, and for that matter a stranger on the other side of the planet all satisfy exactly the same counterfactual with respect to my plant, yet nobody wants to say all of them caused its death. The standard remedy is to restrict the field of candidate omissive causes to whoever had some relevant duty, or to whichever failure deviates from the statistically or causally "normal" course of events—but this seems to buy back selectivity only by smuggling normative or default-relative facts into what was supposed to be a purely metaphysical relation. A colleague from the law school who sat in on the session thought this was no real difficulty, since tort and criminal law have long conditioned liability for omissions on an antecedent duty of care; but that only tells us how lawyers manage the problem for their own purposes, not whether the underlying metaphysics can bear the weight, or whether "cause" in the courtroom and "cause" in the seminar room have quietly drifted apart.

I'd like you to write an essay that actually settles this for me rather than surveying it. Lay out the strongest versions of the main positions—permissive counterfactual theories, process-theoretic denials that omissions are ever causes, Helen Beebee's proposal that we should sharply separate causation from causal explanation and treat omissive "causal" talk as explanation without causation, and the various duty- or normalcy-based restriction strategies—and weigh what each costs against what it buys. Then defend a specific verdict: are omissions ever genuine causes, and if so, is there a principled way to block the profligacy of candidate causes that doesn't covertly presuppose the very facts about duty, normalcy, or responsibility that appeals to omissive causation are so often meant to help establish (as in law, but also in everyday blame and praise)? A conditional verdict is fine, provided it is a real one—something like "omissions are causes only relative to a contextually fixed contrast class, and the profligacy worry is thereby a problem for careless paraphrase rather than for the metaphysics" would count; simply concluding that reasonable people disagree would not.
