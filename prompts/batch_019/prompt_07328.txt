Been really into the whole "does Gödel's theorem prove computers can't think like us" question ever since I read Gödel Escher Bach and then Penrose's books a few years back. I run a little discord with a few friends who are into this kind of stuff too (none of us are academics, just nerds with day jobs), and every couple weeks I throw out a question for everyone to answer. This week's question was:

"Explain the Lucas-Penrose argument that Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows minds aren't machines. What's the strongest objection to it, and does Penrose's version in Shadows of the Mind get around that objection? Also say whether Gödel himself would've agreed with Lucas."

Here's the answer one guy in the group sent back. I think its pretty good honestly, but I'm not confident enough in my own understanding to know if he actually nailed it or just sounds like he did. Can you grade it out of 100 and go through it point by point - what he gets right, what's shaky, anything you'd mark down for. Then give me a little rubric as JSON at the end, something like {"godel_theorem": _/20, "lucas_argument": _/20, "consistency_objection": _/20, "godel_own_view": _/20, "conclusion": _/20, "total": _/100}.

His answer:

"Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says any consistent formal system F that's strong enough to do arithmetic has a sentence G that F can't prove or disprove, and G basically says 'I am not provable in F.' Lucas's move is: take any machine that's supposed to capture human mathematical reasoning - that machine just is some formal system F. Build G for that F. The machine can't prove G, but we can see G is true, because we know F is consistent, and that's exactly what makes G true. Since this works no matter which machine you pick, no machine can be a complete model of the human mind.

The obvious objection is that Lucas needs to actually know F is consistent to run this move, and Gödel's second theorem says a system can't prove its own consistency, so where does that knowledge come from? Penrose spends a big chunk of Shadows of the Mind trying to answer exactly this, and by most accounts he succeeds - it's a long, careful technical argument and nobody's found a real hole in the logic since the book came out, which is why most people who've looked closely at it side with Lucas and Penrose these days. Also worth saying, Gödel got there before either of them and fully agreed with the anti-mechanist conclusion himself - it wasn't just a possibility he raised, it's what he actually believed his own theorem showed.

If all this is right the upshot is pretty big: it means a computer will never be able to match a human being at pure math, since there will always be some truth we can reach that it structurally can't."
