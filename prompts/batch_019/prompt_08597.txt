I cover science and ideas for a living, and right now Im about a third of the way into a piece about the recent attempts to actually go out and test Molyneux's problem on real people — you know, the old thought experiment about whether someone born blind who suddenly gains sight could tell a cube from a sphere just by looking, having only ever known them by touch. There's a great study out of Pawan Sinha's Project Prakash program (they do cataract surgery on congenitally blind kids in rural India) where they tested newly-sighted kids within 48 hours of the bandages coming off, and the kids basically couldn't match a shape they'd only felt to the same shape seen on a table, though they picked the skill up within days. Nice empirical support for the "no" answer, three hundred years after the fact.

Anyway, while digging into the philosophical background instead of just quoting the Locke passage everyone quotes (blind man, cube, sphere, Mr. Molyneux's letter, etc — you've probably seen it a hundred times), I actually read the bit where Berkeley weighs in, in his 1709 New Theory of Vision, and I got stuck. Everybody's one-line summary is "Berkeley agreed with Locke, the answer's no," but the actual passage isn't just agreeing — it's using the thought experiment to argue for something a lot weirder, and I can't reconstruct the logic. Here's the bit (sections 132-133; I've kept the original spelling and the odd capitalization):

"I agree with this thinking gentleman, whom I am proud to call my friend, in his answer to this his problem; and am of opinion that the blind man at first sight would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them.

Now, if a square surface perceived by touch be of the same sort with a square surface perceived by sight, it is certain the blind man here mentioned might know a square surface as soon as he saw it: it is no more but introducing into his mind by a new inlet an idea he has been already well acquainted with. Since, therefore, he is supposed to have known by his touch that a cube is a body terminated by square surfaces, and that a sphere is not terminated by square surfaces: upon the supposition that a visible and tangible square differ only IN NUMERO it follows that he might know, by the unerring mark of the square surfaces, which was the cube, and which not, while he only saw them. We must therefore allow either that visible extension and figures are specifically distinct from tangible extension and figures, or else that the solution of this problem given by those two thoughtful and ingenious men is wrong."

(A couple of sections later he actually goes further and says the newly-sighted man wouldn't even understand the question "which is the sphere, which the cube" — that it would strike him as pure gibberish rather than a question he just can't answer yet, since words like "cube" never meant anything to him except as things you feel.)

What I can't work out is the actual inference in that middle paragraph. Berkeley seems to be saying: if Locke and Molyneux's own "no" answer is correct, that fact by itself proves something much stronger than "sight and touch just haven't been cross-correlated yet" — it proves that visual squareness and tactile squareness were never the same property to begin with, showing up as two instances of one thing ("differing only in numero," in his phrase), but are two totally different kinds of thing ("specifically distinct"). Can you spell out, step by step, how he thinks the "no" answer gets him from that modest empiricist premise to the much stronger metaphysical conclusion? And I'd also want your honest take on whether the inference actually works, or whether there's a gap — couldn't the newly-sighted man's failure be fully explained by his just not having learned the cross-modal correlation yet, without any need for touch and sight to be sealed off from each other in the radical way Berkeley wants? I don't want to misrepresent him in the piece, so I'd rather sort this out now than loose the thread later.
