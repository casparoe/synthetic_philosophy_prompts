Random context: I got sent to Weimar for a few months for work (an audit thing, nothing to do with literature) and, mostly out of boredom, started joining this little Thursday-night Goethe reading group that meets above a bar near the Goethe-Schiller archive — a few retirees, a couple of grad students, generally very calm. Last week wasn't calm. We'd just gone through the "Classical Walpurgis Night" scene in Faust II, where Thales and Anaxagoras basically re-enact the old Neptunist/Vulcanist argument over whether rock formations build up gradually or get thrown up in sudden violent episodes, alongside some Eckermann material on Goethe's actual politics, and it set off a real argument between two regulars, Jonas and Petra, that never got resolved before we had to clear out for closing.

(1) Jonas's opening claim was that Goethe's whole political temperment — the horror at the French Revolution, the unfinished play "Die Aufgeregten," famously sitting out the patriotic fever of the 1813-15 Wars of Liberation while basically every other German writer was celebrating Napoleon's downfall — is the exact same instinct that made him a stubborn Neptunist in geology long after most people had moved on to Vulcanism, and that he even staged that fight directly through Thales and Anaxagoras. One coherent worldview, Jonas said: a deep distrust of sudden rupture, whether it's happening to rock strata or to regimes. (2) Petra said that's a suspiciously tidy theory to reach for after the fact — a man whose entire livelihood for decades was a minister's salary from a small absolutist duchy has an obvious, boring reason to fear collapse that has nothing to do with rock formation — and anyway the theory can't handle how genuinely starstruck Goethe was meeting Napoleon at Erfurt in 1808, a guy who'd grabbed an empire by force, which is about as sudden and violent as political change gets. (3) Jonas answered that Goethe wasn't admiring Napoleon as a revolutionary but as the one who'd ended the Revolution and put hierarchy and form back on top of the chaos, so being dazzled by him is completely consistent with hating rupture rather then a betrayal of it — and if Goethe were really just siding with whoever held power, 1813 was the obvious moment to switch loudly, and instead he held back and got branded unpatriotic for it. (4) Petra's comeback was that at this point the theory explains everything and therefore nothing — quietism, Napoleon-worship, and refusing to join a popular war are all apparently "gradualism" now — and that the much simpler story is just a lifelong courtier's nose for stability, dressed up after the fact in the language of morphology.

I've known both of them for like three weeks and have zero standing to referee this, but it's been bugging me all week. Could you go through it move by move — (1) through (4) — and say who actually gets the better of each individual exchange, rather than just picking an overall winner? And if there's a smarter way of characterizing what Goethe's politics actually were that both of them are talking past, I'd genuinely like to hear it. End with a straightforward verdict in your last line on whether Jonas or Petra comes out ahead overall, or whether it's genuinely a wash.
