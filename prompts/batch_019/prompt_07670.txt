I'm a PhD student in international relations currently sitting in on a graduate philosophy seminar on the ethics of armed conflict, and this week's session on the "moral equality of combatants" left several of us more unsettled than anything we'd covered so far. We worked through Walzer's defense of the doctrine in *Just and Unjust Wars* alongside McMahan's revisionist attack on it in *Killing in War*, and while I can reconstruct each side's argument in isolation, our discussion kept collapsing into "Walzer says X, but McMahan says Y" without anyone pressing on where the exchange actually gets stuck once both sides are pushed past their opening moves.

Could you write a dialogue between a convinced Walzerian traditionalist and a convinced McMahan-style revisionist that works through this disagreement in some depth? I'd like them to anchor the exchange in a concrete case rather than staying at the level of slogans: the sort of ordinary conscript, drafted into the army of a state that is the clear and uncontroversial aggressor in a war, who has committed no atrocities and has, so far as he can tell, done nothing but observe the laws of war, killing only enemy combatants in circumstances that would be entirely unobjectionable had his side's cause been just. Have the revisionist press the claim that this soldier is doing something seriously wrong merely by fighting, and that he is liable to be killed by the defending side's soldiers on grounds quite different from ordinary combatant status, while the traditionalist defends the independence of jus in bello from jus ad bellum and the equal permission of combatants on both sides to fight. I don't want the traditionalist to rest on gesturing at epistemic limitations and the practical benefits of a symmetric war convention without saying more, and I don't want the revisionist to rest on simply asserting that justification defeats liability to attack without engaging the best objections to that move. Please have both characters argue at the level of people who have actually read each other's work, let each concede real ground where their own position is weakest, and end wherever the disagreement genuinely runs out rather than forcing an artificial resolution.
