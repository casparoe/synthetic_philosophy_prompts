Over drinks after our philosophy reading group last week, a friend and I ended up spending the better part of an hour re-litigating Pascal's Wager, and for once the sticking point wasn't the well-worn "but which god?" objection. Her position was that the Wager fails on its own decision-theoretic terms even before rival religions enter the picture: once an infinite payoff attaches to salvation, all sorts of diluted "mixed" strategies — resolving to pursue belief only if, say, a coin lands heads, or only if some independent long-shot event occurs — end up carrying exactly the same infinite expected value as wagering for God outright, at a fraction of the cost, so nothing in the argument's own logic can single out full-blown wagering as uniquely rational. I objected that God, on the theology the Wager presupposes, rewards genuine faith rather than a coin flip's worth of hedging, so these diluted strategies don't really buy the same payoff — to which she replied that this move just smuggles in an entire, unearned theory of what God cares about, which is exactly the sort of extra premise the formal argument was supposed to let us avoid needing. We got no further than agreeing that this is somehow the same difficulty Pascal faces when his own imagined unbeliever protests that he cannot simply choose to believe, and Pascal answers not with an argument but with a regimen of practice: take the holy water, have masses said, and belief will follow of itself.

I'd like you to pick up roughly where we left off. Write a dialogue between two people standing outside the church that Pascal's cure sends the unbeliever into — one about to go in, still convinced the Wager is sound decision theory once properly stated; the other blocking the doorway, pressing the mixed-strategy point and the further question of what, if anything, could make Pascal's God indifferent between a sincere convert and a well-engineered hedge. I want two equally sharp interlocutors who actually engage with each other's last move rather than reciting prepared positions, real concessions and real pressure running in both directions, and an ending that hands neither side a clean win but forces both of them to say out loud exactly which extra premise about God's preferences their own position quietly depends on.
