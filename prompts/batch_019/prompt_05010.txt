I cover science and technology for a national magazine, and I've taken the public-member seat on my state's newly created Pretrial Risk Assessment Oversight Board. The board must recommend whether to keep using the actuarial tool that assigns each defendant a single risk score by placing them in a reference class of similar past cases (same charge type, age band, prior-record level) and reporting that class's recidivism rate. One of my fellow board members, a philosopher who works on the foundations of probability, calls this a textbook instance of the reference class problem: choose a narrower or differently weighted reference class and the same defendant gets a materially different score, with no principled way to say which class is the right one -- so the number handed to the judge isn't really a fact about this particular person's chances at all. Other members point out that every actuarial prediction we already rely on, including life-insurance pricing, faces exactly this problem without anyone treating it as meaningless. (Set aside the familiar racial-disparity critique of these tools; that's a separate objection and not what's dividing us here.) Draft the board's recommendation memo, due before we submit our report to the legislature next month: keep the tool as-is, discontinue it, or keep it only under specified conditions -- with reasoning the philosopher could actually respond to.
