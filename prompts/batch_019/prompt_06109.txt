Question: Using the "stock market" framing from Garrabrant et al's logical induction paper, explain what the logical induction criterion actually guarantees about a sequence of price assignments to logical sentences, and explain why this isn't the same thing as ordinary Bayesian updating on proofs as they arrive.

Student's answer: A logical inductor treats each sentence like a stock that pays out $1 if the sentence is eventually proven true and $0 if false, and the "price" the market assigns at each stage is the inductor's credence in it. The logical induction criterion says that no trader whose strategy can be computed in polynomial time can make unbounded profit off the market as more sentences get settled, so the prices can't be exploited by any efficient pattern-spotter even though the market never gets to consult a halting oracle. This is different from ordinary Bayesian updating because there's no fixed hypothesis space or likelihood function being conditioned on; prices move because a trader would otherwise be able to money-pump the market. That's really just an extension of Solomonoff induction's idea of weighting hypotheses by their Kolmogorov complexity, except applied to sentences instead of observation sequences. One of the main results is that the price of every sentence converges as the market runs longer, and because the limiting market has to be coherent, it converges to 1 for every true sentence and 0 for every false one -- which is how logical inductors eventually "know" things like Goldbach's conjecture even without anyone finding a proof.
