I'm about three-quarters through my second novel and stuck on a scene I really want to nail. The book follows five philosophy grad students from the same cohort in the early 2000s, and now, twenty years on, two of them, Mara and Theo, who circled each other on and off since their first-year metaethics seminar, are finally getting married. One of the toasts is being given by Priya, Mara's old officemate, who has spent her whole career on fitting-attitude and buck-passing theories of value and has basically made her name on the wrong kind of reasons problem -- the puzzle of how you tell apart a reason to value something that's actually grounded in the thing's value from a reason that just happens to produce the pro-attitude anyway (like the classic case of a demon whos going to hurt you unless you come to admire something worthless).

I want Priya's toast to actually be structured by that problem, not just namedrop it. She's constitutionally incapable of standing up and listing nice things about the couple without some part of her brain auditing whether each reason she's about to give for why they're perfect together is the right kind (actually tracking who Mara and Theo are) or the wrong kind (the sort that fifteen years of habit, convenience, or sunk cost could've produced just as easily, demon or no demon), and she has to work through that compulsion out loud, in front of a room thats mostly non-philosopher family plus three or four old cohort-mates who'll absolutely clock it if she gets the philosophy wrong, and land somewhere that's actually moving instead of a seminar. Could you write me Priya's toast? I'd like it genuinely warm and a bit funny, and I want the substance of the problem to actually be right, not hand-waved -- I read a couple of papers on this for research but I don't fully trust myself to reconstruct the puzzle correctly on my own, never mind dramatize it.
