I've been doing a lot of on-call rotations this quarter and it's gotten me thinking about something that's more philosophy than engineering, so bear with me. During an incident I basically never get "certain" evidence about anything - a dashboard looks kinda spiky but could just be noise, a teammate says "pretty sure its the cache layer but not 100%", a log line is suggestive but not conclusive. I know regular Bayes' rule from the ML side of my job (spam filters, A/B test analysis, etc), where you condition on some event E that you've actually observed to be true. But a friend who's really into the rationalist blogosphere sent me down a rabbit hole on something called "Jeffrey conditionalization" (also apparently called "probability kinematics"). Richard Jeffrey came up with it using this example about judging the color of a piece of cloth by candlelight - you never actually become certain its green, your confidence just shifts to like 70% green, and the whole updating rule is built to handle that kind of partial, uncertain evidence instead of requiring you to ever fully "learn" E the way strict Bayes conditionalization does.

Here's the part that's been bugging me: apparently, unlike plain vanilla conditionalization, doing a sequence of these Jeffrey-style updates is not commutative in general - so updating on evidence A and then evidence B can leave you with a different final set of beliefs than updating on B and then A, unless some special condition holds between them. And I read that this also messes with the usual "Dutch book" arguments people use to justify why you're rationally required to update via Bayes' rule in the first place - apparently it's genuinely contested whether an equivalent argument even goes through for Jeffrey's rule. That combination kind of freaked me out a bit, because it suggests there might not be one uniquely "rational" way to combine a pile of uncertain signals.

So, given all that: what are the most important implications of this for how a person (or a team, during an incident retro or whatever) should actually go about combining several pieces of uncertain evidence in practice? Concretely - does the order in which I happen to check the dashboard vs. talk to my teammate vs. read the logs actually matter for what I should end up believing at the end, and should that worry me? And does the shakiness of the Dutch-book justification mean I should be less confident that "update like a good Bayesian" is even the right normative bar here, versus just falling back on strict conditionalization whenever I can actually pin evidence down to certainty?
