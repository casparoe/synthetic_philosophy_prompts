Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory says rational agents can have a genuine "risk attitude" on top of ordinary diminishing marginal utility -- a separate function that reweights the cumulative probability of ending up with a good vs a bad outcome before you plug things into the expected-utility formula. The whole selling point is that this lets Allais-paradox-style preferences (paying extra for certainty even when its not expected-value-maximizing) count as fully rational instead of a mistake.

Here's the problem though: if you break a single gamble up into a sequence of smaller choices instead of offering it all at once, an REU-maximizer who just re-optimizes at each step seems to be money-pumpable in a way a plain expected-utility maximizer isn't. Argue that this dynamic-consistency problem is actually fatal to REU as a theory of ideal rationality (not just "good enough for messy humans"), and that the standard fix -- commit in advance to a plan, kind of like resolute choice -- doesn't save it: it either smuggles back in exactly the axiom Buchak dropped to get REU off the ground, or it stops telling you what to actually do once you're standing at the choice node wondering whether to stick with the plan.
