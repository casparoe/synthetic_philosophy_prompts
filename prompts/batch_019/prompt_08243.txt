I have spent some time developing a connection between the Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch model of informational cascades and Eliezer Yudkowsky's case against "modest epistemology" in Inadequate Equilibria, and I would like some outside pressure-testing before writing it up properly.

The core of the BHW model: agents choose sequentially between two options, each holding a private signal about which option is better, and each observing only the choices—not the signals—of those who went before. Once enough predecessors have settled on the same option, a rational Bayesian agent sets aside their own signal and imitates them, since the accumulated public record now outweighs their one private data point. Two features of this result seem underappreciated whenever "the wisdom of crowds" gets invoked against a lone dissenter: once a cascade is underway, later choices carry no further evidential weight at all, since the agents making them are no longer conditioning on their private information; and the resulting herd is informationally fragile, resting on little more than the first couple of signals plus everyone else's silence, so that a single new public signal—or one person simply stating their private information rather than just acting on it—can topple the whole structure.

My tentative claim is that this gives Yudkowsky's anti-modesty argument more rigor than it usually gets credit for: whenever a consensus could plausibly have arisen through this sort of sequential, action-only observation rather than through independent aggregation or the open exchange of reasons, its sheer size is close to worthless as evidence, and the "who are you to disagree with everyone" rejoinder fails for a specific, mechanical reason rather than merely as a matter of temperament.

I would welcome comments on two things in particular: whether I have represented the cascade model accurately, and whether I am overreaching in transplanting it onto informal, real-world consensus formation, where people sometimes do share reasons rather than bare actions, and where the tidy two-option setup of the model may not carry over so cleanly. If the thesis is weaker than I am giving it credit for, I would rather find out now.
