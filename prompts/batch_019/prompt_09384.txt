I don't have any academic background in philosophy, but for the past few months I've been teaching myself by working through primary texts I can find free online, letting one thing lead to the next. I fell into this particular rabbit hole after seeing an offhand mention, in something I read on a forum, of philosophers today who work with "paraconsistent" logics that don't explode into proving everything the moment they contain a contradiction, and who sometimes go further and argue that a statement and its denial might, in a handful of cases, actually both be true. That struck me as such a strange thing to claim that I wanted to go back and see, in Aristotle's own words, why the law of non-contradiction was ever supposed to be beyond question in the first place, instead of just taking someone else's summary of it on faith.

That search took me to Aristotle's Metaphysics, Book IV (Gamma), Part 4, in the old W. D. Ross translation posted on the Internet Classics Archive. Aristotle first admits that the principle itself can't be demonstrated outright, since any proof would need to rest on something even more basic, and then offers what he calls a negative demonstration instead. Here is the passage in full:

"We can, however, demonstrate negatively even that this view is impossible, if our opponent will only say something; and if he says nothing, it is absurd to seek to give an account of our views to one who cannot give an account of anything, in so far as he cannot do so. For such a man, as such, is from the start no better than a vegetable. Now negative demonstration I distinguish from demonstration proper, because in a demonstration one might be thought to be begging the question, but if another person is responsible for the assumption we shall have negative proof, not demonstration. The starting-point for all such arguments is not the demand that our opponent shall say that something either is or is not (for this one might perhaps take to be a begging of the question), but that he shall say something which is significant both for himself and for another; for this is necessary, if he really is to say anything. For, if he means nothing, such a man will not be capable of reasoning, either with himself or with another. But if any one grants this, demonstration will be possible; for we shall already have something definite. The person responsible for the proof, however, is not he who demonstrates but he who listens; for while disowning reason he listens to reason. And again he who admits this has admitted that something is true apart from demonstration (so that not everything will be 'so and not so'). First then this at least is obviously true, that the word 'be' or 'not be' has a definite meaning, so that not everything will be 'so and not so'."

What I can't work out is how much this argument actually delivers. Aristotle seems careful, at first, to avoid simply demanding that his opponent admit "something either is or is not," since he says outright that this would be begging the question — so instead he only asks the opponent to say something "significant," on the grounds that a person who means nothing by his words isn't really saying anything, and so cannot be reasoned with at all. But by the last sentence he is treating that concession — that words must have some definite meaning — as though it already settles that a statement and its denial cannot both be true, and I can't locate the step that gets him from one to the other. Why should "my words have to mean one definite thing" force the conclusion "no statement and its contradictory can both hold"? Is there a premise doing that work that I'm simply not seeing, is Aristotle claiming more than he has actually earned at this point, or have I misunderstood what "signifying one thing" is supposed to establish? I would also be curious, if you happen to know the literature, whether this is the very passage that modern dialetheists — the philosophers who hold that a handful of contradictions really are true — point to when they say Aristotle never actually earns his conclusion, and what exactly they think the argument is missing.
