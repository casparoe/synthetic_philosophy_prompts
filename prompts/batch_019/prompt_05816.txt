So this happened at work and I can't stop thinking about it. A vendor pitched our security team on "quantum readiness" using this expected-value argument: theres maybe a 1-in-2000 chance a real code-breaking quantum computer shows up in the next 5 years, and if it does, everything we've ever encrypted gets cracked retroactively, which they framed as basically company-ending. So even at those tiny odds, the expected loss is way bigger than the $6M they want for a migration project, meaning we'd be dumb not to pay. When our CTO said the real probability is probably more like 1-in-100,000, the sales guy just shrugged and said fine, multiply THAT by the loss and it's still hugely positive EV, so we'd be crazy not to sign. What's bugging me is that this argument "works" no matter how tiny a number you plug in for the odds, so it feels like we could get talked into paying basically anything this way. Is there an actual flaw in this kind of reasoning somewhere, or is this just what taking small risks of huge disasters seriously looks like?
