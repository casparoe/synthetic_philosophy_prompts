My younger sister is getting married in the autumn, and she's asked me to give the toast at the wedding dinner. She and her fiancé actually met in a philosophy PhD program, in a seminar on early twentieth-century ethics, before both leaving academia within a year of each other to become stained-glass conservators; a fair portion of their courtship since has apparently consisted of arguments about whether a window loses something essential once its medieval glass is replaced with a modern reproduction, however faithful. I mention this because it turns out to matter for what I'm asking.

I recently spent several weeks reporting a piece on how G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) — a dry, technical book almost nobody outside philosophy departments has actually read — became something close to scripture for the Bloomsbury Group; Keynes later said its publication felt like "the opening of a new heaven on a new earth." Ever since, I can't stop noticing how well its final chapter fits my sister's wedding, and I strongly suspect the two of them read it closely in that seminar, which raises the stakes for me considerably. Almost everyone who has heard of Moore knows him only for the "naturalistic fallacy." Almost nobody remembers that in that same chapter he argues personal affection and the appreciation of beauty are, by a wide margin, the greatest goods a life can contain, and defends this with some genuinely strange claims: that a beautiful world forever unwitnessed by any mind is still a better world than an ugly one nobody will ever see; that a real beautiful object is intrinsically better than an equally beautiful copy, quite apart from any practical reason you could give for preferring the original — which is uncomfortably close to the actual argument about the glass; and, strangest and most useful of all, that loving someone for their own good qualities isn't the highest form personal affection can take. The highest form, Moore says, is loving someone for how they love other people — a kind of second-order affection he ranks above every other good he names, aesthetic appreciation included.

I'd like the toast built out of this material rather than merely decorated with it. Could you write me a wedding toast, meant to be read aloud in three to four minutes, that puts Moore's actual arguments to work — with the claim about loving how someone loves as its emotional core — and finds a natural place for the glass argument if one exists? About a third of the guests are old classmates from that PhD program who will recognize exactly what I'm doing and will silently grade my accuracy; the rest are family who have never heard of Moore and shouldn't feel like they're missing something. It needs to land as a love letter for both groups at once, not a philosophy lecture with sentiment tacked on at the end.
