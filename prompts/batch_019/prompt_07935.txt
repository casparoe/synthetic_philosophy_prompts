I'm working on a piece about the running argument over whether it's appropriate to say a chatbot "believes" something, and I keep coming back to a specific case that seems to sharpen the stakes: hallucinated citations. Every few weeks a story does the rounds about a lawyer or a student getting caught out because a language model confidently invented a plausible-sounding source, complete with author and page number, and commentators routinely describe the failure in mentalistic terms — "it thought the case existed," "it didn't know it was making this up." I'd like to understand, on the merits, whether that kind of talk is defensible or just a journalistic shortcut we've all gotten lazy about.

The clearest defense I know of comes from Daniel Dennett's intentional stance: his claim that treating a system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires is one of three legitimate levels of description, alongside the physical and design stances, justified whenever it predicts a system's behavior more efficiently than either of the other two. In a 1991 paper called "Real Patterns," he pushes this into a kind of mild realism, arguing that beliefs are real in the way a pattern is real even when nothing in the underlying hardware corresponds to a discrete token for it — comparable to a picture that is objectively present in a field of dots too fine to parse one at a time. On this view, if attributing to a model the belief that its fabricated citation is genuine is simply the most efficient way to predict and explain what it says next, that may be all "belief" was ever going to mean, in humans or machines.

The obvious rival is Paul Churchland's eliminative materialism, which treats the entire belief-desire vocabulary as a folk theory no more likely to survive the advance of a mature cognitive science than caloric fluid survived the rise of thermodynamics. Churchland was explicit, moreover, that the vocabulary he expected to replace it with was connectionist — activation patterns across networks rather than sentences and attitudes. That makes the present moment oddly pointed, since language models are exactly the sort of distributed, weight-and-activation systems he had in mind, and yet almost nobody, including the engineers who build them, has actually stopped talking about what these systems "think" or "know." I'd like you to work through how Dennett and Churchland would each, on their own terms, diagnose the hallucinated-citation case, and then tell me which position you think gives the more defensible account of what is happening when a model states a falsehood with apparent confidence — including whether the fact that the substrate really is a neural network counts as evidence for either side.
