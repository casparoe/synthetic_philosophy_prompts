Over dinner last week, a friend of mine who is finishing a doctorate in social ontology and I ended up in a long, unresolved argument about when a set of individually intelligible actions adds up to a genuinely joint action, rather than a mere aggregate of parallel individual actions that happen to share a common source. She kept pressing Margaret Gilbert's line that a joint commitment can be formed quite minimally, through mutual, open expressions of willingness under conditions of common knowledge, and that once such a commitment is in place it generates obligations to the others that no single party can retroactively define away by insisting she never really meant to act with them. I pushed back with Bratman's more deflationary picture, on which what matters is a structure of individual intentions -- each to the effect that "we" do the thing -- that are mutually responsive to one another and whose sub-plans mesh; where that structure is absent, I argued, calling something a joint action is at best a loose way of describing several individually caused violations that happen to look alike. We never settled it, but the argument turned out to be uncomfortably relevant to a case that has just landed in front of a committee I sit on.

I serve on my university's academic integrity board, which is due to hear a case involving six students from the same private group chat for a graduate econometrics course. The take-home problem set, worth 15% of the grade, was to be completed "individually, though students may discuss concepts and general approaches." Over the course of the week, one student, A, posted her own fully worked solution to the hardest question with the message "here's what I got, in case it helps"; she maintains she never intended it as an invitation to copy. Three students, B, C, and D, submitted that solution nearly verbatim, apparently without ever discussing the matter with one another. A fourth, E, read A's post and then wrote up an independent derivation, in his own notation, that nonetheless follows her method at every step. A fifth, F, was in the same chat and saw the message but says she had already solved the problem, unaided, before A posted. Our honor code distinguishes ordinary "individual violations" from the more severely sanctioned category of violations "committed in concert," but defines the latter only as "two or more students acting together, whether or not pursuant to an explicit agreement." The board is now split down the middle: some members think that "acting together" requires something in the neighborhood of a shared intention or a joint commitment among the specific parties charged, which on these facts is doubtful even for B, C, and D; others think that the shared chat and the pattern of reliance on a common source are enough to make A through E a single collective violation, with F the only student in the clear.

Could you draft the memo I will circulate to the board ahead of Thursday's hearing? It should reach a specific, defensible recommendation on how each of the six students -- A through F -- ought to be charged under our existing "acting together" language (no violation, individual violation, or violation committed in concert), and it should close with a brief proposed amendment to that language so the board is not left re-litigating this same question every year. Please make sure the memo genuinely engages the strongest argument for whichever classification it does not adopt in the borderline cases, rather than just asserting a conclusion -- that is exactly what several of my colleagues will be looking for when they read it.
