I don't have a formal background in philosophy, but I've spent a fair amount of time working through Bertrand Russell's writing on my own, including his well-known discussion of what he calls "denoting phrases" and the sentence "The present King of France is bald." As I understand it, since nothing exists for the phrase "the present King of France" to pick out, and Russell treats such a phrase as having no meaning in isolation, the sentence built out of it cannot express a genuine claim either, so his theory of descriptions establishes that the sentence is meaningless rather than simply false. Given that, I've been trying to work out how far this generalizes: does it mean that everyday sentences about things that don't exist, such as "Sherlock Holmes lives on Baker Street," are really just empty strings of words rather than false statements? And if so, why do logicians still bother specifying the exact conditions under which such sentences would be true or false, instead of treating them as nonsense from the start?
