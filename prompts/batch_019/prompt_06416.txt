Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing over Aristotle's sea battle passage and never really settled anything. We agree on the basic worry — that treating a sentence like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" as simply true or false today seems to close off the future's openness — but we disagree about whether any of the standard formal responses actually resolve that worry, as opposed to merely relabeling it. She thinks branching-time semantics dissolves the puzzle outright; I suspect that the Peircean, Ockhamist, and supervaluationist treatments each just relocate the problem to a different level of the theory. Could you explain, with some precision, how the Peircean and Ockhamist readings of the future-tense operator actually differ in their verdicts on a sentence like that one, why supervaluationism was introduced as an improvement on both, and why the "thin red line" move — postulating a single metaphysically distinguished branch as the actual future — is usually thought to fail? I would also like to understand the objection that supervaluationism cannot make good sense of someone being retrospectively vindicated for a correct prediction, and why a relativist, assessment-sensitive semantics along the lines John MacFarlane has proposed is supposed to do better on exactly that point.
