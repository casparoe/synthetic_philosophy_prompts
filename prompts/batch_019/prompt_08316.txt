I am drafting a courtroom-set novel whose climax turns on a hate-crime trial in which the key piece of evidence is a wiretapped phone call in which the defendant repeatedly hurls a racial slur at the victim. In the scene I am currently revising, the defense attorney tries to inoculate the jury against the recording by having her own investigator read the transcript aloud on the stand, then argues in closing that the investigator — and, by extension, the defendant himself, once forced to admit what he said — was merely quoting the word rather than using it to demean anyone, the same distinction, she insists, that lets a court reporter or a professor read a slur aloud from a historical document without becoming complicit in its use.

Trying to get this scene right sent me down something of a rabbit hole in the philosophy-of-language literature on slurs, where it turns out theorists disagree sharply about exactly why slurs are offensive, in a way that bears directly on whether the attorney's "mention, not use" move should actually work. On Christopher Hom's combinatorial externalism, a slur's offensiveness is rooted in a complex derogatory property that the word contributes to a sentence's truth-conditional content; on presuppositional or conventional-implicature accounts, that same derogatory content is real but sits in a separate, non-asserted layer of meaning; and on Anderson and Lepore's prohibitionism, slurs carry no special semantic or expressive content at all, so that the offense is simply a brute social prohibition attaching to the word-form itself — which is precisely why, on their view, merely mentioning or quoting the word remains offensive in a way that mentioning an ordinary offensive description does not.

I would like the scene to be linguistically honest rather than merely dramatically convenient. Could you construct a short worked example for me — a few lines of invented courtroom dialogue, plus a two- or three-sentence gloss — of a case in which a content-based theory of slurs (whether truth-conditional or presuppositional) and Anderson and Lepore's prohibitionism would issue different verdicts about whether the attorney's mention/use distinction gives her client any genuine linguistic cover? I would like the example to hinge on some specific, decidable feature of the exchange, rather than simply restating that the two camps disagree in the abstract.
