So a friend of mine who does quant trading and I got into it this weekend over the "Dutch book" argument for why your beliefs have to obey the probability axioms. His pitch was: if your credences on some set of outcomes don't add up right (like your odds on A, not-A, and some B are inconsistent with each other), a bookie can build a combo of bets off those exact numbers that loses you money in literally every possible world, and that alone proves coherence is a requirement of rationality. I pushed back that this just shows you shouldn't accept dumb bets, not that your beliefs are somehow false, and he didnt really have a comeback beyond "that's just what coherence means." So I went down a rabbit hole that night (an old LessWrong thread mostly, then whatever it linked to), and it turns out this exact worry is a whole thing in the literature -- theres apparently a name for the gap between a pragmatic reason and an epistemic one, a "converse" Dutch book result that's supposed to show coherent credences can never be exploited this way, a newer version of the argument that swaps out bets for accuracy/scoring rules to dodge the money issue entirely, and even someone arguing the original argument quietly smuggles in a separate principle about avoiding guaranteed losses that it never actually justifies. Apparently the whole thing traces back to some guy named Ramsey and an Italian probability theorist, de Finetti, from the 1920s-30s, which honestly I did not expect going in.

Anyway, I don't want a writeup that just lists what each side says, I can get that from skimming the Stanford Encyclopedia entry myself. I want an essay that actually works through those specific objections -- the betting-quotients-aren't-really-credences worry, the pragmatic vs epistemic gap, what the converse theorem does and doesn't actually license, how the accuracy-based version changes the picture, and the circularity charge -- and then commits to a real answer: does the Dutch book argument, on its own, actually establish that probabilistic coherence is required for rationality, or not? A qualified verdict is fine, something like 'it works, but only once you bolt on an independent dominance principle,' I just don't want it to dodge the question the way my friend did.
