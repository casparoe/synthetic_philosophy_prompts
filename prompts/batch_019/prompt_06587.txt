So this happened at work and I can't stop thinking about who actually had the better argument. I'm a backend engineer at a small crypto trading firm -- we write bots that trade against other firms' bots, and beacuse everything settles through smart contracts, every bot's decision logic is public on-chain before any trade goes through, so both sides can go read each other's code ahead of time. Two of my coworkers, Josh and Nadia, spent all of lunch arguing about how our next bot's negotiation logic should work, and I was too busy eating to referee it in real time, so I want someone neutral to weigh in after the fact.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Josh's opening take: since the other side can read our exact source before ever trading with us, bluffing or looking tough is pointless -- they'll just simulate our code and know our true walk-away point regardless. So he argued we should publish the simplest, most generous rule possible (basically 'always propose an even split, never grab extra') and trust that transparency plus niceness gets reciprocated. (2) Nadia said that's backwards: if you're transparently and unconditionally nice, the optimal bot on the other side is trivial -- it reads your code, confirms you'll always be generous no matter what happens, and then never reciprocates, since it has nothing to gain by being nice back. She said your bot's generosity needs to be conditional on what the other bot's code says it'll do to you specifically. (3) Josh's comeback was that this just pushes the problem down a level: for our bot to check whether their code will actually cooperate with us, it has to evaluate their code, and their code is presumably running the same check on ours -- so our bot's decision depends on a check of their bot, which depends on a check of our bot, and so on forever. He compared it to two people each refusing to say 'I love you' first. (4) Nadia agreed that's a real issue in the abstract but said it's not fatal -- she vaguely remembered reading that you can build pairs of programs that reliably end up cooperating with each other without actually looping forever, using some kind of trick where the program looks for a guarantee about the other program instead of literally simulating it, though she admitted she couldn't reconstruct exactly how the trick avoids the loop. (5) Josh's response was that even granting such a trick exists, it just relocates the problem: two bots that are each cautiously waiting on 'proof' of the other's good behavior could easily both fail to find any, even when both would honestly have been happy to cooperate, and end up stuck in the bad outcome anyway -- which he said seems just as bad as the naive version getting exploited. (6) Nadia's closing point was that sure, that's a real cost, but a conditional strategy that occasionally fails to cooperate with genuinely nice counterparts still seems strictly better than one that's guaranteed to get farmed by literally everyone, so she'd rather tune the conditions than go back to unconditional niceness -- though she admitted she wasnt sure there's a clean fix for the standoff risk.

Neither of them budged and we had to get back to work before it went anywhere. Could you go through this turn by turn and say who's got the better of it at each point (not just who wins overall), and flag anything you think both of them are missing entirely?
