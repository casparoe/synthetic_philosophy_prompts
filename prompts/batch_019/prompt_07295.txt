This term, my epistemology seminar spent several weeks on the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony: Hume's view that trust in what we're told has to be grounded in an independent inductive track record, against Reid's picture of testimony as a basic, non-inferential source on a par with perception, with Coady, Fricker, and Burge filling in the contemporary landscape. During discussion section, two classmates, Owen and Leah, got into an argument that never quite resolved and then carried on over email; I've reconstructed the exchange below from memory and the thread. I don't think either of them was simply right, but everyone else in the group seems to have quietly picked a side, so I'd like an outside, unaligned read.

1. Leah opened by pointing out that small children accept almost everything they are told — the names of things, facts about the world, even the meanings of the very words being used to address them — long before they could have assembled anything like the inductive track record Hume's picture requires. She took this to show that our default stance toward testimony can't be a conclusion from observed reliability, since the very capacity to assess reliability already presupposes a huge stock of testimonially acquired belief; trust, she said, has to be basic rather than derived.

2. Owen granted the developmental fact but said it shows only that trust is psychologically immediate, not that it is epistemically ungrounded. He brought up work on children's "epistemic vigilance," which finds that even toddlers modulate trust by a speaker's track record and confidence and discount claims that conflict with what they already believe — evidence, he said, that reliability-tracking is present early, just sub-personal and inaccessible to introspection, the way visual processing involves inference without feeling like inference.

3. Leah replied that this conceded the substantive point: if the real epistemic work is being done by an uninspectable, sub-personal tracking mechanism rather than by evidence the hearer actually possesses, that is no longer Hume's reductionism — it's a reliabilist story not so different from what anti-reductionists like Burge and Coady are happy to accept, wearing different clothes. Relabeling the mechanism "inference" doesn't make it an inference the hearer could ever produce, check, or be argued out of.

4. Owen pushed back that reductionism was never committed to hearers being able to produce their reasons on demand: Fricker's "local reductionism" only requires that case-specific positive reasons be available to a suitably attentive hearer in the situation — the speaker's apparent sincerity, the mundaneness of the claim, the absence of red flags — not that reliability be established once and for all for testimony in general. Running mostly on autopilot is compatible with that.

5. Leah's response was that "available in principle" just relocates the regress instead of closing it: judging a claim mundane, or a speaker sincere, already draws on an enormous stock of background belief, nearly all of which was itself acquired by testimony. Chase it back far enough, she said, and you either hit an infinite regress or you hit beliefs accepted with no then-available check at all, which was her point from the beginning.

6. Owen's last move was that regresses of justification aren't unique to testimony — perception and induction bottom out too — and that the right response in every such case is that the overall practice earns its keep through long-run coherence and success, not that each exercise of it must be justified from the ground up. He added that if a regress were enough to make something basic, almost anything could be made basic that way.
