I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and next month I'm interviewing a political theorist whose new book grounds "epistemic democracy" heavily in the Condorcet Jury Theorem — the result that majority votes converge on the correct answer as a group grows, provided each voter is independently more likely right than wrong. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the theorem. I want at least one question on whether the independence assumption can survive contact with deliberation, shared media diets, and party cues; one on why we should think average competence on complex, value-laden policy questions still clears 50 percent, given that the theorem was devised with simple factual judgments in mind; one on why the same logic that recommends trusting large, inclusive electorates doesn't just as readily recommend restricting the franchise to the most competent voters, if competence turns out to be unevenly distributed; and one asking her to distinguish the jury theorem's logic from Hong and Page's separate "diversity trumps ability" result, which her book sometimes invokes as though it were making the same point. For each, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close with a question about what finding would lead her to abandon the Condorcet-theorem argument specifically, rather than epistemic democracy as a whole.
