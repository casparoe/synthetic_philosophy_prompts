So I've been reading around in philosophy of religion lately because I'm plotting a novel with a god-character who wants to plan out the whole of history down to the last detail while still insisting her creatures are genuinely, robustly free -- and I fell down a rabbit hole on "Molinism," this old theory where God has "middle knowledge": logically prior to deciding to create anything, he already knows exactly what every possible free creature would do in every possible situation, so all he has to do is pick which set of circumstances to actualize. I brought this up at dinner with two old grad-school friends, Nadia (philosophy) and Owen (theology), and they ended up arguing about it for a solid twenty minutes while I just sat there eating my risotto.

It went roughly like this. (1) Nadia said the scheme is elegant: God isn't forcing anybody's hand, he's just choosing which whole bundle of already-true "if this person were placed in circumstance C, they'd freely do A" facts to make actual -- so providence and free will both come out intact. (2) Owen said it doesn't work, because those "would-do" facts can't be true yet: if the choice is genuinely free -- nothing about the person or the circumstance fixes which way it goes -- and the person doesn't even exist to settle it, then theres nothing that makes it true rather than its opposite. It's just stipulated because the theory needs it to be there. (3) Nadia said that's really a general complaint about wanting every truth to have something "making" it true, and plenty of truths -- necessary truths, truths about mere possibilities -- don't obviously have one either, so Owen can't assume that principle without an argument of his own. (4) Owen said fine, but necessary truths aren't contingent, so there's no "why this way and not that way" to explain -- these creaturely counterfactuals are supposed to be contingent, ungrounded, AND fixed before the person is even a twinkle in anyone's eye, and it's that whole combination that's the problem, not just "conditionals lack grounds" as a general gripe. (5) Nadia came back with: ordinary theism already accepts something structurally the same -- if you think God simply foreknows what a person will freely do tomorrow, you're already accepting that a contingent truth about an undetermined choice is settled "in advance" of anything making it so, so Owen's objection, if it actually works, proves too much. (6) Owen said no, that's different, because the actual future at least eventually happens, so eventually there's something for the belief to answer to -- whereas most of the circumstances God is supposed to have middle knowledge about will never be actual at all, so there's nothing that could ever ground them, not even in retrospect.

Nobody at that table budged and I've been turning it over ever since. Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and say who's got the better of it at each step (or whether its a genuine wash), rather than just declaring an overall winner? And once you've done that, I'd love to hear what you think both of them are missing -- some move that would actually advance the argument instead of just trading intuitions back and forth.
