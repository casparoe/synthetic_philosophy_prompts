A teammate joked "congrats, you've discovered quus" and sent me a summary of Kripke's rule-following paradox, and I've since actually gone back and reread the relevant chunks of *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* instead of doing my actual job, trying to figure out if this is a genuine instance of the paradox or just a cute surface-level analogy.

Here's where I'm stuck: Kripke's argument leans hard on there being a fact about what I *mean*, tied to intention and to norms about what I *ought* to answer (that's basically why the finitude objection and the normativity objection to dispositionalism both have teeth). A trained network doesn't obviously mean anything by its weights, and there's no one it's answering correctly-or-incorrectly *to* in that sense unless we smuggle that in from outside. So maybe what we're seeing is a real underdetermination-by-finite-data problem that just rhymes with Kripke's, rather then an actual instance of it — but I don't fully buy my own argument here.

Can you write a dialogue between two philosophers hashing this out — one arguing the ML case really is a live instance of Kripke's paradox (and has to say what, if anything, plays the role of "the community" from the skeptical solution once there's no ordinary language-user in the picture), the other arguing it's at best a distant cousin and explaining exactly where the disanalogy bites.
