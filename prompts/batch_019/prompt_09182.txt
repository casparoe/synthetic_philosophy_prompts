The question was worth 20 points:

"Robert Nozick's entitlement theory is often illustrated using the 'Wilt Chamberlain' example. (a) Explain what Nozick means by a patterned principle of distributive justice, and how his own entitlement theory differs from one. (b) Reconstruct the Wilt Chamberlain argument and explain precisely what it is meant to establish. (c) Does the argument, by itself, show that redistributive taxation is unjust? Defend your answer."

And here's the student's answer, more or less verbatim:

"Nozick's entitlement theory holds that a distribution of goods is just if it arose through the principles of justice in acquisition, transfer, and rectification: people are entitled to whatever they acquire justly from an unowned state, whatever is freely transferred to them by someone else entitled to it, and whatever is needed to correct past violations of the first two principles. This makes it a historical theory, since whether a distribution is just depends on how it came about rather than on what it looks like at a given moment.

Nozick contrasts this with end-state or patterned principles, which judge a distribution only by whether it matches some pattern, such as equality, or distribution according to need or merit. He argues that basically any such pattern will be upset by ordinary free exchange, which is exactly what the Wilt Chamberlain example is meant to show. Suppose D1 is a distribution satisfying whichever patterned principle you like. A million fans then each choose to pay Wilt Chamberlain 25 cents to watch him play basketball. Wilt ends up with a fortune, so the resulting distribution D2 no longer matches the pattern. But every transfer was free and D1 was stipulated to be just, so by the rectification principle D2 is just too. Because virtually any patterned distribution can be upset the same way, Nozick concludes that liberty upsets patterns: the only way to keep enforcing a pattern like Rawls's difference principle over time is to keep interfering with what people choose to do with their legitimately acquired holdings. That's exactly what redistributive taxation does, so the Wilt Chamberlain case amounts to a direct argument against it -- if repeatedly taxing Wilt to restore the pattern would be unjust, then ordinary redistributive taxation is unjust for the same reason.

Someone might object that D1 was never really just to begin with, since talents are partly a matter of luck. Nozick can reply that the argument only needs D1 to count as just by the lights of whatever patterned theory the objector favors, so it still works against that theory on its own terms."
