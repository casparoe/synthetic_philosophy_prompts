And here's the answer the student turned in:

"Gödel's theological views form a fairly continuous progression rather than three unrelated ideas. It starts with the 1951 Gibbs lecture, where he argues, via a disjunction growing out of the incompleteness theorems, that either the human mind's mathematical power outstrips that of any Turing machine, or there exist absolutely undecidable Diophantine propositions; Gödel himself leaned toward the first horn, which implies minds aren't simply mechanical, physical systems. He then formalizes this same intuition about the immateriality of mind into the ontological proof: using a modal logic of 'positive properties,' he shows a God-like being possessing all positive properties necessarily exists, a proof he kept to himself for years before finally showing Dana Scott, and which only saw print after Gödel died. Finally, in the 1961 letter to his mother, he takes that same underlying premise — that the mind/soul isn't reducible to a physical mechanism — and extends it informally to the question of personal survival: since the soul isn't a machine, there's no physical process, including the death of the brain, that could straightforwardly destroy it, so on Gödel's own rationalist principles it's more likely than not that it persists. So while only the God-proof is fully formalized, all three rest on the same anti-mechanist premise about mind, which is what makes this one unified rationalist project instead of three separate arguments."
