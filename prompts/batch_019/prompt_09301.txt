I've spent the last few weeks slowly working through Simone Weil's short essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," which I picked up after a friend brought it up during an argument about why neither of us can stand voting a straight party ticket. I never studied philosophy or political theory formally, so I've been reading it on my own, slowly, with a dictionary and a notebook close by. What struck me is that Weil isn't just complaining that parties behave badly in practice; she tries to argue that party organization is bad in itself, by pointing to three features she thinks any political party has just in virtue of being one: it exists to whip up collective passion, it exists to put organized pressure on the minds of its own members so they fall in line, and its real first goal, whatever it officially claims, is simply to keep growing without limit. From those three points alone she concludes that every party is a kind of totalitarianism in miniature, and she wants to do away with parties altogether, leaving only individuals standing for election on their own, answerable to no organization.

I keep going back and forth on whether this argument really isolates something specific to political parties, or whether, taken seriously, it would apply just as well to labor unions, churches, or any advocacy group that wants new members and wants the ones it already has to stay loyal. It also reminds me of something I half-remember from reading the Federalist Papers on my own years ago: Madison's argument that the underlying causes of faction cannot be removed without removing liberty itself, so the most a political system can do is control faction's effects through institutional design rather than legislate factions out of existence — which sounds like the opposite lesson from the one Weil wants to draw, since she thinks at least one cause, the party machine, really can be removed. I'd like an essay that actually engages with this tension rather than just summarizing Weil's essay: lay out the strongest version of her three-part argument and the totalitarianism-in-miniature conclusion she draws from it, then the strongest objections — including whether her diagnosis overgeneralizes to non-party organizations, and whether her proposed cure survives the Madisonian worry or simply ignores it. Please end with an actual verdict, not a balanced list of considerations: does the case for abolishing political parties hold up, survive only in some restricted form, or fail outright?
