I'm about a third of the way into a novel set on a federal appellate bench, and I've hit a scene I want to get right rather than fudge. While reading around for background — mainly Lewis Kornhauser and Lawrence Sager's 1986 article "Unpacking the Court," and then Christian List and Philip Pettit's later, more general treatment of the same structure under the name "discursive dilemma" — I ran into the doctrinal paradox: a panel of judges can vote issue-by-issue on the separate legal questions a case turns on and reach one disposition, or vote directly on the bottom-line outcome and reach the opposite disposition, with both procedures perfectly majoritarian and neither one obviously mistaken. I hadn't quite registered before that this isn't just a social-choice-theory curiosity; it's a live methodological fork that real multi-member courts step over constantly, usually without noticing, and it struck me as exactly the kind of load-bearing, non-decorative philosophical machinery I want for this chapter, since here the "merely procedural" choice will decide whether a man's conviction stands or falls.

Here is the scene I've built around it. Three judges on the (fictional) Fourteenth Circuit have just conferenced on the appeal of Marcus Webb, convicted of armed robbery substantially on the strength of a confession taken after he had invoked his right to counsel — an Edwards v. Arizona problem. Reversal requires two independent findings: that admitting the confession was constitutional error, and that the error was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Judge Aviva Korman finds both — error, and harm — and votes to reverse. Judge Daniel Petrosky finds error but finds it harmless, and votes to affirm. Judge Constance Ellis, who isn't in the room for this scene, finds no constitutional error at all, though she notes, almost in passing, that had there been error she would not have called it harmless; she too votes to affirm. Tally the panel issue-by-issue and a majority finds error (2–1) and a majority finds harm (2–1), which mechanically compels reversal — even though Korman is the only one of the three who actually thinks the conviction should fall. Tally the panel on the disposition itself and it affirms, 2–1.

Could you write the scene as a dialogue between Korman and Petrosky, meeting in her chambers that evening before the opinion gets assigned, each trying to talk the other out of their preferred procedure? Korman should argue for deciding issue-by-issue — that this is what the governing two-part standard actually asks of them, and that it's the only way to give Webb a reasoned opinion rather than a bare, unexplained headcount; Petrosky should argue for voting the disposition directly, on the ground that a court's judgment is supposed to state what a majority of its own judges actually believe should happen to the defendant in front of them, not a position that, on this panel, only one judge holds. I want both of them equally sharp, each genuinely landing points the other has to answer rather than deflect, and I'd like the dialogue to make them confront the slightly awkward fact that the procedure each favors also happens to hand them their preferred outcome — neither argument should be allowed to collapse quietly into disguised self-interest. It needn't end in agreement (I'd honestly prefer it didn't), but I don't want Webb's actual liberty to evaporate into pure abstraction along the way. Something in the range of 1,000 to 1,500 words would fit the chapter well.
