So I've been working through Turing's 1950 Mind paper (the "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" one everyone cites for the imitation game) since I retired from the lab a couple years back and started filling in all the philosophy I never got to as a chemist. Near the end he runs through nine objections to his own view, and the last one is literally titled "The Argument from Extrasensory Perception" - I figured it'd be a throwaway joke but he's dead serious, saying the evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming" and that accepting it "does not seem a very big step to believe in ghosts and bogies." That part's the famous "oops, Turing believed in ESP" bit everyone brings up, fine.

What I can't work out, and haven't seen anyone actually walk through, is the bit right before his proposed fix. He considers a machine that answers using a random number generator, and worries that generator "will be subject to the psychokinetic powers of the interrogator" - which seems like a different kind of worry than the telepathy/clairvoyance case, where the threat is the human contestant picking up hidden info paranormally. Here its the interrogator reaching out and physically messing with the machine's hardware instead. Then his actual fix is just to put the competitors in a "telepathy-proof room," on the model of blocking someone with their ear pressed to the wall. Does that kind of barrier do anything at all against a psychokinetic influence, or does it only stop the information-leakage version of the problem? Feels like he raises the psychokinesis worry and then just quietly walks past it - is that a real gap, or am I missing a step in how he's thinking about it?
