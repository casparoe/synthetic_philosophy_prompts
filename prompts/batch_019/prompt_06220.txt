So I was at a house party a couple weeks ago that was full of AI-safety and effective-altruism people (I cover science and ideas for a living, so I end up around that crowd more than you'd expect), and someone used the phrase "Pascal's mugging" like it was just everyday vocabulary. I went and looked it up afterward — it's a thought experiment, coined I think by Eliezer Yudkowsky, where some stranger walks up to you and says, basically, "give me five dollars or I'll use my powers from outside the simulation to torture 3^^^3 people" (some absurd, borderline-incomprehensible number, using that up-arrow notation for figures way past anything a normal exponent could express). The unsettling part isn't the scenario itself, it's the math: if you're doing honest expected-value reasoning and you don't put some hard cap on how large a claimed stake can be, you're supposed to pay the guy no matter how microscopically unlikely his story is, because you can always just gin up a bigger number of victims to outweigh a smaller probability.

What's been stuck in my head since that party is that a couple of the AI-safety people who thought the mugging example was funny make a living on structurally similar arguments — tiny probability of affecting an astronomical number of future people, therefore justified in spending huge resources now — and when I pushed on it a little, nobody had a clean answer for why one is an obvious con and the other is a respectable research agenda. Apparently there's things like bounded utility functions, or a "leverage penalty" that docks your probability estimate in proportion to how many people you're claiming to be able to affect, but from what I can tell even the people who use these fixes admit they dont fully dissolve the discomfort.

Could you write me a short story — contemporary realistic fiction is fine, it doesn't need to be sci-fi unless you want the mugger's actual pitch to be sci-fi-flavored — where a character who has built part of their identity or career around taking tiny-probability/huge-stakes reasoning seriously gets cornered by a genuine, sincere Pascal's mugging (stranger on a train, a weird email, whatever setting you like) and has to decide what to do about it in real time. I don't want a version where the character delivers a tidy philosophical rebuttal and walks away clean — I'd much rather the story sit in how thin the line actually feels between the reasoning they live by and the reasoning that's mugging them, maybe even having them reach for one of the "real" fixes and notice it doesn't quite settle anything. Somewhere around 800-1500 words is probably the right ballpark, but don't sweat hitting an exact count.
