So I was at the bar after an AI-safety meetup a couple weeks ago and ended up half-refereeing an argument between two guys from the reading group who'd both just worked through the old MIRI "tiling agents" paper on the Löbian obstacle. Trying to reconstruct it fairly, it went something like:

Ravi: "This is a genuine ceiling, not a technicality. Löb's theorem says a system can't derive a general schema like 'whatever I (or my successor) prove is true' for an arbitrary statement P — proving that schema for P turns out to require proving P outright. So there's no formal shortcut to trusting a future or successor version of yourself; you can't get the guarantee any cheaper than just solving the object-level problem first."

Sam: "That's Gödel's second theorem wearing a trenchcoat, and it doesn't say what you think it does. A system can be completely sound — everything it ever proves is true — while being structurally unable to prove that fact about itself. That's a limit on self-verification, not on trustworthiness. The successor can still, in fact, be reliable; the obstacle only stops you from certifying it from the inside."

Ravi: "Sure, but 'reliable in fact, unprovably so' isn't much comfort if you're the one deciding whether to hand off control before you find out. The guarantee needs to exist in advance, for the current agent, from its own reasoning — not as some unaccessed fact about the successor. That's specifically the kind of guarantee the theorem rules out."

Sam: "Except the same paper gives you a way to get an advance guarantee anyway: have each successor reason in a strictly weaker proof system than its predecessor, so you're proving soundness of a different, weaker system rather than of yourself. Löb's theorem only bites on self-certification. So 'obstacle' oversells it — it's a constraint on how the handoff has to be shaped, not a dead end."
