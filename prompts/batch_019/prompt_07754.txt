I've been falling down a bit of a rabbit hole researching a piece on the history of parapsychology, and I keep running into Henry Sidgwick's name. He's usually taught to undergrads as basically the last great systematic utilitiarian before Moore came along in 1903 and reoriented the whole field -- author of The Methods of Ethics, famously rigorous and careful, exactly the kind of philosopher you'd expect to have zero patience for table-rapping and ghost stories. Except it turns out he co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, was its first president, and sunk a big chunk of his later career into investigating mediums and telepathy, and by his own admission never landed on solid proof of any of it.

My editors take, which I'm supposed to build the piece around, is that this was basically Sidgwick's version of Newton's alchemy -- i.e. a brilliant, hyper-rational guy with one weird, essentially irrational hobby that we can politely footnote when we teach his "real" philosophy. Before I write that, I wanted to sanity check it with you: is that actually a fair way to tell the story, or is there a more interesting throughline connecting all the ghost-hunting to the ethics that we'd be flattening if we just filed it under "quirky footnote"?
