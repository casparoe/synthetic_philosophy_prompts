I've spent the past several months reporting on how large language models are fine-tuned using human feedback, and a conversation at a recent workshop on AI alignment left me stuck on a question I haven't been able to shake. As I understand the basic setup, human annotators compare pairs of candidate responses and indicate which they prefer; those comparisons are used to train a "reward model" that assigns a score to any given response; and the underlying language model is then optimized to produce whatever scores highest under that reward model. Researchers routinely talk about wanting the model to be "genuinely" helpful and honest, not merely good at scoring well on a flawed proxy — hence all the talk of "reward hacking" and "specification gaming," where a model finds some clever way to rack up reward without doing what anyone actually wanted.

When I pushed two researchers on what "genuinely helpful" could mean, apart from whatever the reward model rewards, they gave answers that pulled in opposite directions. One told me there's no such independent standard: helpfulness is, by definition, just whatever a carefully trained reward model built from good human preference data ends up rewarding, and asking for more than that is a category error. The other insisted this couldn't be right, since then "reward hacking" would be a contradiction in terms — if the reward model's judgment simply constitutes what's helpful, nothing that scores well could ever count as gaming that standard — so there had to be some further fact about what's actually helpful that both the human raters and the reward model are fallibly trying, and sometimes failing, to track.

Neither researcher thought they had merely restated the other's position, and I couldn't settle the disagreement on the spot, though I had the nagging feeling I'd met this argument before, in a much older costume, somewhere far from machine learning. Could you help me get a clearer sense of what's really at stake between these two positions, say whether one of them is simply confused, and tell me whether there's a defensible third view that isn't just a mushy compromise between "the standard is whatever the raters say" and "the raters are tracking a standard that floats free of them entirely"?
