I'm a retired field biologist (spent most of my career studying cooperative hunting in African wild dogs, of all things), and now that I've got time on my hands I've been working through philosophy of action, partly because a friend from my old book club wouldn't stop going on about "collective intentionality" and I wanted to know what the fuss was about. I've gotten pretty absorbed in the disagreement between Michael Bratman's planning theory of shared agency and Margaret Gilbert's "plural subject" account. As I understand it, Bratman thinks shared intention just is a structure of individual intentions (each person intending "that we J"), with meshing sub-plans, mutual responsiveness, and common knowledge of all that -- nothing extra required. Gilbert thinks that's not enough: once two people jointly commit to something, they form a plural subject together, and that generates real obligations and entitlements between them that don't reduce to anybody's private mental states.

Anyways, here's a case I dreamed up on an actual walk (occupational hazard) that I think brings the disagreement into focus. Lena and Tom, old friends, set off up a mountain trail with nothing more explicit exchanged than "shall we?" / "yeah, let's." A couple hours in, the weather turns nasty. Lena privately decides the sensible thing is to turn back, and just assumes Tom would agree if she asked -- so instead of asking, she quietly starts heading down, expecting he'll notice and follow. He doesn't notice for a good fifteen minutes, by which point he's climbed on alone, cold and annoyed, with no idea where she's gone. Did the two of them have a shared intention to reach the summit, and if so, does Lena's silent about-face count as breaking it? Does she owe Tom something -- an apology, an explanation -- beyond ordinary politeness, and if so, why exactly?

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Bratmanian and a committed Gilbertian hashing this case out? I don't want either one to be a pushover for the other -- I want them each genuinely pressing on the weakest spot of the others theory (and, if they're honest, feeling the pressure on their own weak spots too), with real back-and-forth rather than two speeches delivered past each other. What I'm most curious about is whether they end up agreeing about what actually happened on the mountain and merely disagreeing about how to describe or explain it philosophically, or whether the theoretical disagreement cashes out in genuinely different verdicts about whether Lena wronged Tom.
