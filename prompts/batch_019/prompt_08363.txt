Two of us in my epistemology reading group got stuck on Vogel's gas-gauge case last week and never resolved it, so I'd like an outside ruling.

(1) Owen argued the case is fatal to naive process reliabilism. If a driver's fuel gauge is in fact reliable, reliabilism lets each of her gauge-based beliefs about the tank count as knowledge. Repeatedly noting that the gauge's reading matches what she believes the tank contains — a belief she holds only because of that reading — and running an induction over many such matches, she can then conclude "the gauge is reliable": knowledge of the source's reliability acquired without ever checking the tank independently. He called this illegitimate bootstrapping.

(2) Mei replied that this isn't special to gauges or to reliabilism: Cohen showed that ordinary perception has the identical structure. Repeatedly noting that something looks red and is red, then inferring that one's color vision is reliable, is the same bootstrap — yet condemning that collapses into a much broader skepticism, since no basic faculty can vouch for its own reliability except by presupposing that it, or some other faculty, is reliable.

(3) Owen answered that perception and instruments aren't on a par: we must rely on our basic faculties just to gather any evidence at all, so demanding an independent check on them before trusting them is either impossible or viciously regressive. A fuel gauge has no such standing — an independent check (watching the tank run dry) is available, so skipping one is a real, avoidable shortcoming.

(4) Mei pointed out that "a check was available and skipped" is not something bare process reliabilism was ever built to track; it counts a belief as knowledge purely in virtue of how the belief was produced. Adding sensitivity to skipped checks turns Owen's reliabilism into a different, defeater-laden theory — and he still owes a defeater condition that rules out the gauge case without also ruling out the perceptual one, since in both, the only "check" ever run was internal to the very faculty being vindicated.

Could you assess moves (1) through (4) on their own terms rather than just declaring an overall winner, say where each succeeds or fails against what it's actually responding to, flag anything both are missing, and close with a one-sentence verdict on who has the better of the exchange?
