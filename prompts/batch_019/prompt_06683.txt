I have just taken on teaching-assistant duties for a university seminar on the philosophical foundations of artificial intelligence, and this week that means grading exam responses to a question on a part of Turing's 1950 paper I had never previously had much reason to think about closely: his treatment of extrasensory perception as a challenge to the imitation game. Before I finalize marks, I would like an independent, point-by-point assessment of one student's response.

Question: In Section 6 of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Turing discusses the "argument from extrasensory perception" among the contrary views he considers. Explain (a) why Turing thinks the possibility of ESP poses a genuine problem for the imitation game, (b) what remedy he proposes, and (c) whether, in your view, that remedy actually resolves the difficulty.

Student's answer: Turing raises extrasensory perception as one of several objections he takes up in Section 6, and unlike most of the others on his list, he does not simply dismiss it out of hand: he concedes that the experimental evidence collected for telepathy is, in his own words, statistically overwhelming, and he treats the objection as one of the two he finds hardest to dispose of, alongside the difficulty posed by the informality of human behaviour. The problem for the imitation game, on his view, is that if the machine turned out to possess telepathic powers, it could use them to pick up facts about the interrogator that no amount of ordinary verbal reasoning could supply, so that a lucky run of such feats would make the machine look brilliantly intelligent for reasons that have nothing to do with its capacity to think. Turing's proposed fix is to run the game inside what he calls a "telepathy-proof room," which insulates the interrogator from any paranormal influence coming from the machine, leaving the teleprinter exchange as the sole channel of communication the test was meant to rely on. Since this one design change disposes of the last and most serious objection on his list, Turing takes the imitation game, once so modified, to be a fully adequate and complete criterion for machine intelligence, needing no further qualification.
