I've been down a rabbit hole reading about Kepler for a history of science seminar, and the thing that's really messing with my head is his astrology. Everyone learns about the elliptical orbits and the three laws, but it turns out he spent basically his whole career as a working astrologer too -- churning out yearly prognostica as part of his actual job as imperial mathematician, doing individual horoscopes for real patrons (I think he even did one for Wallenstein), and writing a whole treatise, Tertius Interveniens, where he plays referee in a fight between his friend/patron Herwart, who liked traditional astrology, and some physician named Feselius (sorry if I'm butchering that) who'd written a piece trashing the whole practice as superstition.

What's weird is Kepler's "middle" position: he agrees the zodiac signs, the houses, and most of what ordinary working astrologers did for a living were basically made-up nonsense with no physical basis, but he still insisted the geometric "aspects" planets make with each other are physically real and meaningful -- tied to the same harmonic/geometric ideas he uses for planetary orbits and music in Harmonices Mundi -- something about the earth (or souls generally, i think he calls it the anima telluris?) having an instinctive geometric sense that resonates with certain angles. I genuinely can't tell if that's a serious extension of his Pythagorean physics that he actually believed, or just clever cover so he could keep collecting astrology money and stay useful to patrons while still getting to feel superior to the "vulgar" astrologers he clearly had contempt for. Can you write me an essay that lays out the strongest case for each reading -- sincere-and-integrated vs. basically-a-pragmatic-hedge -- weighs them against each other using what we actually know about his life and writings, and then actually commits to a verdict instead of just landing on "honestly, probably a mix of both"? If a mix really is the honest answer, at least tell me which way it leans and why.
