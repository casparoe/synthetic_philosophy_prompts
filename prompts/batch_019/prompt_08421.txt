I'm a bioethics PhD student and sit as the trainee member on my psychiatric hospital's clinical ethics committee. Two years ago, while stable and judged fully competent, a patient with bipolar I disorder signed what our policy calls a Ulysses contract: a psychiatric advance directive listing precise behavioral triggers for an oncoming manic episode and stating that she consents in advance to involuntary hospitalization and medication once those triggers occur, explicitly waiving any right to refuse treatment at that time. She now meets the triggers and is refusing all treatment -- composed, articulate, and adamant that she is simply doing well, which is exactly the reaction she predicted and instructed us to disregard. Our policy says clinicians should "honor" such contracts but must also "respect the patient's contemporaneous wishes," without saying which one controls when the two conflict. Some committee members describe the contract as a constitutional entrenchment clause she wrote for her own future mind; others think that analogy is precisely what fails here. Please draft the short opinion I'll circulate before Friday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should seriously engage the strongest reason for treating her present refusal as authoritative rather than simply invoking the contract.
