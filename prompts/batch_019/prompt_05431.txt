I've followed the Integrated Information Theory (IIT) debates since Scott Aaronson's old "unconscious expander" post, which argues that a sparse but highly-connected network of simple logic gates -- an expander graph that does nothing more mentally sophisticated than run a basic error-correcting code -- can be engineered to have arbitrarily high Φ, IIT's measure of consciousness, apparently making it more conscious than any human brain. A very different, institutional version of that skepticism arrived in 2023, when over a hundred consciousness researchers signed an open letter arguing that IIT should be publicly labeled pseudoscience, citing its untestability and reacting partly to the wave of publicity a Templeton-funded adversarial collaboration against Global Neuronal Workspace Theory had just generated for it. What complicates the pseudoscience charge is that the collaboration's own results were genuinely mixed for both theories -- each had some central predictions borne out and others not -- which is an odd thing for a supposedly untestable theory to produce. Write an essay weighing the Aaronson-style formal objection against this sociological and empirical story, and reach a real verdict rather than "reasonable people disagree": is pseudoscience the right label for IIT, a defensible-but-overstated one, or a category error that conflates a flawed measure of consciousness with an untestable theory? Say explicitly what your verdict implies for how journals and funders should treat the theory now.
