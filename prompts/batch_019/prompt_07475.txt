My AP Physics teacher, Mrs. Alvarez, is retiring after thirty-one years. Every fall, before we started the unit on Newton, she had us read a few pages of Émilie du Châtelet's "Discourse on Happiness," because she said the woman who translated the Principia and reworked its proofs in the new calculus deserved to be known for more than that one achievement. She has asked whoever speaks at her retirement lunch to skip the usual lines about how she will "live on through her students" or to praise her for her "selflessness" — she says du Châtelet's essay makes clear why both would ring false. I am giving the toast, to a room that is half colleagues and family who have never read the essay and half former students who will notice if I get its argument wrong. Drawing on what du Châtelet actually says happiness requires — passions, illusions, study, and independence from other people's opinions — please write a short toast that is genuinely warm about her without leaning on the sentiments she has ruled out.
