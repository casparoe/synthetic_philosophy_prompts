I've spent the last several months reporting on the rise of prediction markets—real-money platforms like Polymarket and Kalshi, play-money ones like Manifold, and the rest of that ecosystem—and on the older academic case for them associated with economists like Robin Hanson, whose proposal for "futarchy" rests on the idea that letting people bet on outcomes is an unusually good way of dragging dispersed private information out of individual heads and into a single public number. In the course of background reading, I kept running into a result that sits awkwardly next to that optimism: the "no-trade theorems" that grew out of Aumann's work on common knowledge and were later formalized by Milgrom and Stokey. As I understand the argument, if two risk-averse, expected-utility-maximizing traders share a common prior, and it is common knowledge between them that a trade is on offer, then the sheer fact that one side is willing to trade should lead the other to update in a way that removes any purely information-based reason to accept it—so trade based purely on one side knowing something the other doesn't shouldn't occur at all between agents who are rational in just this sense. And yet that is apparently exactly what happens, in enormous volume, on every market I've been covering.

Economists I've put this to tend to wave the tension away by noting how unrealistic the theorem's assumptions are: real traders don't share a common prior, lack common knowledge of one another's rationality, trade for hedging or liquidity reasons that have nothing to do with private information, or are simply overconfident about what they know. Fair enough, as far as it goes. But it strikes me that none of this obviously rescues an inference I run into constantly in financial and political journalism, including my own: that heavy trading volume and deep liquidity on a prediction market are themselves evidence that the market is doing serious epistemic work—successfully extracting dispersed private information and pressing it into the price. If the no-trade result is on the right track, that inference might have things backwards.

So here is the position I'd like you to argue for, as carefully as you can: that the volume of speculative trading on a market is not, by itself, evidence that the market is aggregating dispersed private information, and that treating heavy trading—as opposed to, say, features of the price-setting mechanism itself—as proof of a market's epistemic credentials rests on a misreading of what the no-trade literature actually shows. Please engage seriously with the standard rejoinders rather than just setting them aside: heterogeneous priors, bounded rationality, hedging demand, overconfidence, and the Grossman–Stiglitz point that noise trading is precisely what lets informed trading survive and get compensated at all. Any defense of my claim has to survive contact with whichever of those stories is actually doing the most work in explaining real-world trading volumes.
