A friend of mine who trained in behavioral economics before drifting into philosophy of action and I spent most of a long train journey last week arguing about weakness of will. Her position, held with a confidence I found hard to unsettle, was that the traditional puzzle — how someone can judge that one course of action is best and yet perform another — simply dissolves once a hyperbolic discount function is built into the agent's preferences: what looks like acting against one's better judgment is, on her view, nothing more than a preference reversal that was there all along, so there is no further philosophical explanandum left over. I tried the Davidsonian line that this conflates a change in one's all-things-considered judgment with acting against a judgment one still holds at the moment of action, but she had a cleaner discounting-based redescription ready for every example I offered, and I am not sure I landed a single point before we reached our stop.

As it happens, I co-host a small philosophy podcast, and next month's guest defends more or less my friend's position in print: that weakness of will just is the behavior of a hyperbolic discounter, and that "acting against one's better judgment" either reduces to this or picks out nothing real. I would like to do better in the interview than I managed on the train. Could you draft eight to ten questions for her that go beyond inviting a restatement of the reductionist thesis? I want at least one question on each of the following: whether her view can distinguish weakness of will from a mere shift in what the agent judges best, given that the classical puzzle requires the agent to still hold the judgment she acts against; Holton's resolution-based alternative, pressed specifically on whether a perfectly time-consistent, exponential discounter could still count as weak-willed on his picture, and what that possibility would do to her reductionism if so; Ainslie's picoeconomic model of bargaining and bundling among a series of temporally situated selves, and whether the agency that model posits is compatible with calling weakness of will "nothing more" than a discount curve, or smuggles back in exactly the kind of agency her reductionism was meant to dissolve; and precommitment devices in the Ulysses-and-the-sirens vein, where I'd like the question to bear on whether needing to bind one's future self counts as an exercise of will, or as evidence that will was never really the operative notion to begin with. For each of these, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that she cannot simply retreat to the same redescriptive move twice.
