My friend Priya and I went to one of those free-vacation timeshare presentations this weekend (the "sit through 90 minutes and get a free night at the resort" kind of thing), and we ended up arguing about it for like an hour on the drive home. Before we even walked in I'd read that a surprisingly large chunk of people who go to these things end up buying, way more than seems normal, because the sales reps are trained to wear you down for hours with countdown timers, "today only" bonuses, free gifts, and constant one-on-one pressure. Priya's argument was basically: you already know that by hour three you'll probably feel convinced this is a good deal, so isn't the rational move just to believe its a good deal right now, before you've even sat down? Otherwise, she said, you're basically claiming that your own future self, who'll have heard way more of the pitch then you have now, is going to be wrong - and isn't it kind of arrogant to think your less-informed self right now knows better?

I told her that seemed backwards, because whatever I end up believing by hour three will have nothing to do with the timeshare actually being good value and everything to do with being tired and manipulated, but she said that's exactly what everyone who ends up changing their mind always says in hindsight, and that I can't just trust my future judgment when it's convenient and dismiss it when it's not. I didn't have a good comeback in the car and it's been bugging me since. Is Priya actually onto something with this "you should just adopt the belief you already know you'll predictably end up with" logic, or is there a real, principled difference between a case like this and cases where deferring to your future self clearly does make sense, like trusting your future self's opinion after they've had time to read more about something or think it through carefully? I'd love to actually understand where the line is instead of just going with my gut.
