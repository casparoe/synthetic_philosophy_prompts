I serve on my state's parole board. Before every hearing we're now handed a computer-generated score estimating the percent chance that the person before us will be arrested again within three years, produced by a model trained on thousands of past cases that shared certain measurable traits with him. This week an inmate's attorney is raising an objection unrelated to bias against any group -- her point is narrower than that. She will argue that the number is meaningless for her client specifically, because he belongs to several different groups we could just as easily have scored him against -- his age bracket, his offense type, everyone who completed his vocational program -- and each yields a different percentage, with nothing in the statistics telling us which one to trust. This has bothered me since the tool arrived, and I doubt finer-grained data would fix it, since narrowing the group enough just leaves him standing alone. Our chair has asked each of us to submit a short written opinion, to be read into the record before Thursday's vote, on how much weight the score deserves and why. Please draft mine: commit to a definite recommendation, give reasons a skeptical colleague could push back on, and take seriously that the score, for all its faults, still seems to outpredict a parole board's own unaided judgment.
