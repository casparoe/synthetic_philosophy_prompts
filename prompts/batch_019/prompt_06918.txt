I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology and decision theory, and this week's meeting, which was meant to be a quick warm-up on Kelly's betting criterion before we moved on to bargaining theory, ran forty minutes long because two of my students got stuck in a disagreement that the rest of the class couldn't settle either. The toy example on the handout, loosely in the spirit of the classic horse-racing set-ups used to motivate the Kelly criterion, was a repeated wager: on each round you may stake any fraction of your current bankroll on a coin that comes up heads with probability 0.6; heads doubles whatever you staked, tails loses it, the rounds are independent, and you can keep playing for as many rounds as you like. The handout's answer key simply asserted that the growth-optimal stake is 20% of the current bankroll every round (the "Kelly fraction," f* = p − q for an even-money bet), but working out why turned into the argument that ate the rest of the session.

(1) Renata opened by arguing that the truly rational policy is to stake your entire bankroll every round, since the expected multiplier per round is 0.6 × 2 + 0.4 × 0 = 1.2, and maximizing expected value is simply what rationality requires when facing repeated independent gambles: over enough rounds, the law of large numbers guarantees you come out ahead. (2) Theo objected that staking everything guarantees eventual ruin: the instant you lose one round you're at zero for good, and since you'll almost surely lose at least one round eventually if you keep playing, your wealth goes to zero with probability 1 no matter how favorable each individual round is; the cheerful expected-value figure, he said, is being carried entirely by a vanishingly unlikely path in which you never lose, which tells you nothing about what happens to any actual bettor. (3) Renata conceded the ruin point but said this only shows you should stake less than everything, not that there's a uniquely correct fraction: once you're below full commitment, how much to stake is simply a matter of risk preference, and the specific 20% figure — which Theo had been defending — is nothing more than what a bettor with logarithmic utility happens to prefer; a less risk-averse bettor is free to stake 30% or 50%, and there's no further fact that makes 20% in particular the rational choice. (4) Theo replied that this misses what's distinctive about the Kelly fraction: it isn't just one utility function's preference among many, because a bettor who stakes 20% every round will, with probability approaching 1 as the number of rounds grows, end up wealthier than a bettor who persistently stakes any other fixed fraction, whatever that other bettor's utility function happens to be. That, he said, is a dominance result, not a preference, and it's exactly the sense in which maximizing the bet's long-run geometric growth rate is objectively privileged over maximizing its ordinary (arithmetic) expected value or appealing to an arbitrary risk-tolerance parameter.
