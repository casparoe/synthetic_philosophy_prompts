Bell's theorem is typically summarized as showing that no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce the correlations predicted by quantum mechanics, often glossed as "quantum mechanics is nonlocal." But the locality assumption used in deriving the Bell/CHSH inequalities is actually a conjunction of two logically separate conditions -- what Jarrett and Shimony called parameter independence (an outcome on one side cannot depend on which measurement the distant experimenter chose to perform) and outcome independence (an outcome on one side cannot depend on the distant outcome, given both measurement settings and the hidden state). Please explain, at an intuitive rather than formal level, what each condition really amounts to, why deriving the inequality requires both together, and why it is philosophically significant that quantum correlations apparently violate only outcome independence while respecting parameter independence -- that is, why this is generally taken to make the theorem's challenge to relativistic locality subtler than a simple "faster-than-light influence" story.
