I've belonged to a small, Bayesian-inclined reading group for the better part of a decade now — the kind of group that started as a habit of reading LessWrong and Overcoming Bias posts aloud to each other and slowly turned into a real friendship — and three weeks ago we lost its founding member, an actuary in his late forties, to a fast-moving cancer. He was the sort of person who could explain Ramsey's own representation theorem for subjective probability at a dinner party and make it sound like the most obvious thing in the world, and Frank Ramsey was, without exaggeration, his intellectual hero: not only for the probability and decision theory, but for a short, strange, and much less-discussed piece Ramsey wrote not long before his own death at twenty-six — later collected, if I remember rightly, under the dry title "Epistemology" — in which he explains, quite deliberately, why he refuses to feel humbled or diminished by the size and age of the universe.

Ramsey's argument, as my friend liked to summarize it over drinks, is not the usual "don't worry, none of it matters anyway" move. It is closer to a claim about category: importance was never the kind of property that scales with size or duration in the first place, so weighing a friendship, a joke, or an afternoon's good argument against the lifespan of the stars is a confusion about what kind of thing value is, not a hard truth we are obliged to make peace with. He kept a photocopy of the piece taped inside his desk drawer for as long as I knew him, and once he was diagnosed he became almost comically insistent, in the dry humor he allowed himself, that no one at his funeral was to reach for cosmic consolation on his behalf.

I have been asked to give the eulogy at his memorial next week, and the room will split in an uncomfortable way: half of it is our reading group and his colleagues from the actuarial world, who will know exactly what I mean if I say "Ramsey's perspective" and will notice at once if I get the philosophy sloppy or sentimental; the other half is his sisters and his oldest friends from before any of us knew him, who have never heard of Frank Ramsey and understandably want to hear something that sounds like comfort. The trouble is that his own view rules out most of the easy moves: I cannot tell them he is "watching over us" or "part of something bigger now," since that is exactly the sort of cosmic-scale consolation he thought was a category error rather than a comfort, and I cannot frame his life as a tragedy cut short relative to everything he might still have done, since that measures him against the very scale his essay spent its few pages rejecting. Please write the eulogy: six or seven minutes, working entirely within what he actually believed, using his own argument as the source of the comfort rather than something I have to talk around, so that it reads as precise to the people who knew exactly what he thought and as simply true and consoling to the people who didn't.
