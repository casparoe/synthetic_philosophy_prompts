I'm drafting a novel set in imperial China in which a county magistrate, trained in the classics, must rule on a case that turns on the old principle that close kin should not be compelled to testify against one another. In preparing the scene I've been rereading the source of that principle, this exchange from the Analects in James Legge's nineteenth-century translation:

The Duke of She informed Confucius, saying, "Among us here there are those who may be styled upright in their conduct. If their father have stolen a sheep, they will bear witness to the fact." Confucius said, "Among us, in our part of the country, those who are upright are different from this. The father conceals the misconduct of the son, and the son conceals the misconduct of the father. Uprightness is to be found in this."

What I can't settle for myself is why Confucius locates uprightness (直, zhi) in the concealment itself, rather than treating the concealment as an understandable but regrettable falling-short of it. Is he redefining the virtue around the relationship, so that, as certain "role ethics" readings of Confucius argue, there is simply no role-independent standard of honesty for the son to fail to meet here? Or does a more universal notion of honesty survive intact, with Confucius instead making a separate claim about which loyalty a workable social order has to protect first? I'd like to see the strongest version of each reading laid out, since it will determine how self-aware, and how troubled, my magistrate is allowed to be about his own ruling.
