I'm auditing a graduate seminar on games with imperfect recall this term, largely because it bears on some independent reading I've been doing about how autonomous agents ought to reason about self-locating uncertainty -- the sort of situation where a system might be duplicated, or have its memory partially wiped, and has to act without knowing which instance of itself it currently is. Last week the seminar spent an entire session on Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver, and I've been turning it over ever since.

The setup itself is simple enough: a driver on a highway passes two intersections that look identical from inside the car. Exiting at the first is worth 0, continuing past it and exiting at the second is worth 4, and continuing past both is worth 1. Because the driver can't tell the two intersections apart, she has to commit to the same probability of continuing at whichever intersection she happens to be at. I can follow the calculus showing that continuing with probability 2/3 maximizes her expected payoff from the standpoint of someone planning the whole trip before setting off. What I can't get an intuitive grip on is the second half of the story: once she's actually sitting at an intersection and tries to reason "in the moment," she seems to need to already know her own strategy in order to work out how likely it is that this is the first intersection rather than the second -- and she needs that likelihood in order to work out what her strategy should be. Our instructor mentioned that this is more or less why Aumann, Hart, and Perry pushed back on Piccione and Rubinstein's original framing, but the seminar moved on before really unpacking the disagreement.

Could you walk me through, at a gut level rather than through the formal fixed-point machinery, why this circularity shows up in the first place, why it amounts to a genuine conceptual problem rather than just an awkward way of describing an ordinary mixed strategy, and what -- if anything -- it's supposed to show about the limits of standard Bayesian updating once your own strategy is what generates the very probabilities you're updating on? If it's not too much of a detour, I'd also appreciate it if you touched on why this example tends to get brought up in the same breath as the Sleeping Beauty problem, since I've seen the comparison made more than once but never actually explained.
