I teach a formal epistemology seminar, and this term a couple of students who moonlight in the CS department dragged the group into Löb's theorem and that whole MIRI-adjacent "tiling agents" literature on self-modifying reasoners. You know the basic result: for any sentence P, if a system can prove "provable(P) implies P," it can already prove P outright -- which is also why Gödel's second incompleteness theorem falls out as a special case (just let P be a contradiction). The upshot for AI-safety types is the "Löbian obstacle": a proof-based agent can't get free-floating trust that "whatever I (or a successor built the same way) can prove is true" without that trust already smuggling in the very thing it was supposed to buy -- so an agent can never fully certify a successor's soundness from the inside using its own proof standards, only weaker approximations (the parametric-polymorphism trick from that literature gets you one grudging step of trust, not a general schema). What's been eating up our office hours is how much this actually matters philosophically. One student insists the Löbian obstacle is just a fully rigorous, formal cousin of the old regress problem for epistemic self-trust -- Sextus's problem of the criterion, the Cartesian circle, "how do I know my faculties are reliable without already using them" -- and that it shows something permanent about any reasoner trying to certify it's own future reliability from inside its own head. Another thinks it's an artifact of demanding proof-theoretic certainty specifically, and that it basically dissolves once you move to probabilistic successors (reflective oracles, logical induction) where trust is calibrated credence rather than derivability.

Write me an essay that actually takes a side on this. Lay out enough of the technical picture (the Löb result, the tiling-agents motivation, maybe a toy example like the 5-and-10 problem or the procrastination paradox, if either is useful) that a reader who's forgotten their modal logic can still follow the stakes, then weigh the "this is a permanent structural fact about self-trust for any reasoner" reading against the "it's a proof-fetishism artifact that softer probabilistic reflection substantially defuses" reading. Don't hedge your way out at the end -- tell me which one you actually buy, or defend a specific hybrid verdict (e.g. "real for logically omniscient deductive agents, much diminished but not zero for bounded probabilistic ones") if that's where the arguments actually lead you.
