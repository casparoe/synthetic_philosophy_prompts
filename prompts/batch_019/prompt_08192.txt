I've spent the last few months reporting a story on organ transplantation, and one week on a hospital ward has stayed with me in a way I still haven't managed to reason my way out of, so I'd like to lay the puzzle out for you rather than simply write around it in the piece.

The first family I sat with had a mother who had suffered a catastrophic bleed at the base of her brain. The neurologists ran the standard battery of tests — checking for reflexes in her eyes and throat, watching for any drive to breathe on her own when briefly disconnected from the ventilator — found nothing, and pronounced her dead. On the ventilator, though, her heart kept beating, her skin stayed warm, her kidneys kept producing urine, and for the two days it took her children to gather at the hospital, she looked, to any untrained eye, far more like a sedated patient than a corpse. Her son accepted the doctors' pronouncement without much apparent difficulty and agreed to donate her organs. Her daughter refused to accept it, and asked that nothing be done to her mother until her heart stopped "on its own." Down the corridor that same week was a second family in what struck me as almost the mirror-image situation: their father's heart had stopped after the ventilator was withdrawn, and the transplant team, following protocol, let a few silent minutes pass on the monitor — not attempting resuscitation, since none had been authorized — before pronouncing him dead and taking him to surgery, even though, as a nurse mentioned to me almost in passing, a heart stopped for that short a stretch can sometimes still be restarted.

I haven't been able to shake the sense that these two pronouncements, both routine and presumably both correct by the hospital's own lights, can't really rest on the same underlying idea of what it is for a person to have died — and that the daughter down the hall wasn't simply refusing to accept an established fact so much as reasoning from a different, and not obviously worse, notion of death than the one the doctors were using. I mentioned all this over dinner to a couple of philosopher friends and came away only more confused, since they couldn't even agree with each other about which family, if either, had the more defensible view. I would be grateful for your help thinking it through: is there a principled way of deciding when a person has actually died — one that could show the son and daughter, or the two transplant teams, that one of their views is simply mistaken? Or is "the moment of death" ultimately more of a useful convention we impose than a fact we discover?
