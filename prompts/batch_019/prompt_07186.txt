So I've been part of an AI-safety reading group that just worked through some of the classic inverse reinforcement learning papers, and it struck me that the whole setup — watch an agent act, then try to back out the reward function it must be optimizing — is basically the same move economists make with revealed preference theory, just dressed up in MDPs and policies instead of budget sets and consumption bundles. Both run into an underdetermination problem almost immediately, though. On the IRL side there's the well-known fact that the all-zero reward (or any constant reward) trivially "explains" any observed policy, since every policy is optimal for it, so you need extra machinery like max-margin or max-entropy IRL just to get something non-degenerate — and even then, I think there's a result by Armstrong and Mindermann (if I'm remembering the name right) showing you can't cleanly seperate "reward" from "how irrational the agent is" without smuggling in some assumption like a simplicity prior over possible planners. On the econ side, you've got WARP/SARP/GARP and Afriat's theorem telling you exactly when a finite set of price-quantity choices can be rationalized by some well-behaved utility function, but that function is only ever pinned down up to an ordinal, monotone-increasing transformation anyway, so there's underdetermination baked in from the start there too.

Specifically: is the underdetermination problem in IRL genuinely worse, or different in kind, from the one in revealed preference theory, given that IRL is trying to handle sequential choice and often wants something closer to cardinal, state-by-state reward comparisons, whereas classical revealed preference theory is basically static and has always been perfectly happy to settle for purely ordinal utility? And of the two literatures, which do you think has produced a more satisfying partial fix for its version of the problem — econ's move of just giving up on cardinality, or IRL's various priors and regularizers (max-entropy, Bayesian IRL, etc.)?
