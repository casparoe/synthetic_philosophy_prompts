I'm a philosophy PhD student focusing on bioethics, serving as the student representative on my university's hospital ethics committee. My formal epistemology seminar just covered I. J. Good's theorem: cost-free evidence can never have negative expected value for a rational, expected-utility-maximizing agent. The committee is now split by a live case. A patient at 50% risk for Huntington's disease, an incurable adult-onset condition, has finished pre-test counseling and had blood drawn, but wants to defer indefinitely whether she is ever told the result. A health economist on the committee argues against this on the grounds that a rational patient can only gain, in expectation, from information she stays free to ignore, so the hospital shouldn't help her suspend access to an already-generated, decision-relevant result rather than deciding now, once and for all. The genetic counselors say this misapplies Good's theorem and would gut the "right not to know" that the protocol exists to protect. Draft the committee's short written opinion on this request and on future cases like it: a concrete recommendation, with any limits or review points, that shows exactly where—if anywhere—the value-of-information argument breaks down, rather than simply asserting patient autonomy against it.
