I sit on the neuroethics advisory committee for a university hospital's functional neurosurgery program. Next month we have to circulate a written recommendation on a protocol that has split the committee more than anything else we've reviewed this year: a trial of deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression and anhedonia that departs from our existing DBS protocols in one key respect. Rather than having the clinical team set and adjust stimulation parameters on a fixed schedule, the device would give patients a handheld trigger to deliver brief pulses to reward circuitry -- the target is the medial forebrain bundle -- whenever they judge they need it, on the model of a patient-controlled analgesia pump. The investigators are candid about the precedents this raises -- Heath's mid-century work on septal self-stimulation in psychiatric patients, and the older animal literature on self-stimulation of reward pathways going back to Olds and Milner -- but argue that a hard daily cap on pulses and mandatory weekly clinical review make this trial nothing like those cases.

Several committee members aren't reassured. One of our neuroscientists points out that self-stimulation of dopaminergic reward circuitry, on Kent Berridge's incentive-salience account, is better understood as driving "wanting" than "liking": a patient could become motivationally captured by the trigger even if the stimulation delivers little hedonic benefit and no improvement in the depression the trial exists to treat. Other members regard this as just the "wireheading" worry familiar from discussions of reward tampering in AI systems and from Nozick's experience machine -- the fear that letting a subject obtain a reward signal directly, rather than through whatever process that signal is supposed to track, produces a benefit that doesn't fully count -- and they don't think a philosophical worry of that kind should override the autonomy of a competent adult who has exhausted standard treatments for genuinely disabling depression.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- approval, rejection, or approval with specific modifications to the protocol -- argued in enough detail that the investigators would know exactly what to change if the answer isn't a flat yes. And it should take the reward-tampering worry and the autonomy and suffering considerations equally seriously, rather than treating either side as an obvious mistake.
