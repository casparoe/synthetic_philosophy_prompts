I am drafting a historical novel set in Oxford in the spring of 1956, centered on a philosophy don loosely modeled on G. E. M. Anscombe, who campaigns against the university's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree on the grounds that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki made him a murderer. Before committing to the scene where she makes her case, I read a draft aloud to my writing group, and two of its members — one with a background in philosophy, the other a military historian who has written on the Pacific theater — ended up in a long argument about whether the reasoning I had given my protagonist actually holds together. I could not settle it myself, so I would like your help working through their exchange and saying who had the better of it at each stage, since I do not want to put an argument in her mouth that does not work, or that is not really the argument Anscombe herself made.

1. The historian opened by defending Truman along broadly double-effect lines: the intention behind the bombs was to shock Japan's leadership into surrendering and so end the war quickly, saving lives on both sides. The deaths of the two cities' inhabitants, though horrifically foreseen, were not themselves the goal, so however terrible, this is a case of foreseen but unintended harm, not murder.

2. The philosopher answered that this mischaracterizes the intention: Hiroshima and Nagasaki were not chosen as narrowly military-industrial sites but as cities, precisely because the destruction of a populated area was the mechanism by which the intended shock was to be produced. One cannot claim to intend only the shock while merely foreseeing the deaths that constitute it, any more than someone who terrorizes a population by killing civilians can say the killing was incidental to the terror.

3. The historian replied that this proves too much: by that logic, any bombing campaign meant to weaken enemy morale through destruction — the firebombing of Tokyo, say, or the RAF's area-bombing campaign against German cities — would count as "intending" the civilian deaths involved, erasing the very distinction between terror bombing and ordinary collateral damage that double effect is supposed to preserve.

4. The philosopher granted that the line is harder to draw in those cases, but held that Hiroshima falls clearly on the terror-bombing side of it: the committee that chose the targets is generally understood to have specifically wanted a city that had escaped conventional bombing, so that the new weapon's effects would be unmistakable and total — which only makes sense if the population's fate, and not merely some factory's, was the point of the exercise.

5. The historian's last word was that this still assumes we can read Truman's mind: he may sincerely have intended only to demonstrate a weapon terrible enough to end the war, treating the deaths as a certain but unintended by-product of that demonstration, in which case the case for murder collapses regardless of which city was chosen.

Could you go through these five moves and say who has the better argument at each one, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether Anscombe's actual case against Truman even depends on settling the question of his private intentions the way both of my readers assumed it did, since that is the piece I most want to get right before I let my protagonist say it aloud.
