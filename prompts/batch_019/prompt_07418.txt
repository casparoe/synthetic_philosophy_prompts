I started a new job three months ago at a mid-sized architecture firm, and I have become quietly fixated on an unwritten rule I stumbled onto during my first week: nobody sits in the chair nearest the window in the second-floor conference room, even when the room is full and it is the only seat available. I noticed that I had begun avoiding it myself before anyone told me to, and last month, when a visiting client's assistant sat there during a crowded design review, I watched two colleagues manufacture reasons to hover near her until she got up, at which point someone quietly set a box of material samples on the seat. Nobody I have asked can explain where the rule came from, and a couple of people have denied there is one at all, generally right after demonstrating that there very much is.

This sent me back to a philosophy of language seminar I audited years ago, where we covered David Lewis's account of convention: a regularity that perpetuates itself because it is common knowledge, within the relevant population, that everyone conforms and expects everyone else to conform, together with the fact that some other regularity could easily have taken its place instead. That description fits my conference room fairly well, except for one detail that keeps nagging at me: I never had any common-knowledge-generating interaction with my colleagues before I started avoiding that chair, and most of them, as far as I can tell, never had one with each other either. Reading around the topic since, I have come across more explicitly evolutionary accounts of how norms spread and stabilize in populations that lack anything like Lewis's mutual-knowledge structure - the sort of stochastic, imitation-driven story associated with Peyton Young's models of convention formation, or with Cristina Bicchieri's work distinguishing mere coordination from norms backed by genuine normative expectations - and these seem to fit the speed and the low-grade enforcement of my office's rule rather better.

What I would like is a dialogue between a philosopher who defends something close to Lewis's original analysis and a social scientist who thinks the common-knowledge requirement is either unnecessary or actively misleading once you are trying to explain how a norm takes hold among people who are, for the most part, strangers to one another. I would like them to use my chair as a running example, or a comparable one of their own choosing, and I would especially like the Lewisian to have a substantive answer to the objection that nobody in my office actually has common knowledge of anything, rather than simply restating the theory, and the evolutionary theorist to have a substantive answer to why breaking the rule provokes something closer to mild disapproval than to mere surprise. They need not reach a resolution, but each should be engaging the other's strongest point rather than a caricature of it, and I would rather the exchange end with the disagreement sharpened than smoothed over.
