I'm a software engineer, and about a year ago I agreed to serve as the "unaffiliated member" of my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Federal regulations apparently require at least one voting member with no scientific background and no institutional ties, specifically to represent outside community interests during protocol review; I was nominated after a neighbor who already sat on the committee suggested me. Until now the role has mostly meant deferring to the veterinarians on rodent husbandry questions and nodding along at discussions of the "three Rs" (replacement, reduction, refinement). This month, though, we're voting on a renewal that has split the committee in a way that framework doesn't obviously resolve: a terminal neuroscience protocol involving a dozen macaques, who would undergo implant surgery and months of tethered recording sessions before being euthanized for tissue analysis, in support of a brain-computer-interface program aimed at restoring movement or communication to paralyzed and locked-in patients.

Most of the committee wants to handle it the way we handle everything: weigh expected human and scientific benefit against animal suffering, and approve once the refinements look reasonable. But the committee's "nonscientist" seat is held by a philosophy professor, who keeps pointing out that this cost-benefit approach already assumes an answer to a question nobody has defended — that animal welfare should be handled on a straightforwardly aggregative, utilitarian basis, while human welfare is protected by much stricter constraints that can't be traded off at all. She traces this to Robert Nozick's old discussion in Anarchy, State, and Utopia, where he floats something like "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people," then seems to undercut his own proposal by asking what, besides species membership, could justify that split, given that some animals we're prepared to treat this way are by most measures more cognitively and emotionally sophisticated than some humans we would never treat this way. He even imagines hypothetical "superbeings" who would stand to us exactly as we stand to animals, meant to test whether we'd accept our own reasoning turned back on us. Nobody, including her, thinks this settles the vote, but it's clearly more than a debating trick, and I don't have the philosophical background to know how much weight it should carry against a protocol with a serious, well-documented human medical payoff.

Could you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate to the committee before the vote? I need an actual recommendation — approve, approve with specific named conditions, or reject/table pending revisions, with a realistic timeframe attached — and reasoning that engages the Nozick-style objection on its merits rather than retreating to the three Rs or the regulatory minimum. I'd also like it to fairly state the strongest version of whichever position it ends up not recommending, since I'll have to defend whatever I circulate to people who have clearly thought about this longer than I have.
