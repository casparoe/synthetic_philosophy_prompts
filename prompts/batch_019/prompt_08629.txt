Last month's physical left me with a number I can't stop turning over: the ASCVD calculator my doctor used put my ten-year cardiovascular risk at 7.5%, which happens to be the threshold at which guidelines start recommending a statin. The calculator only takes a handful of standard variables (age, cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking status, and so on) and doesn't factor in a coronary artery calcium scan, which is far more specific to me but comes from a smaller, differently selected body of research. When I mentioned this at dinner, two friends of mine -- an epidemiologist and a data engineer -- ended up arguing about what my "7.5%" actually tells me, and I haven't been able to settle it since.

(1) The epidemiologist said the number is close to meaningless as a claim about my risk in particular: it's an average over a crude class defined by a handful of standard variables, and once more specific, well-attested evidence about me exists, quoting the coarser figure just throws away information already available. (2) The data engineer answered that "always prefer the narrower class" can't be the rule, since pushed to its limit it puts me in a class of one person with no frequency data attached at all -- some coarseness is unavoidable, and a calculator validated on huge cohorts is a defensible place to stop. (3) The epidemiologist granted that we always stop somewhere, but denied that all stopping points are equally arbitrary: a calcium score of zero is itself backed by large, well-studied cohorts with known event rates, so using it trades one well-supported class for a narrower, still well-supported one, not for "n=1." (4) The data engineer replied that "well-supported enough" is exactly the kind of discretionary judgment the epidemiologist had just been objecting to: reasonable people could draw that line in different places, so nothing about a uniquely correct reference class had been established -- only the same judgment call, relocated one level down.

This feels like a real-life version of what philosophers call the reference class problem, and neither of them budged. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, then say whether there's a defensible way to choose a reference class here that neither raised -- and what that would mean for how seriously I should take my 7.5%?
