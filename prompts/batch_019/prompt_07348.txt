Question: "Augustine is often cited as history's paradigm case of an absolutist about lying. In 200–300 words, explain (a) what, on Augustine's view in De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, actually makes an utterance a lie; (b) how he ranks the different kinds of lies from worst to least serious; and (c) why he refuses to make an exception even for the 'officious lie' told only to protect someone from serious harm, despite ranking it as the mildest kind. Support your answer with at least one specific case Augustine discusses."

Student answer: "Augustine holds that what makes a statement a lie is not simply that it is false, but that it is asserted with the intention to deceive while the speaker believes it to be false; on this view one could in principle lie by asserting something that happens to be true, and one does not lie by asserting something false through honest error. In De Mendacio he sets out eight kinds of lies in a strict hierarchy of gravity: lies about religious doctrine are worst, followed by harmful lies that benefit no one, harmful lies that benefit someone else, lies told purely for the pleasure of deceiving, and lies told to flatter, down to a cluster of lies that harm no one and even help someone — a lie that protects another's property, a lie that saves a life, and, mildest of all, the jocose or jesting lie, since here there is no malice and usually no harm. Even so, Augustine refuses to treat the officious lie — told only to spare someone serious harm — as permissible. In Contra Mendacium, addressed to Consentius, he insists it would be wrong to lie even to trap heretics into exposing themselves, and he makes the same point with his well-known case of the murderer who comes to the door asking where an innocent friend is hiding: even here, Augustine says, one must not lie, though one may stay silent or simply refuse to answer. His reasoning is that lying corrupts the liar's own soul regardless of the consequences, so a good intention can never justify it — a stance that later made him a natural precursor to Kant's own absolute prohibition on lying."
