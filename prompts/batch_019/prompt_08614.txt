The question as posed to students read:

"In the 1938 'Lectures on Religious Belief,' Wittgenstein resists treating belief in a Last Judgement as an empirical hypothesis held with some degree of confidence on the available evidence. (a) Explain what work the notion of a 'picture' is doing in his account, and why he thinks the unshakeability of such a belief is not a mark of unusual certainty. (b) Explain his complaint against Father O'Hara, and how it follows from the point made in (a). (c) Some commentators describe the resulting position as 'Wittgensteinian fideism.' Explain where this label comes from and assess how well it fits what Wittgenstein actually says."

The student's answer, in full, was:

"Wittgenstein's central move is to deny that believing in a Last Judgement is the same kind of mental state as believing that it will rain tomorrow. An ordinary empirical belief is answerable to evidence: more evidence should make you more confident, and a reasonable person revises the belief when the evidence runs out. Religious belief, on Wittgenstein's account, does not work this way. Someone who believes in a Last Judgement will not be talked out of it by counter-evidence, but this is not because they are unusually stubborn or unusually well-supplied with proof; the belief simply isn't answering to that kind of question. Wittgenstein's word for this is a 'picture': the Last Judgement functions less like a prediction and more like an image that organizes an entire life, showing up in what someone fears and hopes for and how they act, rather than being one more item in a network of probabilities. That is why he says the unshakeability of the belief does not make it more 'reasonable' in the ordinary sense — reasonableness of that sort already presupposes that the belief is the kind of thing evidence bears on.

This is also what lies behind his irritation with Father O'Hara. O'Hara wanted to defend the faith the way a sensible modern person defends any other claim, and Wittgenstein's complaint is that O'Hara's apologetics were too thin on actual argument: he was trying to run a religious claim on evidence that would not convince anyone in a law court, and so, judged by the ordinary standard of reasonable belief, O'Hara simply had not done enough to earn the belief he was defending. O'Hara comes out looking unreasonable by ordinary evidential lights, and this is exactly what draws Wittgenstein's criticism.

Later commentators, following Kai Nielsen, have called this general picture 'Wittgensteinian fideism': since a religious language game is only intelligible from within, on its own terms, it cannot be judged true or false, or criticized at all, by anyone standing outside the practice and applying ordinary evidential standards — rationality itself is local to a form of life, so an outsider's objections never really make contact with it. This was Wittgenstein's own name for his considered position, and I think it is basically correct: once you grant that religious and scientific 'belief' are two different language games, with the same word doing two unrelated jobs, it follows immediately that no rational criticism from outside either game can ever get a grip on it — which is exactly the conclusion Wittgenstein is after."
