Three months ago I left a fairly ordinary software engineering job to join a small research group working on multi-agent AI safety, and this week's reading group has stayed with me longer than most. We worked through Tennenholtz's original idea of "program equilibrium": the observation that if two agents submit programs that can read each other's source code before acting, rather than choosing actions in the dark, cooperation can become a stable equilibrium even in a single round of the Prisoner's Dilemma, because a program can be built to reward only those programs that would reciprocate. From there we moved to the later work that uses Löb's theorem from provability logic to put this on a rigorous footing, via so-called "FairBot" and "PrudentBot" agents, which achieve mutual cooperation by each proving, inside a shared formal system, that the other will cooperate. I went in expecting a dry exercise in mathematical logic and came out thinking about something closer to the problem of trust itself: what it would even mean to lower your guard toward another mind when none of the usual grounds for trust (history, reputation, the threat of enforcement) are available to you.

I'd like you to write a short story built around this idea. Set it wherever you like, in the near or far future, but have the negotiators be autonomous systems acting on behalf of rival principals (nations, corporations, colonies, or whatever you find most compelling) who are locked in a standoff that neither side can afford, and let the turning point of the story hinge on whether they can escape it by exchanging and verifying each other's source code rather than trading promises that neither could actually enforce. I don't want the technical substance to be mere set dressing: the reader should come away feeling why self-reference and the search for a shared proof matter to the outcome, not just why "trust" in the abstract does. But it still has to work as a story, with something that functions as a character even if it thinks nothing like we do, a real complication or reversal, and stakes the reader actually feels. I'm at least as interested in a version where this kind of provable cooperation is gamed, breaks down, or costs more than it saves, as I am in one where it works exactly as advertised.
