Our teacher's rubric splits it into three parts: defining what actually makes a ruler "tyrannical" (5 pts), the usurper-vs-legitimate distinction and who's allowed to act against each type (8 pts), and connecting it to the sedition passage (7 pts) -- 20 total.

Here's what he wrote, word for word: "Aquinas says a government counts as tyrannical when it's being run for the private benefit of the ruler instead of the common good of everyone -- basically the corrupt version of monarchy, same as in Aristotle. He splits tyrants into two types: people who just seized the throne with no right to it in the first place, and people who got installed properly but then started ruling badly. For the first type Aquinas is fine with pretty much anyone taking action against them, since there was never any legitimate authority there to begin with -- it's really just self-defense for the community. For the second type he's a lot more cautious, and says it has to go through whoever holds public authority to remove him, not random individuals, partly because he doesn't trust private citizens to judge fairly and partly because he's worried it'll cause more chaos than it fixes. The deeper reason for all this, I think, is that Aquinas is basically running an early version of the social contract, like Locke does later: the ruler only has authority because the people agreed to be governed by him for their own good, so once he stops actually doing that he's broken the deal, and they're free to remove him, with force if it comes to it. That's also why he can say in the Summa's sedition section that getting rid of a tyrant isn't really "sedition" -- since the tyrant broke the contract first, he's technically the one who started the disorder, not the people who rise up against him. So under this view, any tyrant, whether he seized power illegally or just turned bad after being crowned fairly, can licitly be killed once his rule stops benefiting the common good, since at that point his authority isn't even real anymore anyway."
