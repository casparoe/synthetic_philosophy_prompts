I run my high school's philosophy club, and next Thursday we're hosting a recorded Q&A with a visiting speaker -- a philosophy professor from the university down the road whose new book argues that hypocrisy typically strips the hypocrite of the standing to blame others, even in cases where what they're saying is perfectly true. I got interested in this topic after a long argument with a friend on the debate team about whether it's fair to dismiss a public figure's environmental scolding just because they fly private, and while preparing for the interview I read through R. Jay Wallace's argument that blame is a kind of "moral address" that hypocrites forfeit the right to issue, along with some of G.A. Cohen's writing on the classic tu quoque reply. What struck me is that Cohen seems to want to preserve the idea that a hypocrite can still be correct, whereas our speaker's whole framework depends on cleanly separating being right from being entitled to say so.

Could you draft seven or eight interview questions for this conversation? I'd like at least one question that forces her to explain exactly what "standing" is supposed to add once everyone has already granted that the hypocrite's underlying charge is true, one that tests the scope of the forfeiture (does snapping at your sibling for lying once cost you the standing to blame anyone for anything, or only that specific person for that specific offense), one on whether and how quickly standing can be earned back through apology or reform, and one that puts to her the skeptical worry, associated with philosophers like Macalester Bell, that "standing" talk is doing no real work and that what actually bothers us about hypocritical blamers is just garden-variety arrogance. For each question, please add a short, specific follow-up that anticipates the answer she's most likely to give and pushes on it, rather than a generic prompt to elaborate. I'd rather avoid any warm-up questions that someone could answer after only skimming the book's jacket copy.
