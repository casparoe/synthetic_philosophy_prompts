I'm about a third of the way into a generation-ship novel, and one of my favorite threads is how the descendants of the original crew end up with all these little unwritten rules that nobody ever voted on — which side of the main corridor you walk down, how you queue outside the hydroponics bay, who gets to speak first at the monthly assembly. My editor pointed me toward David Lewis's old book on convention as research (I was a lit major, so game theory is not exactly home turf for me), and it's honestly been great for showing how a rule like that can just solidify out of repeated coincidence and everyone expecting everyone else to keep doing it, rather then some founding committee sitting down and deciding it on day one.

Here's where I've talked myself into a corner, though. Lewis is pretty insistent that a convention is arbitrary — some other regularity, like walking on the left instead of the right, would've worked exactly as well. Which seems to mean that since nobody on my ship actually has a real stake in a custom being one particular way rather than another, switching it should be pretty painless the second somebody points out the alternative works just as fine — there's no vested interest to overcome, just habit. I'd originally written a subplot where a young reformer has to fight a whole political battle to get one of these customs changed, but now I'm wondering if I should cut that and just have her point out the arbitrariness and let the ship adopt the new rule within a scene or two, since that's presumably what the theory predicts. Does that track, or is there some reason arbitrary conventions might actually be hard to shift even when literally nobody disagrees that the new one is just as good?
