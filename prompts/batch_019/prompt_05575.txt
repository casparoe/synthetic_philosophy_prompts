I'm designing a session on the Turing test for my philosophy of mind seminar, and I want my students to engage with Section 9 of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" rather than wave it off as a curiosity. Turing does not dismiss the ESP objection: he concedes, "How we should like to discredit them!" -- yet concludes that the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming" and that the objection is "quite a strong one" against his own imitation game. His own rebuttal, that science can "get along very nicely if one forgets about" ESP, he calls "rather cold comfort," since he fears thinking may be exactly the kind of phenomenon where ESP is "especially relevant." His proposed fix is a "telepathy-proof room," and he even worries that an interrogator's psychokinesis could bias a machine's random-number generator.

Suppose Turing had lived to see the later career of experimental parapsychology, in particular Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" studies: ordinary, well-regarded psychological methods that appeared to yield rigorous statistical evidence for precognition, followed by an unraveling that became a landmark episode in psychology's replication crisis. Drawing on Turing's own epistemic habits elsewhere in the paper -- his discomfort at disbelieving what the numbers told him, and his separate insistence, in the "Arguments from Various Disabilities" objection, that hasty generalization from insufficient data is untrustworthy -- reconstruct how he would most plausibly revise, or refuse to revise, his 1950 verdict. Would the Bem affair read to him as belated vindication of the unease he couldn't shake, or as exposing his own inference from "overwhelming" statistics as exactly the error later diagnosed in Bem's work -- and, if the latter, what would that concession cost the rest of his case for machine intelligence? Answer from Turing's own commitments, not from parapsychology's current reputation.
