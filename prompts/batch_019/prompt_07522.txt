I'm several drafts into a novel that follows a woman from her diagnosis as a teenager through to her early forties, and one thread I keep returning to is the sense that the diagnosis didn't just describe her — it seemed to remake her, and then, in a smaller way, to be remade by her and by people like her. Researching this sent me down a rabbit hole that ended with Ian Hacking's writing on "looping effects": his claim that classifying people, unlike classifying rocks or quarks, can change the people so classified (what he calls an "interactive kind," as opposed to an "indifferent" one), whose altered self-understanding and behavior then feed back and put pressure on the classification itself, sometimes to the point that we might say the kind of person in question was, in his phrase, partly "made up" rather than simply found. The trouble is that every case I can find treated in any depth — multiple personality, the modern category of child abuse, the successive redrawings of autism, the declassification of homosexuality — has already been picked over so thoroughly, by Hacking himself and by his commentators, that I don't trust myself to notice anything in these examples that hasn't been said better elsewhere, and I would rather my novel not lean on borrowed insight.

A friend from my writing group, who trained as a clinical psychologist before she turned to fiction, thinks I'm overcomplicating matters: in her view, most of what I want to call "looping" is really just people learning a new word for an old experience and adjusting their behavior accordingly, which she doesn't think is philosophically special at all. I'd like your help getting past both problems at once. Could you set out one specific psychiatric or quasi-psychiatric classification — not one of the four named above, and ideally something recent or contested enough that it hasn't yet hardened into textbook history — and trace the loop through in real detail: how the category, or some revision to it, changed the self-understanding, conduct, or collective organizing of the people it was applied to, and how that change then pressed back on the diagnostic criteria, the surrounding institutions, or the experts involved? I'd also like you to take on my friend's objection directly, saying what, if anything, distinguishes a genuine looping effect from mere awareness-driven behavior change, and to venture a view on whether the category you choose is best described as substantially made up by the classifying, or as something real and antecedent that classification only sharpened.
