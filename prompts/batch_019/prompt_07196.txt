Over the past year I've been reading my way through the free will debate on my own -- no formal philosophy background, just a lot of late nights following footnotes. I started with the standard framing of determinism versus libertarian free will, but the piece that really stuck with me was P.F. Strawson's essay "Freedom and Resentment." His move seemed almost too clever: instead of asking whether determinism is compatible with some deep, metaphysical kind of freedom, he grounds moral responsibility in the "reactive attitudes" we already have toward one another -- resentment, gratitude, guilt, and so on -- and argues that these belong to a "participant" stance toward other people that we can't simply trade in for the cool, managerial "objective" stance, even in principle. Ordinary excuses like "he didn't mean it" work inside this participant framework, while things like severe mental illness or being a small child push us into the objective stance entirely, so that we start managing or treating the person rather than resenting them.

Here's what's been bugging me. Strawson explicitly sets himself against both the "pessimist," who thinks we need some further, more-than-natural kind of freedom to make responsibility legitimate, and the "optimist," who thinks the whole practice can be fully justified just by pointing to useful social consequences like deterrence and reform, with nothing metaphysical required. But when I try to reconstruct his actual argument against the pessimist, it looks mostly like a psychological claim: we simply cannot, as a matter of human nature, sustain the objective attitude toward everyone across the board, and probably wouldn't want to even if we could, since so much of what we value is bound up with these attitudes. That might be true, but it doesn't seem to be the same thing as showing that resentment, guilt, and blame are actually deserved or fair, if every action really is the product of causes stretching back before the agent was born. Couldn't a committed skeptic about responsibility just accept all of Strawson's psychology and still insist that we're merely stuck with an unjustified practice, rather than shown to be entitled to it?

Could you write me an essay that lays out Strawson's argument carefully, presents the strongest version of this "he changed the subject instead of answering it" worry, and then gives the best Strawsonian reply available -- for instance, the idea that demanding a further, "external" justification for the whole practice might itself be a confused or illegitimate demand, comparable to demanding a justification for induction from entirely outside the practice of reasoning inductively. I'd like you to actually take a side rather than presenting both views neutrally and leaving it there: a nuanced verdict is fine (for instance, "he answers one question but quietly swaps in a different one, and here's why that swap is or isn't good enough"), but tell me plainly where you come down and why.
