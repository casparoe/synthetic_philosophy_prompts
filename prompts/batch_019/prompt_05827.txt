Question: In "Internal and External Reasons," Bernard Williams argues that all genuine reasons for action are internal reasons. Explain (a) the role of the agent's "subjective motivational set" S in his preferred model of practical deliberation; (b) what the example of the man who wants a gin and tonic is meant to establish; and (c) why Williams doubts that a coherent notion of an external reason can be given. Then state one influential objection to Williams's view and indicate how an internalist might respond to it.

Student answer:

Williams's internalism holds that a statement of the form "A has a reason to φ" is true only if that reason can be reached by a sound process of deliberation from some element already in A's "subjective motivational set," S. Importantly, S is not limited to first-order appetites: Williams explicitly includes dispositions of evaluation, patterns of emotional reaction, personal loyalties, and long-term projects, so the view is compatible with agents having reasons to keep promises or help friends and not only to satisfy narrow self-interest. Because every reason must ultimately be anchored in S, and because deliberation is essentially the process of identifying which available action will most efficiently bring about the satisfaction of some desire the agent already has, Williams's theory is best understood as a sophisticated descendant of the Humean, means-end picture of practical reason: to have a reason just is to have an action available that is causally apt to satisfy an element of S.

The gin-and-tonic case illustrates the model nicely. A man wants a gin and tonic and believes, falsely, that the glass in front of him contains one; it is actually full of petrol. Since his S contains a desire for a gin and tonic and he believes that drinking from the glass will satisfy it, the internalist model tells us that he has an internal reason to drink from the glass: the reason is generated by his own desire together with his belief, regardless of whether that belief happens to be true. This brings out one of internalism's chief attractions: it explains an agent's reasons from that agent's own perspective, rather than requiring some independent, agent-neutral fact about what he ought to want.

Williams doubts that external reasons make sense because he cannot see how learning of such a reason could, by itself, explain any change in what a person is motivated to do. Any credible story of how a new consideration comes to move someone seems to have to invoke some element already present in that person's S, so the external reason collapses back into an internal one. McDowell has objected that this underrates moral education: acquiring practical wisdom can give a person a new perceptual sensitivity to reasons that were simply not available to her before in any form, so that the change is not just the unpacking of a pre-existing S. An internalist could reply that this sensitivity must still connect to some more general antecedent concern, such as a standing concern to become a better judge of situations, so McDowell's case does not obviously establish the existence of a bare external reason.
