I'm a junior, and I'm the elected student representative on my school's Discretionary Grant Committee — two teachers, our assistant principal, and me — which decides how to spend a $15,000 gift an alumna left the school on the condition that it fund "a single project that will most benefit our students" and not be split across several. We are down to two finalist proposals and vote next Tuesday. Proposal A, from the peer counseling club, would train and supervise a team of student listeners and fund a part-time counselor to oversee them, plausibly easing real, ongoing distress for a wide cross-section of the student body. Proposal B, from the robotics team, would send our squad, which qualified for the World Championship for the first time this year, to compete, and would fund an outreach initiative that team members would run afterward at local middle schools to recruit more girls into robotics — which several of us see as both a rare, formative opportunity and a lasting pipeline for the team.

Two committee members are convinced that one proposal simply must be the better use of the money and that we just haven't found the consideration that shows which one; one of them proposed padding Proposal B with an additional, indisputably worthwhile outreach session to settle the matter, but when we discussed it, none of us could say the addition actually made B better than A overall, even though nobody denied it was a genuine improvement on its own terms. That struck me as odd rather than merely difficult, and when I mentioned it reminded me of Ruth Chang's work on "hard choices" and her idea that two options can be "on a par" — comparable, yet neither better, worse, nor exactly equal — I got blank looks and was asked to actually spell out what that would mean for what we're supposed to do. It matters procedurally, too: our committee's guidelines require us to record "the reasons the funded project is the best use of the gift," which assumes there is a best use to be found, and I'm no longer sure that assumption is safe.

Could you draft the recommendation memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's vote? It should reach and defend one concrete recommendation for what the committee should do, and on what timeline — including whether we should postpone the vote, and for how long, if you think that's warranted; it should explain, in terms the committee could actually put in its official record, whether "we just haven't found the deciding consideration yet" is the wrong diagnosis of our situation, and why; and it should take the strongest version of the case for whichever proposal it doesn't end up favoring seriously, rather than brushing past it.
