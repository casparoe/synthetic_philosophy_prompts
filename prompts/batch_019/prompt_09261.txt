I'm several years into a historical novel set in Fustat in the 1190s, during the period when Maimonides is completing the Guide for the Perplexed. The story is narrated partly through a fictional student modeled loosely on Joseph ben Judah, the disciple to whom Maimonides addresses the Guide's introduction. One scene I keep reworking is a private lesson in which the student, troubled by the Guide's cooler treatment of ritual law, presses his teacher to explain plainly why God would command animal sacrifice in such exacting detail if the whole institution was only ever meant to be outgrown in favor of prayer. I read fairly widely around the Guide and the medieval debates it provoked when I first started this project, and sat in on a couple of university lectures on the subject, but that was years ago, and I no longer trust my memory of the finer points enough to know whether the scene does justice to the actual philosophy or just to a novelist's tidy simplification of it.

Here is the exchange as it currently stands:

The student: "Master, if the offerings were only ever a concession to what our fathers knew in Egypt and Canaan, why command them with such precision — the unblemished lamb, the measure of flour, the appointed days — rather than forbidding the altar outright and teaching us from the first to pray?"

Maimonides: "You ask why a physician, finding his patient reared since infancy on a coarse and heavy table, does not tear the dish away at once, but substitutes by degrees foods that resemble the old ones in shape though gentler in their effect, until the patient can at last be brought, without revolt, to a regimen he would once have refused outright. Our fathers had known no other language of devotion than the altar, learned in the house of bondage and among the star-worshippers round about. Had the Law commanded them, in a single stroke, to abandon this and serve the Creator only in the silence of the heart, it would have asked of that generation what no generation could give. So the offerings were kept, but turned from the star to Him who has no likeness; and what began at the altar was always intended to end, in gentler and later ages, as the service of the lips and of the heart."

What I'd most value is a candid check on how faithful this is to Maimonides' actual argument in Guide III:32 and its surroundings, rather than to the flattened version that circulates in survey treatments. Have I got the logic of the "concession" argument right? And does putting a physician's analogy in his mouth distort the kind of case he actually makes — I recall he reasons instead from Israel's detour through the wilderness rather than the short road to the Philistines — or have I simply dramatized the same reasoning in a voice suited to a man who was, after all, a working physician? I'm also aware that he elsewhere insists a commandment's general purpose can be sound even where its particular details are arbitrary, and I can't decide whether that distinction quietly supports the speech or exposes a seam in it. Finally, does the scene as written leave itself too exposed to Nahmanides' objection — that Abel's and Noah's offerings, being older than any Israelite exposure to idolatry, sit badly with the whole theory — or is there a way for the dialogue to gesture at an answer without collapsing into a lecture?
