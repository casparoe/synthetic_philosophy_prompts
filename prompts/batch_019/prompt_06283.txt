My father died a little over six weeks ago after a long illness, and about a month into what I would simply have called grieving, my GP diagnosed me with Major Depressive Disorder and recommended I start an antidepressant. When I asked why ordinary mourning was now being treated as a disorder, she explained, almost apologetically, that DSM-IV used to contain a "bereavement exclusion" that blocked a diagnosis of depression for a period after a major loss, but that this exclusion was dropped in DSM-5, so grief-triggered symptoms are no longer treated any differently from symptoms with any other trigger. That conversation has stayed with me, and I've since found myself reading around the philosophy of psychiatric classification instead of just accepting or rejecting the diagnosis on gut feeling.

I mentioned all this to a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD, and she pointed me to the long-running dispute between naturalist and normativist theories of disease: Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory, on which disease is simply statistically species-subnormal functioning relative to a reference class, fixed without appeal to values, versus normativist positions on which calling something a disease is always partly an evaluative judgment. She also mentioned that Jerome Wakefield has tried to split the difference with his "harmful dysfunction analysis," which requires both a genuine failure of some evolved mechanism and a judgment that the failure is harmful by prevailing social standards, and that Wakefield has used grief-related depression specifically as a test case for that account.

I'd like an essay, pitched at an intelligent non-specialist, that reconstructs the strongest versions of the naturalist and normativist positions, explains what Wakefield's hybrid view is supposed to add and whether it genuinely escapes the objections that face the other two, and then takes a clear, argued stand on the question that actually started all this: is low mood of the sort I've been experiencing in the weeks after my father's death the kind of thing that can correctly be called a disease or disorder at all, as opposed to a normal, non-pathological reaction to loss? Please don't leave the conclusion open-ended; if the honest answer is "it depends," say exactly what it depends on.
