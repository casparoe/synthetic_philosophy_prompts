I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and last week's session was on Jim Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism. Two of my students, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing about it at the café afterward long after the rest of the group had drifted off; I wasn't there for the whole thing, but both gave me their own accounts afterward, which agreed on the substance if not the moral, and I've reconstructed the exchange below. I'd like your take on it.

(1) Elena opened by praising what she took to be the argument's real selling point: unlike the Dutch Book argument, it never mentions bets, prices, or preferences. It condemns non-probabilistic credences purely because some rival assignment is guaranteed to be more accurate in every possible world, which makes it a genuinely non-pragmatic, alethic case for probabilism.

(2) Marcus objected that this self-image is misleading, since the theorem only goes through once "legitimate" measures of inaccuracy are restricted to strictly proper scoring rules -- and propriety is exactly the property that makes a rule useful for eliciting honest forecasts out of a self-interested forecaster, a decision-theoretic idea lifted straight from the statistics of weather forecasting and betting markets. So underneath the "purely epistemic" theorem sits a pragmatic notion, smuggled in at the point where "legitimate" gets defined.

(3) Elena replied that this confuses origin with justification: where propriety historically came from doesn't settle whether it can be defended on purely epistemic grounds now, and it can be. An improper rule lets some probabilistic credence function expect a rival function to be more accurate than itself, meaning the agent's own doxastic state recommends deserting itself for no evidential reason at all -- a defect of self-undermining, nothing to do with expected payoffs.

(4) Marcus responded that this "immodesty" defense computes expected accuracy using a probability function in the first place, so at most it shows that if you're already a probabilist, you should also want a proper rule. It can't neutrally justify propriety for an argument whose whole job is to tell you to become a probabilist, on pain of using probabilistic apparatus to police the very premises used to argue for probabilism.

(5) Elena's last word was that this isn't circular, only local: you run the immodesty test separately on each candidate probability function, using only that function's own resources, so no particular credence function's rational standing is assumed in advance. You're simply asking, of each candidate in turn, whether it survives being judged by its own lights -- prior to, and independently of, any verdict about which candidate is uniquely rational.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each, whether Elena or Marcus has the better of it, or whether it is a genuine standoff, and why? I'd then like your own verdict on the exchange as a whole: does Marcus's circularity worry leave any real residue once Elena's move (5) is on the table, or has he simply failed to find a target? And is there anything about the relationship between propriety and probabilism that both of them are overlooking, and that would help move the dispute forward?
