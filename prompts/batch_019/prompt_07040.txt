I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, Ines, a retired bridge engineer, suffers a minor stroke and afterward finds that she can no longer simply take for granted certain things she never previously thought to doubt — not grand Cartesian worries about deceiving demons, but small, vertiginous lapses, as when she stands in her own kitchen and is, for a few disorienting seconds, unable to be sure it is hers. I would like this premise to rest on more than generic "amnesia fiction," and have accordingly been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty for its texture and its argument alike. In doing so I have run into a tension between two of his central images that I cannot resolve on my own — my training being in literature rather than philosophy — and that bears directly on how convincingly I can render Ines's condition.

In §§96–99, Wittgenstein writes: "It might be imagined that some propositions, of the form of empirical propositions, were hardened and functioned as channels for such empirical propositions as were not hardened but fluid; and that this relation altered with time, in that fluid propositions hardened, and hard ones became fluid. … The mythology may change back into a state of flux, the river-bed of thoughts may shift. But I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself, though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other. … And the bank of that river consists partly of hard rock, subject to no alteration or only to an imperceptible one, partly of sand which now in one place now in another gets washed away, or deposited." Yet roughly two hundred and fifty remarks later, in §341 and §343, he offers what strikes me as a considerably more static picture: that "the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn," and that "if I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put."

What I would like help thinking through is whether these two images are genuinely compatible descriptions of the same phenomenon, or whether Wittgenstein is simply reaching for two metaphors that pull against each other. The door-and-hinge image seems to require that the hinges hold still, at least for as long as the game of opening and shutting doors is being played, whereas the river-bed image explicitly allows that the very propositions that are "hard rock" at one time may become "sand" at another, and vice versa. Is there a principled way of distinguishing a hinge's fixity within a given practice or moment from any claim about its fixity across a lifetime — and if so, what does that distinction suggest about how a single certainty, such as Ines's confidence that she is standing in her own kitchen, could plausibly loosen on its hinge, so to speak, without the whole surrounding structure of her beliefs coming loose along with it?
