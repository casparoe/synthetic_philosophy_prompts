A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about whether a rational agent should always want more information before making a decision. I invoked Good's theorem -- the result that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of cost-free evidence is never negative -- to argue that the answer is essentially yes. My friend countered with a case from information economics in which publicly revealing a fact in advance destroys an insurance market that depended on people not yet knowing how things would turn out, and took this as proof that more information can leave everyone worse off. Neither of us could say with any precision where Good's guarantee actually stops applying, so we ended the night unconvinced by each other. Could you lay out exactly what the theorem does and doesn't establish -- which assumptions are doing the real work -- and explain whether examples like my friend's genuinely violate it, or simply describe situations that were never within its scope to begin with?
