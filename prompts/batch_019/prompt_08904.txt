My grandfather died last month, and settling his estate has turned into a running argument between my mother and my uncle that I keep getting drawn into, purely as a listener. He left them a joint bequest that the estate can pay out either as a lump sum now or as a larger total spread over the next fifteen years, and the two of them have been arguing for weeks over whether it is even rational to prefer the lump sum simply because it arrives sooner. My mother has a philosophy background and my uncle works in insurance, so the disagreement has gotten more technical than I expected, and I would like help sorting out who actually has the better argument at each step.

(1) My uncle opened with the plain claim that a dollar now is worth more than a dollar in fifteen years, full stop -- that is exactly why anyone who lends money charges interest, and no further justification is needed.

(2) My mother answered that this conflates two different things: the interest argument is about what money can do for you over time -- grow, be invested, be put to use -- not about money mattering more simply for arriving earlier. If neither of them intends to invest a cent of it, she said, there is no growth-based reason to prefer the earlier payout.

(3) My uncle conceded that point but said he would still rather have the identical amount now than in fifteen years, holding growth fixed, and that this plain preference for sooner over later isn't a bias needing defense -- every discount rate anyone actually uses, in economics or in ordinary life, already incorporates some rate of pure impatience.

(4) My mother replied that widespread agreement is not the same as justification. She asked him whether a pain he is guaranteed to feel next year matters less than the identical pain guaranteed for tomorrow, everything else held fixed. If not, she said, then mere distance in time cannot be what is doing the work -- any more than distance in space would make a stranger's suffering matter less -- so his "pure impatience" is exactly the thing that needs justifying, not something he gets to assume.

(5) My uncle answered that the future is genuinely uncertain in a way the present is not: he might not be alive in fifteen years, the fund could underperform, inflation could erode the difference, and pricing all of that in isn't a philosophical bias, it's ordinary prudence.

(6) My mother said risk is real but is a completely different consideration from pure time preference, and it cuts against him: once you have actuarially corrected for exactly those risks -- his odds of survival, the fund's default risk, expected inflation -- there should be nothing left over to discount for lateness itself. Any preference for the earlier money that survives that correction is precisely the "impatience" she is asking him to justify, and the fact that it is widespread does not make it rational.

Could you work through this turn by turn and say who has the stronger case at each numbered point, including any step that strikes you as only partly right or where they are talking past each other? I would also like to know whether there is a consideration relevant to pure time preference that neither of them raises. Please close with a short JSON block scoring each numbered turn from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly correct), followed by a brief justification for each score.
