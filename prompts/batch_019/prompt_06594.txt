I'm working on a documentary for this year's National History Day competition, and I chose to focus on Aquinas's position on tyrannicide after my AP European History teacher mentioned, almost as an aside, that Aquinas is constantly cited as a forerunner of the medieval and early-modern "right to resist tyranny" tradition. The more I've read, the more that reputation seems too simple: his actual position in texts like De Regimine Principum and his earlier commentary on the Sentences turns out to be far more hedged than the standard story suggests. Through my teacher, I've arranged a fifteen-minute recorded interview with a professor of medieval political thought at a university near us, who has written on how Aquinas's doctrine was taken up and pushed further by later scholastic writers, and since I only get those fifteen minutes, I don't want to spend them on questions she could answer just by restating the summary already sitting in every source I've found: "Aquinas allowed killing usurpers but not legitimate kings who turn tyrannical."

Could you help me draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual tensions in Aquinas's position rather than inviting her to restate it? I'd like at least one question on the distinction he draws between a ruler who is a tyrant by defect of title (a usurper) and one who is a tyrant merely by the exercise of an otherwise legitimate office, and on why forcible resistance is so much easier for him to justify in the first case than the second; at least one on his argument that reserving tyrannicide to public authority guards against ambitious men assassinating decent rulers under the pretext of tyranny -- pushed on whether that is really a principled conclusion of his natural-law premises or just a prudential override of them; and at least one on the passage where he frames tyranny as a scourge God permits for the sins of the people, which seems to counsel repentance over resistance, especially in cases where no higher authority is left to appeal to. For each of these, please add a follow-up keyed to the answer the professor is most likely to give -- for instance, if she retreats to "the community can still act corporately," a follow-up asking what Aquinas thinks a suffering population should do when no such corporate body exists or can safely convene. I'd like to end on a question about whether the more permissive tyrannicide doctrines of later writers such as Juan de Mariana, whose defense of the practice became a scandal after Henry IV of France's assassination in 1610, are a faithful extension of Aquinas's own principles or a real departure from them.
