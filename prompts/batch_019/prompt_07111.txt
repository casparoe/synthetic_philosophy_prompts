A friend and I had a long argument last night about the centipede game, and neither of us walked away convinced. She maintained that backward induction is simply the correct way to reason about it: since the last player should always take rather than pass, the second-to-last player should anticipate this and take instead, and this reasoning unravels all the way back to the first move, so a rational player ought to defect immediately even in a hundred-round version of the game. I granted that each step of that regress looks valid in isolation, but argued that the overall argument is somehow self-undermining, since reaching almost any node beyond the first already presupposes that someone failed to do what "common knowledge of rationality" was supposed to guarantee. She thought I was simply refusing to accept an uncomfortable conclusion. I know this connects to a real dispute among game theorists—Aumann and Binmore, among others, disagreed sharply over whether common knowledge of rationality remains a coherent premise once play has left the equilibrium path—but I would like your take in intuitive rather than formal terms: what exactly goes wrong, if anything, with the backward-induction argument, and does the paradox reveal a genuine flaw in the reasoning or merely the limits of our intuitions about rational play?
