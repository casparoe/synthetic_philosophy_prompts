At my school's spring carnival, the robotics club sold 400 raffle tickets for a single prize, and my friend Theo held just one of them. The winning number had already been drawn and posted on the office door, but nobody had walked over to check it yet. At lunch, someone asked Theo whether he'd won, and he said, flatly, "No, I didn't win." He turned out to be right. Afterward, two other friends of mine, Nora and Jamal, got into an argument about whether Theo was entitled to say that outright rather than something like "probably not."

1. Nora said Theo had no basis for a flat "no": he hadn't checked, so all he really had was the fact that 399 of the 400 tickets lose. Good odds, she said, aren't the same as knowing.
2. Jamal said that standard would wreck ordinary speech, since none of us hedges every claim we aren't literally certain of ("the pool closes at nine," "my bike's still outside"), so singling out the raffle is arbitrary.
3. Nora answered that there's a genuine asymmetry: no matter how large you make the raffle, we'd never let Theo say he "knows" he lost before somebody checks, yet far shakier claims, like the bike still being outside, count as knowledge without a second thought.
4. Jamal replied that this just shows people are bad at estimating real-world odds: computed honestly, the chance of that bike being stolen during any given lunch period is probably far below 1 in 400, so there's no real asymmetry left to explain.
5. Nora said that even granting the numbers, the raffle guarantees that exactly one confident "I didn't win" will turn out false the moment all 400 ticket-holders assert it, in a way nothing guarantees for the bike.
6. Jamal answered that the same is true of any large enough batch of everyday confident claims -- some fixed fraction of "my flight won't be delayed" assertions are always wrong, too -- so a predictable error rate can't be the disqualifying factor either.

Could you go through their six points in order and say who has the better of it at each step, or whether it's genuinely a tie, and then note any consideration you think both of them are missing entirely?
