So I'm a phil PhD student (formal epistemology, mostly working on self-locating belief stuff) but I've been sitting in on an AI safety reading group that's mostly CS people, and we just worked through the "Embedded Agency" sequence by Garrabrant and Demski. It kicked off a running argument between two people in the group, Owen and Mina, that spilled over into slack for like two days and I don't think either of them actually convinced the other of anything. Since I'm apparently "the philosopher" I got asked to weigh in and I honestly don't trust my own verdict here, so I wanted to lay out how the argument actually went and get your take, move by move rather then just an overall winner.

(1) Owen's opening claim was that the whole embedded agency problem is more radical than people give it credit for: since an embedded agent is strictly smaller than the environment it's in, it can never contain a hypothesis that's literally isomorphic to that environment (the realizability / grain-of-truth issue), so calling its behavior "approximately Bayesian" isn't just an idealization the way, say, treating gas molecules as point particles is -- its a category error, because the Bayesian setup assumes exactly the kind of god's-eye hypothesis space that's structurally unavailable to something embedded in what it's modeling. (2) Mina pushed back that this proves too much: bounded/approximate versions of Bayesian updating still get real convergence guarantees without a literal grain of truth, so at most this shows classical Bayesianism needs patching for boundedness, not that it's the wrong foundation, any more than the impossibility of frictionless planes makes Newtonian mechanics a category error. (3) Owen's reply leaned on the decision-theory side: the Löbian obstacle for tiling agents shows this isn't just approximation noise, because even an agent with unlimited compute that only acts on things it can prove hits a wall trying to trust a successor (even an exact copy of itself) using the same proof system, purely because of how Löb's theorem constrains self-referential trust -- that's a structural problem, not a resource problem, and the 5-and-10 problem shows the same self-reference issue can make a perfectly logical agent talk itself into the worse option. (4) Mina said that's exactly what logical induction and Vingean reflection are for -- logical induction gives you a principled way to handle self-referential logical uncertainty without paradox, and Vingean reflection lets an agent trust a smarter successor's judgment without proving its conclusions in advance, so these are open-but-tractable research problems, not proof the expected-utility framework is bankrupt, and Owen is sliding from "hard open problem" to "wrong foundation" without earning it. (5) Owen's comeback was that logical induction is fundamentally a theory of belief-formation, not of embedded decision-making, so it doesn't actually touch the 5-and-10 problem or give you an embedded replacement for "maximize expected utility," meaning Mina is overselling how solved any of this is.

Could you go through (1) to (5) and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's a framing that would dissolve part of their disagreement that neither of them is using -- and if either of them is quietly misusing one of these terms (grain of truth, Löbian obstacle, Vingean reflection, logical induction), I'd love that flagged too since I couldn't fully pin it down myself.
