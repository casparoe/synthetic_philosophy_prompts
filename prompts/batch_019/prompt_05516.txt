So at my company we've got an AI ops agent (basically an LLM with tool access) that handles first-response for prod incidents now—restarts pods, rolls back deploys, flips feature flags, scales services up or down—without a human approving each individual action. The whole point was cutting time-to-mitigate before anyone even wakes up at 3am. We call it Argus internally, kind of a joke name that stuck. Two weeks ago it responded to a minor latency blip by chaining together a rollback plus a scale-down plus a flag flip, and that combo, none of which was crazy on its own, cascaded into a two hour checkout outage on a saturday.

We do blameless postmortems for everything, thats just how we run things, but this is the first incident where the "actor" wasn't a person making a judgment call in the moment — it was a policy thats been continuously fine-tuned on months of past incident logs, so literally nobody, including the team that built and maintains it, can point to the specific decision that caused the cascade or say with confidence what they'd change to stop a similar chain next time. I mentioned this to a friend who did a philosophy minor and all she offered was "oh that's an old problem in new clothes," which was not helpful, lol.

Im on the incident review board that has to sign off on the postmortem and decide what happens to Argus's permissions going forward, and we're split about three ways. Some people want to just run our normal blameless process on it — no names, fix the system, move on, treat it like any other outage. Others think that's a dodge: blameless postmortems were built around human error, and they worry it quietly stops making sense once there's no human in the loop who actually made the call, and that if we don't pin accountability somewhere concrete we'll just keep handing autonomous agents more permissions while nobody's actual job is to say no. And a couple people want to just strip Argus's write access entirely and call it done, which feels like an overcorrection to me though I can't fully say why. Could you draft the memo I need to bring to Thursday's board meeting? It should land on one concrete recommendation (what happens to the permissions, on what timeline, and who if anyone is accountable and for what), and it needs to actually engage with the "blameless doesn't work here" argument instead of just asserting our normal policy still applies.
