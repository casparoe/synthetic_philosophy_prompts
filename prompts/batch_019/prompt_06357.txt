Had a long, kind of ridiculous argument with my friend Dan last night about Henry Sidgwick, and I wrote up my side of it afterward because I couldn't let it go. Curious what you make of it.

Dan's position: Sidgwick spending like two decades of his life running the Society for Psychical Research — testing mediums, investigating haunted houses, chasing telepathy — is basically an embarrassing footnote, totally seperate from his actual philosophy, maybe even evidence he wasn't as rigorous a thinker as everyone says. My position is that it's not a footnote at all, it's forced on him by a hole he dug in his own ethics.

Here's the argument. Methods of Ethics ends without Sidgwick being able to settle what he calls the dualism of practical reason: rational egoism (maximize my own good) and universal benevolence/utilitarianism (maximize the good generally, weighing my own good as no more important than anyone else's) both look like self-evident deliverances of practical reason, and he can't find a way to rank one above the other or derive one from the other. He basically says — don't hold me to the exact wording — that unless something guarantees virtue and self-interest coincide in the end, the whole "cosmos" of duty is at risk of collapsing into chaos. The thing that used to supply that guarantee was theism: God balances the books eventually. But Sidgwick had already resigned his Trinity fellowship back in 1869 because he couldn't honestly keep affirming the religious tests, so he doesn't get to just help himself to that postulate anymore. So if you still want ethics to be a rationally unified system, and you refuse to fake a belief you don't have, the only move left is to go look for actual empirical evidence that personal identity survives death. That's what the SPR work is. The Creery sisters experiments, the Census of Hallucinations (something like 17,000 people surveyed on whether they'd had an apparition coinciding with a real death — correct me if I'm misremembering the number) — none of that is idle Victorian curiosity, it's a guy trying to empirically patch a hole he found in his own book.

There's also this coda that I think makes the story almost too good: Gurney died in 1888, Sidgwick himself died in 1900 still basically unconvinced either way, and then Myers died in 1901 — after which mediums with no contact with each other supposedly started producing these "cross-correspondences," fragments that only made sense combined, as if someone was designing evidence specifically to survive the fraud objections Sidgwick always raised while he was alive. Dan thinks the fact that I find this satisfying is itself a warning sign.

Dan's actual counterargument, to be fair to him: he thinks I've got the motivation and the epistemology backwards. If Sidgwick needed this result for his ethics to hang together, that's a reason to distrust his judgment about the evidence, not a reason to respect the project — and he did get burned, the Creery girls turned out to be signalling to each other. Dan also says I'm sliding between two totally different questions: whether egoism is rationally binding is a question about the structure of reasons, and no fact about ghosts or survival could ever settle a question like that, so the whole research program is a category mistake on its own terms, independent of whether it found anything.

Can you go through this and tell me: is the history basically right (I'm shakiest on the Creery sisters and Census details); does the move from "unresolved dualism" to "needs empirical proof of survival" actually survive Dan's category-mistake objection, or does he land it; and is there a more boring explanation for the psychical research — grief, professional curiosity, his wife Nora's own deep involvement — that I'm underrating just because my version makes a better story. Dont go easy on it, I want to know if Dan actually won.
