My mother died this spring after a short illness, and sitting with our family's imam through the funeral rites pulled me back into a question I had filed away since childhood: what it means to say that God alone is the true cause of everything that happens. That sent me back to Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, which had previously been just a footnote to Hume for me. I host a small podcast on the history of philosophy, and next month's guest is a scholar known for defending occasionalism as live metaphysics rather than a historical curiosity. On the regularity of nature, she tends to fall back on the line that God's "custom" (ʿāda) is just as dependable as any natural law, so nothing of scientific value is lost by denying causal powers to creatures.

Draft nine or ten interview questions that do not just have her restate the position. I want at least one question on exactly what the cotton-and-fire example from the Seventeenth Discussion of the Tahafut al-Falasifa is meant to rule out -- causal powers, necessary connections, or both -- one that presses whether the "custom" move does any explanatory work beyond a bare regularity theory of laws, one on whether the kasb (acquisition) doctrine secures genuine human responsibility or simply relabels the problem, and one that puts Ibn Rushd's (Averroes's) objection to her directly: that denying necessary connections destroys the possibility of demonstrative knowledge. Give each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, sequence them so that her "it's just God's reliable custom" deflection gets progressively harder to use, and end on what, if anything, could count as evidence against occasionalism.
