Since retiring after nearly forty years running a biochemistry lab focused on metabolic signalling, I have finally been giving myself a proper, if rather belated, education in philosophy. I have lately been working through Nietzsche's late writings, and I was struck by how much weight he places, especially in the "Why I Am So Clever" section of Ecce Homo, on questions of nutrition, climate, and daily regimen. He treats these as anything but trivial: he explicitly ranks nutrition, place and climate, and one's manner of recreation above the traditional objects of moral and religious concern, and suggests that dietary and climatic errors, more than any defect of intellect, are responsible for a good deal of the pessimism, resentment, and bad philosophy in the history of ideas. He is quite specific in his likes and dislikes: he is contemptuous of German and English cooking, blames beer for a certain heaviness he detects in the German spirit, praises the light cuisine he enjoyed in Turin, and treats alcohol as one of the two great European "narcotics," alongside Christianity, by which people numb their instincts instead of mastering them. All of this is bound up with his own chronic digestive troubles and migraines, which he came to regard as inseparable from his philosophical temperament, and which drove him, over decades, to a highly idiosyncratic and self-experimental regimen of diet and travel.

This left me wondering how Nietzsche might have reacted to a development in the management of appetite that plainly could not have been on his radar: GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide, which pharmacologically blunt hunger by mimicking a gut hormone and let people lose substantial weight and reshape long-standing eating habits with comparatively little exercise of what we would ordinarily call willpower. Two quite different cases seem available from his own texts. On one reading, his physiological pragmatism, his willingness to experiment ruthlessly with diet, drugs, and climate in pursuit of vitality, and his impatience with any "nature knows best" moralizing about means, suggest he would have welcomed a technology that frees the spirit from the tyranny of appetite so that its strength could be spent on higher, creative self-overcoming instead. On another reading, his standing objections to narcotics and to any shortcut that dampens the instincts rather than disciplining them suggest he would have regarded such a drug as one more variety of the decadence he diagnosed in alcohol and in priestly morality alike. A former colleague, hearing me pose the question over dinner, waved it away as unanswerable in principle, but I suspect the exercise is worth attempting nonetheless.

I would be grateful if you could work through this tension using what Nietzsche actually says about diet, physiology, and self-mastery, rather than a generic impression of "what Nietzsche would think about modern life," and tell me which reading strikes you as more faithful to his thought, or whether a more interesting synthesis of the two is available. I am especially curious whether you think this is a case where his physiological materialism and his ethics of self-overcoming genuinely pull in different directions, or whether, on closer reading, that apparent tension dissolves.
