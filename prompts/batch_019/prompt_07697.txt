I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but I spent a good part of last year implementing a distributed consensus protocol, and somewhere in there I fell into some reading on epistemic logic — the kind of thing that shows up as Kripke structures in model-checking tools, but that turns out to have a much older life in philosophy. Between the "muddy children" puzzle and the coordinated-attack problem, I ended up at Hintikka's original possible-worlds treatment of knowledge, and from there at something that's apparently a long-standing embarrassment for the whole framework: the problem of logical omniscience. If you define an agent's knowledge as truth in every world she considers possible, you get for free that she knows every logical validity and every consequence of anything she already knows. That's a strange thing to build into a theory of knowledge, since real reasoners, human or artificial, routinely fail to see what follows from what they believe — that gap is, among other things, most of what makes debugging a profession.

Reading around for how logicians patch this, I kept running into two moves: impossible-worlds (or non-normal-worlds) semantics, which admit extra "worlds" where the ordinary laws of logic don't fully hold, so that an agent's epistemic alternatives needn't all be logically well-behaved; and awareness logics in the style of Fagin and Halpern, which bolt an explicit "awareness" function onto the standard model, so that an agent's real, reportable beliefs are her idealized (fully omniscient) beliefs filtered down to whatever she currently happens to be aware of. The more of this I read, the more both moves started to look, to my engineer's eye, like they buy their fit to the data by hiding an unconstrained parameter inside the semantics — which impossible worlds count as accessible, which formulas belong to the awareness set — and then hand-tuning that parameter after the fact to match whatever inferential gaps the particular agent already has, rather than predicting or explaining them.

I'd like you to argue for that suspicion as rigorously as you can: that neither impossible-worlds semantics nor awareness-style logics genuinely solve the problem of logical omniscience, because both achieve fit to actual (non-omniscient) reasoning only by smuggling the very pattern to be explained into a free parameter of the model, rather than deriving that pattern from independent principles. Please engage honestly with the strongest replies available to a defender of either framework — for instance, that awareness sets generated syntactically from a fixed vocabulary or a subformula relation are a principled constraint rather than an ad hoc one, or that formal semantics was never in the business of explaining the psychological origin of an agent's blind spots to begin with — and tell me plainly how much of the original suspicion survives once those replies are taken seriously.
