"Pragmatism starts with Peirce's 1878 essay 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear,' where he argues that the entire meaning of a concept is exhausted by the conceivable practical effects we attribute to its object — the pragmatic maxim. James took this idea and, in his 1907 lectures, built it into a full theory of truth: a belief is true if it 'works,' if it has cash value in concrete experience, if acting on it leads to satisfactory results. Peirce's own account of truth is really the same theory scaled up to the long run: a belief is true just in case it is the opinion the community of investigators would ultimately settle on if inquiry were pursued far enough, and the object represented in that final opinion is what we mean by 'the real.' So for both men, truth just comes down to the practical workability of a belief — James tests it against one person's immediate experience, Peirce against science's indefinite future consensus.

By 1905, though, Peirce decided he needed to reclaim sole credit for the method before it became permanently associated with James and F.C.S. Schiller in the public mind, so he coined 'pragmaticism,' a word he said was 'ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers.' Both men agreed, in any case, that truth is not a fixed, eternal correspondence between a belief and an observer-independent reality, and both rejected the rationalist idea that important truths could be established by pure reason alone, independent of experience."
