For a philosophy elective at my high school, we each have to write a term paper on a major figure from the utilitarian tradition, and I chose Henry Sidgwick after my teacher described his book *The Methods of Ethics* as the most careful and rigorous defense of utilitarianism ever written, well beyond anything Bentham or Mill managed. While researching his life for background, I was surprised to learn that in 1882 Sidgwick became a founding member and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and that for roughly the last two decades of his life he personally investigated mediums, séances, and reported cases of telepathy, apparently without ever obtaining evidence that satisfied him. That struck me as a strange contradiction: here is a philosopher famous for his intellectual honesty, careful enough that he had resigned his Cambridge fellowship years earlier rather than affirm a religious creed he was not fully convinced of, and yet he spent a substantial part of his career on what looks, from a modern vantage point, like Victorian ghost-hunting.

My inclination is to treat this as a genuine mark against his reputation for rigor in my paper, arguing that if his judgment could go so wrong about something like psychical research, we should be at least a little more cautious about trusting the soundness of his other, more celebrated philosophical arguments. Before I commit to that framing, though, I wanted to ask: is that a fair conclusion to draw, or is there some real connection between the psychical research and the philosophical work he's actually remembered for that ought to change how I think about this?
