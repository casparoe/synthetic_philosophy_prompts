I sit on my presbytery's candidates committee, which reviews ordination exams before they go to the floor. One candidate -- sharp guy, did a philosophy MA before seminary -- wrote in his atonement paper that he wants to take an exception to the Confession's language on penal substitution. His argument leans on that old David Lewis essay where Lewis tries to rescue the doctrine by comparing it to paying off someone else's debt, but then basically concedes the comparison only works if punishment doesn't have to fall on someone who actually deserves it -- which the candidate takes to mean that penal substitution survives only as a kind of legal fiction, not the real retributive justice the Confession seems to describe. Some commitee members want to call this a denial of an essential doctrine and end his process right there; others (myself half included, honestly, since I couldn't explain what "in our place" means any better than he does) think refusing exceptions like this would quietly screen out a lot of people who've actually thought it through. We report back to presbytery in two weeks -- can you draft the committee's recommendation memo: approve the exception, deny it, or send him back for revisions, with reasoning solid enough that both he and the stricter members would have to actually engage with it?
