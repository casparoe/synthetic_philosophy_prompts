I'm a retired astronomer (spent most of my working life doing faint-object photometry, so I've got a professional soft spot for arguments about digging weak signals out of noise) who's been falling down a philosophy of religion rabbit hole in retirement, partly egged on by a friend with an actual philosophy degree who keeps sending me papers. A few weeks back I read up on the modern "argument from divine hiddenness" -- Schellenberg's version, roughly, that a perfectly loving God would make sure every open-minded, non-resistant person capable of belief actually ends up believing, and since such people plainly exist and don't believe, that counts as evidence against such a God. That made intuitive sense to me, so out of curiosity I went back to Pascal's Pensees to see what he'd made of "hiddenness" three hundred and fifty-odd years earlier, half expecting some ancestor of the modern worry. Instead I ran into this passage (fragments 585 and 586 in the old public-domain translation I'm using, filed under a section header called "The Fundamentals"), and it seems to be doing something with the idea of hiddenness that I can't quite untangle:

"That God has willed to hide Himself.—If there were only one religion, God would indeed be manifest. The same would be the case, if there were no martyrs but in our religion.

God being thus hidden, every religion which does not affirm that God is hidden, is not true; and every religion which does not give the reason of it, is not instructive. Our religion does, all this: Vere tu es Deus absconditus. [Footnote in my edition: "Truly thou art a hidden God."]

If there were no obscurity, man would not be sensible of his corruption; if there were no light, man would not hope for a remedy. Thus, it is not only fair, but advantageous to us, that God be partly hidden and partly revealed; since it is equally dangerous to man to know God without knowing his own wretchedness, and to know his own wretchedness without knowing God."

What's tripping me up is the actual shape of the reasoning. In the first bit Pascal seems to treat the sheer fact that God is hidden as a mark in favor of his religion rather than an embarassment to explain away -- almost the mirror image of how a Schellenberg-style argument uses that same fact. Then the second paragraph seems to shift to a different kind of argument entirely, something like a calibration claim where too much light and too much darkness are each bad for their own separate reasons. Could you help me work out, fairly closely, what the argument actually is in each paragraph, whether they're really one argument or two stitched together, and whether Pascal here is a genuine rival to the modern hiddenness argument or is just quietly answering a different question than the one Schellenberg is asking? If you can tie it back to what I gather were his Jansenist commitments about human corruption and grace, even better, since my hunch is that's doing more work under the hood than it looks like at first glance.
