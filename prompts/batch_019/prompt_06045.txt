We covered Aumann's agreement theorem last week (same priors plus common knowledge of posteriors means no agreeing to disagree), and this week I want to complicate that picture using Rubinstein's e-mail game, but I'm mostly working from memory of a paper I read back in grad school, so theres a decent chance I've smudged some detail along the way.

Here's the paragraph as it currently stands: "Common knowledge is usually introduced as the limit of an infinite tower of mutual knowledge - I know P, you know P, I know you know P, and so on forever - and its tempting to think of ordinary near-common-knowledge as a decent approximation to it, the way a long finite decimal approximates an infinite one. Rubinstein's e-mail game is the cold shower for that thought. Two players face a coordination problem where one action is the sensible default and another pays off handsomely, but only if both players take it and a rare, special state of the world happens to hold. Whoever learns the special state holds has her computer automatically fire off a confirmation e-mail, the other computer automatically fires back a receipt, and so on back and forth, with every single message independently getting lost with some small fixed probability. Each extra round trip buys the pair another layer of 'I know that you know that I know...' regarding the state, and as the odds of any one message vanishing shrink toward zero, the expected number of round trips - and so the depth of mutual knowledge on offer - grows without bound. You'd think that after, say, a few hundred successful confirmations, the players should feel entitled to coordinate on the risky payoff. Instead, Rubinstein shows that whatever the message count, the only equilibrium has both players defaulting to the safe action regardless - a thousand rounds of confirmation buy them nothing over zero rounds. So it seems common knowledge just isn't the kind of thing that comes in degrees the way, say, credence does: either you have the whole infinite tower, or the coordination benefits it's supposed to secure don't show up at all, no matter how close you get."
