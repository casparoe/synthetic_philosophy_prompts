I'm taking a graduate seminar this term, cross-listed between the philosophy and computer science departments, on formal models of rational belief for agents who are uncertain about logic as well as about the world, and this week's session centered on Garrabrant et al.'s 2016 paper "Logical Induction." As I understood the setup, a logical inductor is a computable sequence of "prices" that a market of traders assigns, day by day, to sentences of a formal language, subject to the Logical Induction Criterion: no efficiently computable trading strategy can exploit the sequence for unbounded profit as deductive time passes. Our professor spent most of the session on the result she considers the paper's strongest philosophical payoff: logical inductors satisfy an asymptotic, trader-theoretic analogue of what's sometimes informally called "conservation of expected evidence" -- no efficiently computable strategy can reliably predict, in advance, which direction the market's credence in a fixed sentence (call it φ) will move on the following day. She presented this as evidence that logical induction genuinely extends Bayesian rationality to logically non-omniscient agents, rather than merely patching around the problem.

That's the part I haven't been able to talk myself into, and I would like some help pressure-testing it before I commit to it in my term paper. In the ordinary Bayesian case, conservation of expected evidence is compelling because a credence can only move in a predictable direction if the agent is failing to use evidence that is, in some sense, already available to a more attentive version of itself -- the future self's information really does exceed the past self's. But a logical inductor's credence in φ can swing sharply between day n and day n+1 purely because the market has been handed one more round of internal deduction and trading; nothing about the world, and no new sentence, has been observed in between. In discussion section, a classmate argued that this disanalogy is illusory: for a logically non-omniscient agent, completing one more step of deduction just is a piece of genuine evidence (if nothing else, about the agent's own future outputs), so the empirical and logical cases are not so different after all. I was not persuaded -- this seems to trade on a much thinner notion of "evidence" than the one doing the normative work in the ordinary case -- and I suspect the worry is a cousin of older complaints about logical omniscience and the problem of old evidence (I recall, perhaps imprecisely, that Ian Hacking raised something along these lines about personal probability decades ago).

Could you write a sustained argument, of roughly 700 to 900 words, for the position that logical induction's conservation-of-expected-evidence-like guarantee, however mathematically genuine, fails to vindicate deductive belief revision as rational in the way that ordinary conservation of expected evidence vindicates empirical belief revision -- because the logical inductor's formalism contains no internal analogue of the "already-available-but-unused evidence" that does the normative work in the standard Bayesian argument? Please engage directly with my classmate's objection rather than setting it aside, feel free to construct the strongest version of the opposing view before explaining why it ultimately fails, and say so plainly if you end up more persuaded by the opposing view than by my own.
