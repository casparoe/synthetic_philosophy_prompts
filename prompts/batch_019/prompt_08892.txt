I'm a philosophy student in my third year, currently taking a seminar on Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy, and I've just been asked to deliver the eulogy at my grandmother's memorial next Saturday. For more than thirty years she ran a small study group out of her living room that worked slowly through Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā line by line -- it's actually the reason I signed up for this seminar in the first place -- and by the end of her life she could talk about the catuṣkoṭi (the tetralemma: it is, it is not, it is both, it is neither) and the two truths as casually as other people discuss the weather. The service will include a dozen or so members of that group, who will know immediately if I get the philosophy wrong, and something like fifty relatives who have never heard the word "śūnyatā" and are, understandably, just there to grieve and be comforted.

Here's my actual problem. The two most natural things one says at a funeral are precisely the two positions her own philosophy spent decades declining to hold. "She's in a better place now, watching over us" quietly assumes a self that persists on its own, independent of the causes and conditions that composed her -- exactly what dependent origination rules out. But "she's simply gone, full stop, and that's all there is to say" is no improvement; she used to say, half-joking, that this was the more dangerous of the two mistakes, since taking emptiness to mean plain non-existence is like grabbing a snake by the wrong end. What I can't stop thinking about is that the tetralemma itself -- refusing "she exists after death," "she does not," "she both does and doesn't," and "she neither does nor doesn't" all at once -- might be less a piece of logic to mention in passing than the actual honest shape of what can be said here.

Could you write that eulogy for me? Something that would take five to seven minutes to read aloud. It needs to hold up for her students as a genuinely careful piece of Madhyamaka reasoning, not a watered-down version of it, but it cannot land on everyone else in the room as a logic lecture delivered over a casket -- it still has to work as a eulogy, and actually comfort fifty people who don't have the vocabulary, once both of the easy consolations have been taken off the table. I'd like the four-fold refusal to shape how the eulogy actually moves, sentence by sentence, rather than getting named once in the middle and set aside.
