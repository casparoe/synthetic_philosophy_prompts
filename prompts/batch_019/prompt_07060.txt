I cover artificial intelligence for a science publication, and I keep running into a disagreement among interpretability researchers that ordinary reporting hasn't equipped me to adjudicate. Some point to findings like the small transformer trained only to predict legal Othello moves, which turned out to encode a decodable model of the board state, or a language model in which amplifying one identified direction reliably made it fixate on the Golden Gate Bridge — to them, this shows that networks build genuine internal representations, not just statistical patterns that we are free to describe however suits us. Others doing similar work with sparse autoencoders have shown that the same activations yield a different inventory of "features" depending on how many the method is asked to find: single features reliably split into several narrower ones as you allow more, with no principled place to stop. They take this as evidence that the "features" on offer are more like a coordinate system we impose than a fixed set of things the network was already tracking. Underneath the jargon, this is a dispute about realism: are these systems discovered to contain representations, or are we projecting a representational story onto statistics that would look different under a different analysis? For a piece I'm writing, I want an essay that takes both positions seriously on their technical merits and defends a specific, considered verdict — a qualified conclusion is fine, so long as it commits to something rather than merely surveying the dispute.
