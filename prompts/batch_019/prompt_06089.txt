After thirty‑five years running a molecular biology lab, I retired a few years ago and, somewhat to my own surprise, fell in with a small philosophy‑of‑science discussion group that meets monthly at our local library. We have lately been working through some of the literature on biological information, and last month's meeting, which was supposed to be a fairly gentle discussion of the reading, turned into a real argument between two of the regulars, Frank and Elena, over whether the genetic code is a code in any philosophically serious sense or just a convenient figure of speech borrowed from communications engineering. I have been turning their exchange over in my head ever since without managing to settle it to my own satisfaction, so I would like to lay it out move by move and ask you to referee.

(1) Frank opened by arguing that talk of DNA as a "code" is a metaphor and nothing more: what actually happens is a causal chain running from a sequence of nucleotides to a sequence of amino acids, which could be described just as accurately in the vocabulary of chemistry as in the vocabulary of "encoding" and "decoding"; we reach for code‑talk only because we invented codes for our own purposes first. (2) Elena replied that this understates something real: Crick posited a separate "adaptor" molecule, later identified as transfer RNA, precisely because there is no direct chemical fit between a codon and the amino acid it specifies, and a translation system that needs a dedicated go‑between to enforce an assignment not dictated by stereochemistry is exactly what marks it off from ordinary causation. (3) Frank came back by noting that Crick himself later called the code a "frozen accident," that some researchers have since reported modest stereochemical affinities between certain amino acids and their own codons or anticodons, and that in any case an arbitrary causal hookup still is not a code in any interesting sense unless something in the system actually interprets it rather than merely reacting to it. (4) Elena granted the stereochemical point but denied that interpretation requires a conscious interpreter: on a selection‑based account of meaning, a mechanism counts as tracking a rule, rather than simply obeying a chemical necessity, if natural selection shaped it to respond systematically and correctably to that rule, and she pointed to codon‑reassignment experiments, in which the "rule" gets edited independently of the chemistry on either end, as evidence that translation fits this description. (5) Frank objected that this criterion proves too much, since plenty of evolved, systematic, and in some sense arbitrary lock‑and‑key relationships in biology — hormone–receptor binding, immune recognition — would then also qualify as codes, draining the word of any real content; he added that singling out DNA this way has always struck him as reflecting our own interest in genes, given that other developmental resources, such as maternal cytoplasm or chromatin marks, are just as causally necessary and never get called codes. (6) Elena's closing move was that not every selected mapping has the structure that makes the genetic code distinctive: it involves three independent parts — codons, amino acids, and a separate adaptor system linking them — that can be experimentally rewired against one another, as the very codon‑reassignment work Frank had cited shows, whereas in ordinary receptor binding the "reader" and the signal are not independent in that way; this three‑part, freely rewireable structure, and not arbitrariness or selection by itself, is what she thinks should count as the mark of a genuine code.

I did not weigh in at the time and have no settled view of my own to defend here. Could you go through the six moves in turn and say, for each, whether it strikes you as basically sound, overstated, or mistaken, and why, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether you think either of them has overlooked a consideration that would actually settle the matter, and whether you think this is ultimately the kind of dispute that further facts about molecular biology could settle at all, or whether it is a disagreement about the concept of a code that the biology alone can never adjudicate.
