Consider the exchange:

B: Jordan's grant report is due tomorrow.

(a) [5 pts] Using Grice's framework, explain how B's utterance implicates that Jordan probably isn't coming to lab meeting. Identify the relevant maxim(s) and spell out the reasoning a cooperative hearer would need to run in order to calculate the implicature.

(b) [5 pts] With reference to this example, explain what it means for a conversational implicature to be cancellable, and what it means for one to be non-detachable.

(c) [5 pts] A classmate argues that B's utterance also carries a conventional implicature, over and above the conversational one. Evaluate this claim.

(d) [5 pts] Compare B's implicature to the implicature carried by "Some of the reviewers recommended acceptance" (which implicates that not all of them did). Is B's implicature generalized or particularized? Explain the distinction and justify your classification.

Student answer:

"B's reply looks at first like a non-answer, since 'Jordan's grant report is due tomorrow' says nothing directly about lab meeting. But if we assume B is being cooperative, A can reason as follows: a cooperative speaker is expected to be relevant (the Maxim of Relation), so the due date must bear on whether Jordan is coming. Given the shared background assumption that finishing a grant report the night before it's due usually takes precedence over attending lab meeting, A infers that Jordan probably won't be there. This inference isn't guaranteed by logic alone — it depends on this particular context and this particular background assumption, which is exactly what makes it a calculable implicature rather than an entailment.

The implicature is cancellable: B could continue, 'Jordan's grant report is due tomorrow, but she said she'd still swing by for the first ten minutes,' and this would not be contradictory, which shows that the inference that Jordan isn't coming is not part of what B strictly said. The implicature is also non-detachable: this means that we can cancel it by adding a further clause like the one above without generating any contradiction, which shows that the implicature attaches to what is said rather than to the particular sentence used to say it.

The classmate's claim about a conventional implicature is partly right. 'Due' expresses a deadline obligation, and it conventionally implicates that meeting that obligation takes priority over lab meeting, in much the same way that 'but' conventionally implicates a contrast between the two things it conjoins. So there is a conventional implicature layered on top of the conversational one, even though the further inference that Jordan won't attend is still worked out pragmatically.

Finally, B's implicature is particularized rather than generalized, since it depends on the specific context of being asked about lab meeting — if someone asked B, 'why does Jordan seem stressed?' and B answered 'her grant report is due tomorrow,' there would be no implicature about lab meeting at all. 'Some of the reviewers recommended acceptance,' by contrast, implicates 'not all of the reviewers recommended acceptance' in virtually any context, which is what makes it generalized. This one arises from the Maxim of Quantity: since 'all' would have been the more informative word, and a cooperative speaker says as much as is required, the use of the weaker word 'some' licenses the hearer to infer that the speaker wasn't in a position to assert the stronger claim."
