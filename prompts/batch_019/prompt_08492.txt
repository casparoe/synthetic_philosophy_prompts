So a few of us have had this running group chat for about a year now where we take turns picking something from the AI-risk / rationalist blogosphere to dig into, and this month we landed on coherence arguments -- the idea that you can "money-pump" an agent whose preferences are irrational in certain ways, and that this is supposedly part of why we should expect really capable AI systems to end up behaving like expected-utility maximizers. None of us have any formal background in this, we're mostly just programmers and one nurse, so were doing this the slow way: rereading Yudkowsky's old "coherent decisions imply consistent utilities" post and a couple of the responses to it over and over until it clicks. Since it was my turn to host this week I wrote up a little three-part question to check whether people actually got the argument instead of just nodding along, and one of the regulars sent back the answer below. I'd like to give him honest, useful feedback, but I'm self-taught on this stuff same as him, so I don't fully trust my own judgement on whether he's actually got it right or just sounds confident.

Here's the question I wrote:

"(a) Explain, step by step, how a money-pump works against an agent with cyclic preferences over three options A, B and C (say A > B > C > A). (b) Does the same kind of argument establish that a rational agent's preferences also have to be complete -- i.e. that it must have a preference, or be indifferent, between every pair of options? Explain why or why not. (c) Explain how the answers to (a) and (b) are supposed to support the claim that a sufficiently capable, goal-directed AI will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer, and give one reason someone might doubt that this really tells you much about how such a system will actually behave."

And here's what he sent back:

"(a) Say the agent holds C and its preferences are A>B>C>A. Since it prefers B to C, it'll pay a small fee to swap C for B. Since it prefers A to B, it'll pay a fee to swap B for A. And since it prefers C to A, it'll pay a fee to swap A back for C -- and now it's holding C again, exactly where it started, except poorer by three fees. You can run the loop as many times as you like, so an agent with a preference cycle can be drained of an unlimited amount of money without ever ending up better off. This is basically the reason transitivity has been treated as a core rationality constraint ever since von Neumann and Morgenstern set up expected utility theory: if your preferences cycle, you're a walking money pump.

(b) Yeah, I think it's the same underlying argument. If the agent has no preference at all between, say, X and Y, that's just a gap sitting in what would otherwise be a full ranking, and a gap can be looped through the same way a cycle can: offer the trade X-for-Y, then immediately offer Y-for-X back, each time for a small fee, and since the agent has nothing telling it to refuse either trade, it takes both, over and over, quietly bleeding money exactly like the cyclic agent did. So incompleteness ends up exploitable for basically the same structural reason cyclicity is -- any break in a full ranking, whether it loops back on itself or just has a hole in it, gives a trading partner a way to pump you.

(c) Put together, (a) and (b) are supposed to show that any agent whose preferences aren't representable as maximizing expected utility with respect to some probability and utility function is leaving free money on the table purely because of the shape of its preferences, regardless of what environment it's in. That's the basis for the claim that a sufficiently optimized, goal-directed AI will behave like an EU-maximizer even if nobody designed it that way -- competitive or evolutionary pressure will favor agents that don't have exploitable holes like that. The reason to doubt this tells you much about actual behavior is that "maximizes expected utility for some utility function" is an incredibly weak constraint -- you can pretty much always reverse-engineer a utility function after the fact that rationalizes whatever the agent already did, so knowing a system is "coherent" in this sense doesn't really pin down anything about what it will do."
