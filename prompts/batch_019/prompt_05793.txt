At a recurring game night with some friends from a decision theory reading group, the two-envelope paradox came up, and two of the regulars, Owen and Naomi, got into an extended disagreement about how the paradox is actually resolved -- one that the rest of us couldn't settle. The setup is the standard one: you are handed one of two indistinguishable envelopes and told, truthfully, that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other; you may open your envelope and then decide whether to keep it or switch. The tempting but supposedly fallacious argument runs as follows: let X be the amount in your envelope; with probability 1/2 the other envelope contains 2X and with probability 1/2 it contains X/2, so its expected value is 1.25X, so you should switch -- reasoning that appears to go through for any X whatsoever, even before you have looked, which is what makes the case paradoxical rather than simply wrong.

Owen opened by arguing that the error is a scope confusion: X is treated as a fixed, known quantity in one line of the derivation and as a variable ranging over your own uncertainty in the next, and once you commit to any actual, legitimate probability distribution over the amount in the smaller envelope, the 50/50 split is not uniform across every value of X, so "always switch" simply does not follow once the expectations are computed correctly. Naomi answered that this only postpones the problem: the puzzle never supplies a prior, and under a principle of indifference it seems natural to treat your envelope as equally likely to be the larger one regardless of what X turns out to be, so the real diagnosis is that no legitimate, countably additive probability distribution can make every positive amount equally likely -- meaning the scenario as usually described is quietly incoherent, not just badly reasoned about. Owen replied that this can't be the whole story, since one can build variants of the puzzle around a perfectly legitimate, properly normalized prior -- for instance, letting the smaller envelope's amount be 2^k units of currency, for k = 0, 1, 2, ..., with probability that decreases geometrically in k but slowly enough that the distribution's mean is infinite -- under which the correctly computed expected value of switching again exceeds X for every single value you might observe, with no impossible "uniform over all reals" assumption anywhere in sight; so "you would need an improper prior" cannot be the deep fix. Naomi's closing move was that this is really her own diagnosis in different clothes: whenever the relevant expectation fails to converge, ordinary expected-value reasoning stops being trustworthy, so the moral is still that the switching argument covertly relies on a probability structure that need not hold, and she had simply been too narrow at first in tying that structure specifically to impropriety rather than to divergence more generally.

Neither of them conceded, and I genuinely couldn't tell who came out ahead. Could you go through the four moves in order -- Owen's opening diagnosis, Naomi's reply, Owen's geometric-prior counterexample, and Naomi's closing response -- and say for each one whether it is basically correct and why, rather than just giving an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Naomi's closing move is a legitimate defense of her original diagnosis or whether it quietly concedes Owen's counterexample by retreating to a broader claim while presenting it as the same one, and whether you think there is a more precise way of stating what actually goes wrong in the paradox that neither of them quite reaches.
