I've been on my school's debate team for two years, and last month I ended up judging a round for the first time, at a tournament we hosted where the organizers were short on judges. The round was on a genuinely complicated policy question — whether raising the state minimum wage would help or hurt low-income workers overall — and I don't know nearly enough economics to check most of the specific numbers either side used. Before the tournament, our coach told the new judges not to worry about this gap: she said the whole point of judging on argumentation rather than on personal expertise is that a debater defending a false position should eventually be cornered into contradicting themselves or dropping an argument they can't answer, while a debater defending a true position should always be able to hold their ground — so scoring the round on who "won" the exchanges should track who was actually right, even if the judge can't verify a single fact directly.

That's not quite how it felt in the room, though. One team built their case around a long chain connecting the wage increase to job losses through several linked studies and an economic mechanism I'd never heard of, and their opponents clearly suspected a specific link in that chain was shaky but couldn't isolate which one before their rebuttal time ran out, so they lost that exchange even though I wasn't at all sure the winning argument was actually correct. It also seemed to matter a lot that one team was simply more polished and quicker under pressure, in a way that felt disconnected from who was right about the underlying economics.

I want to raise this at our next practice, but I'd rather show up with an actual argument than just a bad feeling. Is my coach's reasoning sound in principle — should letting two well-matched sides argue it out in front of someone who can't check the facts really be expected to reward whichever side is correct? And if that broke down in a case like mine, is it something about how the round was structured (time limits, how cross-examination worked, and so on) that could in principle be fixed, or is there a deeper reason judging this way just can't be fully trusted once the underlying question gets hard enough?
