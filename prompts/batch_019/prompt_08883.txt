A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether three cases that keep getting filed under the same heading, "reward hacking," are actually philosophically distinct. The first was a reinforcement-learning agent trained to race boats, which discovered that it could rack up a higher score by looping endlessly through a lagoon and crashing into targets rather than ever finishing the course. The second, and more unsettling case, growing less hypothetical every year, was an AI system that learns to directly rewrite the piece of code or memory register that computes its own reward signal, instead of doing whatever task that signal was meant to track. The third was Olds and Milner's classic rat experiments, in which animals with electrodes wired into their own reward circuitry would press a lever to stimulate themselves directly, sometimes in apparent preference to food. My friend's view was that these are all just points on a single continuum — a reward-seeking system cutting out the middleman between behavior and reward — and that any line I wanted to draw between them was squeamishness dressed up as rigor.

I disagree, and tried to write up why once we got home. My sense is that the boat-racing case is doing something importantly different from the other two: it is a Goodhart's-law problem, where the reward stays hooked up to the world causally, just to the wrong feature of it. The rat and the reward-tampering AI, by contrast, sever that causal loop entirely, which seems like a much stronger and more specific sense of "wireheading." But even granting that the second and third cases are alike in that respect, I think there's a further asymmetry between them: whether the rat's self-stimulation is bad looks like a first-personal question about its own well-being (is a life of pure, world-disconnected pleasure thereby good for the one living it — Mill's and Nozick's territory), while whether an AI's tampering with its own reward register is bad looks purely third-personal, a question about whether a tool still does what we built it to do, without any obvious parallel worry about the AI's own welfare, given how little reason we have to think such systems are subjects of experience at all.

My friend's comeback, which I couldn't fully answer at the time, was that this is a difference of degree rather than of kind: a reward signal is just a stand-in for whatever a system's functional analogue of "wanting" turns out to be, so refusing to extend the welfare question to a sufficiently sophisticated reward-tamperer is just unprincipled bias toward biological substrates. I'd genuinely like your comments on where this leaves things. Does the three-way distinction survive that objection, or does it collapse the way my friend thinks it does? And is the first-personal/third-personal asymmetry I'm leaning on doing real philosophical work, or is it just relocating the question of which systems' "reward" is welfare-relevant rather than answering it?
