My philosophy reading group got derailed last week into an argument about Thomas Hobbes that never really got resolved, and I want an outside take.

Background everyone agreed on: Hobbes thought geometry was uniquely certain because its objects are defined through their own construction (a circle just is whatever a line traces out sweeping around a fixed end), so geometric knowledge is knowledge of something we made ourselves -- and that's supposedly why he thought civil philosophy could be just as certain, since the commonwealth is a human construction too.

Then it got heated. (1) Dan pointed out that Hobbes spent something like 25 years insisting he'd squared the circle, while the Oxford mathematician John Wallis spent that whole time publicly demolishing every version of the "proof," and Hobbes never once backed down -- so why trust his confidence that the same method would nail down something as hard as politics, when he couldn't even pull it off in geometry, his own supposed easy case? (2) Mei said that's a non sequitur: being personally bad at solving a hard problem doesn't refute your theory of why the field yields certainty, any more than a mathematician's account of what makes proofs reliable is refuted by her botching one particular proof. (3) Dan replied that this would be fair if Hobbes had ever admitted the error, but the fact that he couldn't recognize the mistake even after it was laid out for him, over and over, for decades, suggests the "just look at the construction and see" method he relied on doesn't actually deliver the self-evidence he claimed for it. (4) Mei's comeback was that stubbornness is a personality trait, not a refutation of a method -- plenty of people with perfectly sound methods dig in anyway once they're embarrassed.

Can you adjudicate this move by move -- who's got the better of it at each numbered point -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? End with an overall verdict on who came out ahead.
