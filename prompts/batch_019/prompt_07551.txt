I've followed the rationalist blogosphere's long-running argument about epistemic deference -- when it's reasonable to just adopt an expert's conclusion rather than work through the reasoning yourself -- but that debate is almost always about empirical or forecasting claims. I sit on my hospital's ethics committee, which also oversees our clinical ethics consultation service, and we're facing a version of the same question about moral judgment. Currently our consultants only facilitate, helping the care team and family reach their own reasoned resolution rather than handing down a verdict. A proposal backed by risk management and several intensivists tired of weeks-long futility disputes would instead let consultants issue documented 'directive' recommendations, in defined case types such as intractable futility disputes and surrogate conflicts, that the team must implement much as it would a subspecialty consult, whether or not it's independently persuaded by the reasoning. The chief objection isn't procedural but epistemic: consultants disagree with each other about first-order ethical questions the way cardiologists don't disagree about ejection fractions, so a team that merely implements an unpersuasive recommendation arguably achieves moral compliance without moral understanding -- and some members worry this lets everyone's sense of responsibility go slack, since no one in the room actually reasoned their way to the conclusion. Draft the committee's decision memo: recommend adopting the directive model, keeping the facilitative one, or some hybrid, with a concrete policy and a review date, and take that objection seriously on its own terms rather than treating it as mere friction against efficiency.
