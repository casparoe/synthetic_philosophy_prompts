Here's what makes the Wall such an annoying test case: on one hand you've got real background regularities to point to -- the USSR pulling back its backing of Eastern Bloc regimes under Gorbachev, the East German economy quietly going bankrupt, the whole one-party setup losing legitimacy once Hungary opened its border that summer and people could just leave through there instead of over the Wall. That's exactly what a covering-law theorist wants: subsume the collapse under a general pattern about what happens to satellite regimes once the patron withdraws support and the economy can't deliver. But the actual night it happened turns on this almost absurd contingency: Schabowski goes into that press conference under-briefed, gets asked when the new travel rules take effect, and basically improvises something like "immediately, as far as I know," when that isn't what was actually decided -- and then a few hours later a border guard who can't get a straight answer out of his superiors just decides on his own to open the checkpoint rather than start shooting into a crowd. No general law predicts that a mid-level spokesman misspeaks in exactly that way on exactly that night. My classmate's whole response to this was "well, that's why narrative explanation exists," which felt like it dodged the actual challenge instead of meeting it.
