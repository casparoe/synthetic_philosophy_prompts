And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"Galen Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that nobody can be 'truly' or 'ultimately' morally responsible for their actions. The core idea is that what you do flows from the way you are — your character, values, desires — so if you're going to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be truly responsible for being the way you are. But to be responsible for that you'd have to have chosen it, and to have chosen it in a responsible way you'd already have needed some prior set of values to choose by, which you didn't choose, since choosing that would require a still earlier self, and so on back. Since we're finite beings, we can't have actually completed this regress, so we can never be the true, self-caused origin (causa sui) of our own characters.

Because the whole argument rests on this regress, it really only bites if the causal chain running back through your character is fully determined. If some of the mental events that shape your character turn out to be genuinely undetermined — a matter of quantum-level chance, say — then the regress can bottom out in a free, undetermined choice that isn't itself the product of an earlier self, wich blocks the argument. This is basically why libertarians about free will think indeterminism buys them a way out that compatibilists don't have.

Strawson's father, P.F. Strawson, had already considered this whole debate in 'Freedom and Resentment,' arguing that our reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, and so on — are not things we could give up even if determinism turned out to be true, and that these attitudes are basically what our moral responsibility practices consist in. Galen's Basic Argument builds on this naturalistic insight: he agrees that we can't help feeling and treating each other as responsible, and takes this to show that in the only sense that actually matters for human life, we are responsible, even though the further metaphysical question his father was trying to sidestep remains technically open."
