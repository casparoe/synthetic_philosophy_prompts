I don't have any formal education in philosophy, but over the past year I've been working my way through John Stuart Mill's On Liberty on my own time, largely because his name keeps coming up in online arguments about censorship and hate speech. I felt I had finally worked out the logic behind his position on free expression, and I laid it out at length in a comment on a forum last week. My reasoning went like this: Mill's entire case for tolerating even offensive or hateful speech is really just one application of his harm principle. Since merely voicing an opinion, however ugly, doesn't by itself inflict the sort of direct injury on other people that would justify the state stepping in, almost all speech short of something like direct incitement has to be permitted. On that reading, anyone today who invokes "harm" to justify restricting hate speech is simply misusing Mill's own term, since he obviously meant something narrower and more concrete by "harm" than hurt feelings or offense.

A couple of people in the thread objected that I was collapsing Mill's actual argument into something far simpler than what's in the book, but neither of them could quite say what I was leaving out, and re-reading my notes on the first two chapters hasn't settled it for me either. So I'd like an outside opinion: was my reconstruction basically sound, and my critics simply inarticulate about their own objection, or is there some real piece of Mill's reasoning about free speech that a summary like mine leaves out entirely -- something beyond just where to draw the line on the word "harm"?
