I did jury duty for the first time last month (a fairly minor bar-fight assault case) and something from deliberation has been bugging me since, so bear with me. The prosecution's key witness was a guy who wouldn't really make eye contact, kept loosing the thread of his own timeline under cross, and admitted he'd been arrested twice before for unrelated stuff. A bunch of jurors basically said "he seemed shifty, I'm not buying it" and moved on from his testimony pretty quick. We convicted anyway on other evidence, but afterward a few of us grabbed drinks, and two of the other jurors, Meg and Dan, got into a genuine argument about whether that dismissal was okay. I've turned it over a bunch since and honestly can't tell who's more right.

Meg, who'd sat through a work training that touched on Miranda Fricker's book on epistemic injustice, said this was a clean case of testimonial injustice: the jury gave the guy a credibility deficit based on a prejudicial stereotype (evasive = liar) that just happens to track things like neurodivergence and trauma responses rather than actual honesty. Dan — who, it turned out, had read the same book years ago for a class — said not so fast: Fricker's testimonial injustice is specifically about prejudice tracking someone's social identity, and nobody in that room was thinking about the guy's identity, they were reacting to demeanor and a record, and would've reacted the same to anyone who came off like that, so at worst its ordinary bad epistemic practice, not identity-based injustice. Meg answered that "demeanor" isn't some neutral signal floating free of identity — whose eye contact or narrative style even reads as trustworthy is itself shaped by identity-linked norms, so the "neutral" proxy was contaminated by identity all along, just indirectly. Dan came back that if she's right about that, the honest label is hermeneutical injustice, not testimonial injustice, since the jury lacked the interpretive resources to read the guy correctly in the first place. Meg said that stretches Fricker's concept out of shape, since ideas like autism, trauma responses, and cultural variation in eye contact already circulate plenty in the wider culture — the jurors just didn't reach for them, which is closer to garden-variety culpable ignorance than a structural gap in collective understanding. Dan's last point was that you can't even tell from inside your own head, in the moment, whether your snap read on a witness is prejudice or legitimate caution, so Fricker's proposed fix (some cultivated "virtue" of testimonial justice) is asking jurors to reliably self-correct for something they have no real-time introspective access to.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of each point — actually engaging with whether Fricker's testimonial/hermeneutical distinction holds up the way each of them is using it, rather than just splitting the difference diplomatically — and flag anything about the case you think both Meg and Dan are missing entirely? End with a one-line verdict on who came out ahead overall, or say so if it's genuinely a wash.
