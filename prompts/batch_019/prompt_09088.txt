For the past few weeks I have been running a small side project at work in which two separate instances of the same language model play a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against one another, each told only that its counterpart is "another instance of the same model." Cooperation rates are far higher than any stranger-versus-stranger baseline would predict, and a colleague reviewing my write-up wants to describe this as an empirical vindication of Douglas Hofstadter's notion of superrationality. Before that claim goes into my report, I want to pressure-test it against the actual structure of Hofstadter's argument rather than against a loose paraphrase of it. That argument turns on more than the two players behaving alike: each must recognize the other's decision procedure as strictly identical to its own, treat its own choice as evidentially, rather than causally, linked to the other's, and use that recognition, together with the symmetry of payoffs, to rule out the mixed outcomes and conclude that mutual cooperation dominates mutual defection. Please argue that high cooperation rates between two instances of one model do not, on their own, entitle me to call the behavior superrational in this sense, even granting that the two instances are in fact running identical code.
