I'm putting together the discussion questions for a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and this week's unit is on split-brain cases as a stress test for theories of the unity of consciousness. My students already have the standard empirical picture in hand: the classic commissurotomy studies, the striking dissociations produced by lateralized tachistoscopic presentation, the occasional conflicts between the two hands, and Gazzaniga's observations about the left hemisphere's tendency to confabulate confident explanations for behavior actually initiated by the mute right hemisphere. What I want them to grapple with is the interpretive question that this evidence is supposed to settle but arguably doesn't: how many subjects of experience are we dealing with in these patients?

I'd like you to write an essay that stakes out a position on this question by engaging seriously with at least the following four views, though you needn't restrict yourself to just these. Puccetti has defended an unusually strong duality thesis: that even intact, unsplit brains house two separate minds or persons, one dominant and speaking, one mute, and that commissurotomy merely unmasks a duality that was there all along rather than creating one. Against this, defenders of a one-mind view (Marks is a representative example) hold that the patient remains a single subject whose capacities for integrating information have been regionally disrupted, without any literal doubling of consciousness. Nagel, in his classic paper on brain bisection, refused both verdicts, suggesting that the very question of how many minds are present may be ill-posed -- that our ordinary concept of a unified subject was never designed to yield a determinate answer once the brain has been surgically divided. And Bayne has proposed a dynamic alternative on which the patient's consciousness switches between unified and disunified phases depending on the task at hand, rather than being continuously one or continuously two.

In weighing these, I want you to take seriously the methodological objection that threatens to undercut the whole debate: that at least some of the classic evidence for hemispheric independence may be produced by cross-cuing -- one hemisphere picking up subtle postural, behavioral, or affective cues generated by the other -- rather than by two genuinely separate streams of experience. Don't hedge at the end: tell me how many streams of consciousness you think a split-brain patient has (or explain, in Nagel's spirit, why that demand itself is confused), and say what your answer implies about whether unity of consciousness is the kind of thing that admits of degrees in ordinary, intact subjects too.
