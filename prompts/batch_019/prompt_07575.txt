I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I recently went down a rabbit hole after a podcast episode introduced me to the "situationist" challenge to virtue ethics: the argument, built on social psychology, that people don't actually have the stable, cross-situational character traits -- honesty, courage, and so on -- that virtue ethics assumes, and that what we call "character" is mostly a predictable reaction to whatever situation a person happens to be in. My first instinct, as a programmer, was to treat this as basically an overfitting problem -- inferring a stable trait from too few noisy behavioral samples -- but a philosopher friend pointed out that the debate actually turns on specific, named studies and specific rebuttals, not just a general statistics complaint, and that most popular retellings lean heavily on the Stanford Prison Experiment, which I gather has since taken a serious hit to its credibility (coached guards, a script, no real controls). That's left me wanting to see how much of the actual philosophical argument survives once that particular study is set aside.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of roughly equal ability -- one a committed situationist, one a committed defender of virtue ethics -- arguing the issue out without leaning on the Stanford Prison Experiment at all? I'd like the situationist to build the case on sturdier ground: the old finding that children's honesty barely correlates across different tests and settings, the study showing that people who had just found a dime were dramatically more likely to stop and help a stranger pick up dropped papers, and the one where seminary students running late walked right past someone who looked like they needed help, even on their way to deliver a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan. Then have the virtue ethicist respond with more than a shrug: the specific line some philosophers take that virtuous character just is the disposition to respond well to different situations, so situational variability was never evidence against it to begin with. I want the situationist to push back hard on whether that move just makes virtue ethics unfalsifiable, and if the virtue ethicist retreats to saying traits are simply narrower than assumed -- "honest with friends" rather than "honest," say -- I want the situationist to point out how much that concession already gives away. Have the dialogue close with each philosopher naming one concrete finding or thought experiment that would actually change their mind.
