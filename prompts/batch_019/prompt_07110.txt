A friend and I spent a good chunk of last night arguing about a passage that gets surprisingly little attention relative to the trouble it causes: the long footnote on animals tucked into the "Constraints and Animals" section of Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*. He wants to keep his signature machinery intact — inviolable side constraints, rooted in self-ownership and the separateness of persons, that forbid using someone merely as a means even to produce a better aggregate outcome — but he's visibly unsettled about whether that machinery extends to animals. At one point he all but proposes a slogan along the lines of "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people," only to immediately worry that this licenses horrifying treatment of animals whenever the aggregate human payoff is large enough, and he never quite tells us how to fix that. He also toys, without resolving it, with whether the suffering built into predation itself is something a moral view owes us an answer for.

My friend and I ended up disagreeing about how that unresolved footnote should bear on two things Nozick did not live to see (he died in 2002). The first is the Nonhuman Rights Project's 2022 habeas corpus case on behalf of Happy, an elephant held at the Bronx Zoo, which New York's highest court ultimately rejected — a case that turned exactly on whether an animal can hold something functioning like a right against confinement, as opposed to merely having her interests weighed against the zoo's. My friend thought Nozick's attachment to inviolable side constraints should have pushed him toward the losing side, treating Happy as the kind of being whose confinement can't be justified by aggregate welfare comparisons alone; I thought the "utilitarianism for animals" half of his slogan commits him to the majority instead. The second is the wild-animal-suffering research and advocacy that has grown up since — people and organizations (the Wild Animal Initiative, for instance) seriously arguing that we may have obligations to intervene against predation and starvation in nature — which seems to force exactly the question about predation that Nozick raised but ducked.

Could you work through, as carefully as you can from Nozick's actual commitments rather than from generic animal-ethics considerations, what position he most likely would have staked out on the Happy litigation and on interventionist wild-animal-suffering arguments? I would especially value it if you resisted the temptation to hand down a tidy verdict and instead told me honestly where his own framework — by his own admission, in that footnote — simply runs out before the question is settled.
