I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, and since I hung up my lab coat a couple of years ago I've fallen down the rabbit hole of philosophy of probability, of all things. It started because of something from my own field: I leaned on maximum-entropy methods for stat mech for most of my career, and someone in my new philosophy discussion group (a bunch of retirees, we meet at the library on Thursdays) brought up Bertrand's chord paradox as a challenge to that whole style of reasoning. You probably know it: Bertrand, back in 1889, asked for the probability that a "randomly chosen" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and depending on which perfectly reasonable-sounding method you use to generate the random chord — random endpoints on the circumference, a random point along a random radius, or a random point anywhere in the disk taken as the midpoint — you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4. Three different, mutually incompatible answers, each one seemingly a innocent application of the "principle of indifference."

Naturally I brought up Jaynes' old invariance trick (priveledge the method that's invariant under the translations, rotations and rescalings the physical set-up actually has, which picks out 1/2) and figured that settled it, but two of the others in the group — one's a retired analytic philosopher, so I probably should've seen that coming — weren't having it, and it turned into a two-hour argument over whether the principle of indifference can be rescued at all, or whether Bertrand basically killed it off in 1889. I went home afterwards and wrote up all the "escape routes" from the paradox I could remember or dig up, and now I'd like an outside opinion. Could you rank the following six responses to Bertrand's paradox from most to least philosophically defensible, where "defensible" means roughly: does the most to rescue indifference-style reasoning as a legitimate way of assigning probabilities, or else gives the most honest account of why it can't be rescued here?

1. Jaynes-style invariance/maximum-entropy solution (pick the method invariant under the problem's natural symmetry group)
2. Reference-class dissolution: deny there's a genuine paradox, since "a random chord" isn't a well-defined event until a generating procedure is fixed, so each of the three answers is simply correct relative to its own implicit measure
3. Subjectivist retreat, de Finetti-style: give up on objective/logical probabilities here entirely, since unconstrained degrees of belief needn't converge without more information
4. Restrict the principle of indifference to naturally individuated, finite outcome spaces, and just deny it was ever meant to cover continuous parametrisations like this one
5. Propensity/frequentist response: refuse to assign any probability until an actual physical randomising set-up is fixed and frequencies are measured; armchair reasoning alone can't settle it
6. Full pluralism: there's no uniquely correct answer even relative to a "natural" procedure, and all consistent answers remain equally legitimate
