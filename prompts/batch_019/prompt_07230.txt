This past summer I attended a two-week program on rationality and decision theory, and one of the lectures covered "money pump" arguments for why a rational agent's preferences must be transitive. The example was simple enough: if I strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a sufficiently clever trading partner can charge me a small fee to swap C for B, then B for A, then A for C, and I would happily pay every fee, only to end up back with C but strictly poorer than when I started. Our instructor said most people treat this as a decisive argument against cyclical preferences, but then described two quite different strategies an agent with such preferences might use to avoid actually being pumped: "sophisticated choice," in which one foresees the entire trading sequence in advance and simply refuses the very first offer, and "resolute choice" (a term I believe comes from the decision theorist Edward McClennen), in which one does not refuse any individual trade as such, but instead commits in advance to the whole plan of never trading once the cycle begins, and sticks to that commitment even at the intermediate steps where deviating would look locally better.

What I have not been able to work out is whether resolute choice genuinely rescues the rationality of someone whose underlying preferences really are cyclical, given that the resolute chooser still seems to have the "bad" preference (C over A) throughout and merely refuses to act on it, whereas the sophisticated chooser behaves as though that preference had simply been overridden from the start. To complicate things further, I have also come across the suggestion that essentially the same inconsistency can be exposed through a single, one-off choice among a cleverly constructed menu of trade-sequences, with no sequence of choices over time involved at all. If that is right, it seems to threaten the idea that the money pump is a genuinely "diachronic" phenomenon, which would matter a great deal, since sophisticated and resolute choice are both strategies for managing an agent's relationship to its own future self, and would be beside the point if the underlying problem never needed a future self to begin with.

Could you compare sophisticated choice and resolute choice as responses to the money-pump argument for transitivity, and argue for a view on which of the two, if either, does more to vindicate the rationality of an agent with genuinely cyclical underlying preferences, rather than simply relabeling or relocating the inconsistency? As part of your answer, please also address whether the availability of a purely synchronic, single-choice version of the money pump undermines the relevance of both diachronic strategies, or whether diachronic and synchronic rationality can, in this context, still be sensibly kept apart.
