Something happened on my condo board a couple weeks back that's been bugging me ever since, and I can't tell if we stumbled onto something genuinely tricky or if I'm just overthinking a dumb procedural fluke. We're a tiny building, nine units, so the board is only three of us (me plus two other owners, no lawyers in the bunch). We had to vote on a special assessment to fix a structural crack in the parking garage, and going in, all three of us agreed the assessment should only pass if two separate things were true: first, that the crack is actually bad enough to need a full repair rather than a patch-and-monitor job, and second, that we could pay for it without dropping the reserve fund below the legally required minimum.

So we polled the two questions seperately first, just to get everyone's read. A majority of us (2 of 3) said yes, it's serious enough to need the full repair. A different majority (2 of 3, but not the same two people) said yes, we can afford it. Given the rule we'd all agreed on beforehand, both conditions clearing majority should mean the assessment passes. But then one of us said "let's also just take a straight yes-or-no vote on the assessment itself, to be safe" — and on that vote it failed, 2 to 1. Nobody changed their mind about either underlying question between the two votes. It's just that I was the only one who'd said yes to both conditions, so I was the only "yes" on the direct vote, while the other two had each said yes to one condition and no to the other — which sank it on a straight vote even though each condition individually had majority support.

Now the three of us are stuck arguing about which vote actually reflects the board's real decision, and whether we're obligated to redo it "the right way." I don't even know what the right way would be. Is there some principled reason to prefer voting issue-by-issue and then applying the rule we agreed on, versus just taking a direct vote on the bottom line? Or is this the kind of situation where both methods are equally legitimate and we just got unlucky with how people's views happened to line up? It's a small-stakes example, but it's kind of freaking me out about how much a vote's outcome can depend on arbitrary choices about procedure rather than on what people actually think.
