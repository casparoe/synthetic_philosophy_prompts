I'm part of my high school's philosophy club, and for our last meeting the student who runs it handed out a short packet contrasting two rival theories of what it means for a biological trait to have a "function." This felt very on-brand, since we'd just finished a unit on natural selection in AP Bio. One theory was Robert Cummins's causal-role account, on which a trait's function is whatever causal contribution it currently makes to some capacity of the system we're trying to explain. The other was the etiological (or "selected effects") account associated with people like Ruth Millikan and Karen Neander, on which a trait's function is the effect it was actually selected for by natural selection in the past. To get us arguing, our club leader brought up feathers: the fossil record from places like Liaoning suggests that feathered dinosaurs had simple, filament-like feathers for insulation, and probably display, long before anything resembling powered flight existed, and that flight only became possible once feathers were later co-opted, or "exapted," for that purpose. Two of my friends, Nadia and Owen, ended up arguing about this for the rest of lunch, and I've been turning their exchange over ever since without managing to decide who actually came out ahead.

(1) Nadia said the moral was obvious: strictly speaking, the function of feathers is insulation (and maybe display), full stop, because that is what they were selected for; flight is a side effect that happened to be useful, so calling flight a "function" of feathers is really just loose, everyday talk that a careful biologist shouldn't take literally. (2) Owen objected that this can't be the whole story: modern flight feathers, with their asymmetric vanes and the hooked barbules that zip them into a rigid surface, have been shaped by tens of millions of years of selection specifically for generating lift, so refusing to call flight a function just because insulation came first arbitrarily privileges whatever happened earliest over everything that came after. (3) Nadia answered that she wasn't privileging origins for their own sake; she was happy to admit that flight became a real, additional function of feathers later on, once selection actually started favoring feathers because they helped birds fly. Her point, she said, was only that this still has to be established historically, layer by layer, and that you can't just read a function off of what a trait happens to do for an animal today. (4) Owen replied that this concession gives away the game: if a function can be "earned" the moment some benefit starts getting reinforced by selection, then in practice you identify a trait's function by first noticing what it currently does for the organism, and only afterward going looking for a selective story to back it up. He said it's more honest to just admit, the way Cummins does, that relative to the capacity we care about here (getting off the ground), feathers' present contribution to lift simply is their function, with no need to settle any question about natural history first.
