So a few nights ago I fell down a YouTube rabbit hole (started with a standupmaths video) and ended up reading about the two envelope paradox — the one where you've got an envelope with some cash in it, and you know the other envelope has either double or half what's in yours, and there's this argument that says you should always switch because the "expected value" of the other envelope works out to 1.25 times whatever's in yours. I already read that this argument is broken somehow, and that the usual fix is that it secretly needs a probability distribution that's spread out evenly over literally all positive numbers, which can't actually exist as a real distribution (it wouldn't add up to 1), so the "always switch" reasoning is smuggling in something impossible. I explained that part to my AP stats teacher and she thought it was a fun example, though she hadn't heard of what comes next.

But then I found out about something called Cover's strategy (after a guy named Thomas Cover) that genuinely broke my brain a little. You look at the amount in your own envelope, then pick a totally random number T from basically any distribution you want (an exponential distribution or whatever), and you switch if your amount is below T and stay if its above. Apparently this actually gets you MORE then 50% odds of ending up with the bigger pile, no matter what the two actual amounts are, even if someone rigged the envelopes in advance with fixed amounts like $10 and $20 and there's no randomness on their end at all. That seems like it should be impossible given what I just said about there being no fair prior to exploit — isn't the situation still totally symmetric between the two envelopes? Could you walk me through, on a gut level, why this trick actually works and why it doesn't secretly contradict the first resolution? I don't need the full rigorous proof, just something that would make it click.
