I'm a retired geophysicist - spent about thirty-five years doing paleomagnetic and seismic work, mostly on rift systems - and since retiring I've finally been reading my way through some philosophy of science, which I somehow avoided my whole career despite apparently doing it every day on the job. Right now I'm stuck on inference to the best explanation (IBE), which as far as I can tell is just the fancy label for "go with whatever hypothesis best fits the data," something every working scientist does without ever naming it. But I ran into an objection that's needled me more than expected: the idea, I think due to van Fraassen, that IBE can only ever hand you the best explanation among the ones somebody has actually thought up, with no guarantee the truth is even in that pile.

What makes it personal rather than abstract for me is that this is basically what happened in my own field with continental drift. Wegener's idea got rejected by almost the whole geological establishment for decades, not because the coastline fits or the trans-Atlantic fossil and rock-strata matches were weak, but because he couldn't supply a plausible mechanism, and "no mechanism" got treated as close to disqualifying. Then seafloor spreading and mantle convection turned up in the 50s and 60s, and the "worst" explanation on the table turned out to have been the right one all along. By the usual lights of IBE - simplicity, fit, unification, mechanism - the geologists weren't being sloppy, they were just choosing from a bad lot, and it cost the field decades.

I still have a stake in a fight that smells similar: the long-running argument between the mantle-plume camp and the "plate hypothesis" side (Foulger and that crowd) over what's actually driving hotspots like Hawaii and Yellowstone. I lean plume myself, mostly on grounds of unification and because the seismic tomography images seem to show deep conduits under a few of them, but I cant shake the feeling this is structurally the same bet the geologists of the 1930s made with such confidence, just from the other side of it this time. So: what are the most important implications of the best-of-a-bad-lot problem, and of what the continental drift case seems to illustrate about it, for how much weight I should actually be putting on IBE-flavored confidence in a case like the plume/plate debate - as opposed to just admitting the honest position might be "we probably haven't hit on the right hypothesis yet"?
