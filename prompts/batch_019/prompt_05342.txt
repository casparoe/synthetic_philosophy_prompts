I've spent the past few weeks reporting a feature on how old philosophical disputes about the meaning of probability keep resurfacing in very practical guises: in the "replication crisis" literature, in how forecasters communicate election or hurricane probabilities, in how oncologists explain a "70 percent response rate" to an individual patient. To make sure I wasn't flattening the underlying debate for a general audience, I sat in for a few weeks on the graduate seminar on the philosophy of probability run by a professor I studied under years ago. She's currently buried in midterms and asked a couple of us auditors with some background in the material to do a first pass on grading a handful of short-answer responses before she does her own read-through, mainly to flag anything that looks either clearly solid or clearly shaky so she can spend her limited time on the borderline cases. I agreed, but I'd like a more careful second opinion before I hand anything back, since I don't fully trust my own eye not to either wave through something that's actually confused or dock a student for something that's really defensible.

The question was: "Compare the frequentist, propensity, and subjectivist interpretations of probability with respect to how (if at all) each makes sense of assigning a probability to a single, non-repeatable event — for instance, the claim that a particular asteroid has a 1-in-100,000 chance of striking Earth in 2100, or that a particular patient has a 70% chance of responding to a given treatment. For each interpretation, state its account of single-case probability and the chief technical difficulty it runs into as a result. Close with a short paragraph on which interpretation you find most defensible and why."

Here is the student's answer in full: "On the frequentist view, probability just is a long-run relative frequency, so strictly there's no such thing as the probability of a single, unrepeatable event: an asteroid either will or won't hit Earth in 2100, and that one-off event doesn't have a frequency of its own. Von Mises dealt with this by proposing that we assign the single case a value borrowed from the narrowest reference class for which we have reliable statistics — e.g., asteroids of similar size and orbit — and treat that frequency as a practical stand-in, even though strictly the individual asteroid doesn't 'have' a probability. This is essentially the reference class problem: the same asteroid belongs to many different classes (near-Earth objects, objects discovered in the last decade, objects tracked by a particular observatory) with different associated frequencies, and nothing in the theory tells us which class is the right one.

Propensity theory, which Popper developed partly to escape this bind, locates probability not in a sequence of outcomes but in the single trial itself: the propensity is a dispositional property of the generating setup (the asteroid's current orbit, mass, and the bodies perturbing it) to produce impact or non-impact with a certain strength, so a single, unrepeated case can perfectly well have a propensity even if it never plays out more than once. The chief difficulty here is what's sometimes called Humphreys' paradox: propensities are supposed to be forward-looking, causal tendencies, but the probability calculus requires that any conditional probability have a well-defined inverse via Bayes' theorem, and it's not clear it makes sense to speak of the 'reverse propensity' of an observed impact to have been caused by a given orbital configuration.

Subjectivists sidestep the whole problem, since on their view a probability just is a degree of belief, and beliefs can obviously be held about one-off events. The Ramsey/de Finetti Dutch book argument shows that if your credences don't obey the probability axioms, a bookie can construct a set of bets that guarantees you a loss no matter what happens, so coherence is really the only formal constraint — and two agents who are shown the same evidence about the asteroid's orbit should, if they're both rational, converge on the same number, since the probability just tracks what the evidence supports. The obvious cost is that this can make probability sound like a fact about a person's head rather than about the asteroid, which sits uneasily with how confidently scientists report impact probabilities.

If I had to choose, I'd lean toward the propensity interpretation for a physical case like this one, since it's the only view that takes single-case objective chance at face value without either denying that the asteroid has a probability or reducing that probability to someone's opinion."
