So I'm on this small volunteer panel that helps my state's gaming board decide on petitions from people who put themselves on the lifetime gambling self-exclusion list and later want back in. I got asked to join a few years back after watching my cousin's gambling problem drag our whole family through the wringer, so I've mostly just been reading up on this stuff on my own since then, no formal training or anything. We've got a case for next month's meeting that's been bugging me: a guy signed up for the lifetime version of self-exclusion about eleven years ago, right after he lost his house and maxed out three credit cards, and he specifically picked lifetime over the 5-year option because his own written statement at the time literally says he 'didn't trust future me for one second' and wanted it to be impossible to undo, even by him. Now he's petitioning to be reinstated - he's held a steady job for years, paid off all his debt, got married, and has a counselor's letter saying theres no sign of a problem anymore. His whole argument is that it's not fair to hold a decision he made in the worst moment of his life against him forever, especially since people who picked the 5-year option just get to come back automatically once their time's up.

Our panel is split right down the middle on it. Half the group think the entire point of offering a "lifetime" choice in the first place is that it's the one option nobody's supposed to be able to talk him out of later - not a counselor, not the casino, not even a calmer, more put-together version of himself ten years down the road. That's the whole reason he picked it over 5 years to begin with, and if we undo it now just because his story is sympathetic, we've basically proven "lifetime" doesn't actually mean anything, which screws over everybody else on that list who's counting on it being unbreakable specifically to protect them from themselves. The other half thinks it's messed up to treat one desperate decision made by a guy in crisis as a permanent life sentence no matter what he does afterward or how long it's been, like there's no possible way for a person to ever earn their way back regardless of how much evidence there is that he's changed.

I need to actually write up our panel's recommendation memo to send to the full board before that meeting - not a "here's both sides, you decide" summary, but a real call on his specific petition (deny it, grant it, grant it with conditions, whatever we think is actually right) with something like a timeline attached to it, and it has to genuinely wrestle with whichever side we don't end up going with instead of just brushing it off in a sentence. Could you draft that memo for me?
