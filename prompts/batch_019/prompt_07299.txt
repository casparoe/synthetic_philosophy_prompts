I'm drafting a seminar paper on Maimonides's theory of ta'amei ha-mitzvot (his rationales for the commandments), and I keep running into a tension that I don't think dissolves just by noting that two different genres are at work. In Guide III:32, Maimonides accounts for the entire sacrificial cult as a concession to a historically contingent psychological fact: the Israelites, raised in a milieu where sacrifice was the universal idiom of worship, could not have been asked to abandon it outright, so God redirected the practice toward Himself as a way station en route to purer, non-sacrificial worship. Yet in the Mishneh Torah's Laws of Kings (Hilchot Melakhim 11:1), he rules flatly that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and reinstate the sacrificial service in full, with no hint that this is a lesser or transitional mode of worship. I'd like you to explain, as precisely as you can, how, if at all, these two claims can be held together: please actually attempt the reconciliation, or explain why none is available, rather than retreating to the observation that a legal code and a philosophical treatise are simply doing different things, since Maimonides is clearly willing elsewhere to let his code carry philosophical weight.
