Question (20 points). Russell introduces his theory of descriptions in "On Denoting" partly to handle the puzzle generated by the sentence "The present King of France is bald."
(a) [8 pts] Give Russell's logical analysis of this sentence, stating the three claims that jointly make up its content on his view, and explain why the theory allows him to call the sentence false rather than meaningless.
(b) [8 pts] "The present King of France is not bald" is ambiguous. Using Russell's own terms "primary occurrence" and "secondary occurrence" for the denoting phrase, give the two logical forms this sentence can take and state which is true and which is false.
(c) [4 pts] Explain the main respect in which Russell's treatment of this sentence differs from Frege's.

Student's answer:

(a) Russell analyses "The present King of France is bald" as short for the conjunction of three claims: that there is at least one King of France, that there is at most one King of France, and that whatever is King of France is bald — symbolically, ∃x(Kx ∧ ∀y(Ky→y=x) ∧ Bx). Since France currently has no king, the first conjunct is false, so the whole conjunction is false. The sentence therefore comes out false rather than meaningless, which is exactly what Russell wants: "the present King of France" is not a name that fails to refer to some shadowy subsistent entity, as Meinong would have it, but an "incomplete symbol" that has meaning only in the context of a whole sentence.

(b) The ambiguity turns on the scope of the negation relative to the description. On one reading, we first assert that a unique King of France exists and then deny baldness of him: ∃x(Kx ∧ ∀y(Ky→y=x) ∧ ¬Bx). Since there is no King of France, this reading is false. On the other reading, we deny the whole conjunction as a unit: ¬∃x(Kx ∧ ∀y(Ky→y=x) ∧ Bx) — "it is not the case that a unique bald King of France exists." Since this is indeed not the case, this reading is true. Russell calls the first reading — the false one — the phrase's "secondary occurrence," and the second — the true one — its "primary occurrence," since here it is the negation that comes to dominate the whole proposition.

(c) Frege agreed that "the present King of France" fails to denote anything, but he located the trouble differently. For Frege, the phrase simply has no sense, given that there is no king for it to present, and a predicate cannot combine with a term lacking sense to yield a complete, truth-evaluable thought, so the sentence is neither true nor false. Russell rejects this outcome. Because descriptions are quantificational rather than referential, every sentence built from them has a perfectly determinate truth value, and the King of France sentence is simply false, full stop.
