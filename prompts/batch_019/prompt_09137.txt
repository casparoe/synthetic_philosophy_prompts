Okay so this is gonna sound like a random combo of two totally different classes, but I promise it connects. In Theory of Knowledge we've been going through part of Plato's *Protagoras*, and there's this argument near the end where Socrates basically claims nobody ever does the wrong thing on purpose — like if you truly and fully know what's good for you in a given moment, you will always choose it, no exceptions. What looks like "giving in to temptation" or weakness of will, he says, is actually just an error in weighing things: your mind is bad at comparing a smaller pleasure right now against a bigger pleasure or pain later, so you're not choosing badly on purpose, you're just measuring wrong, like an optical illusion but for value instead of size. My teacher called it his "art of measurement" argument, and the whole point is that if we had a real science of measuring pleasure and pain accurately, akrasia (weakness of will) basically couldn't exist.

Separately, in AP Psych, when we did the marshmallow-test/self-control unit, my teacher brought up this behavioral econ concept called hyperbolic discounting, which is basically that people don't discount future rewards at a steady rate — we discount really steeply for stuff that's close in time and way more gently for stuff that's far away, so the exact same two options (like $50 now vs. $100 in a year) can flip which one you'd pick depending on whether "now" is literally today or "now" and "later" are both pushed a year into the future. I was explaining this to my friend at lunch and it hit me that its basically a repeatable, predictable pattern of people acting against thier own better judgement, over and over, in the same direction, not just a one-off "oops I messed up my mental math" type mistake.
