I'm a senior at my high school taking an elective called "Introduction to Comparative Philosophy," and for our current unit our teacher gave us a course packet of excerpts from Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, mostly drawn from Jay Garfield's translation, along with some of his commentary. We had just finished two weeks on classical logic — the law of excluded middle, the law of non-contradiction — right before starting this unit, so I went in assuming the "tetralemma" (the four-part form: something is X, is not X, is both, is neither) would behave the same way everywhere once I understood the basic idea. Instead I got stuck comparing two verses that use the identical four-part structure to do what look like opposite things, and I don't trust myself to resolve it on my own before our short paper is due.

The first is chapter 18, verse 8, on examining the self. As best I can reconstruct it from the packet (I'm paraphrasing from memory rather than quoting it exactly), it says that everything is real, is not real, is both real and not real, and is neither real nor not real — and that this fourfold description just is the Buddha's own teaching. That reads to me as Nāgārjuna affirming all four horns at once, as though things genuinely have this fourfold character. But a few chapters later, in chapter 22, verse 11, on examining the Tathāgata, he runs through the same four options again — this time about emptiness itself, whether something is empty, not empty, both, or neither — and says that none of the four should be asserted at all, and that such language is only used for ordinary, practical purposes. So the very move that apparently gave the correct description of things in chapter 18 is something you are explicitly forbidden from asserting in chapter 22, about a question that seems just as fundamental.

My teacher mentioned, without really explaining, that some scholars take this to mean Nāgārjuna is deliberately committed to true contradictions at the limits of thought (something to do with "paraconsistent logic"), that others think there is no real contradiction once you separate conventional truth from ultimate truth, and that others read the whole tetralemma as more of a therapeutic device for detaching people from views than as a set of claims about how things stand. I don't yet have a good enough grip on any of those three positions to know which one actually earns its keep here, and I would rather understand the verses themselves than reach for whichever secondary source I find first. Could you help me work through what is actually happening logically between these two verses — whether Nāgārjuna is contradicting himself by his own standards, given that he relies on very ordinary reductio arguments elsewhere in the text — and say which of the three readings my teacher gestured at, if any, you think actually resolves the tension, especially regarding what it would mean for emptiness to apply even to itself?
