So I've been down a rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm writing on "coherence arguments" in AI safety circles — the claim you sometimes hear that any sufficiently capable AI will end up acting like it has consistent, transitive preferences, because otherwise it could be "money pumped," bled dry by a series of trades it happily agrees to one at a time but that leave it strictly worse off overall. Chasing the argument back through the older philosophy behind it (some 1950s decision theory, then the whole resolute-choice debate that followed), I realized I don't fully understand where its force is actually supposed to come from, and I think watching two smart people fight about it would help me more then another survey paragraph would.

Here's the scene I want: a flea-market stall, three items laid out on a blanket — a harmonica, a pocketknife, and a snow globe — and a trader whos arranged things so the mark prefers the harmonica to the pocketknife, the pocketknife to the snow globe, and (here's the cyclical bit) the snow globe to the harmonica. The trader offers swaps one at a time, always in whatever direction the mark currently prefers, always for a small coin on top. If the mark just answers honestly every time, they end up back where they started, three coins poorer, and the trader can run the whole thing again forever. I want two rational-choice theorists watching this from the sidelines and arguing about what it actually shows. One says the only sane conclusion is that the mark's preferences were incoherent from the start, full stop — no amount of cleverness at the table rescues an ordering that's broken at the root, and the fix has to happen there. The other thinks that's too quick: the mark only gets pumped because they're re-deciding fresh at every step with no memory of the plan; a chooser who commits to the whole sequence up front, or who simply declines the first trade once they can see where the cycle leads, never loses a coin, keeps the cyclical preferences completely intact, and is therefore no less rational than anyone else. On this second view, the lesson of the money pump is about foresight and self-binding over time, not about the shape of your preferences at any one moment.

Could you write me that dialogue? I want each theorist to actually have to deal with the other's best shot rather than just restate their own view louder — so the "your preferences are broken" theorist should have to answer the point that even ordinary transitive agents rely on some resoluteness against temptation, and the "commit to the plan" theorist should have to answer the worry that a cleverer trader could design a menu that catches even a fully resolute mark, or that refusing a trade you'd otherwise want just quietly imports the very transitivity the mark was supposed to be doing without. Don't bother declaring a winner — I'm mainly curious what the strongest version of each side actually sounds like back to back.
