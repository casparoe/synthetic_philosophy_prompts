I'm prepping next week's seminar on supertasks and got sidetracked thinking about something probably too niche for class but perfect for you. You know Thomsons lamp — flip it on, then off, then on again, halving the interval each time, so infinitely many switches happen inside one minute flat, and the puzzle is whether it's on or off once the minute's up. Leibniz obviously never heard of it, but he actually wrote a ton about exactly the kind of infinity this trades on: the actual-vs-syncategorematic (potential) infinite distinction, the "labyrinth of the continuum," and his principle of sufficient reason. If you dropped Thomson's lamp on his desk, what do you think he'd actually say — using his own machinery, not just a generic "infinity is baffling" shrug? Would he say the setup illegitimately treats an unfinishable series as though it were completed, invoke PSR to argue the lamp's final state can't be a real fact about the world, or take some other Leibnizian route entirely? Bonus points if you can say whether his verdict lands closer to Thomson's own "this is paradoxical" or to Benacerraf's later "the end-state is just undetermined, no big deal."
