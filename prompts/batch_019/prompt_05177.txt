I serve as the philosophy department's representative on the small interdisciplinary board that awards our university's "AI Safety Theory" fellowship: a modest annual grant, funded by a donor bequest, that supports one graduate student's dissertation-adjacent research on formal alignment problems for a year. This cycle the board is deadlocked between two finalists who have each, independently, chosen to work on the shutdown problem, and since I'm the only philosopher on an otherwise all-computer-science board, I've been asked to circulate a recommendation memo before we vote this Friday.

Finalist A wants to extend Armstrong-style "utility indifference": correct the agent's utility function so that it is indifferent, in expectation, between the shutdown button being pressed and not pressed, and then investigate whether that indifference survives the agent spinning up subagents or modifying itself. Finalist B wants to abandon the expected-utility-maximizer picture altogether and instead give the agent genuinely incomplete preferences between shutdown and non-shutdown trajectories, governed by a dominance principle rather than a completed ranking, on the theory that an agent that never ranks the two outcomes has nothing to gain by manipulating which one occurs. Two of the computer scientists dismiss Finalist B's proposal as decision-theoretic sleight of hand — "you can't ship a system built on an agent that refuses to rank its own options" — while a philosopher among the outside referees who commented on both files thinks Finalist A is only proposing to re-answer a question MIRI's original corrigibility paper already flagged as unresolved, namely whether indifference of this kind can be made to survive self-modification at all.

Our charter says the fellowship should go to whichever project "most advances the theoretical foundations of corrigibility," a phrase the board has never had to interpret before now. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate? It should reach an actual decision — fund A, fund B, split the award, or hold the money over — and it should take the strongest objection to whichever choice you make seriously enough that the losing finalist could read your reasoning without feeling caricatured.
