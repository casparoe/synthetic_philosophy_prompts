And here's what they turned in:

"(a) Dennett distinguishes three stances we can take toward a system to predict what it'll do. The physical stance uses the laws of physics and the system's actual state, useful for e.g. predicting when a fridge will stop working. The design stance predicts behavior from what the system is designed to do, assuming it functions as intended -- e.g. predicting a calculator will output 4 when you press 2+2, without caring about the wiring. The intentional stance goes further: we treat the system as a rational agent and ascribe to it whatever beliefs and desires it ought to have given its situation, then predict it'll act to satisfy those desires given those beliefs. The design and intentional stances differ mainly in that the design stance is available whenever we know (or assume) the blueprint, while the intentional stance gets used when attributing rationality is the most efficient way to predict behavior, even if a design-level story is available in principle.

(b) Dennett worries this makes belief-talk sound like a fiction we impose for convenience, with nothing in the world making it true. In 'Real Patterns' he answers this with Conway's Game of Life: at the pixel level all that's happening is cells switching on and off by simple rules, but at a higher level you can see 'gliders' moving across the grid. The glider isn't some extra thing added on top of the pixels, yet it's a genuine, objective pattern -- you get a far more compact and predictively useful description of the grid by tracking gliders than by tracking every pixel. Beliefs are real in exactly this way, on Dennett's view: patterns that are actually there in a system's behavior, identifiable and exploitable for prediction, even though -- just like the glider is nothing over and above the pixels -- a belief is nothing over and above the underlying representations, so that a belief just is whatever internal state is playing that functional role, the same way Fodor thinks a belief is a sentence in the language of thought that gets tokened at the right moment. That's why this counts as a genuine realism about belief and not eliminativism.

(c) Since we already know exactly what's mechanistically going on inside a large language model -- it's predicting the statistically most likely next token given its training data -- the design stance already gives us a complete story of why it outputs what it outputs. On Dennett's view that means the intentional stance isn't needed here: we should say the model doesn't really believe anything, it's just doing next-token statistics, and calling that a "belief" is exactly the kind of loose talk Dennett is trying to avoid. So the honest answer is no, an LLM doesn't believe what it says, since a lower-level stance already explains its behavior fully."
