I spent my working life as a field biologist, mostly studying corvid behavior (crows and jays), and only got properly into philosophy after I retired a few years back. I now go to a little reading group at the library on Thursdays, mostly retirees like myself, slowly working through the early modern philosophers. Last week's session on Descartes' Discourse on the Method, Part V, turned into a real argument between two of the regulars, Ruth (a retired social worker) and Gordon (he taught high school English for thirty years), and since nobody could referee it on the spot, I said I'd write the whole exchange up properly and get an outside opinion.

(1) Ruth opened by saying Descartes' famous "beast machine" doctrine amounts to the claim that animals are unfeeling automatons, and that this is supposedly the very view that let later Cartesian vivisectionists shrug off a dissected dog's yelping as just noise from a machine -- so whatever it's fine print says, the doctrine is monstrous and did real damage. (2) Gordon pushed back that this is the textbook caricature: what Descartes actually denies animals, in the Discourse and even more clearly in his letters -- especially to Henry More, the Cambridge Platonist he was arguing with -- is thought in his technical sense, a reflective, language-using rational soul, not sensation as such. He pointed out that Descartes compares animal behavior to the many things our own bodies do with no input from the mind at all (blushing, digesting, flinching before we've "decided" to), which suggests animals might have organ-driven passions with nobody home, so to speak, rather than literally nothing going on. (3) Ruth answered that this concession doesn't help much in practice: in that same correspondence, Descartes says -- if I've got the gist right -- that his view conveniently clears us of any guilt in killing and eating animals, unlike the scrupulous Pythagoreans, which only makes sense if whatever "sensation" he grudgingly grants them isn't the kind of thing that could impose a moral claim on us; so the sophisticated reading still lands in the same place as the caricature. (4) Gordon said that conflates two seperate questions -- what animals metaphysically have, versus what morally follows from it -- and Descartes could simply be wrong about the second without it being true that he asserted the crude version of the first that gets handed around in textbooks. He also thought the actual argument for denying animals reason -- the twin test earlier in Part V, where flexible language use and general adaptability are treated as the only reliable marks of a thinking thing, right down to the detail that people born deaf spontaneously invent their own signs while a parrot can be taught to say words without ever showing it's thinking about what it says -- deserves to be judged on its own terms rather than dismissed by association. (5) Ruth granted the distinction but said the argument fails on its own terms anyway now: corvids and apes routinely solve novel problems they were never trained on, and vervet monkeys give acoustically distinct alarm calls for different predators, which looks a lot like exactly the flexible, general-purpose responsiveness Descartes bet no animal could ever show (which, full disclosure, is exactly my old wheelhouse, though I made myself stay out of it). (6) Gordon started to answer that tool use and alarm calls are still narrow, task-specific competences, nothing like the open-ended business of responding appropriately to anything at all said in one's presence -- but we had to break for tea before he got to finish, and by the next meeting everybody had half lost the thread.

Nobody switched sides, and my own sense is that they're each onto something and each missing something, though forty years of watching crows didn't exactly train me to referee arguments about crows. Could you go through moves (1) to (5) and, for each one, say how right or wrong it is -- old habits die hard, so if you want to score them the way I used to grade lab notebooks, -2 for clearly mistaken up to +2 for clearly right, that would suit me fine, followed by a sentence or two of justification each time? Then, separately: how do you think Gordon's cut-off rebuttal in (6) should actually be completed, and does it end up working? And is "sensation without thought" -- feeling that isn't felt by anybody, so to speak -- even a coherent thing to attribute to an animal, or is that just a fancier way of restating what Ruth accused Descartes of back in (1)?
