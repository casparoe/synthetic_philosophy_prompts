I've been a reader of LessWrong and Overcoming Bias since roughly the Sequences era, and, like most people who cut their teeth in those circles, I met Robert Aumann first through the agreement theorem — the result Robin Hanson leans on constantly in his writing about why honest truth-seekers shouldn't be able to agree to disagree. It was only last month, chasing footnotes down an old Slate Star Codex comment thread, that I came across a far less-discussed 1995 paper of his, which proves that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in any finite game of perfect information. The centipede game is the standard illustration: two players who could keep handing an ever-growing pot back and forth are instead supposed to unravel the whole game from the very last move backward, so that the first player defects immediately even though both would obviously do better by cooperating for a few more rounds. What I hadn't appreciated until now is how much ink has been spilled over whether that theorem actually explains anything about rational play, or simply smuggles in its conclusion through the way the setup is framed.

The responses I've managed to piece together turn out to be genuinely different moves rather than restatements of the same objection. Aumann himself has defended the result by treating rationality, and common knowledge of it, as dispositional facts about the players that hold even at nodes the theorem itself says will never be reached, and has accused critics of applying a double standard they would never apply to ordinary equilibrium analysis. Binmore has answered that this is exactly what makes the theorem vacuous: common knowledge of rationality cannot be doing real explanatory work at a node whose very reachability already refutes it. Philip Reny went further, showing that in some extensive-form games common knowledge of rationality at every node isn't merely awkward but strictly impossible to construct in any coherent epistemic model. Pettit and Sugden, and separately Bicchieri, relocated the paradox inside the game itself: a player who has just watched an opponent "irrationally" deviate has no theory-internal license to keep treating that opponent as rational for the rest of play, which quietly undercuts the very reasoning that was supposed to justify defecting one move earlier. Ben-Porath showed the result is strangely fragile to a seemingly bookkeeping choice, disappearing once the factive "knowledge" operator is weakened to mere common probability-one belief. Stalnaker tried to locate the real work in an explicit belief-revision policy for off-path surprises, and showed that different, equally rational-looking policies can instead rationalize forward-induction-style cooperation. And McKelvey and Palfrey mostly stepped outside the a priori dispute altogether, showing experimentally that almost nobody actually defects immediately and fitting the data with a model of noisy decision-making plus uncertainty about how altruistic one's opponent is.

A friend from my old meetup group thinks Aumann's defense is basically unanswerable once you take the counterfactuals seriously; another thinks the whole theorem became a historical curiosity the moment McKelvey and Palfrey ran their experiment.
