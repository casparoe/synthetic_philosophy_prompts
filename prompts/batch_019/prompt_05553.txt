I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and it's only since retiring that I've taken up philosophy in earnest, mainly through a reading group that meets monthly at the local university. At our last session, on the fine-tuning argument, two of the regulars, Renata and Josip, kept arguing well after everyone else had gone home. I've been turning their exchange over in my head ever since, but I don't trust my own verdict on it, so I'd like yours.

Renata opened with the argument in its standard form: the constants governing gravity, the strong nuclear force, and the cosmological constant occupy an extraordinarily narrow range that permits life; chance makes it fantastically unlikely that we'd find ourselves in such a universe, while design makes it just what we should expect. So the fine-tuning favors design over chance.

Josip answered that this overlooks a selection effect: we could only ever find ourselves in a universe whose constants permit observers, so noticing that we're in one tells us nothing, design or otherwise -- exactly as a firing-squad survivor learns nothing from noticing he's still breathing, since he wouldn't be around to notice anything if the shots had landed.

Renata replied that this runs two different questions together. Why some observer or other finds itself in a life-permitting universe is indeed explained away by the selection effect; why the constants take life-permitting values in the first place is not. The firing-squad survivor, she said, is still entitled to suspect the marksmen missed on purpose, even though, conditional on his survival, of course that is what he observes.

Josip granted the distinction but raised a different problem: weighing chance against design requires a probability distribution over the constants' possible values, and since nothing tells us those values are bounded, let alone which measure to place on them, calling the life-permitting range 'narrow' or 'improbable' is not yet a well-posed claim -- it is an intuition of surprise dressed up as a calculation.

Neither one conceded anything. Could you work through the four moves in order, saying for each whether Renata or Josip has the better of it and why, and close with your own sense of what each of them is still missing once all four moves are on the table?
