A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about what has to be true for two people to be doing something together, in the full sense of "together," rather than each merely doing their own thing in a way that happens to mesh with what the other is doing. The case that started the disagreement was mundane: two coworkers divide a task so cleanly, and trust each other's competence so completely, that they barely discuss it at all, each one simply doing their part because it is obviously in their interest to do so, given that the other is doing theirs. I wanted to say that this does not yet amount to "acting together" in any interesting sense unless each person's plan is somehow answerable to the other's plan as such, so that something like a shared commitment is doing real work, over and above two private calculations that happen to converge. My friend, who has read far more early modern philosophy than I have, thought this was needless mystification, and cited a passage from Hume's Treatise that she claimed already delivers everything one could want from a theory of cooperation, without any appeal to "shared intention" or "joint commitment."

The passage comes from Book 3, Part 2, Section 2 of the Treatise ("Of the origin of justice and property"; paragraph T 3.2.2.10 in the standard modern numbering), where Hume argues that a convention need not, and typically does not, involve any promise:

"This convention is not of the nature of a promise: For even promises themselves, as we shall see afterwards, arise from human conventions. It is only a general sense of common interest; which sense all the members of the society express to one another, and which induces them to regulate their conduct by certain rules. I observe, that it will be for my interest to leave another in the possession of his goods, provided he will act in the same manner with regard to me. He is sensible of a like interest in the regulation of his conduct. When this common sense of interest is mutually express'd, and is known to both, it produces a suitable resolution and behaviour. And this may properly enough be call'd a convention or agreement betwixt us, tho' without the interposition of a promise; since the actions of each of us have a reference to those of the other, and are perform'd upon the supposition, that something is to be perform'd on the other part. Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, tho' they have never given promises to each other."

Hume goes on, later in the same passage, to say that language and money arise from conventions in exactly the same way, without anyone ever promising anything. Before I take this back to my friend, I would like help working out exactly what is doing the argumentative work here. When Hume says that each party's sense of common interest must be "mutually express'd, and known to both," is that mutual expressing-and-knowing meant to be a substantive extra ingredient, or is it just a description of the fact that each has formed accurate expectations about the other? And when he adds that the rowers' actions "have a reference to those of the other" and are performed "upon the supposition" that the other will reciprocate, has he not already helped himself to something very close to the interlocking, mutually responsive intentions that later writers on shared agency insist on, even while denying that anything as strong as an agreement in the fuller sense is present? I recall, from a seminar I once took, that philosophers such as Michael Bratman and Margaret Gilbert set a considerably higher bar for two people to count as genuinely acting jointly - something involving a standing commitment to support the other if the plan runs into difficulty - and I would find it useful to know whether Hume's rowers clear that bar, or whether they are really just two people rowing near each other for compatible reasons rather than two people rowing together.
