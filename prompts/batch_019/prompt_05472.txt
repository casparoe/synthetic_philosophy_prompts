In the theory of imprecise probabilities, an agent's credal state is represented not by a single probability function but by a set of probability functions, so that a given proposition's probability is pinned down only up to an interval. One of the framework's most discussed and counterintuitive features is dilation: cases in which, whichever outcome of some new piece of evidence actually obtains, conditioning the entire set on that outcome widens the interval assigned to a target proposition rather than narrowing it. Explain, at an intuitive level, how learning something can make an agent's opinion about a proposition strictly less precise no matter how the evidence turns out, and explain why some take dilation to show that imprecise credal states cannot be updated in a way that respects ordinary norms of learning from evidence, while others deny that it reflects any real irrationality.
