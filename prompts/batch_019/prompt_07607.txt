Okay so this has been rattling around in my head since a thread on a rationalist-adjacent forum where someone dropped the Pascal line "justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical" as a total mic-drop about legitimacy, and everyone just nodded along like its self-evident. I finally went and read the actual fragment instead of the recycled pull-quote, and it turns out there's a little cluster of three fragments around it in the Pensées that make the whole thing murkier, and honestly more interesting, than the one-liner suggests. Here's the chunk, Trotter's translation, numbered the way the Brunschvicg edition has it:

297. Veri juris. — We have it no more; if we had it, we should take conformity to the customs of a country as the rule of justice. It is here that, not finding justice, we have found force, etc.

298. Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might and, for this end, make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just.

299. The only universal rules are the laws of the country in ordinary affairs and of the majority in others. Whence comes this? From the might which is in them. Hence it comes that kings, who have power of a different kind, do not follow the majority of their ministers. No doubt equality of goods is just; but, being unable to cause might to obey justice, men have made it just to obey might. Unable to strengthen justice, they have justified might; so that the just and the strong should unite, and there should be peace, which is the sovereign good.

What I can't settle on my own is what Pascal is actually doing across these three, and I'd rather get real help thinking it through than another "here's what the famous quote means" gloss, since I've already got that part from about a dozen quote-of-the-day sites. Two things are tangled up for me. First: is 298 basically a resigned, almost Hobbesian description of how legitimacy actually works -- force wins, we retroactively baptize the winner "just" -- which Pascal seems to more or less bless in 299 by calling the resulting peace "the sovereign good"? Or is something less settled going on, given that elsewhere in the Pensées he's got that whole "reason of effects" bit, where people get sorted by how many times they've flipped their view of custom and rank: ordinary people honor it at face value, the "half-clever" see through it as mere force and get contemptuous (Pascal more or less blames this group for a lot of actual social disorder), and the fully clever circle back around to honoring it outwardly while knowing better inwardly, for the sake of peace. Which of those three stances is fragment 298 actually being written from? It reads to me like it could be any of them depending on how cynically you take that last line.

Second, Pascal's whole project here is a Christian apology, written out of a pretty Augustinian background, so how should this sit next to Augustine's line that with justice removed, kingdoms are just "great robberies"? Augustine's version reads as flat condemnation, but Pascal's fragments just shrug and say uniting force and justice -- i.e. letting the strong count as just -- is what buys you peace, full stop, no condemnation attached. Is that a real disagreement with Augustine, or is Pascal making a narrower, purely descriptive point about how justice has to work among fallen men absent grace, one that doesn't actually contradict Augustine's more theological claim at all? And relatedly (ignore this part if it's a stretch) -- is any of this secretly doing apologetic work, i.e. is Pascal quietly using "purely human justice collapses into force" as a reason you need something outside the human/political order to ground a justice that isn't just force with better PR, or am I overreading an apologetic function into what's really more of a standalone, almost La Rochefoucauld-style political aphorism that happens to be sitting in his notes?
