My grandma Ruth died last week at 89. Around here she was "the bird lady" — ran the county Audubon chapter for something like twenty years, kept a shoebox with a heat lamp in the kitchen for orphaned fledglings, the whole deal. What most people outside the family don't know is that sometime in her seventies she fell down a reading rabbit hole — started, kind of randomly, with Mill's essay "Nature," then Darwin's "devil's chaplain" letter to Hooker, then eventually she was deep into Oscar Horta and Brian Tomasik–type essays on wild animal suffering — and came out the other side genuinely convinced that wild nature is, for most animals most of the time, closer to a horror show than a harmony: starvation, parasites, getting eaten alive, r-selected species where almost every single offspring dies within weeks of being born. She used to get into real arguments about this with her friend Dale at the wildlife refuge, who thought all her "compassionate conservation" talk about individual animals mattering was a nice-sounding way of dodging basic ecology — his whole thing was "let nature take its course, dont go soft and start intervening for individuals" — and Ruth thought that was a moral cop-out dressed up as wisdom.

Before she got too sick to write, she left my mom a note saying she did NOT want anyone at the funeral saying she's "at peace, back among the animals now," or "returned to nature," or anything about the circle of life or nature's harmony, because she'd have found all of that both saccharine and, by her own lights, straightforwardly false. She loved individual animals fiercely precisely because she thought their default situation was bad and worth interrupting whenever she personally could reach one. I'm giving the eulogy this Saturday to about a hundred people — most have never heard the phrase "wild animal suffering" in their life and just knew her as the sweet lady with the finch feeders, but Dale's gonna be sitting right there in the third row, plus a couple of her rehab-clinic friends who are just as read into all this as she was. Can you write it for me? It has to be honest to what she actually believed, it can't strawman Dale's side either, it can't turn into a lecture on population dynamics, and it still has to land as comforting instead of bleak for a room full of people who came to grieve, not to have nature documentaries ruined for them.
