I'm a software engineer and sit on the Model Risk Committee at the consumer-lending fintech I work for -- the body that has to sign off before any new underwriting model goes into production. Our data science team wants to replace our credit-default model with one that, for applicants who fall into regions of the feature space where training data is thin or where several reasonable submodels disagree sharply, returns an interval or small "credal set" of default probabilities (something like "somewhere between 4% and 35%") instead of a single confident-looking number like "17.2%." Their argument is that the current model's precision is itself a kind of dishonesty: in those thin-data regions we have no real grounds for preferring 17.2% to 12% or to 25%, so reporting one number manufactures a false sense of exactness -- the same instinct that makes people balk at assigning one sharp probability to an Ellsberg urn whose composition is genuinely unknown.

Compliance and our risk officer are pushing back hard, and I've spent a couple of weekends trying to get up to speed on the underlying issue -- imprecise probabilities, credal sets, some of Walley's original work on the subject -- since I have no formal background here and would rather follow the actual argument than just defer to whoever sounds most confident in the room. Compliance's objection is mostly operational: our loss-reserve calculations and our adverse-action notices to rejected applicants both require a single point probability, so somebody downstream has to collapse the interval into one number anyway, which just relocates the false precision rather than removing it. Our risk officer's objection runs deeper: she points out that an interval wide enough to almost always contain the eventual outcome is nearly impossible to prove wrong, which she worries would let us quietly dodge accountability whenever the model performs badly, and she's circulated Adam Elga's argument for why rational credences should always be sharp, which, as I understand it, shows that genuinely imprecise credences can license a sequence of individually reasonable decisions that leaves you guaranteed worse off than some alternative, no matter how things turn out. She thinks that's exactly the trap we'd be building into a pipeline that makes many linked lending decisions over time rather than a single one-off bet.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the full committee before next Tuesday's meeting? I need a clear recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with conditions -- on whether we deploy the imprecise-probability model, backed by reasoning specific enough that either the data science team or compliance could push back on a particular step rather than just the bottom line. If you recommend deployment in any form, say concretely how the interval gets turned into the single number our downstream systems and regulators require, and by what rule; and whichever way you come down, engage seriously with the strongest objection on the losing side rather than waving it away.
