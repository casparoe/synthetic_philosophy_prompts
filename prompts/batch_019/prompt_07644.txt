A few months ago I reported irregularities I had found in my employer's accounts to our external auditor. It was clearly the right thing to do, but I already knew that regulatory whistleblower programs sometimes pay out a share of whatever is recovered, and I'll admit that knowledge was part of what got me to actually follow through, alongside the plain wrongness of what I had found. When I described the situation to a friend, she said that having the money in the mix meant the act did not really have moral worth, only rightness. That objection has stayed with me longer than I expected, and I would like to see the dispute argued out properly rather than settled by gut feeling.

Write a dialogue between a Kantian, who holds — in the spirit of Kant's shopkeeper example and Barbara Herman's refinement of the motive-of-duty requirement through "rules of moral salience" — that full moral worth requires duty to be the action's governing motive even when other motives are present, and a critic, in the spirit of Nomy Arpaly's discussion of Huckleberry Finn, who denies that acting from duty or from any consciously held correct principle is necessary, holding instead that what matters is direct responsiveness to whatever features make an act right. Have the Kantian press the critic on whether this kind of unprincipled responsiveness is reliable in cases where sympathy or self-interest would point the wrong way, and have the critic press back on whether "rules of moral salience" does any independent work or merely redescribes reasons-responsiveness in the vocabulary of duty. At some point, have both apply their view to a case structurally like mine — an act that is right, done partly from recognition of that fact and partly from a foreseen personal payoff — and let it emerge whether they actually disagree about that case's verdict or only about how to describe it. Neither should concede outright, but the exchange should end with the real point of disagreement stated plainly.
