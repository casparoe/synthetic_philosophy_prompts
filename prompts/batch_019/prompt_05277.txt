For an independent research project in my high school's philosophy elective, I've been reading through Eliezer Yudkowsky's original write-up on "Coherent Extrapolated Volition" (CEV) — the proposal that a sufficiently advanced AI shouldn't just act on what people currently say they want, but on an idealized version of their wishes: roughly, what we would want if we knew more, could reason more clearly, were more the people we wish we were, and had had more time to grow together. When I brought this up with my philosophy teacher, she pointed out that CEV is really a machine-learning-era descendant of older "idealized preference" or "full information" theories of a person's own good, where philosophers such as Richard Brandt and Peter Railton argued that what is good for you is whatever your fully rational, fully informed counterpart would want on your behalf. While reading around this (including the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on well-being), I ran into an objection that has stuck with me: if the idealized version of a person is different enough from who they actually are, in what sense are its preferences still that person's own? It seems to create a dilemma — idealize too little, and you haven't corrected for the ignorance and bias you were trying to filter out in the first place; idealize enough to actually fix those defects, and you may end up with something closer to a stranger's preferences wearing the original person's name.

I would like to see this tension argued out properly rather than just described. Could you write a dialogue — imagine it as two philosophers debating the point at a small workshop on AI alignment — between one character who defends using idealized or extrapolated preferences (treat the CEV version and the "full information" version as close enough to count as the same underlying move) as the correct standard for a person's good, and a second character who presses the objection that sufficiently strong idealization severs the tie to the actual person, so that the result can no longer speak for their good at all. Please make the two equally capable, and have them genuinely respond to each other's specific moves rather than restating their opening positions: at some point I'd like one of them to force the issue with a concrete case (say, someone whose fully idealized self would end up with values or commitments their present self would be horrified by) and have the other actually engage with that case rather than deflecting it. I'd also like one exchange devoted to whether the "coherent" part of CEV — merging many people's idealized volitions into a single target — makes the underlying problem worse, since people who don't currently disagree about much might still turn out to have idealized selves that never converge. By the end, I don't need them to resolve anything, but I would like it to be clear whether their disagreement is really about a contingent, empirical matter (how much idealization tends to change a person in practice) or about something more basic (what it even takes for a set of preferences to count as belonging to someone in the first place) — I suspect a lot of arguments about CEV quietly assume an answer to that second question without ever addressing it directly.
