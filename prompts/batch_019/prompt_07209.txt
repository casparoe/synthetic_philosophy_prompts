For an article I am writing on the decision theory of big life choices, I sat in on the Q&A after a workshop talk on transformative experience. The speaker had argued that a choice such as whether to become a parent resists ordinary expected-utility reasoning because, in L. A. Paul's terms, the relevant experience is both "epistemically transformative" (you cannot know in advance what it is actually like) and "personally transformative" (living through it reshapes your core preferences). A commenter in the audience pushed back, and the exchange went roughly as follows.

1. He said the problem dissolves once you allow imprecise credences: you do not need to know exactly what parenthood is like, only to hold a range of credences over a rough space of possible experiences -- informed by testimony, memoirs, or research on affective forecasting -- and then apply a standard rule for choice under such imprecision, such as maximin or a Hurwicz-style weighted average of best and worst cases.

2. She replied that this move presupposes you already have the right space of outcomes to be imprecise over. Her actual point, she said, was that before undergoing the transformation you may lack the very concepts needed to carve up the space of possible experiences correctly, so introducing imprecise credences just relocates the mystery rather than resolving it.

3. He answered that this proves too much: every choice involves some unconceived possibilities, since no real decision problem ever specifies the fully detailed "grand world" that Savage contrasted with the tractable small worlds we actually reason within -- so if unconceived possibilities defeat rational choice here, they defeat it everywhere, which is absurd.

4. She said the cases are not symmetric. In an ordinary decision, the categories used to sort outcomes stay fixed while credence over them is imprecise; here, it is the categories themselves -- the evaluative standpoint from which the outcome would eventually be judged -- that the transformation is liable to rewrite. That, she argued, is a different, and deeper, kind of uncertainty than simply not knowing where you will land within a fixed space of possibilities.

Neither gave ground, and the moderator moved to the next question.
