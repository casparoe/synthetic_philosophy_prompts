So I've been trying to teach myself some economics/decision theory type stuff at night, mostly because running my hardware store feels like one long series of bets and I figured I should understand the betting part better. A couple weeks ago I fell down a rabbit hole on something called the "Ellsberg paradox." The classic version is something like: you've got one jar where you're told exactly how many red vs black balls are in it (say it's an even split), and a second jar where the red/black mix could be anything at all and nobody will tell you. Almost everybody would rather bet on the first jar, even in situations where you can't really justify that with any actual probability you'd assign to the second jar - like your preference doesn't line up with thinking either color is more likely in there. They call this "ambiguity aversion," and apparently it's supposed to be a different thing from just plain old risk aversion. (Random tangent but apparently this is the same Daniel Ellsberg who leaked the Pentagon Papers years later - small world.)

Anyway this hit way too close to home, because I think it's basically why I've been dragging my feet for two years on taking my store online instead of just opening a second physical location. With a second store in a similar neighborhood I've basically got the odds - foot traffic counts, what my competitors pull in, all of it. Going online feels like the mystery jar: shipping costs, return rates, ad algorithms, I don't have a solid number for any of it, even though a couple people whose judgement I trust think the expected payoff online is probably just as good or better. So could you lay out the most important implications of the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion, once you actually take them seriously, for someone facing decisions like mine? Like, is this just a bias I should be actively overriding because it's quietly costing me money, or is there a genuinely rational case for treating "the odds are unknown" as worse than "the odds are bad," even when the expected value math comes out looking similar? And does whatever the answer is scale up at all - does this paradox explain anything about why entire markets seem to punish new, hard-to-price stuff way more than old, familiar risks that are arguably just as dangerous?
