I'm a PhD student in theoretical computer science working on denotational semantics, where constructing the least fixed point of a monotone operator over a complete partial order is a bread-and-butter technique. I've recently been working through Kripke's "Outline of a Theory of Truth," and the formal construction feels like home turf: build up the extension and anti-extension of "true" under the strong Kleene valuation scheme, take the least fixed point, and watch the Liar sentence land in the extension gap instead of forcing a contradiction. What I don't yet have a real feel for is the so-called revenge problem. Informally: why can't the very fact that the Liar sentence is gappy be stated inside the object language itself, as "the Liar sentence is not true," without regenerating the paradox — when that looks like exactly the resource the construction needs in order to describe its own result? I'd like an intuitive, rather than fully formal, walkthrough of where a concrete revenge sentence breaks the construction, plus a sense of whether Gupta and Belnap's revision-theoretic verdict that the Liar is merely "unstable" is genuinely less vulnerable to the same kind of revenge, or whether it just relocates the problem one level up.
