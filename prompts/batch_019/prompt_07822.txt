Since retiring from a career in experimental physics I've fallen down a bit of a philosophy rabbit hole, mostly the formal/probabilistic end of things since the math feels like home turf. A few of us old colleagues run an informal betting pool on politics and sports, and we've always priced conditional claims like "if the incumbent looses her primary, she wins the general anyway" by just plugging in our best guess at the conditional probability P(wins general | loses primary) — I definately thought that was just the obviously correct way to do it. Then I read about David Lewis's triviality results, and apparently he proved you can't equate the probability of a conditional with the corresponding conditional probability in general (only in degenerate cases), which seems to kind of pull the rug out from under what we've been doing this whole time without any of us noticing. So: can you lay out the most important implications of Lewis's triviality results here? Specifically, does it mean our conditional bets don't really have determinate truth conditions at all, and should that change how we actually price and settle them — or is this more a technical curiosity that doesn't really bite in practice?
