About a year ago I got into an argument with a friend about inheritance tax that sent me down a rabbit hole into Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* — I have no formal background in philosophy at all, but I ended up reading the whole thing on my own over a few months, and the Wilt Chamberlain passage in particular has stuck with me ever since. My nephew is now taking an introductory political philosophy course, and since he knows I've been going on about this book at family dinners, he asked me to look over a practice answer he wrote for a take-home question before he hands in the real version next week. I don't have the training to trust my own gut reaction here, so I'd rather get a careful, external assessment before I say anything back to him.

The question he was given reads: "In *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, Nozick argues that no patterned principle of distributive justice can be stably maintained without continuous interference in people's lives. Using the Wilt Chamberlain example, reconstruct this argument, and then explain how a defender of patterned justice (such as G. A. Cohen) might respond to it. Does the response succeed in showing that patterned principles can survive Nozick's challenge?"

Here is what he wrote:

"Nozick's entitlement theory says a distribution of holdings is just if it arises from a just starting point through legitimate steps: acquisition, voluntary transfer, and rectification of past injustice. This is a historical principle, unlike 'patterned' or 'end-state' principles, which judge a distribution only by its structure at a given time — for instance, strict equality, or shares handed out according to need or merit — regardless of how that structure came about.

The Wilt Chamberlain example is supposed to show that any such pattern will be upset by people's free choices, and that stopping this requires unjust interference. Say a society starts at distribution D1, which matches whatever pattern you like, such as equality. Chamberlain, a basketball star, then signs a contract so that everyone who watches him play puts an extra twenty-five cents of their ticket price directly into a box for him. A million fans do this over a season because they enjoy watching him, so he ends up with $250,000, far more than anyone else, giving us D2, which breaks the pattern. Nozick's claim is that D2 is just anyway, since every step from D1 to D2 was a voluntary transfer that violated nobody's rights. So if the pattern is to be kept, the state has to either forbid these exchanges or keep clawing back Chamberlain's earnings afterward — in Nozick's words, liberty upsets patterns, and maintaining a pattern means constantly interfering in people's lives.

Cohen's reply is that this is too quick. He points out that a one-off tax on what Chamberlain ends up with doesn't stop anyone's choice to attend the games or hand over their quarter — the transfers happen exactly as before. All that is removed is some of what Chamberlain holds afterward, which is a much smaller intrusion than an outright ban on the transfers themselves. Cohen also notes that Nozick's own framework already restricts liberty in the same way, since even his minimal state has to be funded by taxation that takes money from people without their individual consent, just like any patterned scheme would. So Nozick has no principled basis for treating the liberty to keep everything you receive as more sacred than the liberties his own state already overrides, which means the Wilt Chamberlain argument only shows that patterns cost something in liberty, not that this cost is unjust — so it doesn't really refute Rawls-style theories, it just means they, too, owe us a justification for the restrictions they impose.

I think Cohen's response works: since Nozick concedes his own state needs taxation, he can't turn around and object to Rawlsian redistribution purely on the grounds that it interferes with liberty, so the Wilt Chamberlain argument doesn't establish much more than the truism that every theory of justice restricts somebody's liberty somehow."
