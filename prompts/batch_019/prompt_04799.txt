My friend Priya and I got into a whole thing this weekend about, of all people, Kurt Gödel's religious views. It started because she'd read some piece (Rebecca Goldstein's book maybe? or a blog post, I honestly don't remember) mentioning that Godel believed in an afterlife on basically rationalist grounds - something like, the world is fundamentally rational and everything has a sufficient reason behind it, so it would be almost absurd for a mind as intricate as a human personality to just get snuffed out after 80-odd years. I think there was some analogy in there to nature not bothering to fully develop something only to throw it away, but I might be mangling the actual comparison. Priya's take was that this is just Godel being a total crank outside of math, dressed up in fancy Leibniz-y language, and that there's no real arguement there at all, just wishful thinking from a guy who was already having paranoid episodes toward the end of his life.

I pushed back, because I recall he actually wrote up his core philosophical commitments as an explicit list at some point - things like "the world is rational," "materialism is false," "there is incomparably more knowable a priori than is currently known" - and that the afterlife claim falls out of that whole rationalist package rather than being some random standalone belief. That's when it got heated: Priya said fine, but isn't it kind of self-undermining, maybe even hypocritical, for the guy who literally proved incompleteness - that no sufficiently strong consistent formal system can prove every true statement about arithmetic - to also be so committed to the idea that everything has a reason and the world is fully intelligible? Doesn't incompleteness basically poison that whole rationalist well? I said no, and that I think Godel himself would've argued incompleteness actually supports his rationalism rather than undermining it, since it shows truth outruns any single formal system, which is central to his case against minds being mechanical, not evidence against the world being intelligible. Neither of us could actually nail the argument down well enough to settle it, and it ended in a stalemate.

So: could you reconstruct Godel's rationalist case for an afterlife as carefully as you can, in terms of its actual logical shape and not just "genius believed weird stuff," and then adjudicate the incompleteness question for us? Is Priya right that there's a real tension between his "everything has a sufficient reason" rationalism and what incompleteness shows, or is there a way they're actually compatible, or even mutually reinforcing, once you're careful about what "reason" and "provable" are doing in each context? Please actually pick a side rather than just presenting both views neutrally - that's kind of what we need to settle this.
