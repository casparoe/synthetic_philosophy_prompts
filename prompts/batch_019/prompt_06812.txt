A friend and I are both taking a seminar on practical reason this term, and last night, fueled by too much coffee, we got into a long argument about internal versus external reasons for acting — specifically, about how much Bernard Williams's famous distinction actually owes to Hume. My friend's position was that Williams's internalism is really just old Humean wine in new bottles, dressed up with talk of a "subjective motivational set" and "sound deliberation" but committed to exactly the same view about reason and motivation that Hume had already laid out two and a half centuries earlier. I wasn't convinced, but I couldn't quite say why, and we went to bed without settling it.

The passage she kept returning to comes near the end of Book II, Part III, Section III of the Treatise, where Hume writes that "reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them." A few lines later he adds: "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger. 'Tis not contrary to reason for me to chuse my total ruin, to prevent the least uneasiness of an Indian or person wholly unknown to me." A little further on, though, Hume allows one qualification: a passion, or the action it grounds, can still be called "unreasonable" in a secondary sense, when it rests on a false belief that some object exists, or when it settles on means that simply will not achieve the end actually in view.

What I'd like help with is this: does Williams's internal reasons thesis really inherit Hume's strong claim that reason is inert and can, at most, correct errors of fact or of means — or does his notion of "sound deliberation" license something Hume's own escape clauses do not, such as an agent coming to have a genuinely new motivation, not merely a more efficient route to an old one, through imaginative reflection on her existing motivational set? If there is a real gap here, could you spell it out with a concrete case where Hume's framework and Williams's would hand down different verdicts about whether someone has a reason to act? And does that gap bear at all on the standard complaint that internalism struggles to explain what reason, if any, a cruel or self-deceived person has to change?
