In my graduate seminar on metametaphysics this term, we are spending two sessions on the question of when a metaphysical dispute is "merely verbal," and I have been trying to find a clean way to present David Chalmers's proposal in "Verbal Disputes" before the students take on the primary reading themselves. The case study we keep returning to is the dispute over unrestricted composition: universalists hold that for any objects whatsoever, however scattered or gerrymandered, there is some further object that is their fusion, while their opponents deny this, typically restricting composition to some more special condition on the parts (organic unity, causal integration, and so on). Eli Hirsch wants to diagnose this dispute, and a good many others in analytic metaphysics, as merely verbal, on the grounds that the rival camps are simply employing different but equally legitimate meanings of the existential quantifier: this is his "quantifier variance" thesis. Ted Sider denies this; he thinks that at most one candidate meaning of "exists" actually carves reality at its joints (what he elsewhere calls a privileged, joint-carving quantifier, or "Ontologese"), so that the dispute, appearances notwithstanding, is substantive.

What I would like help with is this. Chalmers does not want to simply borrow Hirsch's quantifier-variance thesis in order to call the composition dispute verbal; he tries to supply an independent, more general test for verbality, one meant to apply to disputes well outside ontology as well. Could you explain, in a way that would make sense to a bright student who has not yet read the paper, what that general test actually comes to, and why satisfying it is supposed to be a substantially stronger result than merely observing that the two sides "mean different things" by their words? I would like the intuitive, gut-level version of the idea first, before any formal apparatus, followed by a careful walk-through of how the test is meant to apply, step by step, to the composition case specifically.

Finally, and this is the part my students most reliably get wrong, I would like you to pin down exactly where the residual disagreement between Hirsch and Sider re-enters once the test has been run: is it that Sider thinks the test's conditions are not actually satisfied in this case, or does he grant that they are satisfied and relocate the substantive dispute one level up, into a further disagreement about which candidate quantifier meaning we ought to privilege? Getting that distinction right, rather than just asserting that Chalmers "basically agrees with Hirsch," is exactly what I am struggling to convey clearly to them.
