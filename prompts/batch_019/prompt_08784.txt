Question (worth 20 points, written as a 45-minute timed response):
"Newton devoted more manuscript pages over his lifetime to theology and Biblical chronology than to physics, yet almost none of this material was published while he was alive. Drawing on both his private writings and his public scientific works, evaluate the claim that Newton's antitrinitarian convictions were continuous with, rather than merely incidental to, his natural philosophy. Your answer should address: (a) one concrete episode in which Newton's private theological views had public consequences for his career; (b) the theological argument advanced in the General Scholium added to the second edition of the Principia; and (c) at least one specific way in which Newton's conception of God's relation to the physical universe generated a dispute with a contemporary natural philosopher."

Student response:
"Newton's theological heterodoxy was not a private hobby walled off from his science; it shaped his career and his physics alike, and the record suggests continuity rather than coincidence.

The clearest career consequence came in the 1670s. As a fellow of Trinity College, Newton was obliged by the college statutes to take holy orders within seven years of receiving his MA or else vacate his fellowship. Newton's private study of early Church history had convinced him that the doctrine of the Trinity was a corruption introduced by Athanasius and ratified at Nicaea rather than a teaching of the primitive Church, and he could not in conscience be ordained into a church that required him to affirm it. Rather than lose his position, he obtained a royal dispensation from Charles II in 1675 that permanently excused the holder of the Lucasian professorship from the ordination requirement. He pursued the same conviction on paper: around 1690 he sent John Locke a manuscript arguing that two of the New Testament's clearest prooftexts for the Trinity, 1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16, were later scribal corruptions rather than part of the original text, though he got cold feet and the piece was not published in his lifetime. This is of a piece with his Socinianism: on his view, Christ was a creature with no existence prior to his human birth, so no formula that made Christ 'very God of very God' could be true, whether in a church's articles or in scripture itself.

Newton's public works pursue the same commitments at a more guarded distance. The General Scholium, appended to the Principia's second edition in 1713, argues from the exquisite order of the solar system to an intelligent, ruling agent, and insists this Being is properly called 'Lord God' on account of his dominion, not merely 'God' in some abstract sense — a pointed rejection of any reading on which God is merely the world's soul. In the same Scholium, Newton floats the idea that space itself might be the 'sensorium' of this God, the medium by which an omnipresent being perceives things immediately, without needing any further organ of sense.

Finally, this picture of an active, governing deity put Newton at odds with Leibniz. Newton held that the mutual gravitational pull of the planets would gradually destabilize their orbits unless God intervened now and then to set the system right. Leibniz seized on this in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke, arguing that a God who had to keep repairing his own creation was no better than a workman who had built a defective clock — a critique that only bites because Newton's God is a governor still at work in the world, not a deist first cause who winds it up and walks away."
