I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and I've somehow ended up in charge of drafting questions for our program's consciousness-studies colloquium, which is hosting a guest next month who works within the Tononi-Koch tradition and has published extensively in defense of Integrated Information Theory. I got interested in IIT through a seminar on the neuroscience of consciousness last year, and what strikes me most about it, compared to the other theories on that syllabus, is how openly its defenders accept conclusions that would sink almost any other scientific theory -- which is exactly what I want the interview to dig into rather than paper over.

Having listened to a few of this guest's previous podcast and Q&A appearances, I've noticed two moves that come up whenever she's pushed on the theory's weak points. When someone raises Scott Aaronson's old objection -- that you can construct simple, functionally inert systems out of grids of logic gates or expander-graph-like wiring that nonetheless have enormous Φ -- she tends to just bite the bullet and say our intuitions about which systems "deserve" consciousness are irrelevant to the theory's actual content. And when someone raises the unfolding argument -- that any recurrent network can be unfolded into a feedforward network with identical input-output behavior but drastically lower Φ -- she tends to reply that IIT was never a theory of function to begin with, so matching behavior was never something it owed anyone.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's likely to give, that don't just let those two replies stand unexamined? I want at least one question that targets the exclusion postulate directly, since that's the postulate doing the real work of blocking the more embarrassing duplication-of-subjects worries that a naive reading of Φ-maximization would otherwise generate; at least one that presses on the bullet-biting response to Aaronson's construction without settling for a repeated assertion that intuitions aren't evidence; and at least one that pushes on the "not a theory of function" reply by asking what, if not behavior, is supposed to fix which physical systems' causal structures are even worth computing Φ over in the first place. I'd like the sequence to close with a question that asks her to specify, concretely, what finding -- empirical or conceptual -- she would accept as counting against the theory, framed so that simply pointing to the perturbational-complexity-index studies from the anesthesia and disorders-of-consciousness literature doesn't suffice as an answer, since those studies were never really designed to test the theory's more distinctive commitments.
