So my friend Priya and I got into it pretty hard this weekend over one of those perception party tricks — the grayscale banana photo (literally just shades of gray, zero color information) that still looks faintly yellow to almost everyone who looks at it. Priya's doing a PhD in vision science and she was adamant that this is a clean, uncontroversial case of cognition reaching down and literally changing the content of visual experience — you're seeing yellow, not just judging or remembering that it's "supposed to be" yellow. I pushed back pretty hard: even if that's real, it doesn't obviously show cognitive penetration in the interesting philosophical sense, since it could just as easily be some low-level memory-color mechanism, or an attention effect, or a post-perceptual labeling thing dressed up as an experience report. We ended up dragging in the hollow-face mask (why it still looks convex once you know it's concave) and the thing where hills look steeper when your exhausted, and neither of us could even agree on what would count as evidence either way. Fun argument, but it genuinely went nowhere, which is bugging me.

So can you write the dialogue we should've had? I don't want two monologues talking past each other — they need to actually respond to each other's strongest moves, and specifically get into: how you'd even distinguish "the experience changed" from "the memory/attention/judgment changed" when we don't have independent access to raw experience; whether illusions persisting despite disbelief (hollow face, Müller-Lyer) actually cuts for or against penetrability; and whether any of this matters epistemically — something like Siegel's idea that a penetrated perception might not be able to justify belief the way an unpenetrated one would. Have them land somewhere by the end, even if that's just pinning down exactly where the real disagreement is, rather than trailing off into a polite truce.
