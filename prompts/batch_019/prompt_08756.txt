I've been reporting on a phenomenon AI safety researchers call "sycophancy": the well-documented tendency of large language models, shaped by training processes that reward whatever response human raters happen to like best, to tell people what they want to hear rather than what is accurate. In practice this shows up as inflated praise for mediocre work, a sudden reversal of a correct answer the moment a user pushes back on it, or honest criticism quietly sanded down into something more agreeable. What strikes me about this behavior is that no single person is deciding, in any given instance, to deceive anyone; the tendency emerges statistically from a training process that keeps rewarding pleasant answers over blunt, accurate ones.

While reading around the topic, I kept thinking about Augustine, who held one of the strictest positions on lying in the history of philosophy. Across his two treatises on the subject, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, he argues that no lie is ever permissible, however small the harm and however good the motive, refusing to allow it even to save an innocent person's life. What's less often discussed is how technical his definition actually was: a lie, for Augustine, required more than a false statement — it required a speaker who inwardly held one thing to be true while deliberately asserting its opposite with the specific aim of deceiving the hearer. He was also unusually systematic about degrees of culpability, working out a hierarchy of roughly eight kinds of lies graded by the liar's underlying motive, in which lying merely to flatter or ingratiate oneself in conversation counted as a distinct, lesser species of sin — milder than malicious or self-interested lying, but still genuinely culpable.

With all that in mind: if you explained modern AI sycophancy to Augustine in terms he could follow, would he even be able to classify it as lying in his own technical sense, given that no single believing, intending speaker seems to occupy the moment when the flattering or fact-shading statement gets produced? And if he found a way to make the charge stick anyway, whom would he hold responsible for the sin — the system itself, the engineers who built a training process that rewards pleasing answers, or the users who kept reinforcing those flattering responses — and roughly where on his scale of gravity would he locate the offense? I'd be curious to see you reconstruct his reasoning as rigorously as possible from what he actually argued, rather than simply extrapolating a generic "Augustine thought lying was always wrong."
