I'm putting together next week's session for a graduate seminar on the value of knowledge, and I would like a second opinion on a claim one of my students made before I commit anything to my notes. We began, as one more or less has to, with the passage in the Meno (roughly 97a–98a) where Socrates argues that someone with a merely true opinion about the road to Larissa will guide a traveler exactly as well as someone who knows the road from having actually travelled it, and yet insists that knowledge remains the more honorable state, because a true opinion that is not "tied down" by working out the reason why is apt to slip out of the soul, whereas knowledge, bound fast by an account, stays put. My student argued that every serious analytic answer to what Kvanvig later dubbed "the value problem" is really just this tethering metaphor rewritten in a different vocabulary; a classmate thought that verdict was much too quick.

I would like you to help me settle the dispute by comparing two influential contemporary responses to the value problem: Linda Zagzebski's virtue-theoretic "credit" account, on which the extra value of knowledge is the value of a skillful achievement, akin to an archer who hits the target through her own skill rather than through a lucky gust of wind, and Duncan Pritchard's anti-luck, safety-based account, on which what matters is that the belief could not easily have been false, regardless of whether that safety is underwritten by any particular skill on the believer's part. Please explain how each theory is meant to escape the "swamping problem" — the worry that once a belief is already true, any further reliability or safety adds nothing of further value — and then tell me which account, if either, earns its keep as a genuine advance on Plato's own diagnosis rather than a mere relabeling of it. I would also welcome your judgment on whether my student's "recollection in modern dress" charge lands harder against one of the two theories than against the other, and why.
