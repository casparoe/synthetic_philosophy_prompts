Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through Hobbes's *De Corpore*, and Part III, on geometry, has given me an idea for a short essay. Before I try to write it up properly, I would welcome your comments on the following notes.

The core of Hobbes's account is that geometrical objects are defined genetically, through motion: a point is a body whose extension he tells us to disregard as insensibly small, a line is what such a moving point leaves behind, a surface what a moving line leaves behind, and a solid what a moving surface leaves behind. He treats this constructive procedure as the reason geometry can be fully demonstrative: since we ourselves generate the figures according to our own definitions, there is nothing hidden in them for us to be wrong about, in the way we can be wrong about the causes of natural bodies we did not make. He draws the same moral for civil philosophy, since the commonwealth, too, is an artefact of human construction, and so ought to be equally demonstrable.

What makes this interesting to me is the sequel. From 1655, when John Wallis attacked the geometry of *De Corpore* in his *Elenchus Geometriae Hobbianae*, through Hobbes's own *Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematics* the following year, and on almost to his death in 1679, Hobbes kept publishing "solutions" to squaring the circle, duplicating the cube, and trisecting the angle, and kept refusing to accept that Wallis and others had refuted them. My tentative thesis is that this stubbornness is not incidental to Hobbes's philosophy of geometry but almost predicted by it: if certainty rests on being the author of the construction rather than on any external check, then a critic who finds an error in your figure can only look, to you, like someone who has failed to understand it.

I would be grateful for your comments on two things: first, whether I have Hobbes's actual position, and the outline of the Wallis dispute, right; and second, whether you think the thesis in my last paragraph is a genuine insight or a piece of overreaching psychologizing that the historical record does not really support.
