A friend and I spent the better part of a long drive back from a workshop on AI alignment arguing about the Löbian obstacle to self-trust. Her position was fairly deflationary: that once you strip away the framing, all Löb's theorem really shows is that a sufficiently strong formal system can't grant itself a blanket "whatever I prove must be true" license, and that treating this as a deep barrier to AI systems ever building successors they can fully trust reads far more into a narrow result about provability predicates than is warranted. I thought that undersold it considerably, but neither of us talked the other out of their view by the time we got home, and I'd like to think it through more carefully with a clearer head.

As it happens, I TA a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and AI safety, and last week's take-home problem (worth 10 points, split 3/3/4) asked students to work through exactly this material:

(a) [3 pts] State Löb's theorem precisely for a formal system S satisfying the standard derivability conditions.
(b) [3 pts] In your own words, explain why the theorem is thought to create an "obstacle" to self-trust for a formal reasoner.
(c) [4 pts] Briefly explain why this matters for an artificial agent that wants to construct, and fully trust, a successor version of itself.

Here is one student's submitted answer in full:

(a) For any formal system S that can express arithmetic and satisfies the Hilbert–Bernays derivability conditions, and for any sentence P in the language of S: if S ⊢ Prov_S(⌈P⌉) → P, then S ⊢ P. Equivalently, S can only prove the conditional "if P is provable then P is true" for sentences P it can already prove outright — it can never get a proof of P "for free" just by proving that its own provability of P would guarantee P. Setting P = ⊥ recovers Gödel's second incompleteness theorem: since Prov_S(⌈⊥⌉) → ⊥ is just a way of expressing Con(S), a consistent S can never prove Con(S), on pain of proving ⊥ itself.

(b) The obstacle is that a formal reasoner cannot adopt, as a general policy about itself, the schema "whenever I prove something, it is true" — even though this sounds like exactly the kind of principle a rational, self-aware reasoner should be able to endorse. If S tried to build in this reflection principle for every P, Löb's theorem says S would have to be able to prove every such P outright, which is far too strong a requirement for any consistent, non-trivial system. So the very obviousness of "if I proved it, it's true" is exactly what a sufficiently expressive formal system can't grant itself in general.

(c) This is the same limitation, discovered by Gödel, that stops a system from certifying its own reliability; applied to AI, it means that an agent cannot mechanically verify, by proof, that everything a successor agent (built to reason at least as powerfully as itself) proves about the successor's safety is automatically true, since being able to do so in full generality would let the original agent prove its own consistency. This is part of why Bostrom argues that advanced AI systems will tend to resist having their goals modified unless the modification is independently verified: an agent can't get an unconditional, proof-based guarantee that a changed version of itself will still be trustworthy, so from the inside, self-modification always carries irreducible risk. MIRI's work on "tiling agents" tries to get around this by having each agent in a chain trust only strictly weaker successors, whose soundness it can prove without running into Löb's theorem, rather than trying to get full self-trust in one step.
