Money-pump arguments are supposed to show that cyclical (non-transitive) preferences are irrational: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a bookie can trade you down the cycle, charging a small fee each round since you always strictly prefer the new option, and hand you back exactly what you started with, minus your money. But Edward McClennen's "resolute choice" move seems to break this: the agent just resolves in advance to refuse the final exploitative trade, so it never actually gets pumped, even though its preferences are still cyclical at each pairwise comparison -- and a "sophisticated" (Strotz-style) chooser dodges it too, just by never entering a sequence it can foresee its future self won't finish.

So does the money pump actually show transitivity is required for rationality, or only that naive, forgetful sequential choosers are irrational, with the real lesson being about diachronic rationality (how choices over time have to hang together) rather then the shape of your preferences at any one moment? Write an essay on this -- take the resolute-choice objection seriously as the strongest form of the challenge, and actually land on a verdict rather than "reasonable people disagree." Something like "the pump only condemns naive choosers" or "resolute choice is cheating and transitivity survives" both count, just defend one (or your own).
