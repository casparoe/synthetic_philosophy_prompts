> Q3. The literature on cluelessness distinguishes between what Hilary Greaves calls "simple" and "complex" cluelessness, developed partly in response to James Lenman's original worry about consequentialism and the unforeseeable, ramifying effects of our actions. (a) Explain this distinction, giving an example of each. (b) Explain why the distinction matters for whether cluelessness constitutes a genuine problem for act consequentialism.

And here, verbatim, is one student's answer:

> Lenman (2000) pointed out that our actions have effects that ramify indefinitely into the future, and that we are usually in no position to know whether these effects are, in total, good or bad. Greaves (2016) tries to save consequentialism from the sharpest form of this worry by distinguishing two kinds of cluelessness. In cases of simple cluelessness, the unforeseeable effects of an act are ones we have no reason to think point in one evaluative direction rather than the other: for every story we can tell about how an act might, via some butterfly-effect chain, lead to a stranger boarding a plane that later crashes, there is an equally compelling story about how it leads to a stranger being spared such a crash. Because these considerations are symmetric, they cancel out in expectation and can simply be set aside. Complex cluelessness arises when we do have some reason to think the balance of unforeseeable effects is not symmetric — Greaves's own example is something like a health or development intervention whose effects on population size could systematically shift the long-run trajectory of civilization for better or worse — but we have no principled way to weigh the competing considerations against each other. This is the more serious problem for consequentialism, since here we cannot simply bracket the unknown effects: expected-value reasoning requires a credence we are not actually able to form. In effect, complex cluelessness is a special case of the wider problem of moral uncertainty, since, unable to weigh the competing long-run considerations against one another, we are uncertain which of the available acts is actually favoured by the correct axiology. Greaves takes this to vindicate Lenman's original diagnosis, concluding that complex cluelessness shows act consequentialism to be false as a normative theory, at least for the enormous range of real-world acts whose long-run effects are complexly clueless.
