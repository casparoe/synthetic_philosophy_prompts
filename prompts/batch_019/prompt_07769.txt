I've spent the last several years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one thing that keeps coming up in that world — LessWrong especially — is common knowledge: Aumann's agreement theorem, the blue-eyed islanders puzzle, and so on. That's actually how I first got interested in the centipede game: it always struck me as the strategic cousin of those epistemic puzzles, since the whole force of the backward-induction argument seems to rest on exactly the same "common knowledge of rationality" move that does the work in the more purely epistemic cases. These days I TA a graduate seminar cross-listed between economics and philosophy on the foundations of game theory, and this week's problem set asked students to work through backward induction in the centipede game, then engage with Aumann's (1995) epistemic reformulation of it and the further claim that the result is somehow "paradoxical."

Question: (a) Using the standard finite two-player centipede game (perfect information, and common knowledge of the extensive form and payoffs), reconstruct the backward-induction argument for why the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium has the first mover taking (defecting) at the very first opportunity, and state how this solution concept relates to Nash equilibrium in this setting. (b) Aumann (1995) argues that the backward-induction outcome can be derived directly from the epistemic assumption of common knowledge of rationality among the players, without treating subgame perfection as a free-standing solution concept imposed from outside. Explain, in your own words, how common knowledge of rationality is supposed to generate the same unraveling conclusion as ordinary backward induction. (c) This result is sometimes described as "the backward induction paradox." Explain what is supposed to be paradoxical about it, and why this is meant to be a different and more interesting worry than the familiar complaint that no real player could ever carry out such a long chain of hypothetical reasoning.

Student's answer:

(a) In the centipede game, two players alternate between "taking" (ending the game and keeping a larger share of the current pot for themselves) and "passing" (letting the pot grow and handing the decision to the other player), for some fixed finite number of rounds. Backward induction starts at the last decision node: whoever moves last does strictly better by taking than by passing, since passing only leads to a fixed terminal payoff that is worse for the mover than taking outright. Given that the last mover will take, the second-to-last mover does better taking immediately than passing and receiving the smaller payoff that comes from the last mover taking one round later. This reasoning unravels backward through the whole tree, so that at every node the mover strictly prefers to take given what backward induction says will happen afterward — including at the very first node. This is the subgame-perfect equilibrium of the game, and since every finite game of perfect information is guaranteed to have a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium constructed in exactly this way, subgame perfection isn't really picking out anything narrower than Nash equilibrium here: in a game of perfect information like this one, the two solution concepts coincide, and the backward-induction outcome is simply the unique Nash equilibrium of the centipede game.

(b) Aumann's argument recasts this in epistemic terms instead of treating subgame perfection as an equilibrium concept imposed on the tree from outside. He models the players' knowledge with a state space and knowledge partitions in the usual way, and shows that if it is common knowledge that both players are rational — meaning each always chooses, at every node they might reach, whatever action maximizes their payoff given their beliefs about the rest of the game — then the players must in fact behave exactly as backward induction prescribes. Intuitively: rationality alone forces the last mover to take; since this is commonly known, a rational penultimate mover must anticipate it and take as well; and the same reasoning propagates backward exactly as in (a). The interesting move is that the unraveling now falls out of an assumption about what the players know about each other's dispositions, rather than being read directly off a solution concept.

(c) The worry is supposed to be more interesting than the everyday observation that no real, resource-bounded person could actually carry out dozens of nested rounds of "I take because you'd take because I'd take because..." reasoning — that would just be a limitation of particular flesh-and-blood reasoners, not a problem with the argument itself. But once you look at what common knowledge of rationality really requires over a long centipede game, the two worries turn out to be closer than they first look: common knowledge is not a single fact but an entire hierarchy of "everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows..." statements, and Aumann's derivation needs every rung of that hierarchy to hold without exception, all the way through a tree with as many nodes as you like. So the paradox is really the complexity objection again, just relocated from the players themselves to the common-knowledge assumption they are supposed to satisfy, which has to somehow guarantee, in one stroke, that an unbounded stack of higher-order beliefs about each other's rationality never breaks down.
