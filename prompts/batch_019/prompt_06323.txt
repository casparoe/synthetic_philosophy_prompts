So my school has this tiny philosophy elective (like 12 people take it lol) and we've been doing a unit on Descartes. My teacher gave us this bonus reading about an even earlier philosopher, Avicenna (Ibn Sina), who apparently came up with something kind of similar almost 600 years before Descartes did. Its called the "flying man" argument and honestly I think its cooler than the cogito, but the handout has it written in this stiff, translated-from-Arabic style and I keep getting stuck on why certain details are even in there. I tried explaining it to my friend at lunch and realized halfway through that I didn't actually get it myself lol. Here's basically the passage (paraphrasing slightly):

"Suppose that one of us is created all at once, fully grown and complete, but with his sight veiled so that he cannot see any external thing, and created floating in open air or in a void, in such a way that he meets no resistance from the air that he would have to feel, and with his limbs spread apart, not touching one another or anything else. Let him then consider whether he can affirm the existence of his own self. He will find that he has no doubt that his self exists, even while he affirms nothing of his limbs, his heart, his brain, or anything external to him. He would still affirm the existence of himself without affirming that it has any length, width, or depth, and if in this state he could picture a hand or some other limb, he would not picture it as being part of his self or as something his self depends on. So the thing whose existence he has affirmed turns out to be distinct from his body and his organs, which he has not affirmed at all."

What I don't get is why Avicenna bothers making the setup SO specific. Like why does it matter that the guy is created instantly as a full-grown adult instead of just being a regular adult who happens to be blindfolded and floating right now? And why does it matter that his limbs specifically can't touch each other or anything else, on top of not being able to see anything? It feels like those details are too weirdly precise to just be flavor text, so they must be doing some actual logical work, but I can't figure out what it is. Also my teacher said this is "not quite the same thing" as the cogito but wouldn't explain more when I asked, so if you could help me understand what this argument actually proves overall - is it a full proof that the mind/soul is a totally separate thing from the body, or is it doing something weaker than that? - and how thats different from what Descartes is up to, that would be clutch.
