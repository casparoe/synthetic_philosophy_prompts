Last week a friend of mine, Priya, who is finishing a PhD in social psychology, and I spent most of a dinner arguing about the "Knobe effect," one of the founding results of experimental philosophy. She thinks the whole result is just a philosopher's rediscovery of something her own field had already settled a decade earlier — which, if true, would mean one of x-phi's showcase discoveries isn't really a discovery about philosophy at all. I disagreed, but not as persuasively as I would have liked at the table, so I wrote up the argument afterward and would like your comments before I send it to her. Here is what I wrote:

Joshua Knobe's original study asked people about a company chairman who adopts a new policy purely to increase profits, remarking that he does not care in the slightest about the policy's foreseen effect on the environment. When that effect is harm, a large majority of respondents say he harmed the environment intentionally. When the very same indifferent chairman adopts a policy that happens to help the environment instead, only a small minority say he helped it intentionally. Nothing about the chairman's state of mind changes between the two versions; only the moral valence of the side effect does. This "side-effect effect" is usually presented as a discovery about the concept of intentional action itself: moral evaluation, it seems, has crept into what looks like a piece of neutral folk psychology.

Priya's objection is that this is not news. A decade before Knobe published, Mark Alicke had already shown, with his "culpable control" model of blame, that a driver's apparent blameworthiness for an accident varies with whether he was speeding home to hide something illicit from his parents or to hide an anniversary present for his wife, even when the road conditions and the accident itself are stipulated to be identical across versions. If motive can retroactively inflate judgments of foreseeability and causal contribution quite generally, why treat the intentionality asymmetry as a special discovery about the word "intentionally," rather than one more instance of a familiar mechanism: we settle on how much blame someone deserves, then adjust whatever supposedly factual judgments make that verdict cohere?

I think the objection proves less than it seems to, for a reason neither of us raised at the table. The asymmetry keeps showing up where blame is hard to locate: it generalizes to verbs with little or no blame content, such as deciding and being in favor of, and even to whether an agent can be said to have known that a side effect would occur, which stretches a blame-validation story past where it comfortably applies. More tellingly, later work aimed squarely at Knobe's result found the same asymmetry when the norm at stake is entirely non-moral: a mere statistical or conventional regularity, with no badness and no one to blame. A blame-validation story predicts the effect should disappear once badness is removed; a norm-violation story predicts it should not. On the evidence, it does not.

So my tentative view is that Knobe found something more general than either side usually credits: what structures judgments of intentional action is departure from a norm, not badness as such, and moral valence simply happens to be the most common real-world source of normative deviation. That is exactly why the effect was first noticed in a moral case, and why it is so easy to mistake for evidence of blame doing the work.

But I can already hear the comeback, and it is the one that worries me most: if norm violation is doing the explanatory work, Priya can simply fold moral badness back in as one species of norm violation and relabel Alicke's model "norm-based validation" rather than "blame-based validation." At that point I am not sure anything survives of my disagreement with her.

I would appreciate your comments on two things in particular: whether I have represented Alicke's and Knobe's results accurately, and whether that last worry is a genuine problem for the position I am defending, or whether there is a principled reason the two accounts do not simply collapse into one another.
