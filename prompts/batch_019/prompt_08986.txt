Decades spent testing hypotheses in the laboratory left me suspicious of debates that turn out to be merely verbal, and a puzzle from my recent reading in epistemology looks like a good test case. Epistemic contextualism and subject-sensitive invariantism (the view associated with Jason Stanley and John Hawthorne, and the leading form of what is usually called pragmatic encroachment) are both motivated by Keith DeRose's "bank case": our willingness to say that someone "knows" the bank will be open on Saturday drops once the cost of being wrong rises, even though the person's evidence remains exactly the same. The two theories, however, locate this shift quite differently: contextualism puts it in the semantic standard fixed by the attributor's own conversational context, whereas subject-sensitive invariantism puts it in the subject's practical situation, which determines whether she actually knows regardless of who is talking about her. Please work through a case that pulls these two loci apart: a speaker in a relaxed, low-stakes conversation ascribes knowledge to someone who, unbeknownst to the speaker, is about to stake a great deal on that very belief. Tell me what each theory predicts about the truth of the speaker's ascription in that scenario, and then, on the strength of that result, whether the two views are substantive rivals or merely notational variants of a single underlying idea.
