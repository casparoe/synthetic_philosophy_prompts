**Question (20 points total).** Fitch's paradox purports to derive, from the "knowability thesis" (every truth is in principle knowable) together with the assumption that some truth is never actually known, the conclusion that every truth is known.
(a) [8 pts] Reconstruct the derivation with enough precision to identify exactly which two epistemic principles, beyond the knowability thesis and the non-omniscience assumption, are doing the logical work.
(b) [6 pts] Explain what the paradox is standardly taken to show about verificationist theories of truth, and why realists about truth are untouched by it.
(c) [6 pts] Neil Tennant's "restriction strategy" avoids the paradox by restricting the knowability thesis rather than abandoning it. Explain the restriction, and assess the common objection that the restriction is merely ad hoc.

**Student's answer.**
"Assume every truth is knowable, and suppose for contradiction that some truth p is never known, so 'p and not-Kp' is true. By the knowability thesis, this whole conjunction must itself be knowable, so it's possible that K(p and not-Kp). But this can't be possible: if you knew both p and that you don't know p, you'd run straight into the KK-principle, since knowing that you don't know p is itself a piece of introspective knowledge about your own epistemic state, and stacking that on top of knowing p directly produces a state that's incoherent given how self-knowledge is supposed to work. So the supposition that there's an unknown truth has to be dropped, and the knowability thesis collapses into full omniscience: every truth is known.

This is exactly the trouble for verificationism about truth. Verificationists such as the logical positivists held that a statement's truth just consists in its being verifiable, which is why they are committed to the knowability thesis in the first place; Fitch shows this commitment is far more costly than it looks, since it forces the positivist all the way to omniscience. A realist, who allows for truths that outrun anyone's possible evidence, never asserts the knowability thesis and so has nothing to fear from the derivation.

Tennant's response keeps the knowability thesis but restricts it to what he calls 'Cartesian' propositions — roughly, propositions that aren't already guaranteed, by their logical form, to produce a contradiction if known. Since 'p and not-Kp' is exactly this kind of self-undermining proposition, excluding it isn't a patch aimed narrowly at Fitch's argument; the restriction is stated in independently motivated logical terms rather than in terms of 'whatever blocks the proof.' I find this reasonably persuasive: a restriction only earns the label 'ad hoc' when its sole virtue is dodging a counterexample, and Tennant's has an independent rationale even though it was arrived at by working backward from the paradox."
