I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental optics and laser physics before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down a philosophy of science rabbit hole. Lately I keep circling back to structural realism -- the idea, I think originally Worrall's, that what carries over from one scientific theory to its successor isn't the ontology (the "furniture" a theory says the world is made of) but the mathematical structure, the equations and relations themselves. Given my background it's probably no surprise that the example which grabbed me is Fresnel's equations for light surviving almost intact into Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, even though the underlying picture went from an elastic-solid ether to a field with no medium at all. I find that pretty compelling, but then I think about phlogiston, or caloric theory, where it seems like a lot less structure actually survived into the oxygen theory of combustion or the kinetic theory of heat, and I start to wonder if Worral just cherry picked his best case.

There's also this "Newman objection" I ran across, which as I understand it says that if realism is only ever about abstract structure, almost any sufficiently large set of objects can be arranged to satisfy that structure, so the claim becomes close to empty unless you smuggle some extra content back in. And then there's a further split between people like Worrall, who think we only ever have epistemic access to structure (epistemic structural realism), and people like Ladyman and French, who want to say structure is all there really is, objects included (ontic structural realism) -- which honestly still confuses me, since I dont know what a "relation" is even supposed to be a relation between if not objects.

Anyway, I'd love an essay that actually wrestles with this instead of just describing the position: is structural realism a good way out of the tension between the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic meta-induction, or does it basically collapse once you push on things like the Newman objection and the messier historical cases? Weigh the considerations properly, the way a referee would've forced me to back when I was submitting papers, but don't just leave it at "reasonable people disagree" -- tell me where you actually land and why.
