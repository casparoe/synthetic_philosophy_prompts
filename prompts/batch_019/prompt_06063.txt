I've spent a good chunk of the last few winter evenings re-reading Scott Alexander's old Slate Star Codex review of Andy Clark's "Surfing Uncertainty," together with Kaj Sotala's chapter-by-chapter LessWrong summary of the same book, and then following their citations out into the more technical philosophy-of-cognitive-science literature. What strikes me is how quickly the popular rationalist-sphere version of this material collapses Karl Friston's free energy principle and "predictive processing" into a single tidy story: the brain is a hierarchical Bayesian prediction engine that minimizes surprise, or its variational free energy bound, by updating a generative model against incoming prediction error, and by acting on the world to make the model's predictions come true. Once I went looking at the more argumentative corners of the literature, though, it became clear that this tidy story papers over a real disagreement about what the underlying mathematics is actually a theory of, and about where exactly the "inferring" is supposed to be happening.

I'd like you to compare two rival ways of reading the free energy principle and its relationship to predictive processing. On one side is what's often called the internalist or inferentialist reading, associated above all with Jakob Hohwy, on which the Markov blanket that statistically separates an organism's internal states from external ones functions as a kind of one-way veil: the brain can only ever infer the hidden causes of its sensory input from behind that boundary, so perception and bodily action end up as instruments in the service of an internal generative model rather than as partly constitutive of cognition itself. On the other side is the ecological or enactive reading pressed by critics such as Bruineberg, Kiverstein, and Rietveld, and gestured at (if never fully embraced) by Clark's own attempts to marry predictive processing with embodied and extended-mind views, on which the very same formal apparatus of Markov blankets, generative models, and variational free energy is read as describing a self-organizing organism-environment system in which coupling with the world is not merely instrumental but partly constitutes perception and action.

Does the free energy principle, considered strictly as a piece of mathematics about self-organizing systems, actually favor one of these readings over the other, or is it genuinely neutral on the internalist/enactivist dispute, so that the disagreement really turns on philosophical commitments imported from outside the formalism itself? And relatedly, when rationalist-adjacent writing presents "predictive processing" as simply what the free energy principle looks like once implemented in a brain, is it quietly smuggling in the internalist picture as though that picture were forced on us by the underlying theory, when it isn't? Please defend your answer by engaging with what the Markov blanket formalism does and does not entail, rather than simply asserting that one interpretation is the obvious one.
