So I'm a 4th-year in a political theory PhD program, and I've somehow become the resident "philosophy person" on my university's Graduate Student Assembly rules committee -- I think it started because I mentioned once in a meeting that my dissertation has a chapter on Rousseau and the general will, and now people just assume I have opinions about this stuff (fair, I guess). Anyway, a few weeks back our little political-thought reading group did Simone Weil's essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (the one she wrote in London in '43, not long before she died, arguing that whatever government France set up after the war should just do without parties altogether). Her argument isn't the usual "parties are too polarizing" complaint, it's structural: she thinks a party is basically defined by three things -- it's a machine for manufacturing collective passion, it exists to put collective pressure on the private judgement of its own members, and its real goal, underneath whatever the platform says, is just its own unlimited growth. Put those together and she thinks belonging to a party is more or less incompatible with actually pursuing truth and justice, whatever the party's politics happen to be.

This is suddenly relevant to my actual life because our Assembly has quietly grown two "caucuses" over the past couple years (a funding-focused STEM bloc and a more left organizing bloc), and they now run joint slates for exec positions, whip votes on resolutions before they even reach the floor, and have frozen out two senators for voting off-bloc. A pretty fed-up longtime senator has introduced a bylaws amendment to ban coordinated slates and bloc whipping outright, so every candidate and resolution gets considered on its own. The rules committee is split right down the middle and wants a recommendation memo before we meet again next Tuesday, and since I'm apparently the "theory" person I got voluteered to draft it.

Could you write that memo for me? I want it to seriously engage with whether Weil's argument actually gives us a reason to ban the caucuses, or whether this is really just ordinary Madison-style faction stuff, where organizing is an inevitable and arguably useful response to a big, confusing, low-information body -- new senators and international grad students in particular seem to genuinely depend on the caucuses to know how to vote on things they'd otherwise have no context for. Please don't just split the difference to be diplomatic, I need an actual recommendation (ban it, keep it, or some concrete middle option like banning whipped votes specifically or requiring caucuses to disclose their positions in advance) with reasoning solid enough that I could defend it to the caucus reps' faces on Tuesday.
