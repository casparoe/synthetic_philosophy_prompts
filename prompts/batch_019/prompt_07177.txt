A friend of mine, a few years into a psychology PhD, and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether the replication crisis is at bottom a frequentist problem. Her view was that most of the damage traces back to p-values and the way null-hypothesis testing invites optional stopping and multiple comparisons, and that moving to Bayesian analysis — Bayes factors, or posteriors under a specified prior — removes the temptation, since by the likelihood principle it shouldn't matter, evidentially, when you stopped collecting data or how many other comparisons you happened to run along the way. I argued that this proves too little: even granting the likelihood principle, a researcher with enough discretion over model specification, choice of prior, and which of several defensible analyses to report is still walking through what Gelman calls a garden of forking paths, and none of that discretion disappears just because the arithmetic at the end is Bayesian rather than frequentist. She accused me of moving the goalposts by folding in things no statistical framework could fix, like incentives and publication bias; I accused her of keeping the goalposts narrow enough that only her preferred framework could reach them. Neither of us budged.

I'd like a more rigorous version of my side of this argument, because it isn't just a dinner-table dispute for me. I sit on the editorial board of a mid-sized journal in my subfield, and after a second high-profile failed replication involving work we've published on effects — social priming, ego depletion, and the like — of exactly the kind that has taken the biggest hits over the last decade, the board has been asked to settle, by our next editorial meeting, between two rival reforms to our statistical reporting requirements for authors. Proposal A keeps null-hypothesis significance testing but adds mandatory preregistration and moves the threshold for claiming a new discovery from p < .05 to p < .005. Proposal B drops significance testing altogether in favor of mandatory preregistered Bayes factors against a specified prior, with no fixed decision threshold, on the grounds that this better reflects graded evidence. Board members on each side are explicitly invoking the philosophical debate between frequentism and Bayesianism, not just practical convenience, as their reason for favoring one proposal over the other.

Could you draft the memo I'd circulate to the board? It should recommend one proposal outright, or a specific hybrid if you think that's genuinely warranted rather than a way of avoiding the question, and it should justify the recommendation by reference to the actual mechanisms that produced our subfield's failed replications rather than by declaring one statistical philosophy more "correct" in the abstract. It should also take seriously the strongest argument for whichever proposal it doesn't adopt, engaging directly with my friend's underlying claim that one of the two frameworks is structurally better suited to resisting researcher degrees of freedom: say plainly whether you think that claim holds up, and why.
