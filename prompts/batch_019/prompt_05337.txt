Robert Aumann's theorem on backward induction and common knowledge of rationality claims to show that if it is common knowledge among the players of a finite game of perfect information that everyone is rational, then play must follow the backward-induction path. In the centipede game, this means a rational first mover stops the game on the very first move, forgoing the much larger payoffs both players could secure by continuing to cooperate. Critics such as Ken Binmore, Philip Reny, and Robert Stalnaker have argued that this conclusion is not really forced by common knowledge of rationality alone, since the argument depends on what players are entitled to believe about each other's continued rationality once play reaches a node that a rational player would supposedly never reach, and reasonable belief-revision policies can disagree on this point. Write an essay that weighs these positions and reaches a conclusion about whether common knowledge of rationality genuinely compels the backward-induction outcome in games like the centipede game. Do not end on a non-committal note: defend a specific verdict, for instance that backward induction is a strict requirement of rationality regardless of off-path beliefs, or that it holds only relative to further substantive assumptions that the critics are right to reject, or some other precise position of your own choosing.
