I'm a PhD student in philosophy drafting part of a seminar paper on Bernard Williams's internalism about practical reasons, and I'd like an outside read before I take it further. The section below responds to John McDowell's well-known objection, in "Might There Be External Reasons?" (1995), that Williams's notion of a "sound deliberative route" is too narrowly instrumental to rule out external reasons. Here is the section:

Williams's 1979 case against external reason statements rests on what I'll call the explanatory constraint: a reason statement earns its keep only if it can figure in an intelligible account of an agent's coming to be motivated. An internal reason statement satisfies this automatically, since it is true only if there is a sound deliberative route from some element of the agent's subjective motivational set, S, to the relevant action. Williams's challenge to the external reasons theorist is to explain what could make an external reason statement true given that, by hypothesis, its truth does not depend on anything already in S. His diagnosis is that any story about an agent coming to be moved by such a reason will end up smuggling in some element of S after all — a standing concern to be rational, a susceptibility to argument, a latent capacity for sympathy — at which point the "external" reason has quietly become internal relative to that element, and the externalist's original claim has lost its content.

McDowell's reply — that Williams's "sound deliberation" is instrumental reasoning by another name, and so cannot capture cases in which coming to appreciate a reason (say, through the acquisition of virtue) is not a matter of tracing out what was already implicit in one's antecedent concerns — is usually treated as the live alternative to Williams's picture. I want to suggest the exchange is less substantive than it looks, because of a concession Williams is both willing and, on his own terms, obliged to make: S can contain not just first-order desires and projects but general, largely dispositional capacities — for sympathy, for imaginative identification with others, for being struck by considerations one had not previously weighed. Once S is stocked this generously, there is no case McDowell wants to describe as recognition of an external reason that cannot be redescribed, without strain, as the sound deployment of some such standing capacity. An unsympathetic husband can be told he has reason to be kinder to his wife not because kindness answers to some specific desire he already owns, but because ordinary human beings have a capacity for sympathetic response that correct deliberation could engage — and that, on Williams's own terms, is enough to make the reason internal.

This generosity, though, is not free. The internal/external distinction was supposed to explain why we can withdraw a reason claim about a committed amoralist — someone for whom, ex hypothesi, no deliberative route from anything in S leads to the relevant motivation — while an externalist supposedly could not. But if merely possessing the general human capacities suffices to seed S, then only a fairly exotic incapacity, rather than mere unwillingness or a differently ordered set of concerns, will ever let an agent escape the reach of internal reasons — and the distinction stops tracking the cases it was invented for. The obvious repair is to individuate S more finely, requiring not just possession of a capacity but its being already "engaged" in some more specific sense; but this turns "engagement" into exactly the question the amoralist case was meant to settle, without supplying any independent criterion for answering it. My tentative conclusion is that the Williams–McDowell debate, staged this way, occupies an unstable middle position: generous enough about S to win every hard case, but not principled enough about what that generosity excludes to preserve the distinction's original point.

I'd welcome comments along three lines: first, whether I'm being fair to both Williams and McDowell here, or have flattened one of them in a way that loses its real force (I'm particularly unsure whether my gloss on McDowell does justice to the "second nature" idea behind it); second, whether the trivialization objection in the final paragraph actually succeeds, or whether there's a principled, non-question-begging way for an internalist to individuate S that would block it; and third, whichever step in the argument you'd press hardest on if you were refereeing this for a workshop.
