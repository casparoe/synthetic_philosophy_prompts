Hey, so I've been reporting on this "wisdom of crowds vs. algorithmic feeds" story for a while now and I keep getting stuck on Condorcet. He's the 18th-century guy behind the Jury Theorem, the result that says: if you've got a group of voters or jurors who are each individually just a bit more likely than a coin flip to get the right answer, and they decide independantly, then as you add more of them the majority verdict becomes almost certainly correct. It's basically the mathematical backbone for "trust the aggregate, not the individual," and it gets invoked constantly in arguments for democracy, prediction markets, aggregating expert forecasts, whatever. But the entire result leans on that one word, independent — and a network-science researcher I interviewed for the piece pointed out that recommender algorithms, social proof, and cascades are basically a machine for manufacturing correlated errors, i.e. exactly the condition the theorem needs to NOT hold.

What's been bugging me is that Condorcet wasn't some naive optimist who assumed aggregation always works — the very same 1785 essay that contains the jury theorem also contains his voting paradox, where he shows majority rule can produce cyclical, incoherent group preferences. So he'd already clocked one failure mode for collective decision-making and kept believing in the project anyway (he wrote his whole "progress of the human mind" manifesto while hiding from the Terror that eventually killed him, so the guy did not lack for reasons to despair and still didn't). What I can't get a handle on is what he'd say about *this* failure mode specifically — correlated rather than cyclical — since it's not something that existed as a nameable phenomenon before mass media, let alone algorithmic curation.

So here's my actual question: based on what you know about how Condorcet actually reasoned — the probabilism, the Enlightenment commitments, how he handled the paradox he did already know about — do you think he'd treat modern echo chambers and information cascades as a fixable design problem (redesign institutions so the "jurors" become independent again), as basically fatal to using his theorem to justify democratic or crowd-based decision-making, or would he just say the theorem was always a stylized idealization and none of this actually changes anything? I'd love an actual argued answer, not just "he'd probably say X" — reconstruct the reasoning he'd likely have used, if you can.
