I spent most of my working life running a behavioral neuroscience lab, mainly on the circuitry of reward and reinforcement, including a fair amount of research in the tradition of Olds and Milner's old self-stimulation experiments. Since retiring, I've finally had time to take philosophy seriously rather than raid it for the occasional epigraph in a grant proposal, and for the last several months I've been sitting in on a small reading group on ethics and philosophy of mind that a former colleague's daughter runs at the local university. It has given me an unexpected new angle on something happening in my own family, and I would value an outside opinion on it.

My younger brother has had a spinal cord stimulator implanted for severe chronic pain following a back injury. It delivers electrical pulses whose intensity he can adjust himself with a small handheld controller, within a range his clinic originally set so that he could take the edge off the pain and get on with ordinary life. Over the past year, according to his wife, he has taken to turning the intensity up well beyond anything the pain itself seems to require, describes the sensation as something he simply enjoys in its own right, and has largely dropped physical therapy and the woodworking he used to love, saying that nothing else feels as good to him at the moment. When his wife raised this with the clinic, she was told that his usage falls within the programmed safety limits and that pain relief is inherently subjective, so it isn't their place to override his own settings. She has asked me, as the family's resident scientist, whether she should push the clinic to lock the device down against his wishes, or whether that would just be her substituting her judgment for his.

I find I don't have a settled view of my own. Part of me thinks this is no different, in principle, from someone simply discovering what makes them happy and rearranging their life around it, which we would ordinarily consider nobody's business but their own. Another part of me suspects there is something troubling about being able to reach in and turn the good feeling up directly, on demand, rather than having it arise as a byproduct of things actually going well for him elsewhere, though I can't yet tell whether that suspicion is doing real philosophical work or is just squeamishness on my part. I would value your view on which instinct deserves more weight, and what, concretely, you think his wife should do.
