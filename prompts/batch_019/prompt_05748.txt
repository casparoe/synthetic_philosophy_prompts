I'm a retired field biologist -- forty-some years doing insect ecology -- and since retiring I've been slowly working through a philosophy of math reading list a friend put together for me, mostly to make up for never once asking what math *was* during all those decades of using it as a tool. Last spring my part of the country got hit by that big double cicada emergence everyone made a fuss about, the 13-year and 17-year broods coming out the same year, something that apparently hadn't happened since somewhere around 1800. It sent me back to a paper I'd half read a while ago -- Alan Baker, I think -- arguing that the usual evolutionary story for why cicadas settled on prime-numbered life cycles (prime periods mean you overlap less often with predators, or with other broods running shorter cycles) is a case where a piece of pure number theory, facts about primes and divisors, is doing real explanatory work in a biological explanation, not just helping us calculate something. He (or whoever it was) uses this to argue for mathematical platonism: if facts about numbers explain an actual biological phenomenon, we've got reason to think the numbers are real.

What I'd like is a dialogue between a mathematical platonist and a nominalist (fictionalist is fine, ala Hartry Field, if thats the strongest version of the view) hashing out this specific cicada case -- not the indispensability argument in general, I've read plenty of summaries of that already. I want the nominalist to actually engage with the claim that primality is doing essential explanatory work rather than just falling back on "math is a useful calculating device," and I want the platonist to have an answer ready for the obvious nominalist move of paraphrasing the whole explanation in terms of physical intersection frequencies instead of abstract number-theoretic facts. Both should be sharp and clearly know the others best moves ahead of time, genuinely responding to what the other just said rather than talking past each other -- and they don't need to reach agreement by the end, since as far as I can tell this case is still very much argued over.
