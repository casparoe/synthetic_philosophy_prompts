I'm a retired physicist who got into philosophy after retiring, and lately I've been reading about decision theory. I think I've finally worked out why so many philosophers are convinced rational agents must maximize expected utility. The money-pump argument seems airtight to me: if your preferences arent transitive, a bookie can string together a cycle of trades that drains you of cash for certain, kind of like proving a perpetual motion machine impossible. So intransitive preferences just are irrational, full stop, no different in kind from a logical contradiction, and there shouldnt be much left to argue about. Given that, why do so many philosophers still treat it as an open question whether rationality actually requires expected utility maximization?
