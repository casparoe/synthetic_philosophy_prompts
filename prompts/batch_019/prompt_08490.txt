Question (worth 20 points): Explain the central disagreement between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease. Using Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory as your example of naturalism, present one influential normativist objection to it, and assess how damaging the objection is.

Student's answer: "Naturalists such as Boorse hold that whether an organism is diseased is a matter of biological fact rather than evaluation. On Boorse's biostatistical theory, a part or process functions normally when it makes its statistically typical contribution to individual survival and reproduction, relative to a reference class fixed by age and sex; disease is simply a statistically subnormal contribution of this kind, one that reduces some functional ability below the species-typical level. Because 'statistically typical' is a purely descriptive notion, Boorse takes disease ascriptions to be value-free, and since for Boorse a person is ill exactly when they are diseased, this also removes any evaluative residue from decisions about who counts as a legitimate patient. Normativists reject this picture. They argue that deciding which effect of a part counts as its function already requires deciding which outcomes are good for the organism, so labeling a process a dysfunction smuggles in the very evaluation Boorse wants to avoid. Nordenfelt presses this point while keeping Boorse's basic framework, arguing that health should still be cashed out in terms of statistically normal species functioning, except that the relevant statistical baseline has to be fixed by what we value rather than read straight off biology. A further problem for Boorse is that a condition which is harmful but universal in a population, such as near-universal tooth decay in a sugar-heavy society, would count as healthy on his view, which looks like a cost of the theory rather than a successful defense of naturalism."
