I'm several chapters into a novel about two brothers, Owen and Malcolm, who haven't spoken honestly to one another in the thirty years since Malcolm walked out on the family during a crisis that nearly broke it. The scene I'm currently stuck on takes place the evening before their mother's funeral, which will force the two of them into the same room for the first time in years. It unfolds as a long conversation between Owen and Ruth, an old family friend who spent her career as a philosopher working on moral responsibility. Owen has nursed a very specific, well-rehearsed resentment toward Malcolm for decades, and Ruth, without quite trying to talk him out of it, wants to unsettle it.

Ruth's position is close to P.F. Strawson's in "Freedom and Resentment": that resentment, indignation, and gratitude aren't verdicts we reach after some metaphysical inquiry into free will, but part of what it is to treat someone as a full participant in a personal relationship, and that we retreat into the "objective attitude" — regarding a person as something to be managed, treated, or explained rather than answered to — only when some specific fact marks them off from full participation, the way madness or extreme immaturity might, and not simply because their action, like every action whatsoever, had a causal history behind it. Owen, who has been reading more hard-determinist philosophy than is good for him, answers with something in the spirit of Derk Pereboom's skepticism about desert: if Malcolm's decision to leave was itself just one more link in a causal chain running back through his own upbringing, then what exactly does Owen's resentment track about Malcolm that would make it fitting rather than merely a habit of feeling he can't shake — any more than Malcolm could have shaken whatever produced the decision to leave in the first place?

Could you write this conversation out as a real dialogue — something like ten to fourteen exchanges in total? I'd like Ruth to have to confront the difference between "you can't help feeling this way" and "this way of feeling is warranted," rather than resting on the point about participant relationships as though it settled the matter on its own, and I'd like Owen to be genuinely troubled by her best answer rather than simply getting to restate his objection until the scene runs out. I don't want either of them to be quietly a mouthpiece for the "correct" view — I want to come away from the exchange still unsure, at least for a moment, which of them has the better of the argument, so that I can decide honestly what happens next in the novel rather than having the philosophy secretly already decide it for me.
