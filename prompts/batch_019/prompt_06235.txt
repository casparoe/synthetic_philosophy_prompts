Three weeks ago my wife gave birth to our son, and at the small family dinner we hosted once we were home, my sister-in-law (who is finishing a PhD in evolutionary biology) and my uncle (a retired mechanical engineer with strong opinions on most subjects) ended up in a surprisingly detailed argument about male nipples that I have not been able to stop turning over since.

(1) She said male nipples are a textbook spandrel: both sexes follow an identical developmental template until the genes that trigger sexual differentiation switch on, nipples form before that point, and boys simply inherit them as a free byproduct. No special adaptive story about male nipples is required.

(2) He said that cannot be the whole story: nipples are not literally free (there is some infection risk, and rare tumors and gynecomastia do occur), and selection has had ample time to switch off their development in males the way it switched on distinct external genitalia. If it never bothered, that persistence itself calls for an explanation rather than a shrug.

(3) She said that is precisely the move Gould and Lewontin warned about: inferring, from the mere persistence of a trait, that there must be a hidden offsetting benefit or a hidden reason the "obvious fix" would be even more costly. That is a story invented to protect the adaptationist picture, not a claim supported by any actual evidence about selection on this trait.

(4) He said fine, but "it is just a byproduct, stop looking" has exactly the same defect in reverse: she does not actually know that no selection has acted on male nipple size, sensitivity, or timing, and people have floated pair-bonding and signaling functions. Declaring it a spandrel and closing the inquiry is just as unearned as declaring it an adaptation and closing the inquiry.

She did not concede the point; she answered that the burden of proof still favors her side, since her account only assumes that a harmless byproduct was never actively dismantled, while his requires demonstrating some currently operating benefit, which is the far stronger claim. He disagreed that the burden falls so neatly on one side, and by then the conversation had moved on to dessert.
