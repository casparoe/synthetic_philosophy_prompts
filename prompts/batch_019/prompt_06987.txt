"In the Seventeenth Discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazali attacks the philosophers' (i.e., Avicenna's) account of natural causation using the example of fire and cotton. Explain (a) what Ghazali thinks actually happens when the cotton burns, and why this leaves room for miracles, and (b) whether Ghazali's position amounts to the same claim about cause and effect that Hume later makes, or whether there's an important difference between the two. Support (b) with reference to the specific mechanism each one proposes. (150–250 words)"

And here's Dana's answer:

"Al-Ghazali's target in the Seventeenth Discussion is the philosophers' (mainly Avicenna's) claim that causes necessitate their effects by their own nature, so that fire simply must burn cotton on contact. Ghazali argues instead that there is no necessary connection between the two events at all: fire has no inherent power to burn, and the burning we observe is really produced directly by God at the moment of contact, following a regular habit rather than any built-in necessity in the fire itself. Because the link is only habitual and not necessary, God is free to withhold the usual effect whenever He likes, which is exactly what happens in a miracle like Abraham surviving the fire unburned. This move — inventing the idea that all real causal power belongs to God alone rather than to creatures — is what lets Ghazali deny natural necessity without denying that events reliably follow one another.

On the comparison: I'd say Ghazali and Hume are basically making the same argument. Both deny that we ever observe or could know a necessary connection between cause and effect, and both trace our belief in causation to habit/custom rather than to insight into how things really work. Hume just secularizes the same skeptical point Ghazali already made about causal power centuries earlier."
