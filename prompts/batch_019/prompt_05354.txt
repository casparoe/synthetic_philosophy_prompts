I spent thirty-six years running a physical chemistry lab focused on reaction-rate theory, and in retirement I have been filling my mornings with a self-imposed course in decision theory, largely because a certain argument I kept running into felt oddly familiar. The standard money-pump case — you prefer apple to banana, banana to cherry, and cherry to apple, so a patient trader walks you around that cycle, charging a small toll at each swap, until you have paid real money to end up holding the very fruit you started with — struck me immediately as a rational-agency analogue of a cyclic process on a potential energy surface. A conservative field lets you assign every point a potential precisely because the work done going around any closed loop is zero; a utility function does the same job for preference, and it can only exist if there is no loop to exploit, which is to say, if preference is transitive. Intransitivity looked to me like a landscape with a hidden downhill path that loops back on itself: a standing invitation to extract free value by going round and round.

What I have not been able to settle for myself is how to weigh the two remedies for this that keep turning up in the dynamic-choice literature. Edward McClennen's resolute choice has the agent commit in advance to a plan for the whole sequence of trades and then stick to it even at a later node where, taken on its own, defecting would be the locally preferred move — so the pump never actually extracts anything from her, at the cost of her sometimes acting against her preference-of-the-moment. Sophisticated choice, the strategy that grows out of Strotz's and Hammond's work on dynamic consistency, instead has the agent reason by backward induction: foreseeing at the very first node what she, or a later self, would actually do further down the cycle, she simply declines to take the first step whenever she can see that it leads nowhere good, so the intransitive preference never gets fully acted upon in the first place. I gather a structurally similar pump can be built against agents whose preferences are merely incomplete rather than outright cyclic, and that resoluteness and sophistication both get offered as remedies there too, which makes me suspect that more than a single axiom is at stake.

Could you compare resolute choice and sophisticated choice as responses to the money-pump argument? I would particularly value your view on two things: first, whether either strategy actually vindicates the rationality of the underlying intransitive or incomplete preferences, or whether both merely relocate the irrationality into a diachronic policy that has to keep compensating for a synchronic defect that never really goes away; and second, if you had to judge between them, which comes closer to showing that money-pump arguments do not, after all, establish a purely synchronic requirement of transitivity and completeness on rational preference.
