For this year's DECA case-study round, our chapter had to analyze a negotiation between two co-founders of a small food-truck business, one of whom lines up a lucrative private catering contract that would only ever benefit her if the partnership talks fell apart. Our faculty advisor used the case to introduce us to formal bargaining theory, starting with John Nash's 1950 solution to the bargaining problem, which selects the point on the frontier of feasible outcomes that maximizes the product of each side's gain over what they would get if negotiations collapsed. She then mentioned that Ehud Kalai and Meir Smorodinsky proposed a rival solution in 1975 that instead looks at the very best each side could conceivably hope for, given only that the other side does no worse than its fallback, and gives both sides the same fraction of the distance from that fallback to their respective "ideal."

What's been bothering me since then is that these two solutions apparently cannot both satisfy two properties that each sound, on their own, like non-negotiable requirements for any genuinely fair bargaining rule. Nash's solution satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives — removing options that nobody was going to choose anyway shouldn't change the outcome — but, as far as I can tell from trying to work through an example on my own, it can fail a monotonicity property that Kalai and Smorodinsky's solution has instead: it seems possible for one side's own best-case outcome to improve, with the other side's best case held fixed, and yet for Nash's rule to award that very side a smaller slice than it got before. If I've set that up correctly, this feels like a real strike against Nash, since "your share fell even though your options only got better" seems like exactly the kind of thing a genuinely fair procedure should never allow.

So rather than confirming that reaction, I'd like your help building the strongest case possible for sticking with Nash's solution and its independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives property anyway, treating the monotonicity worry as a cost worth accepting rather than a decisive objection, instead of switching to Kalai and Smorodinsky's rule. Could you ground the argument in a concrete negotiation — you're welcome to develop the food-truck case further or invent a different scenario entirely — rather than resting on the two bare axioms, and make sure to address what, if anything, independence of irrelevant alternatives actually safeguards that the monotonicity property does not?
