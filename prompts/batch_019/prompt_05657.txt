I sit on my university's research integrity committee, and I've been asked to draft the recommendation memo for Thursday's meeting on a case I haven't been able to stop turning over in my head. A twelve-author paper from one of our labs was found, after an outside reviewer flagged irregularities in the underlying images, to rest on fabricated data in two of its central figures. The postdoc who ran those specific experiments has admitted to fabricating them. The other eleven authors, including the senior/corresponding author who designed the study, wrote the grant, and supervised the lab, maintain that they never saw the raw data and had no reason to suspect anything was wrong. Nobody on the committee disputes that the paper should be retracted or that the postdoc faces a misconduct finding. What's actually dividing us is what, if anything, should happen to everyone else — especially the PI, whose name lent the paper its credibility and who reviewed and approved the final manuscript, but who did not fabricate or knowingly rely on fabricated data.

I spent most of a dinner arguing about this with a friend of mine, a lab head herself, who has no patience for what she calls "guilt by association dressed up as accountability." Her position: responsibility tracks what a person actually did or knew, full stop — punishing the PI for a postdoc's fabrication is scapegoating, and treating authorship itself as a standing liability will just teach scientists to manage their co-authors as risks rather than trust them as collaborators. My position: putting your name on a paper isn't merely disclosing a causal contribution; it's undertaking a joint commitment to the work as a whole, which is exactly why our authorship policy, like most journals', requires every listed author to formally agree that they're answerable for the integrity of the entire paper, not just their own piece of it — and that commitment can generate real obligations, and real liability when those obligations go unmet, that aren't reducible to what any individual signer separately knew or caused. Neither of us moved the other an inch, and I suspect our committee will split along much the same lines.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before the meeting? I want a concrete, role-differentiated recommendation — something distinct for the postdoc, the mid-list coauthors, and the PI, rather than a generic call for "further review" — together with the reasoning behind it. And because my friend's objection is exactly the one I expect to hear again on Thursday, the memo should meet it head-on rather than talk past it: it should say plainly what the real cost is of holding people accountable for failures they neither caused nor knew about, and, if it still ends up recommending that, why that cost is worth bearing.
