I'm a nurse educator, not a philosopher, but I've been asked to give the address at this spring's pinning ceremony, where graduates receive their nursing pins in front of a room of families with a couple of philosophy colleagues scattered in the back row. A student in this class recently got a medication-dosage calculation right on an exam for entirely the wrong reason, and only found out when a preceptor walked her back through the actual reasoning during clinical rotations; I can gesture at that story only obliquely, since I don't have her permission to tell it outright, but it's what has me thinking about Socrates' old puzzle in the Meno about the road to Larissa -- a traveler with a true opinion gets there just as reliably, on any single trip, as one who actually knows the way, so what really justifies prizing knowledge over a merely lucky correct belief? Could you draft a seven-minute speech that uses Socrates' own answer to that question, rather than a slogan about "understanding mattering," to tell these new nurses why the difference matters -- in language that will land as well with the families in the room as with my two colleagues?
