My partner and I just got back our prenatal carrier-screening results: it turns out we're both carriers of the same recessive condition, and the genetic counselor kept describing this as a "coding error"—a single changed letter that garbles the instructions for a protein. That phrase has stuck with me, because I remember from college biology that the genetic code isn't a metaphor invented by science writers: molecular biologists in the 1960s worked out a literal cipher linking each three-letter codon to the amino acid it specifies. So it seems to follow that a mutation like ours doesn't merely correlate with a health outcome the way smoking correlates with lung cancer—it encodes something false, the way a typo turns a true sentence into a false one. If that's right, how should I think about "silent" mutations, which change a codon's letters without changing the amino acid it produces—are those simply true statements, where ours is a false one? I'd like the conceptual picture here, not the biochemistry I could look up myself.
