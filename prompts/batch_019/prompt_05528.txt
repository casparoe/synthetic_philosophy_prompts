So my friend Priya and I got into a whole arguement last night that somehow spiraled out of a board game into philosophy (don't ask how) - she brought up this old thought experiment, Max Black's spheres, from a metaphysics class she took years ago. You know the one: Black imagines a universe that contains literally nothing except two spheres, exactly alike in every property - same size, same material, same age, everything - just floating there symmetrically, forever, with nothing else around to serve as a reference point. His point is that even though there's no property that could ever tell the spheres apart, there are obviously two of them, not one, which he thinks sinks Leibniz's principle that anything sharing all its properties with another thing has to just be that thing. Priya's counter (which she swears comes from an Ian Hacking paper, though she couldn't produce it and I haven't read it either, so we might both be mangling it) was that "two" is doing all the work before the thought experiment even gets going - that whether it's secretly one sphere or really two depends on facts about the shape of space itself, like whether space loops back on itself so that going far enough in one direction just returns you to where you started, and that Black is quietly assuming an ordinary space where that can't happen. We went back and fourth on this for like an hour and got nowhere, and I want to keep chewing on it.

Can you write me a short story that stages this same bind without just retelling the sphere example? I want actual characters and stakes - maybe someone who finds out they have an exact duplicate, or a crew on a ship that keeps circling back through some weirdly symmetric pocket of space, or honestly whatever premise you think works better - where the plot itself hinges on whether we're dealing with two indiscernible things or one thing we're mistakenly counting twice, and where that question can't just be settled by looking harder or gathering more data. I don't want the ending to shrug and call it a mystery either - I'd like the story to actually commit to an answer, or at least make vivid why committing to one is so hard, instead of staying neutral for the sake of staying neutral.
