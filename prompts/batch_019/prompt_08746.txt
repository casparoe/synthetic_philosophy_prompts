So I've been lurking on LessWrong/ACX-adjacent forums for something like a decade now, and the "just bound your utility function or Pascal's mugger will pump you for infinite dollars" move comes up all the time. A while back I went down a rabbit hole and found this obscure-ish 2004 decision theory paper (Nover & Hájek, I think it was in Mind, called something like "Vexing Expectations") that constructs a gamble called the Pasadena game. I think it's honestly a way more devastating argument for bounded utility than Pascal's mugging ever was, but I basically never see it brought up outside of decision-theory-nerd circles, so I want to pressure-test the idea here.

Here's roughly how the game goes: you flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time. If that happens on flip n, you get a payoff of (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n / n -- and crucially, these payoffs are stipulated to already be denominated in utility, not dollars, precisely so nobody can pull the usual Bernoulli move of invoking diminishing marginal utility of money to make the problem go away. If you naively compute the expectation by summing over n = 1, 2, 3, ... in the obvious order, you get the alternating harmonic series (1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ...), which converges to ln(2). But that series is only conditionally convergent, not absolutely convergent -- the sum of the absolute values is the divergent harmonic series -- so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem you can reorder those exact same terms to sum to literally any real number you want, or to diverge to infinity, or to oscillate forever without settling down. So the "expected value" here isn't just infinite or vaguely undefined, its actively incoherent: there's no privileged order in which a countably infinite set of coin-flip outcomes is supposed to be added up.

What I want is for you to actually commit to a specific, fairly strong thesis here instead of hedging across a bunch of positions: argue that the Pasadena game shows bounded utility isn't just a nice patch for exotic edge cases like Pascal's mugging, but close to a necessary condition for expected utility maximization to even count as a coherent decision rule. That is, argue that any agent with an unbounded utility function -- even a strictly concave, risk-averse one, as long as its unbounded -- is in principle vulnerable to some Pasadena-style construction, whereas an agent with bounded utility is automatically immune, since bounded payoffs weighted by any probability distribution (which always sums to 1) give you absolute convergence for free. Don't just gesture at that intuition, actually make the case for why it generalizes beyond the one gamble Nover and Hájek happened to write down, and deal with the standard objection that boundedness has its own ugly cost -- namely that it seems to force indifference between sufficiently good outcomes past some threshold, no matter how much better one is than the other.
