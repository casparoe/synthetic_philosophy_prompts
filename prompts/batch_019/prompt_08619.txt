I cover neuroscience and health for a science magazine, and a piece I did a few years back on epilepsy surgery is apparently what got me tapped as the "community member" on my hospital's clinical ethics committee — every committee needs one warm body who isn't a clinician, and I guess writing about the place once qualifies. We've got a case going to a vote this Thursday that's split the room more than anything else I've sat in on, and my own draft memo isn't good enough yet, so I could use help getting it into shape.

The patient, a woman in her 40s I'll call Ms. R, had a complete corpus callosotomy about four months ago — her drop attacks were bad enough that the partial section they tried first wasn't cutting it. Since then she's developed a textbook case of what the neuro team keeps calling alien hand, with real intermanual conflict: her left hand will unbutton her shirt when she says shes cold, reach over and take food off other patients' trays, and twice now has grabbed a staff member's wrist hard enough to leave a mark, once going for a lighter a visitor had out. Ms. R is adamant that "she" didn't do any of that, that the soft-restraint protocol nursing wants for her unsupervised hours is humiliating and unnecessary, and that her refusal should be the end of it. Nursing and her neurologist think the refusal doesn't settle much, since, in their words, "the hand isn't taking instructions from whatever part of her is doing the refusing." Two committee members actually used the phrase "two people in one skull" last meeting, which a third dismissed as sci-fi nonsense letting us dodge a boring risk-management call. Our restraint policy just requires consent from "the patient" or a substitute decision-maker if she lacks capacity — it was written for people with one obviously unified will, and nobody anticipated this.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before the vote? I need an actual recommendation — approve the restraint protocol, deny it, or something narrower — with a time frame for reevaluation, and it has to seriously grapple with Ms. R's side (the dignity and autonomy objection, and the worry that "her hand never asked your permission either" proves way too much) rather than just defaulting to whatever risk management wants because that's the safe institutional move.
