For a while now I've been thinking on my own about whether a society can ever be entitled to make something illegal simply because most people consider it deeply immoral, even where no one is harmed by it. Digging into this question eventually led me to the argument between Lord Devlin and H.L.A. Hart that grew out of Britain's 1957 Wolfenden Report. What interests me most is one specific strand of Devlin's case in *The Enforcement of Morals*: his "disintegration thesis," which holds that a society's shared morality binds it together in much the same way as its institutions of government, so that undermining that morality on a large enough scale amounts to something like treason against the society itself. Hart, in *Law, Liberty, and Morality*, answers partly by distinguishing a society's actual, current moral opinions ("positive morality") from whatever moral standards might survive rational scrutiny ("critical morality"), and argues that Devlin's thesis only appears to work because it slides between the two. I'd like you to compare their positions on exactly this point: does Hart's distinction genuinely dissolve the disintegration thesis, or does it just push the same underlying difficulty back a level? And, taking a side, whose overall view of where the criminal law's proper limits lie do you find more defensible today?
