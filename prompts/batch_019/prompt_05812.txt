Question 4 (20 points). Bertrand's chord paradox asks for the probability that a chord drawn "at random" on a circle is longer than the side of the circle's inscribed equilateral triangle, and famously admits different answers (1/3, 1/2, 1/4) depending on how the randomization is set up. (a) Briefly explain the three classical constructions and why they disagree. (b) State precisely what you take the paradox to show about the principle of indifference. (c) Explain how E. T. Jaynes proposed to resolve the paradox, and assess whether his proposal rehabilitates the principle of indifference as a general method for assigning probabilities under ignorance, or only addresses this particular case.

Student's answer, verbatim:

"Bertrand's paradox concerns a circle and the chords that can be drawn inside it. If we ask for the probability that a 'random' chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, we get different answers depending on how we operationalize 'random.' If we pick two independent uniform points on the circumference and connect them, the probability is 1/3. If we pick a random radius and then a uniformly distributed point along that radius, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point, the probability is 1/2. If we instead pick the midpoint of the chord uniformly over the area of the circle, the probability is 1/4. Since all three constructions seem like reasonable ways to cash out 'a chord chosen at random,' and the principle of indifference (first defended by Bayes in his 1763 essay and later systematized under that name by Keynes) tells us to assign equal probability to each of a set of possibilities when we have no reason to favor one over another, the paradox shows that the principle is radically underdetermined: 'no reason to favor one possibility over another' depends on how the possibility space is parameterized, and different, equally natural parameterizations are indifferent over different things. A related case is van Fraassen's cube factory: a factory makes cubes of some size, and being indifferent over the side length gives a different distribution over the volume than being indifferent over the volume gives over the side length, even though nothing in the setup tells us which quantity to be indifferent over.

Jaynes's response is to notice that the physical setup generating the paradox — for instance, tossing a straw onto a circle drawn in the sand — carries certain invariance requirements that any admissible probability distribution must satisfy: the answer should not change if we rotate the circle, rescale it, or translate it slightly, since none of these operations changes the physical problem being posed. When these three invariance requirements are imposed jointly on the density over the line's parameters, exactly one of the three classical constructions, the random-radius method, turns out to be the unique maximum-entropy distribution satisfying all of them at once, which is how Jaynes arrives at 1/2 as the privileged answer. Because this shows that there is, after all, a uniquely correct way to be indifferent once we are careful about specifying the invariances relevant to the actual physical randomizing procedure, Jaynes's argument fully rehabilitates the principle of indifference: the appearance of paradox was really just equivocation about which symmetries were in play, and once the physical setup is fixed precisely enough there is always exactly one answer that indifference reasoning will deliver."
