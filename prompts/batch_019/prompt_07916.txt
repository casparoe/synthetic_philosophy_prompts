I spent forty years doing experimental condensed-matter physics, and in retirement I have been reading my way through philosophy of physics with more enthusiasm than system. A colleague recently pointed me to some popular writing by cosmologists on so-called Boltzmann brains, and although I already knew the basic idea — that a random thermal fluctuation is astronomically more likely to produce a single deluded, free-floating brain, complete with false memories of an orderly universe, than to reconstitute an entire galaxy full of ordinary observers — I had not appreciated that this is treated as a genuine constraint on cosmological model-building rather than a mere curiosity. As I understand it, in any eternally inflating cosmology with a long-lived (metastable) de Sitter vacuum, the nonzero Gibbons–Hawking temperature of empty space guarantees a low but steady rate of Boltzmann-brain nucleation forever into the future, whereas observers like us are produced only in the finite window that follows each pocket universe's hot big bang; over infinite time this seems to entail that a randomly selected observer is overwhelmingly likely to be a Boltzmann brain, and some physicists treat this as grounds for rejecting outright any theory with that feature.

What I have not been able to settle for myself is whether that last inferential step is actually defensible, and this is where I would like your help. Please write a dialogue between two philosophically literate physicists who disagree about it. One holds that Boltzmann-brain-counting is a perfectly legitimate empirical argument, structurally no different from rejecting a theory because it predicts that one's instrument should almost always be reading noise rather than signal, so that a cosmology on which typical observers are Boltzmann brains stands genuinely disconfirmed. The other holds that the whole maneuver is illegitimate: it quietly presupposes some resolution of the "measure problem" of how to weigh infinite populations of observers against one another, a problem with no agreed solution and on which the verdict reverses depending on the measure chosen; and, worse, the argument is self-undermining, since a reasoner who really were a Boltzmann brain would have no business trusting the very chain of reasoning that led her to conclude she probably is one.

I would like the two characters to be equally sharp and to take each other's objections seriously rather than merely scoring points: the defender of typicality should have a genuine answer to the self-undermining worry rather than simply conceding it, and the skeptic should press hard on what, if anything, could privilege one measure or reference class over another. If you happen to know of proposals that try to dissolve the whole issue at the level of physics itself, by arguing that a properly quantized, genuinely stationary de Sitter vacuum need not fluctuate the way the semiclassical thermal picture suggests, feel free to let one of the characters raise it, though the dialogue need not arrive at a tidy resolution.
