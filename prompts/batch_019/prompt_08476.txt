I spent about thirty-five years as an analytical chemist before I retired, and for the last year or so I've been working my way through the classical utilitarians for fun - Bentham, Mill, and now Sidgwick's "Methods of Ethics," which is rougher going than the other two put together. What really stopped me in my tracks though was finding out, almost by accident via a footnote, that Sidgwick spent nearly the last two decades of his life as a founder and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, personally testing mediums, chasing down reports of haunted houses, and helping run big statistical surveys like the "Census of Hallucinations" along with his wife Eleanor. For someone whose actual ethical writing is so dry and careful, that struck me as an odd second career - a bit like a metrologist moonlighting as a dowser. So I had a go at writing up my own little theory of what was going on there, and I'd like your honest read on it before I inflict it on my philosophy discussion group next month.

My theory, roughly, is that the psychical research wasn't a hobby seperate from the ethics, it was more or less forced on him by a hole he'd dug for himself in the Methods. He argues there that practical reason hands us two equally self-evident principles that can conflict - maximize your own good, or maximize the good impartially considered - and he can't find grounds to always rank the second over the first unless some further fact holds, basically a moral government of the universe extending past death, that makes duty and self-interest come out the same in the end. He'd already burned his bridges with revealed religion (he famously resigned his Trinity fellowship rather than keep affirming the Thirty-Nine Articles), so I read the psychical research as his attempt to get that same reassurance - evidence of personal survival, of a universe that keeps score - through empirical means instead of scripture, basically trying to rescue utilitarianism from its own "dualism" with seance transcripts instead of Sunday sermons. What I find almost more striking is that by his own account he never got evidence that satisfied him and seems to have died still unconvinced, so the whole project ends up looking like a monument to a problem he couldn't solve any other way.

Could you go through that argument and tell me, point by point, where I've got the history or the philosophy wrong, where I'm stretching things to fit a tidy story, and whether the "dualism of practical reason" is really doing the work I want it to do there or whether I'm flattening it? I'd also be curious if there's a better-supported version of this thesis already out there in the Sidgwick literature that I should just go read instead of reinventing it badly. Better you tell me it doesn't hold up than my reading group does.
