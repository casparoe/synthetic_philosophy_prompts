I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of facts and structured propositions, and this week's session spent way too long circling around Bradley's regress -- the old worry that if a relation R actually holds between a and b, we seem to need a further relation to tie R to a and b, and then another relation to tie that one in too, and so on forever. Half my students think the regress is a genuine proof that relations can't do the metaphysical work we want from them (uniting particulars and universals into a fact, or terms into a unified proposition rather than a mere list), and the other half think its basically a parlor trick that dissolves once you stop picturing a relation as an extra object sitting alongside its relata. I'd like a reasonably decisive outside take to bring into next week's session -- there's also a small bottle of wine riding on this between me and a colleague down the corridor, so "it's complicated, both sides have a point" won't really cut it here.

So: here are seven standard-ish responses to the regress, some aimed more at facts/states of affairs and some more at the unity of the proposition specifically, but close enough in spirit to compare -- (1) Bradley's own move of biting the bullet and denying relations are ultimately real, in favour of the undifferentiated Absolute; (2) Frege's appeal to the unsaturatedness of concepts, i.e. concepts are inherently "gappy" and so need no further glue to combine with objects; (3) early Russell's distinction (from the Principles of Mathematics) between a relation as it actually relates its terms and that same universal merely considered as an abstract term in its own right; (4) Armstrong's treatment of states of affairs as fundamental, with instantiation as a non-relational tie rather than a further relatum; (5) Bergman-style "nexus of exemplification" views, where the tie is sui generis and doesn't itself need connecting to what it connects; (6) deflationary responses that just deny the unity of a fact or proposition needs any substantive metaphysical explanation at all; and (7) the "it's a benign regress" move, which says an infinite series here is harmless as long as each further relatedness-fact is automatically entailed rather than a fresh ontological commitment.
