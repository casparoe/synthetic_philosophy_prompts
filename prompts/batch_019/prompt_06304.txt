I write novels for a living, but the book that has occupied me for the better part of two years is something different: a family memoir centered on my great-aunt Marta, who worked at a small regional newspaper in the early 1950s. Family legend has always maintained that Marta did not merely cover the story that got a corrupt alderman voted out of office, but manufactured the anonymous tip that set the investigation in motion — essentially inventing a source so that her editor would let her run it. I have heard this, independently and unprompted, from three different relatives: Marta's younger sister (my grandmother), a cousin who worked alongside her at the paper one summer, and Marta's own daughter. Their accounts agree on every point but one small detail — whether Marta arranged the tip alone or with a colleague's help — and there is no letter, diary, or other document to settle the matter either way; Marta died before I was born.

For most of those two years, I assumed the book would end up as a small private printing for the family, and when I recounted the episode at a reunion last summer, I stated flatly that Marta had invented the source. No one so much as blinked; it felt like something I simply knew, on the strength of three matching, independently obtained accounts. Matters look different now that a respected press wants to acquire the manuscript for genuine distribution: their legal reader flagged that very sentence, because I have changed the town's name and the alderman's, but apparently not enough to keep either from being identifiable, and the alderman in question has a grandson who sits on the county bench. On the evidence I actually possess, the reader insists, I do not know that Marta invented the tip — I merely have good reason to suspect it — and the sentence must be softened to something like "family lore holds that." Nothing about my evidence has changed between the reunion and the acquisition offer; only what is riding on being wrong has. My writing group split cleanly down the middle on the question, and my agent, half in jest, told me I had "known it well enough for a family reunion, but not well enough for print."

I am not asking for legal advice — I have people for that — but I cannot shake the feeling that there is a genuine puzzle underneath this, rather than simply a difference of opinion, and I would value an outside, clear-headed perspective on it. Does it really make sense to say that the very same body of evidence left me in a position to know this at the reunion but leaves me without that knowledge now, given that nothing evidential separates the two moments? And if that turns out to be coherent after all, does it tell me anything about which reaction I ought to trust — my own initial confidence, or the press's newfound caution?
