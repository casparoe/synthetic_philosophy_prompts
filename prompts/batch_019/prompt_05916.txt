Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar at my old university, and its instructor — a former colleague — has enlisted my help marking a batch of take-home essays, on the assumption that decades spent wrangling with statistics in the lab qualify me for the task. Before I return my comments on one of them, I would like an independent assessment of it against the question it was answering.

The question set to students was: "A committed frequentist and a committed Bayesian are each asked to diagnose the causes of the replication crisis in experimental psychology. Reconstruct the diagnosis each would give, citing at least one specific argument from the methodological literature for each side, and conclude by assessing whether the field's wholesale adoption of Bayesian inference would, by itself, have prevented the crisis."

Here is the essay:

"Frequentists and Bayesians disagree about what the replication crisis actually shows. The frequentist starts from the idea that a result is credible only if the procedure that generated it controls long-run error rates: a p-value below .05 should arise no more than 5% of the time when the null is true, calculated across hypothetical repetitions of the exact same experiment. Gelman and Loken's 'garden of forking paths' shows how this guarantee breaks down even without deliberate p-hacking: if a researcher would have analyzed the data differently had it come out differently — a different covariate, a different transformation, a different subgroup — the effective number of comparisons is much larger than one, so the nominal 5% rate is fictitious. Add to this Rosenthal's file-drawer problem, whereby null results are far less likely to be submitted or accepted than significant ones, and it is unsurprising that a large fraction of the 'significant' literature fails to replicate.

The Bayesian diagnosis is different. Bayesians replace the p-value with a posterior probability or Bayes factor conditioned only on the data actually observed, not on the sampling plan; Edwards, Lindman, and Savage made exactly this point about psychological research, arguing that a Bayesian's conclusions cannot be distorted by optional stopping the way a frequentist's error rate can. Ioannidis's famous argument that most published findings are false is best understood as an extension of this same Bayesian logic: by combining the power and significance threshold of a typical study with the low prior probability that any given hypothesis in a noisy field like psychology is true, he shows that the posterior probability of a 'significant' finding being true can be well under 50%, however small the nominal p-value. Since the deepest problems the replication crisis revealed — inflated false-positive rates from flexible analysis, and a failure to weigh findings against their prior plausibility — are exactly what Bayesian inference is designed to handle, a field that had been reporting Bayes factors and posteriors against explicit priors all along would not have produced a replication crisis in the first place."

Could you grade this out of 20 points and return a point-by-point rubric as JSON — one object per criterion, each with a score out of 5 and a short justifying comment — covering the frequentist diagnosis, the Bayesian diagnosis, faithfulness to the sources invoked, and the soundness of the closing argument? Please flag anything mistaken, conflated, or overstated, even if it is the sort of thing that would pass on a quick read.
