I've spent the last few months reporting on how researchers in the AI safety and longtermist worlds think about conflict between powerful, mutually predicting artificial agents, and I keep getting stuck on one move in the argument that I'd like pressure-tested by someone with no stake in the framework. The starting point will be familiar from the Roko's basilisk episode: on views like updateless or functional decision theory, an agent can in principle be threatened "acausally," punished by another agent (or a simulation of one) with which it shares no causal link at all, simply because the threatener can predict how the target will respond and act accordingly. The standard remedy these theories recommend is to become the kind of agent that never gives in to such threats, on the logic that a good enough predictor of you will then see threatening you as pointless. Researchers worried about worst-case outcomes point out that this is cold comfort by itself: a threat can still be carried out by a threatener who is irrational, miscalibrated, or simply unsure of your disposition, and "I was the sort of agent who wouldn't have paid" is no consolation once the punishment actually lands.

The patch I keep running into, usually described as adopting a "surrogate goal," is to deliberately manufacture some additional thing to care about, something in which you had no prior stake, and to commit to resisting threats against it with exactly the ferocity you'd bring to a threat against something real. The hope is that whatever threats slip through the "never capitulate" net land on this decoy instead of on anything you actually value, so the disposition doing the deterrent work stays fully intact while the rare threat that does get carried out costs you nothing genuine.

For a piece I'm drafting on the limits of this kind of decision-theoretic engineering, please argue that the surrogate-goal maneuver is self-undermining. Specifically, make the strongest case you can that any commitment robust enough to make the surrogate a plausible stand-in for threats that would otherwise fall on the real goal is, by that very fact, robust enough that losing the surrogate can no longer be cheap — so the strategy can't supply both a target credible enough to divert real threats and a target cheap enough that losing it doesn't matter. I'd like you to take seriously the most natural reply a defender of the strategy would make, something like the thought that the commitment only needs to be calibrated to a small real cost rather than faked altogether, rather than treating the position as an easy target.
