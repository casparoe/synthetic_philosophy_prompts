I'm a novelist, and I'm writing the eulogy for my undergraduate philosophy advisor, who died last month after thirty years running a dog-and-cat rescue out of her farmhouse alongside her academic career. Left to my own instincts I'll round her off into something warmer and tidier than she actually was, so I need the philosophy to stay load-bearing, not decorative. Her scholarly reputation rested on one stubborn argument, developed out of Christine Korsgaard's work on Kant and animals: that Kant's own test for moral standing — being an end in itself, not a mere means — doesn't actually require rational agency, only having a good of one's own, so his own principles commit him to direct duties toward animals, even though Kant himself concluded that those duties are merely indirect, owed to our own character rather than to the animal. She loved teaching the passage where he says a man does no wrong to his worn-out dog by having it shot, only wrong to himself, precisely so she could take it apart. Half the pews will hold colleagues who know this argument cold; the other half will be thirty years of rescue volunteers who think of her simply as the woman who took in strays. Her one instruction for the service was that nobody call her view "animal rights" — she always insisted her conclusion was that animals are ends in themselves in Kant's own technical sense, not bearers of rights in the looser sense that phrase implies, and she thought the two were easy to blur. Write the eulogy so the volunteers feel her rescue work and her philosophy were one conviction, without collapsing that distinction or putting a conclusion in Kant's mouth that he explicitly rejected.
