As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I recently joined a mathematical society's research-standards committee as its public member; the committee is now drafting its first policy on AI-generated results. The case forcing the issue: a search system devised both the strategy and the case-by-case verification for a resolution to a long-standing graph-coloring conjecture, and a proof assistant has checked every branch, so mathematicians are about as sure of it as of any theorem in the literature. But unlike the four-color theorem, where humans at least supplied the reduction strategy and machines only ground through the cases, here no person, and nothing inspectable in the system itself, can say why the construction works or what else it would generalize to. Roughly half the committee wants to certify it as a theorem like any other, since mathematics answers to truth, not to anyone's feeling of understanding; the other half wants to log it as verified-but-unexplained, withholding theorem status and prize eligibility until someone supplies an account. Draft the committee's policy memo: recommend a classification and a review process, with reasoning solid enough to give the losing side something real to answer, not just a restatement of the winners' premises.
