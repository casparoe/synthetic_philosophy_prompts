I'm about two-thirds through a novel about a mountain search-and-rescue guide named Reyes, and the back half of the book turns on a question I didn't expect to turn into a whole philosophy detour: is Reyes actually brave, or has she just never been properly tested? There's a flashback where she freezes during a rescue in a storm, and everyone in the story (including her) reads it as plain cowardice, but I want to leave open a more unsettling possibility -- that the courage was there and something external kept it from showing up. My partner, who's finishing a philosophy MA, told me this is basically the textbook problem philosophers ran into trying to define "dispositions" like fragile or soluble (or brave) using simple if-then conditionals, e.g. "x is fragile iff x would break if struck" -- and that this kind of analysis gets wrecked by two different counterexamples people call finks and masks. The examples she gave me were a dead wire hooked up to some kind of "fink" gadget that charges it the instant it's touched, so it conducts anyway, and a poison thats always given together with an antidote so it never actually kills anyone, even though its still a poison, chemically speaking. She said David Lewis tried to patch the simple analysis by requiring that the disposition's underlying causal "basis" stick around through the whole triggering event, and that this fixes the wire case but does basically nothing for the poison one.

What I can't get a satisfying gut-level handle on is why that one patch saves the fink cases but is useless against masks -- what's the actual structural difference between "something removes the basis at the critical moment" and "something blocks the basis from doing its thing," such that a fix for one leaves the other completely untouched? I don't need the full formal apparatus, just a clear-headed walkthrough of the underlying logic, detailed enough that I can use the distinction on purpose: I want one Reyes scene to read, in hindsight, like a genuinely masked disposition (the courage never left, something external smothered it) and a different scene to read like a finkish fake-out (it only looked like courage because of lucky timing, not because the trait was ever really there). Feel free to just use the wire and poison examples, or whatever the standard ones are -- I mainly want to stop name-dropping "fink" and "mask" at my writing group like I know what I'm talking about, and actually understand the mechanics.
