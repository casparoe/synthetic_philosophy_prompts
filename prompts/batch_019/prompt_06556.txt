At work we have an informal lunchtime reading group that works through papers on AI safety, and last week's session on the "Löbian obstacle" ended in a disagreement I haven't been able to settle myself. For context: the obstacle refers to a result from provability logic, Löb's theorem, which says that for a sufficiently expressive theory T, if T proves the statement "if T proves P, then P" for some sentence P, then T already proves P outright. The worry raised in AI safety circles (originally from Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's work on "tiling agents") is that this seems to block a self-modifying AI from formally certifying that a future, updated version of itself is trustworthy, since doing so naively requires exactly this kind of reflection principle.

Two people in the group, Marcus and Sana, went back and forth on what this actually implies, and both made points I found hard to dismiss. Marcus opened by arguing that the obstacle is close to a genuine no-go result for safe self-improving AI: if a formal system can't even certify its own soundness without Löb's theorem forcing it to already prove whatever it's trying to certify, then any AI that claims to "trust" a future, modified version of itself is either being formally careless or is quietly resting on an inconsistent system, which could "prove" anything at all. Sana pushed back that this overreads the theorem: it only rules out an agent granting itself an unrestricted reflection schema, not self-trust in general, and the "tiling agents" literature gets around it by having each successor certified relative to a strictly stronger proof system than the one before it, which she took as evidence the obstacle is avoidable in practice, even if not eliminable in principle. Marcus replied that this just relocates the problem rather than solving it: you either eventually run out of strictly stronger systems to appeal to, or you need an unbounded hierarchy of them, and calling an infinite regress "solved" is generous. Sana's rejoinder was that the very same demand — a proof of one's own reasoning's soundness before trusting it — would show that humans can't rationally rely on their own future judgment either, since none of us has a soundness proof for our own cognition, yet we manage this through track record rather than proof; holding AI to a standard that nothing, including us, actually meets seemed to her like the wrong bar to set.
