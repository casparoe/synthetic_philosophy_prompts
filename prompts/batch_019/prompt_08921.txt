After a long career in experimental nuclear physics, I have recently begun reading in the philosophy of science, and the argument that keeps drawing me back is the pessimistic meta-induction against scientific realism. Every discussion of it I come across leans on the same three exhibits -- phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous ether -- so I would like to test the argument on a case closer to my own training and less worn by repetition: the proton-electron model of the atomic nucleus. Most physicists accepted this picture until James Chadwick's 1932 discovery of the neutron forced its abandonment, in part because it could not account for the measured spin and quantum statistics of nuclei such as nitrogen-14. Please write a dialogue between a scientific realist and a philosopher defending the pessimistic meta-induction who take this episode as their shared touchstone. I would like the realist to try some way of salvaging realist commitments across the transition -- for instance, by arguing that only certain parts of the old picture were ever doing genuine explanatory work -- and the antirealist to press on whether such a distinction can be drawn without the benefit of hindsight. Have the two engage each other's specific moves rather than restate their general positions, and let neither side simply carry the day.
