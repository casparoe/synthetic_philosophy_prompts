I've been asked to give the best man speech at the wedding of two close friends, and I want to build it around a piece of bargaining theory that, once you notice it, describes their courtship uncannily well: the "commitment race" dynamic that shows up in Schelling-style brinkmanship and, more recently, in discussions of multi-agent AI systems (I'm thinking of the way people like Andrew Critch use the phrase). The basic idea, as I understand it, is that in a bargaining situation the side that manages to commit first, credibly and irrevocably, tends to come out ahead -- but when both sides reason this way at once, you get a race toward earlier, more rigid, less deliberated commitments than anyone can really afford, which is the same mechanism behind everything from burned boats to doomsday devices. I've known the groom since a LessWrong meetup in Boston a decade ago; he now works on multi-agent alignment problems and has given at least one talk with the phrase in its title. The bride does arms-control-adjacent policy work and can talk for an hour about Thomas Schelling without being asked twice. Roughly a third of the room will catch the reference and will judge it as specialists; the rest are relatives who have never heard of a Schelling point and just want to hear how these two people fell in love.

Fortunately the couple handed me the perfect material without meaning to. By their own telling, they spent the first couple of months of dating each strategically underplaying how attached they already were, for exactly the reasons a bargaining theorist would predict, until, independently and without telling each other, they each sat down and wrote the other a long letter -- and sent them the same week. I want the toast to name that standoff honestly, as the small, low-stakes commitment race that early dating so often is, and then turn on the observation that a wedding is structurally the opposite of one: not a unilateral, secret, first-mover grab for leverage, but a commitment made in the open, at the same time, in front of everyone, which is exactly what defuses the pathology the theory describes. Could you write this toast for me? It should run about four or five minutes read aloud, contain exactly one clean and correct explanation of what a commitment race is, pitched so the non-technical half of the room gets it on first hearing, and I would rather it be a little less funny than get the game theory wrong, since several guests work on this professionally and will notice. Above all, it needs to read as a tribute to these two specific people, not a LessWrong post with their names swapped in.
