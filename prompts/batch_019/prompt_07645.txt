So in my philosophy club we went down this rabbit hole on the mind-body problem and I got kind of stuck on something called the "causal exclusion argument" (I think Jaegwon Kim is the guy most associated with it?). From what I can tell it goes something like: if physical events already have complete physical causes lined up (neurons firing, whatever), and you don't want to say every effect has two separate sufficient causes just lying around, then there's no real "room" left for mental stuff like beliefs and desires to actually cause anything, unless you just say mental states literally ARE physical states. My friend thinks this basically means non-reductive physicalism (wanting mental properties to be real and not just identical to brain states) is a dead end. But I was poking around online and found out some people, I think it was List and Menzies or something, think the whole argument only bites if you assume causation is like a limited resource that gets "used up," and that if you swap in a counterfactual/difference-making theory of causation instead, mental causes and physical causes stop competing for the same job in the first place, so the "exclusion" kind of dissolves.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people who each hold one of those views hashing it out - like an "exclusionist" who thinks this is a real, forced choice you can't escape, versus a "dissolver" who thinks the whole problem is just an artifact of assuming the wrong theory of causation? I want them to actually respond to each other's strongest points instead of just repeating their own side (neither one should be a strawman, I want the smartest version of both), and it'd help me a ton if they kept coming back to one concrete example the whole way through, like reaching for a coffee cup because you want caffeine, so I can actually picture where the mental cause and the physical cause are suppose to be "competing." It doesn't need to end in agreement, but I'd love it if by the end it's at least clear whether the two of them think this is a genuine, unsolved problem about the mind, or just a symptom of using a bad theory of causation to begin with.
