I've spent the past few weeks reporting a piece on how philosophers and AI researchers formally model rational belief, and one issue keeps coming up that I haven't been able to fully resolve on my own: the "problem of logical omniscience." As I understand it, on the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge — where an agent counts as knowing P just when P holds in every world she hasn't already ruled out — knowledge comes out automatically closed under logical consequence, so any agent knows every tautology and every logical consequence of anything she knows, no matter how many steps of proof it would actually take to derive it. That struck me as pointing to something genuinely deep about how these formalisms represent minds, rather than a defect specific to one particular logic.

When I mentioned this to an AI safety researcher I interviewed for the piece, he was fairly dismissive. In his view, the whole issue is an artifact of treating belief as all-or-nothing, and it mostly disappears once you move to the Bayesian framework that economists and machine-learning researchers actually use: there, an agent's attitude toward a proposition is a graded credence rather than a flat "knows"/"doesn't know," so nothing forces a credence to jump to 1 just because a proposition happens to be a theorem. On his account, probabilistic models of belief were never in the business of assuming logical omniscience to begin with, and the whole debate is essentially a relic of old-fashioned modal logic that Bayesians simply sidestepped. Before I repeat that in print, I'd like a second opinion: does moving to a probabilistic, credence-based picture of belief actually let you escape the problem of logical omniscience, or would I be passing along something that only sounds right? I'd like to understand precisely what the Bayesian move does and doesn't buy you here, since I want the piece to represent the state of the debate accurately rather than repeat a tidy-sounding shortcut.
