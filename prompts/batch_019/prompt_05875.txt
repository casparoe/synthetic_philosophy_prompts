I was at a panel last month for a piece I'm working on tied to the general relativity anniversary (its been 110 years since Einstein's field equations), and the most interesting bit of the whole night actually happened afterward, at the reception, when two of the panelists — an astrophysicist and a philosopher of science who works on confirmation theory — got into a real argument over drinks. It started when the astrophysicist called Mercury's perihelion precession "the single best piece of evidence anyone's ever had for GR." The philosopher stopped her and said that's actually a weirder claim than it sounds, because the ~43 arcseconds-per-century wobble had already been known since Le Verrier flagged it back in 1859, more than fifty years before Einstein worked it into general relativity — so by the time GR "predicted" it in 1915, the probability of that evidence, for literally everyone involved, was already 1. On ordinary Bayesian updating, he said, learning something you already know can't move your credence in anything, so strictly speaking this couldn't have functioned as evidence for GR at all, even though every physicist since, including Einstein himself, has treated it as a triumphant confirmation. He said there's actually a name for this in his field, "the problem of old evidence" — I think he credited it to a philosopher named Glymour, but I didn't write it down so don't hold me to that.

The astrophysicist wasn't having it: if your theory of evidence implies the most famous confirmation in the history of physics wasn't really confirmation, she said, that's a strike against the theory of evidence, not the physics. The philosopher agreed it's a genuine puzzle, and said the standard move is to say what's really new isn't the fact of the precession itself but the logical fact that GR entails exactly that number, which nobody — not even Einstein mid-derivation — was already certain of, so it's updating on that entailment that does the confirming. She shot back that this just relocates the weirdness: now whether Mercury confirms GR depends on the historical accident of who happened to already know a piece of math and when, which seems to make confirmation a fact about people's homework rather than about the world — and anyway, isn't ideal Bayesian rationality supposed to assume agents already know every logical consequence of what they believe? He more or less conceded that, but said the alternative — just accepting that old evidence literally can't confirm anything, ever — seems even harder to live with. Then, almost as an aside, he mentioned that some people try to dodge the whole mess with "counterfactual" credences instead — asking what you'd have believed if you somehow hadn't already known the fact — though he admitted he wasn't sure that move doesn't just smuggle in a new arbitrariness of its own.

They went around like this for a while longer and never really landed anywhere, and I haven't been able to stop turning it over since. Could you go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move — who's got the better of it at each stage, not just who "wins" overall — and say whether you think there's an actual defensible way out of this for a Bayesian, or whether it's a spot where the tidy theory just doesn't match how confirmation obviously works in practice?
