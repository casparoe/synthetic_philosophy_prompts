So I'm writing a dissertation chapter on social epistemology and somehow ended up down this economics rabbit hole that I can't climb back out of on my own, so hoping you can help me think it through properly. Starting point is the no-trade theorem stuff -- Aumann's agreement result and then Milgrom and Stokey's extension of it to markets. The rough idea, as I understand it: if two traders share a common prior, its common knowledge that they're both rational, and the only reason either one would want to trade is because they think they know something the other doesn't, then the mere fact that a trade is being offered should make both sides update enough that any gains from the trade evaporate before a deal actually gets struck. So under those assumptions purely speculative trade basically shouldn't happen -- and yet obviously real markets (stocks, crypto, prediction markets, whatever) have insane trading volume every single day, most of which sure looks speculative.

My advisor's response when I brought this up was basically "yeah, that's a well-known tension, there's like four standard ways people try to dissolve it," but she wouldn't just hand me the answer since it's meant to be my chapter. From poking around, the candidate resolutions seem to be something like: (1) traders don't actually share a common prior -- real disagreement about the model of the world, not just about private info, (2) a lot of the volume is noise traders / people trading for liquidity or hedging reasons that have nothing to do with private information, (3) common knowledge of rationality just fails in practice because nobody actually runs the infinite regress the theorem needs, and (4) in real anonymous markets you don't even get common knowledge that a trade is being offered in the relevant game-theoretic sense, since you don't know who's on the other side of your order. Can you write me an essay that actually weighs these against each other (plus anything I'm missing or getting wrong) and defends a specific view about which one is doing the real work, rather than just listing them and shrugging? And then, as the more philosophical payoff: given whatever you conclude, what should we think about whether market prices are genuinely trustworthy aggregators of dispersed information, versus something more like an artifact of a bunch of non-epistemic trading noise? I'd rather you commit to an actual answer here than hedge on every point.
