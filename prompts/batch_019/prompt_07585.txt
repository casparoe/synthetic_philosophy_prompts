Over the past year, my two brothers and I have been trying to work out how to divide our late father's house and the small hardware store he ran, since his will simply asked that everything be split "fairly" among us without specifying numbers. Things have grown tense, and my sister-in-law, who prides herself on being a shrewd negotiator, keeps urging me to get ahead of my brothers by publicly staking out a firm, unmovable position before they do: telling the whole family, in front of the estate lawyer, that I will not accept a cent less than an equal three-way split, and phrasing it in a way that would be humiliating to walk back later. Her reasoning is that whoever locks themselves into a hard demand first, and makes backing down look costly enough, forces everyone else to bend around them, since they no longer have any room left to negotiate with.

What bothers me is that I'm fairly sure my brothers are getting identical advice from their own spouses right now, and if all three of us dig in with loud, irreversible commitments before we've even sat down together, I don't see how we avoid a legal standoff that drags on for years and eats up most of what the estate is worth in fees, leaving everyone worse off than if none of us had tried to gain the upper hand this way. I mentioned this to a friend outside the family, hoping for a neutral opinion, and she said it reminded her of something she'd once read about, in a completely different context, though she couldn't remember the name for it. I can't just dismiss my sister-in-law's advice either, because "commit hard and commit first" seems to be exactly the strategy that works in plenty of other high-stakes standoffs, from strikes to countries facing off over a border. So I keep going back and forth: is there something actually self-undermining about a strategy that only pays off if you're faster or more reckless than the other side, once you allow that the other side might be reasoning about you in exactly the same way? Or am I just dressing up a reluctance to confront my brothers as some deeper principle? I'd like to understand the real shape of the problem here, not just be told to "communicate better" with my family.
