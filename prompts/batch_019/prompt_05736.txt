So my friend Priya and I got into this whole thing last weekend about van Fraassen's Reflection Principle and neither of us really won. Quick version for context: Reflection says if you're already certain what your credence in some claim will be at a later time, your credence *now* should match that future value -- basically, you're supposed to trust your future self the way you'd trust a well-calibrated expert. I brought up the obvious problem case: say you know that three drinks into tonight's party you'll be pretty confident your fine to drive home, even though right now, stone sober, you think that's a bad bet. Naive Reflection seems to say you should start raising your credence now, which is nuts. Priya said van Fraassen saw this coming -- she brought up the paper where he uses the Ulysses and the Sirens myth to argue Reflection only ever applies when you have no specific reason to distrust your future credence, and obviously you do here (you know alcohol wrecks judgment), so it's not a real counterexample at all. I said fine, but then that whole "no specific reason to distrust" clause quietly requires you to already know in advance which future shifts in opinion count as rational updating versus corruption -- which is exactly what Reflection was supposed to help you figure out in the first place. So the fixed-up version is either circular or so narrow it barely applies to anything real. She didnt think "circular" was a fair word for it at all. We went back and forth for like 45 minutes and got nowhere.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers really working through this -- one defending a properly qualified Reflection Principle, one pressing the circularity/triviality worry -- where they actually respond to each other's strongest moves instead of just repeating themselves? I want the defender to have more in the tank than just re-citing Ulysses, and I want the critic to have to seriously grapple with whether "circular" is even the right charge before throwing it around.
