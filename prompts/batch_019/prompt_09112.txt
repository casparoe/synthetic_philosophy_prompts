While sorting through my late grandmother's papers, I found a 1941 exit-visa application that had been denied and then, nine days before her family's scheduled deportation, approved on appeal — a reversal that, by my great-uncle's account, happened only because a clerk misfiled the original rejection. By that same account, everyone born into my family since, myself included, owes their existence to this single clerical accident. The discovery has left me preoccupied with the long-standing debate over historical inevitability: Tolstoy's contention, in War and Peace, that no single person or accident truly moves history, since every outcome results from countless converging causes, against the view that small, contingent accidents can genuinely tip events that would not otherwise have occurred. Please write a short story, unrelated to my own family, that dramatizes this tension rather than resolving it: construct a pivotal moment that can be read equally well as a sheer accident or as the product of deeper structural forces that would have produced the same outcome by some other route, and leave both readings intact by the story's end.
