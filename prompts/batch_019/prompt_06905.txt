I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, but I recently fell down a rabbit hole after a productivity book at work name-dropped Benjamin Franklin's autobiography, so I went and read the actual chapter on his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection" instead of just trusting the paraphrase. What struck me was how much more mechanical and specific his method was than the popular summary suggests: thirteen named virtues, each with its own precise precept, tracked in a little ruled notebook with a row per virtue and a column per day, one black mark per fault noticed on examination; he worked through the virtues one at a time in a fixed order over a week each, on the theory that mastering an earlier one (like Temperance) would make the next easier, cycling through all thirteen four times a year. Two details stuck with me in particular: his own admission that, of all thirteen, "Order" turned out to be his most persistent failure, far worse than he'd expected; and the anecdote about the man who brings an axe to a smith to have the whole head polished, gets tired of turning the grindstone partway through, and decides he actually prefers a "speckled" axe — which Franklin clearly means as a gentle jab at people who talk themselves out of self-improvement, right before he admits that he himself never got remotely close to the perfection he was aiming for, yet still judged the whole exercise worthwhile.

As an engineer, I can't help seeing Franklin's system as a manual, pen-and-paper version of exactly the kind of "quantified self" infrastructure now built into consumer apps: a fixed schema, daily logging, a repeating cycle — basically a personal issue tracker for virtue. But today's habit-tracking apps (Streaks, Way of Life, Loop, and the like) add a few things Franklin never had to reckon with: streaks that impose steep, all-or-nothing penalties for a single missed day, badges and social leaderboards, push notifications engineered with the same attention-grabbing techniques used by social media, and increasingly, AI features that infer your lapses automatically from your calendar, spending, or screen time instead of requiring the kind of deliberate, private nightly self-examination Franklin describes doing himself.

Given how deliberate Franklin was about the rationale behind his own method — the sequencing of virtues to reduce willpower cost, his mockery (via the axe story) of people who rationalize giving up, and his explicit conclusion that the value of the project lay in the steady effort rather than in ever actually reaching the target — what do you think he would have made of these two features of modern habit apps specifically? First, would he have welcomed AI- or sensor-based auto-detection of faults as a natural extension of his "little book," or argued that outsourcing the noticing of one's own faults defeats the purpose, on the grounds that the nightly act of self-examination was itself doing real moral work that a passive log can't replace? Second, would the streak-based, don't-break-the-chain psychology of these apps — which treats a single lapse as a small catastrophe — sit well with a man who built his entire retrospective account around cheerfully admitting he'd fallen far short of his own standard in every single category and still called the attempt a success? I'd like your best reconstruction of how he'd have reasoned about this, grounded in what he actually says about his method, rather than a generic "Franklin loved self-improvement, so he'd love productivity apps" answer.
