In Garrabrant et al.'s theory of logical induction, a logical inductor is a computable sequence of probability assignments (belief states) over arithmetical sentences that no efficiently computable trading strategy can exploit indefinitely, and it is a theorem that such a sequence converges to credence 1 on every sentence the deductive process eventually proves and to credence 0 on every sentence it eventually refutes, while still assigning sensible, non-dogmatic credences to sentences that remain independent of the base theory. Doesn't this mean a logical inductor is, in effect, a computable procedure guaranteed to arrive at the correct verdict on any well-posed arithmetical question, so that we could in principle use one to mechanically settle open conjectures such as Goldbach's, and Gödelian incompleteness turns out to be a merely practical obstacle to mathematical knowledge rather than an in-principle one? I want to make this point in a paper on the epistemic significance of incompleteness, so I'd like to be sure I have the result right before committing to it in print.
