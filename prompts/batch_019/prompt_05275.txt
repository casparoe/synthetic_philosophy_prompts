Turns out it's by George Berkeley (yes, the "to be is to be perceived" guy from my one philosophy elective, apparently he was also a bishop and wrote about money??). The book's called The Querist, from 1735, and it's basically just hundreds of numbered rhetorical questions about money and banking. I got stuck on this cluster of them (skipping 24, which was clear enough on its own):

"23. Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?

25. Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?"

And then a bit further along in the same section: "Whether power to command the industry of others be not real wealth? And whether money be not in truth tickets or tokens for conveying and recording such power, and whether it be of great consequence what materials the tickets are made of?"

Can you help me reconstruct what's actually being argued here? im stuck on two things - first, he lists "a standard, a measure, or a pledge" as views he's rejecting, right alongside "commodity," but a measure or a pledge sound to me basically like a ticket or counter already, so what's the real distinction he thinks he's making? Second, is the "power" move in that last one doing any real work, or is it kind of circular - money is a ticket for "power," but isn't power to command someone's labor just... money again, described differently? I want to know if there's an actually coherent theory of money buried in here (people apparently cite this stuff as a weirdly early ancestor of fiat-currency thinking) or if I'm over-reading three disconnected zingers, since this is now unfortunately relevant to my actual job.
