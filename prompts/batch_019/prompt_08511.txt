I am working on a piece about attempts to formalize what researchers call logical uncertainty -- rational belief about mathematical claims that have not yet been proved or refuted -- which is exactly where standard Bayesian epistemology runs into the problem of logical omniscience, since an idealized Bayesian reasoner is normally assumed to assign probability 1 to every theorem and 0 to every falsehood purely as a matter of logic. Scott Garrabrant and coauthors at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute addressed this in 2016 with a framework called logical induction, which requires only that no efficiently computable "trader" can guarantee unbounded profit betting against the belief sequence in an ongoing market over sentences' eventual truth-values. I can follow that setup mechanically, but I would like a more intuitive, gut-level sense of why ruling out just this one kind of exploitability is enough to force the resulting probabilities to track logical entailment, converge correctly once a proof appears, and assign sensible, non-arbitrary credence to open conjectures -- about, say, the distribution of prime gaps -- in the meantime. Could you walk me through why the no-exploitation criterion has that much power, ideally without retracing the fixed-point construction itself?
