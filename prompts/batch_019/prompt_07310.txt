I'm a junior at my high school, and this spring I was elected to the five-student panel that judges our school's Arts & Letters Awards, an end-of-year prize given out in five categories: poetry, short fiction, visual art, photography, and original music composition. We received about 140 submissions this year, and with only two weeks before results are due at the spring assembly, our faculty advisor proposed a rule to help us finish on time: in any category where a juror doesn't feel personally qualified to judge — I don't read music, for instance, and another juror can't evaluate the two poems submitted in Spanish — that juror may simply adopt the score given by whichever panelist does have the relevant background, rather than sitting through every submission themselves.

The question stopped being hypothetical over one of the music submissions, a recorded original composition that only one of the five of us felt able to assess on its own terms. Two jurors think outsourcing the score here is obviously fine, comparing it to how everyone already relies on critics or aggregated reviews without personally sitting through every film before trusting a recommendation. Two others think a score that isn't grounded in your own encounter with the piece isn't really your aesthetic judgment at all, and that quietly adopting someone else's verdict misrepresents what the award is supposed to certify, however efficient it is. When I described the disagreement to a friend, she said it sounded like what philosophers call "aesthetic testimony," and that some of them think taste requires actually being acquainted with a work yourself, in a way that just believing a fact on someone else's word doesn't.

Our panel meets again next Wednesday to lock in the judging rules before we start reading in earnest, and I've been asked to write the short opinion I'll circulate beforehand recommending what we should do. Could you draft that memo for me? I'd like it to land on one specific, workable policy — for instance, whether pure deference should ever be allowed at all, whether it should be treated differently from a juror who forms their own impression but then weights a specialist's opinion heavily, and how a tie or split vote should be handled under whichever rule we adopt — explain the reasoning clearly enough that the rest of the panel could actually push back on it, and take the efficiency-and-we-already-trust-experts-elsewhere argument seriously rather than just waving it away.
