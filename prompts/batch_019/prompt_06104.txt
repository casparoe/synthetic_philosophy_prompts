Since retiring from a career in theoretical physics, I have been working my way through the philosophical literature on decision theory, and one problem I keep returning to is the Pasadena game devised by Nover and Hájek (2004). A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and if this happens on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^(n−1) × 2^n / n dollars. Summed toss by toss, the expectation is the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges to ln 2; but because the series is only conditionally, rather than absolutely, convergent, the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees that reordering these very same outcomes and probabilities can yield any sum whatsoever, including positive or negative infinity. Nover and Hájek take this to show that the game simply has no expected value, since nothing privileges the toss-by-toss order over any of its rearrangements. That conclusion strikes me as too quick: in my own field, we routinely tame divergent or conditionally convergent sums by adopting a summation procedure justified by the physical structure of the problem, rather than by treating every mathematically available rearrangement as equally legitimate. Please argue that the toss-by-toss order of the Pasadena game is likewise not arbitrary, but is fixed by the causal and temporal order in which the payoffs are realized as the game unfolds, so that expected-value theory can after all assign the game a determinate value of ln 2, contrary to Nover and Hájek's own stronger conclusion that expectation breaks down here entirely.
