I'm a PhD student working on the metaphysics of laws of nature, and for the last couple of weeks I've been stuck on what David Lewis himself called the "big bad bug" for Humean Supervenience, a phrase he coined in the preface to the second volume of his *Philosophical Papers*. The trouble, briefly, is this: on Lewis's Best System Analysis, the objective chances at a time t are whatever a system that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit with the *entire* history of the world — past and future relative to t — says they are. That opens the door to "undermining futures": continuations of history after t to which the chances at t assign positive probability, but which, were they to occur, would themselves make true a different best system, and hence different chances at t, than the ones that assigned them positive probability in the first place. Since the Principal Principle says that rational credence in a proposition, conditional on the history up to t and on the proposition that its chance at t is x, should equal x, undermining futures seem to generate an outright contradiction between Humean Supervenience and a principle that looks close to a conceptual truth about what chance is for.

In "Humean Supervenience Debugged," Lewis tries to patch this with a reformulated "New Principle" that conditionalizes credence on a full specification of the world's chance-relevant history rather than on the bare chance proposition, and Thau's companion paper in the same issue of *Mind* offers a related fix via a tighter admissibility condition; Lewis himself seems to have thought this settled the matter in the Humean's favor, and most people I've discussed this with in my department treat it as closing off what would otherwise be a genuine advantage for anti-Humean necessitarians. I'm not convinced, and I'd like the strongest version of the skeptical case laid out rather than rely on my own half-formed doubts. Could you argue for the position that neither the New Principle nor Thau-style admissibility restrictions genuinely dissolve the big bad bug, but only relocate it — because getting the reformulated principle to deliver the right verdicts requires conditionalizing on something so strong (essentially, a complete theory of chance for the whole world) that chance can no longer play the role the Principal Principle was designed to secure for it, namely guiding the credence of agents who are not already omniscient about how history turns out? Please address the most natural Humean rejoinder — that admissibility can be cashed out purely in terms of what is fixed at or before t, without any illicit forward reference to the future — before explaining why, if you think it does, this still fails to save the principle's intended use.
