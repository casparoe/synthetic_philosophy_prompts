So for my seminar this term — it's called "Russell, Wittgenstein, and the Unity of the Proposition," yes that's the actual title, very niche I know — we spent this week on Bradley's regress. Setup, in case it's useful: take the sentence "Desdemona loves Cassio." Its truth isn't just the three things Desdemona, loving, and Cassio sitting in a pile together — a pile doesn't say anything, doesn't distinguish "Desdemona loves Cassio" from "Cassio loves Desdemona," and isn't true or false. Something has to unify those constituents into one proposition (or fact, depending who you ask). Bradley's move is that if a relation (loving) is what does the unifying, you have to ask how the relation itself connects to Desdemona and Cassio — and if the answer is "via a further relation," you're off on a regress that never actually delivers a unity, just an ever-longer list.

After the seminar two people in my cohort, Devi and Marcus, kept going at this on the walk to the train, and I liked it enough that I wrote down the gist in my notes app afterward because I wanted someone else's read on it. Roughly:

(1) Devi said the regress is genuinely vicious, which means unity can't be supplied by an ordinary relation at all — you need something categorically different (a primitive predication-tie that isn't itself just another term waiting to be related). Otherwise you can't even explain why "Desdemona loves Cassio" differs from "Cassio loves Desdemona," since a bare unordered pile {Desdemona, loving, Cassio} has no way to encode direction.

(2) Marcus said that's overcomplicating things: the regress never actually gets started, because asking "what relates the relation to its terms" is a category mistake — like asking what makes a hammer's hitting the nail hit the nail. Relating just is what a relation does, not a job it needs outside help with.

(3) Devi replied that "a relation just relates, full stop" is exactly the unearned stipulation Bradley's attacking — if bare assertion is allowed to stop a regress, the nominalist could just as well say two red things are "just similar, full stop" and skip positing relations or universals in the first place. Also, "it just relates" still hasn't said which way it relates, so the direction problem from (1) is untouched.

(4) Marcus conceded direction needs its own story but said Russell already gave one, in the Principles of Mathematics: relations have a built-in "sense," so "greater than" running from A to B is a different, oriented fact from the same relation running from B to A, and that sense is a feature of the relation itself, not a further relatum needing its own unifier. No regress, and no bare stipulation.

(5) Devi pointed out Russell dropped that whole picture a few years later for the multiple-relation theory of judgement — trading "the proposition is a free-standing unified thing" for "it's the mind's act of judging that ties the objects together" — and she thinks he only did that becuase the unified-proposition-with-a-built-in-sense story turned out to be unworkable, especially for false judgements (what is a false unified proposition even made of, if not a fact that doesn't hold?).

(6) Marcus shot back that the multiple-relation theory was a worse trade, since it's the very theory Wittgenstein went after for letting in nonsense: on Russell's later view, judging is just an unstructured relation between a mind and a pile of objects "in some order," with nothing to stop someone from "judging," say, that this is identical, or any other combination that couldn't correspond to a possible fact. If anything, Marcus said, Russell's own trajectory shows his first instinct — some primitive unifier, sense included — was closer to right.

Neither of them gave an inch, and honestly it also seemed to me like the whole time they were treating this as a two-horse race between "primitive unexplained tie" and "a relation just relates, by nature, done" — like there wasn't a third option anywhere on the table. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (6), and say who's got the better of the argument at each step, rather than just handing out an overall winner? I'd also genuinely like to know if there's a serious alternative to the two positions they kept bouncing between, since that's the part still bugging me. Feel free to give an overall call at the end too, but don't feel like it has to be "Devi" or "Marcus" — I don't think either of them was right the whole way through.
