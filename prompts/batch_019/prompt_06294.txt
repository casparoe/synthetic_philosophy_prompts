I'm about two-thirds through a novel where the protagonist, Noor, is a field linguist embedded for four years with an isolated community that has no writing system and no bilingual speakers to lean on, trying to build a grammar and dictionary completely from scratch. For the linguistic backbone of the book I've been reading around Quine's old "gavagai" scenario -- a rabbit runs past, the native says "gavagai," and the linguist can never quite be sure if that means rabbit, undetached rabbit-parts, rabbit-stage, or something else entirely -- which felt like perfect material for a slow-burn epistemic crisis in the middle third of the book.

Here's where I've hit a snag though. In the arc I'd plotted, Noor basically brute-forces her way passed the ambiguity: she stays long enough, and cross-references thousands of hours of recordings of what people will assent to or dissent from under every stimulus condition she can engineer (whole rabbits vs. just the ears, yesterday's rabbit vs. today's, one rabbit vs. several), until the sheer weight of behavioral evidence pins down the one correct translation manual -- the same way any noisy estimate gets less noisy the more data you throw at it. My friend, who's finishing a philosophy PhD, insists this whole resolution is a category error, and that Quine's actual point was that no quantity of fieldwork, not even a completed and exhaustive one, would ever settle it. That sounds way to strong to me -- isn't "more data eventually resolves an underdetermined problem" just true, as a general rule? Which of us has this right, and if it's her, what exactly am I not getting about why more data can't do the work I want it to do for Noor?

I don't necessarily need a plot fix (though I'll happily take one) -- mostly I just want to actually understand the shape of Quine's argument well enough that the novel doesn't quietly misrepresent it.
