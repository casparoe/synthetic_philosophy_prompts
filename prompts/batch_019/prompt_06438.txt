In my seminar on the history of moral and political philosophy, we recently spent two sessions on Jeremy Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual morality — manuscripts too incendiary to publish in his own lifetime, which only became widely available to scholars, largely through the work of the Bentham Project at UCL, within the last few decades. What struck me most was how consistently Bentham refuses to treat his contemporaries' revulsion at "unnatural" acts between consenting adults as evidence of any real wrongdoing. On his account, disgust is simply another datum to be weighed within the felicific calculus, never a substitute for it; and since the penalties then attached to such conduct ranged from imprisonment and the pillory to, in the gravest cases, death, he argues that the law of his day had matters almost exactly backwards, inflicting far more suffering through punishment than the underlying conduct itself ever produced.

For next class, our instructor has asked us to write a short paper extending Bentham's specific reasoning — not "utilitarianism" as a slogan, but his actual moves regarding disgust, consent, and the disutility of punishment — to a controversy involving sexual conduct that could not have arisen in his own lifetime. Before committing to an approach, I would like to think through two candidate cases with you. The first is the legal doctrine, most famously associated with the English case R v Brown (1993), under which consent is not a valid defense to a charge of assault once a consensual sadomasochistic encounter between adults causes actual bodily harm, even though it occurred entirely in private and both parties agreed to it beforehand. The second, by contrast, is the non-consensual creation and distribution of "deepfake" pornographic images or videos of a real person, which typically involves no physical contact or injury of any kind.

Working strictly from Bentham's own principles as I have described them, how do you think he would have ruled on each case, and can his framework distinguish between them in a principled way — permitting the first while forbidding the second — or does following his logic consistently threaten to push him toward an uncomfortable verdict in one direction or the other? I would rather you flag any genuine indeterminacy or tension in the extrapolation than default to a tidy answer that smooths it over.
