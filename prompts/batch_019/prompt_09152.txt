I've been working through some philosophy of biology material on rival theories of what it means for a trait to have a biological function, and I keep getting stuck on a few cases that seem to pull the two leading accounts apart. The etiological (or "selected-effects") account, associated with Larry Wright and later refined by writers such as Karen Neander and Ruth Millikan, holds that the function of a trait is whatever effect explains why that trait was selected for and is still present in the population—function ascriptions are, at bottom, claims about a trait's selective history. The rival causal-role account, due to Robert Cummins, instead defines a trait's function ahistorically, as whatever it contributes to some capacity of a containing system, relative to a chosen analysis of how that system works; on this view, the heart's function is to pump blood simply because that is the contribution assigned to it within a sensible analysis of the circulatory system, regardless of how the heart came to be there in the first place.

I'd like you to compare the two accounts by working through three cases that are often used to pull them apart, saying for each which account gives the more defensible verdict and why. First, malfunction: a heart with a congenital defect that has never once managed to pump blood adequately, yet which we still want to say has the function of pumping blood. Second, co-opted traits: structures that arose for one reason and were only later recruited for another, such as feathers, which the fossil record suggests were present on non-flying dinosaurs (plausibly for insulation or display) well before being co-opted for flight, or the more contested case of whale and dolphin pelvic bones—long treated as a textbook vestige—which some researchers now argue are maintained by sexual selection because of the reproductive musculature they anchor. Third, the "liberality" problem: the worry that, absent some further constraint, the causal-role account seems equally happy to say that one function of the heart is to produce a thumping sound audible by stethoscope, since that too counts as a causal contribution to some describable capacity of the body.

Once you've worked through the cases, tell me your overall assessment: is one of these two accounts generally superior, or does the suggestion—associated with Peter Godfrey-Smith—that etiological and causal-role theorists are really answering two different questions ("why is this trait here?" versus "what does this trait do for the system now?") dissolve the disagreement rather than resolve it? Please defend whichever view you end up holding.
