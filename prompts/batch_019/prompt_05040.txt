I spent much of last night arguing with a friend from my seminar on Augustine and late antique ethics, and neither of us budged, so I would like to see the argument made properly by someone outside our back-and-forth. We had been assigned De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium — the latter written against Catholics who argued it was permissible to infiltrate the Priscillianist heretics by posing as heretics themselves — and my friend defends what I take to be the standard reading of Augustine's absolutism: that his blanket prohibition on lying exists to protect the person being deceived, who has something like a standing claim on the truth, so that deceiving him wrongs him directly, much as theft wrongs the person whose property is taken. On that reading, the famous eightfold ranking of lies, running from lies told about religious doctrine at the worst end down to lies told purely to shield someone's body from violation at the mildest, is essentially tracking how much damage each kind does to the deceived party and to the wider fabric of trust that truthful speech sustains.

Over dinner I argued for a different reading: that Augustine's doctrine is at bottom self-regarding, concerned with the liar's own soul and with the integrity of a speech-faculty God gave for a single purpose, rather than with whatever the deceived party stands to lose. What convinces me is that even the most sympathetic category in the eightfold scheme — a lie told purely to save another person's body from harm — is still classified as a sin, however minor, and that Augustine defends this with Paul's rule, in Romans 3:8, against doing evil that good may come, a rule that locates the wrong entirely in what the liar does to himself rather than in any injury to the hearer. His handling of Rahab, whom scripture praises for lying to protect the Israelite spies, points the same way: he credits her with faith and mercy but stops well short of calling the lie itself anything but a fault, forgivable rather than good — a strange thing to insist on if the whole point of the prohibition were to vindicate the person who might otherwise have been deceived. My friend thinks this reading imports a modern, agent-centered concern with moral purity that the fourth- and fifth-century texts don't really support, and that I am cherry-picking the hard cases to make it work.

Could you lay out the strongest version of my argument — that Augustine's ethics of lying is best understood as a self-regarding doctrine of the liar's own integrity rather than an other-regarding doctrine grounded in the deceived party's right to the truth — making full use of the eightfold typology, the Romans 3:8 appeal, and the Rahab case, and engaging seriously enough with my friend's objection to say what, if anything, actually survives it?
