In one of the best-known passages of Leibniz's Monadology (§17), he asks us to imagine a machine built so that it could think, feel, and perceive, and then to picture that machine enlarged, keeping all its proportions, so that one could walk around inside it as inside a mill. Touring the inside, he says, we would find only parts pushing on one another, and nothing among them that could explain so much as a single perception; he concludes that perception and thought can never be explained mechanically, and must instead belong to a simple, non-composite substance rather than to any machine or aggregate of parts, however intricate.

I've been re-reading this passage for a seminar on early modern philosophy of mind at around the same time I've been following the debate over whether large language models genuinely understand the language they produce, or are instead, in Emily Bender and her coauthors' phrase, "stochastic parrots" recombining statistical patterns with no comprehension behind them. It struck me that a transformer is arguably just a modern-day version of Leibniz's mill: an enormous arrangement of simple parts (matrix multiplications over billions of parameters) pushing values around according to fixed rules, with no single part or layer in which anything like understanding could be said to live. But Leibniz's actual position is more than just the mill image: it also involves his distinction between genuine unities (monads) and mere aggregates, his claim that monads are "windowless" and coordinated with the world only through a pre-established harmony rather than any real causal exchange, and his insistence that a natural organism remains an organized whole down to its smallest parts in a way that no man-made mechanism does.

Taking that whole apparatus into account, rather than just the mill passage in isolation, how do you think Leibniz would actually rule on the present-day dispute over LLM understanding? Would his framework simply hand the "stochastic parrots" side a decisive, centuries-old argument, or does the panpsychist strand in his metaphysics -- on which even the smallest bits of matter are ultimately grounded in perceiving monads -- give him more resources than a modern mechanist critic to say that something interesting might, in some attenuated sense, be going on inside the mill after all? I would rather see how the actual moving parts of his system bear on the question than get a one-line verdict.
