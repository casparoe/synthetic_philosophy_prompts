My mother was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's last year, and she now gets through her days with a notebook she constantly updates and rereads -- names, addresses, what she did that morning -- in a way that maps unsettlingly well onto Clark and Chalmers' famous Otto, whose notebook they argued was as much a part of his memory as anything stored in his brain. I'm giving the toast at her sixty-fifth birthday dinner this weekend, for a room split between relatives who've started speaking of her in the past tense and the philosophy reading group she's belonged to for thirty years, who will catch the Otto echo instantly and won't forgive a toast that treats it as decoration. Write a three-to-four-minute toast that is willing to actually defend, or at least seriously entertain, the claim that the notebook doesn't sit outside a shrinking mind but partly constitutes her memory the way Inga's biological recall constitutes hers, while reckoning honestly with the strongest objection to that view -- Adams and Aizawa's charge that being reliably coupled to a resource isn't the same as that resource being part of one's cognition. It has to land on a real position rather than hedge, and it has to move people who've never heard of the extended mind thesis, not read like a paper delivered aloud.
