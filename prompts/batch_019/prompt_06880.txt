I sit on the repertoire committee of an opera company finalizing next season's programming, which includes an unaltered, traditional production of Mozart and Da Ponte's Don Giovanni. A faction of the board, backed by a major donor, argues that staging Giovanni's seductions—including the catalogue aria's tally of conquests—without any directorial signal of condemnation is indefensible in a post-#MeToo climate, and wants either a substantially reconceived staging or the production's withdrawal; other members reply that demanding an opera render an explicit moral verdict on its characters would falsify the work and set a troubling precedent for the rest of the repertoire. Both factions invoke Bernard Williams, who wrote appreciatively about this very opera and was known for resisting the idea that ethical life can be codified into general rules applied from an impartial standpoint. Draft the committee's decision memo: recommend proceeding as planned, reworking the staging and dramaturgy, or withdrawing the production; attach a concrete timeline; and take a real position on what Williams's views actually commit the committee to, engaging seriously with the strongest argument on the side you reject rather than treating his name as a rhetorical trump card for either faction.
