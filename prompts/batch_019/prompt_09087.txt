Hey, I'm putting together a short paper on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition for a workshop next month (might also just steal parts of it for my metaphysics seminar), and I pasted the guts of the argument below. Its pretty rough still, more like structured notes than actual prose, but I want a sanity check before I embarass myself in front of people who work on this stuff for a living.

Here's the core of it:

Bradley's point, stripped down: if a fact is just its constituents (a, the relation R, and b) plus R relating them, we need to ask what makes R actually relate a and b rather than just sit next to them in a list. If the answer is "a further relation R2 holding between R, a, and b," we're off on the regress, and we never reach a stage where we actually have a unified fact rather than an ever-longer inventory of items.

The standard move against this — you see versions of it from Frege's unsaturated concepts all the way through the "special relations/ties don't need further relating, they just relate" line you find in the more recent literature on states of affairs — is basically to designate one ingredient (the concept, the tie, the relation-in-its-relating-role) as immune to the demand that generates the regress in the first place. I don't think this actually solves anything. It just relabels the explanandum as the explanans. Saying "the tie doesn't need tying because it's the kind of thing that just ties" is structurally identical to the old dormitive-virtue joke — it tells you there's a difference between {a, R} the list and [a-R'd-to-b] the fact, but not what the difference consists in.

So I like the more recent act-based line better (the Hanks/Soames-ish view): unity isn't a further ingredient sitting inside the fact or proposition at all, its supplied by something's doing something — an act of predicating, performed by a mind or fixed by convention. That at least locates the unifying work in the right category (an activity) instead of stipulating a magic noun.

Except — and this the bit I can't get past — doesn't the very same question just resurface one level up? An act of predicating F of a still seems to have parts (an agent or act-type, a property, an object), and now I need those unified into one act-token rather than a mere trio. If I can't say anything better than "acts just unify their contents, that's what acts do," I've helped myself to exactly the move I was complaining about two paragraphs ago.

Can you tell me: (1) is the dormitive-virtue complaint against the unsaturatedness/primitive-tie camp actually fair, or am I flattening a view that has more resources than I'm crediting it with; (2) does my last objection actually sink the act-based response, or is there a principled reason acts are regress-immune in a way ties aren't; and (3) is any of this remotely new, or have I just reinvented a wheel from some paper I read years ago and forgot the source of. Brutal honesty over politeness, please.
