Me and my friend Dana got into it last night over whether those hyper-targeted political ads (the ones where an algorithm builds a psych profile off your browsing history and shows you exactly the framing that's going to push your buttons) actually count as manipulation, or whether they're just persuasion like campaigns have always done, just with alot better data now. Dana's position was basically: as long as the ad isn't lying to you, it's fair game, that's just knowing your audience. My position was that something's different when the "audience" is you specifically, reverse-engineered down to your exact fears and insecurities, even if every claim in the ad is technically true. We argued about it for way too long and kept hitting the same wall: neither of us had a definition of "manipulation" that actually did the work of seperating it from ordinary persuasion, without either letting in stuff that's obviously fine (emotional charity appeals, a friend guilt-tripping you into calling your mom) or excluding stuff that felt obviously manipulative to both of us.

Can you write me a dialogue between two people who've actually thought this through carefully, like two philosophers who each have a real, developed position and aren't just repeating "it depends on intent"? I want one of them to hold something like: what makes an influence attempt manipulative, rather than legitimate persuasion, is that it works by exploiting psychological levers - fear, bias, insecurity, whatever - that route around a person's capacity to actually weigh reasons, regardless of whether the person ever finds out how they were influenced. And I want the other to argue that this "bypassing" idea can't be what's really doing the work, since tons of obviously-fine persuasion (emotional rhetoric, appeals to loyalty, even just good teaching) also routes around careful step-by-step reasoning sometimes - and instead defend something more like: what actually matters is whether the method is one the influencer would be totally comfortable being upfront about, i.e. manipulation is fundamentally about concealment, or about disregarding whether the target would go along with it if they understood what was being done to them. I want them to really push on each other's weak points - can the first view avoid calling stuff like MLK's rhetoric manipulative, and can the second view avoid the weird result that a technique stops being manipulation the second it's publicly disclosed even if people keep falling for it anyway. It doesn't need a tidy resolution at the end - honestly I'd prefer if it ended with the disagreement narrowed but still genuinely open, since that's closer to how our actual argument went.
