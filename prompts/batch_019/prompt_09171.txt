I've spent enough time on rationalist blogs and forums that Kurt Gödel's ontological proof of God's existence feels almost like old hat by now — it gets trotted out fairly regularly as an example of taking a strange argument seriously purely on the strength of its logical form. What gets much less attention, I think, is that Gödel also worked out an argument for personal survival after death that he considered independent of any particular religious doctrine, plus a separate, more technical line of thought from his physics that I think bears on the same question, even though he never explicitly connected the two. I've tried to reconstruct both below and would like your comments on the essay before I fold it into something longer.

Gödel's most direct case for an afterlife appears in a letter to his mother, Marianne, apparently written to talk her out of a fear of death. He does not appeal to scripture or to any specific creed. Instead, he starts from what he calls the rationality of the world: the conviction that the way things are arranged is not senseless or arbitrary but has an intelligible point to it, the way a well-constructed proof does. He then observes that a person's intellectual and moral faculties are typically developed to their fullest only very late in life, if ever, and that this development is cut short by death almost as soon as it is achieved, so that hardly any of it is ever put to use. On his view, a genuinely rational world would not be arranged this way; it would not invest so heavily in building something up only to discard it unused. A rational world, he concludes, must therefore include some further existence in which those matured faculties get put to work.

There is a second, more technical argument in Gödel's corpus that I think is relevant, even though he never framed it as being about the afterlife. In 1949, alongside a technical paper presenting rotating-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations that permit closed timelike curves, Gödel wrote a philosophical essay arguing that if such a universe is a genuine physical possibility, then the objective "lapse" of time — time as a real, mind-independent flow that leaves the past behind for good — cannot be a necessary feature of the physical world, and so, he suggests, may not be a feature of our own universe either, appearances notwithstanding. If he is right, the finality we ordinarily grant to death — the sense in which a dead person is simply and objectively "in the past," gone for good — may depend on a picture of time that physics does not actually force on us.

I would appreciate your comments, especially on:
- whether the reconstruction of the "rationality of the world" argument above is fair, or whether I am smoothing over a premise that cannot really bear the weight placed on it;
- whether the two arguments actually cohere: one wants a substantive further existence for the developed self to occupy, the other undercuts the objective temporal framework any such "further" existence would have to be located in;
- whether Gödel's afterlife argument is best read as a structural cousin of Kant's postulate of the immortality of the soul in the Critique of Practical Reason — both derive an afterlife from requirements of rational or moral order rather than revelation — and if so, whether it inherits the same vulnerabilities.
