I've been working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments for a paper on eighteenth-century moral psychology, and keep returning to a passage in Part III, in the chapter on the authority of conscience. There, Smith imagines a "man of humanity in Europe" learning that the whole of China has just been swallowed up by an earthquake. He predicts that the man would feel real sorrow, muse gravely on the fragility of human life, perhaps consider what the disaster means for European trade — and then eat his dinner and sleep that night with his usual ease. That same man, Smith notes, would be thrown into genuine distress at the mere prospect of losing his own little finger tomorrow. Smith's point is not that this asymmetry is admirable, but that natural sentiment is far too weak to make us prefer the fate of unseen millions to a trivial private loss; only "reason, principle, conscience" — the authority of what he elsewhere calls the impartial spectator — can override self-love in such cases.

This sits oddly next to Peter Singer's famous drowning-child argument, from "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," which trades on a structurally similar comparison — ruined clothes versus a stranger's life — to conclude that distance and numbers make no moral difference, and that failing to aid distant strangers is not meaningfully unlike walking past a child drowning in a shallow pond. Given Smith's moral psychology, and his sharp distinction between the strict, enforceable duties of justice and the more discretionary virtue of beneficence, how do you think he would have responded to Singer's argument? Does Smith's own framework, taken on its own terms, give him a principled way to resist Singer's conclusion that we are strictly obligated — not merely praiseworthy — for aiding distant strangers at real but modest cost to ourselves, or does it end up committing him to something close to Singer's position despite himself?
