After a forty-year career as a field herpetologist, most of it spent mapping salamander diversity across the southern Appalachians, I now serve, in retirement, on my state's Natural Heritage Scientific Advisory Committee, which recommends listing decisions to the state wildlife agency under a statute modeled closely on the federal "distinct population segment" (DPS) policy for vertebrates. Since retiring I have also started reading philosophy of biology in earnest, and I have come to suspect that the case currently dividing our committee is less a factual disagreement than a philosophical one wearing a lab coat.

Here is the situation. A graduate student's dissertation, built on several years of tissue sampling, shows that a population of stream salamanders confined to a single ridge system is reciprocally monophyletic with respect to its widespread lowland relative, with roughly 4% mitochondrial sequence divergence between the two — comparable to the divergence separating other population pairs in the genus that everyone already treats as full species. Yet the ridge population is not diagnosably different from the lowland form in any character we can score reliably in the field, and where the two meet at the base of the ridge there is a hybrid zone, some three kilometers wide, within which roughly one individual in seven shows mixed ancestry. Half the committee wants to recommend the ridge population for protected status, on the grounds that it is, by any lineage-based reckoning, an independent branch of the tree of life that will disappear forever if the single old-growth cove forest it depends on is logged next year. The other half worries that we would be doing exactly what a well-known critique in the conservation literature calls "taxonomic inflation": letting the outcome we already favor dictate a supposedly independent scientific verdict, when a leaky hybrid zone and a few percent of mitochondrial divergence, if accepted as sufficient, would qualify dozens of other lineages in the state for the same status — far more than the agency could ever act on. My reading since retiring has not settled this for me: everything from Mayr's biological species concept to de Queiroz's "unified," lineage-based concept can be enlisted by either faction, and at least one serious philosopher of biology I've come across, Ereshefsky, thinks the whole expectation of one principled answer here is misguided. If anything, all this has made me warier of how confidently both sides on my committee talk.

It has fallen to me to draft the committee's recommendation memo to our agency liaison ahead of next month's meeting, and I would like your help with a first version. Please write it so that it reaches a specific recommendation — list, decline to list, or recommend some intermediate status such as candidate designation, if that is defensible here — gives a rationale that a sharp skeptic on the committee could actually engage with rather than wave away, and takes the strongest form of whichever side you do not adopt seriously, rather than dismissing it as either sentimentality or bureaucratic caution. I would rather the memo be explicit that "species" status is doing double duty here as a scientific and a legal category than have it pretend the two coincide neatly.
