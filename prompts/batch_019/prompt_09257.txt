Our school's philosophy club spent last Thursday's meeting on a classic thought experiment about free will: a scientist named Black has wired a chip into Jones's brain that will force Jones to cast a certain vote if Jones shows any sign of choosing differently, but if Jones goes ahead and casts that vote entirely on his own, the chip stays dormant and Black never has to step in. In the version we discussed, Jones does decide on his own, so the chip never activates. The discussion question was: is Jones morally responsible for his vote even though, because of the chip, he couldn't have voted any other way?

Two members, Devon and Nora, kept arguing about it after the meeting ended, and I ended up just listening.

(1) Devon said Jones is obviously responsible, because what makes someone responsible for an action is what actually caused them to do it, not what some machine standing by in the wings would have done if things had gone differently. Since the chip never engaged, it played no role in explaining Jones's vote, so the old idea that responsibility requires "being able to have done otherwise" must be wrong.

(2) Nora said that's too quick: Jones did have an alternative available to him. He could have started to waver, which would have triggered the chip and produced the same vote, but through a completely different route -- an externally forced one instead of a self-directed one. That difference in how the vote comes about is itself a kind of alternative possibility, even if the outcome looks identical from the outside.

(3) Devon replied that this "alternative" is worthless for grounding responsibility, because once Jones starts to waver, he has no say in what happens next -- the chip simply takes over from there. An option you can't actually steer toward a different result isn't a real option in the sense that matters; it's a technicality dressed up as freedom.

(4) Nora answered that Devon is just helping himself to the claim that only "steerable" alternatives count, which is exactly what's being disputed. She also raised a separate problem: for the chip to reliably catch Jones wavering before he votes, there has to be some earlier, detectable sign that fixes how he's going to choose -- but if such a sign guarantees the outcome in advance, the story has quietly assumed that Jones's choice was determined all along, which isn't a fair test for anyone who thinks a truly free choice can't be guaranteed in advance like that.

I couldn't tell who was actually coming out ahead. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (4), and say how much each move really establishes -- where each of them is right, and where they overreach? I'd also be curious whether there's something important about the case that neither Devon nor Nora brought up.
