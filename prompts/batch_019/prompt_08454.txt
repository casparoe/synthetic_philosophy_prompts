At work we build products on top of large language models, and a disagreement that keeps resurfacing on our team's internal channels has stuck with me outside of work hours too. One camp talks about the model behind our chat interface as an agent in the making: something that has, or is rapidly acquiring, its own goals and a persistent "self" that carries across a conversation. The other camp — closer to a framing I ran into while reading around the edges of AI safety discourse — describes the base model as more like a simulator: a system that, given a prompt, generates whichever character or "simulacrum" the context calls for (a helpful assistant, a hostile chatbot, a Wikipedia article, a movie villain), without the simulator itself being identical to any one of those characters, in the way a physics engine isn't identical to any object it renders. The "shoggoth wearing a smiley-face mask" image that circulates among AI researchers is basically a cartoon of this same idea: a vast, alien, next-token-predicting process wearing a friendly, RLHF-trained assistant persona as a mask. (A colleague of mine prefers Nick Bostrom's older vocabulary of "oracles," "genies," and "tools" instead of either agent or simulator, in case a third bucket turns out to be more useful.) I can't tell whether "agent" and "simulator" are competing empirical claims about what's actually happening inside these systems, compatible descriptions pitched at different levels of abstraction, or simply intuitive impressions people reach for depending on which behavior last unsettled or reassured them.

I want the narrower question in focus: whether it is more accurate to say that something in these systems has goals and a standing identity, or whether that is always a projection onto a process which, beneath whichever persona happens to be active, functions more like a mechanism for producing personas on demand than like a persona in its own right. You are free to invent your own setting and characters rather than follow mine, but as a possible starting point, you could follow someone — an engineer, a red-teamer, an ordinary user — whose confidence about which description is correct gets eroded, or sharpened, by something specific that happens over the course of the story. I would rather the philosophical content emerge through concrete, telling detail and dialogue than through a character pausing to explain the theory, and I would like the ending to resist a tidy resolution, leaving the "agent vs. simulator vs. something else" question genuinely open.
