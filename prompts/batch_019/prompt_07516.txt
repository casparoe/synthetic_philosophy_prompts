I'm about halfway through a novel where one of the main characters is loosly based on my grandfather, who got out of Hungary during the '56 uprising and never once went back, and I've hit a snag that turned out to be way more philosophical than literary. Every time I actually sit him down to talk for material, he's rock solid consistant: no regrets, he'd make all the same choices again, the old country means nothing to him now. But then there's what he actually does -- a shoebox of his sister's letters nobody's allowed to even move, he still counts and swears to himself in Hungarian when he thinks no one's listening, and he point-blank refused when the rest of the family wanted to sell the house back home, even though it's sat empty for twenty years and costs us money every single year. My writing partner's take is that I should build the character around the actions, because "that's what people actually reveal, the talking is just pride protecting itself." A friend in my workshop pushed back hard on that, said it's presumptuous to decide someone's own account of their inner life is basically a lie just because the behavior makes a better scene, and that maybe he genuinely doesn't miss the place AND just can't be the one who lets go of the last physical thread to his sister -- and that those two things aren't even in tension with each other.

I can't stop turning this over, and at this point it's less about the book and more just bugging me generally. Is there a genuinely principled way to figure out what someone truly wants or cares about when what they say and what they do pull apart like this, or is "just trust the actions" always going to be a bit of a cheat? And if it's not a cheat, how would you even start untangling a case where the action itself could easily be doing something other than expressing the want in question -- like guilt or duty standing in for love of the place?
