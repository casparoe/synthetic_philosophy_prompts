Of all the diachronic rationality norms debated in Bayesian and rationalist circles, Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle strikes me as the hardest to get a stable grip on. First defended in his 1984 paper "Belief and the Will," the principle holds that a rational agent's present credence in a proposition should equal x whenever she is certain that her own credence in that same proposition, at some specified future time, will equal x. The usual justification is a diachronic Dutch Book: an agent who violates the principle can be sold a combination of bets now and conditional bets for later that jointly guarantee her a loss no matter how things turn out, in a way that parallels the synchronic Dutch Book arguments used to justify ordinary probabilistic coherence.

Yet the principle seems to founder on cases of anticipated non-evidential belief change. Suppose I know that tomorrow, purely as a side effect of a medication with no bearing on the matter, I will become fully confident that I left the stove on, despite currently having excellent evidence that I turned it off. Reflection seems to say that I should already be raising my credence today, conditional on that anticipated future certainty, even though the shift in opinion will be produced by a drug rather than by evidence.

Write an essay evaluating whether rational agents should accept Reflection as an unrestricted norm, whether it can instead be rescued by restricting it to future credences the agent expects to reach without any loss or distortion of her rationality, or whether cases like the one above show that it should be abandoned altogether as a constraint on rational belief. Don't settle for a non-committal "it depends" -- defend a specific verdict, and say what, if anything, should take Reflection's place as a norm governing how much an agent ought to trust her own future opinions.
