I'm at work on a historical novel set among London's cooperative societies in the early 1870s, and to keep the politics honest I've taken to running passages past two friends from my research group who both know the period well. Over dinner last week they ended up arguing about whether John Stuart Mill, in his later years, can fairly be called a socialist, and neither would give an inch. I wrote down the exchange as faithfully as I could.

Frances opened by pointing out that in his Autobiography, Mill describes the ideal he and Harriet Taylor had arrived at — combining the fullest individual liberty with common ownership of the raw materials of the earth and an equal share for everyone in the fruits of combined labour — as going beyond democracy altogether, and says outright that it would place them under "the general designation of Socialists." You don't get a plainer self-classification than that from the man himself.

Marcus answered that quoting the line on its own is a bit of a sleight of hand: Mill frames it explicitly as a distant, ultimate ideal, reachable only once human character had been transformed by education, and adds immediately that for now the most anyone should attempt is improving the existing arrangement of property, not overturning it. Most of what Mill actually wrote about contemporary socialism, in the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, is an unsparing practical critique of Owenite and Fourierist schemes — doubts about what would motivate anyone to do unpleasant work, and about whether production could be organised at all without competition. Calling that body of work "socialist" erases the difference between endorsing a destination and endorsing any route there currently on offer.

Frances came back with the point that "someday, not yet" is exactly how most socialists of the period talked about transition, so it can't be used to disqualify Mill in particular; and in any case his proposals weren't merely hypothetical. He wanted steep limits on inheritance so that large unearned fortunes couldn't simply pass down intact, and across successive editions of the Principles of Political Economy he grew steadily more committed to the view that the wage-labour relationship was a transitional stage that cooperative enterprises, owned and run by the labourers themselves, would eventually replace. That is a programme, not just a mood.

Marcus's reply was that cooperatives which displace wage labour only by outcompeting it, with no state confiscation and no compulsion involved, are not socialism in any sense the people actually arguing about socialism at the time would have recognised; they are a direct extension of the position Mill had held since On Liberty, that institutions should earn their place through open trial rather than being imposed. On his view, the real fault line among Mill's contemporaries was compulsory transformation versus voluntary emergence, and Mill sits about as far toward the voluntary end as anyone, which makes him a certain kind of liberal rather than an unusually patient socialist.

Frances's last word was that voluntary-versus-compulsory is a claim about strategy, not about the destination, and that several avowedly socialist and cooperative movements in Mill's own circle wanted a voluntary transition too; if wanting to avoid compulsion is enough to disqualify someone from being a socialist, Marcus's definition quietly reads most actual socialists of the period out of the category as well.

Neither of them would concede anything, so I said I'd ask someone with no stake in the argument. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of each point, and flag whether there's a shared assumption about what "socialism" even refers to here that's doing more work than either of them notices?
