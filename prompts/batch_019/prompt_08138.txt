So this has been rattling around in my head for like two weeks now. I fell down a wikipedia hole that started with me wondering why some drinking glasses shatter if you just look at them wrong while others survive getting knocked off the counter, and that somehow led me into this whole corner of philosophy about how you'd even define a property like "fragile" or "poisonous" in the first place. The obvious answer is something like "x is fragile if x would break when struck," but apparently there are two classic ways people have tried to break that definition, called finks and masks, and I cant get straight in my head why philosophers treat them as two separate problems instead of just one trick wearing two costumes.

The fink one is that live-wire example — a wire hooked up to a gadget that senses when its about to be touched and cuts the power a split second before contact happens (or a dead wire rigged to do the reverse), so whether the wire "would" shock you doesn't match up with whether its actually live right at that moment. The mask one is the poison-and-antidote case — you swallow poison and an antidote at the same time so you dont die, even though the poison never stopped being poison. Both seem to me like cases where the disposition is genuinely there (or not) but the if-then test just gets fooled, so what I actually want is: is the way a fink breaks the definition structurally the same trick as the way a mask breaks it, or are they damaging it for totally different reasons? I also read that somebody (Lewis, maybe?) patched up the definition specifically to survive finks — does that patch hold up against masks too, or is that a whole separate can of worms? And if you had to say which of the two is still the bigger unsolved headache for anyone trying to save a simple if-then definition of dispositions instead of just giving up on it, which one, and why?
