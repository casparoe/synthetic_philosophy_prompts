I'm a junior at my high school and one of three student representatives on our Honor Board, the panel that hears alleged honor-code violations and recommends a sanction to the Dean of Students. A case before us right now happens to be a real-world version of something I read about for a psychology elective: Joshua Knobe's famous "chairman of the board" study. In it, people are told about a company chairman who approves a new, more profitable program despite saying flatly that he doesn't care one way or the other about a side effect he's been warned the program will cause. When that side effect is environmental harm, most people say he did it intentionally; when the very same setup is changed so the side effect is an environmental benefit, most people say it wasn't intentional at all — even though the chairman's actual state of mind, indifferent to the side effect and focused purely on profit, is identical in both versions.

Our case: Jordan, who runs a peer study-group document for an AP class, used an AI tool to bulk-generate worked solutions to a large bank of practice problems so her group would have more material before exams, which our honor code explicitly permits as a collaborative study aid. Jordan has admitted that before uploading the document, she noticed several of the "practice" problems were identical to that semester's official take-home assignment, which students were required to complete alone — but says she didn't care either way and uploaded it anyway because her only goal was exam prep. Several students in her group went on to submit near-identical answers on the graded take-home. Our code defines a violation as "intentional" if a student "acted with the purpose of securing an unfair advantage, or with foresight that the conduct would do so," and half the Board wants to call this intentional facilitation, which carries a mandatory suspension rather than the warning-level consequence we use for merely reckless violations, largely because the outcome was bad. I can't stop thinking that if the overlap had instead accidentally helped a struggling classmate rather than enabled cheating, this same Board would never have called Jordan's foresight-plus-indifference "intentional" anything.

Before we vote, I'd like a short written opinion I can circulate to the rest of the Board: it should recommend how to classify Jordan's case under our existing rule, and it should propose a standard for "intentional" that the Board could apply consistently in future cases regardless of whether a foreseen side effect turns out to be good or bad. What I don't want is a memo that just waves the Knobe effect around as a "gotcha" against my own Board's instincts — if there is a genuinely respectable reason, rather than a mere bias, for treating foreseen harms and foreseen benefits asymmetrically when we assign blame, I want that possibility taken seriously and argued through, not brushed aside.
