I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on intertemporal choice, and while drafting the theoretical framing chapter of my dissertation, a philosopher on my committee pushed back on something I had been taking for granted: that hyperbolic-discounting models simply are an account of weakness of will, or akrasia. That comment sent me back into the philosophy of action literature, and I've come away with a tension I can't resolve on my own, so I'd like your help working through it in essay form.

The classical puzzle, as Aristotle frames it against Socrates in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics and as Davidson revives it in "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?," is synchronic: the akratic agent acts against a judgment that they themselves hold, at the very moment of acting, to be the better one. Davidson's own resolution — distinguishing an all-things-considered ("conditional") judgment favoring one action from the unconditional, "all-out" judgment that actually issues in behavior — strikes me, now that I understand it, as more of a careful redescription of the puzzle than a genuine dissolution of it. Ainslie's picoeconomics, by contrast, models akratic behavior as the product of a hyperbolic rather than exponential discount curve, which generates real preference reversals as a smaller-sooner reward draws near, and frames the whole situation as a bargaining problem between "successive selves" rather than a single agent's synchronic irrationality. My worry, which is essentially what my committee member was pressing on, is that this quietly changes the subject: "acting against one's current best judgment" becomes "one's judgment of what's best shifting over time" — a different, diachronic phenomenon that seems to leave Aristotle's and Davidson's original puzzle completely untouched. Holton's resolution-based account complicates things further by relocating the failure into the all-too-easy abandonment of a prior intention rather than into the shape of the discount curve itself, and there's a further wrinkle I recall from the discounting literature — I believe due to Sozou — showing that a hyperbolic-looking curve can fall straight out of perfectly rational Bayesian updating on an unknown hazard rate of the reward failing to materialize, which would seem to strip any irrationality out of hyperbolic discounting in the first place.

Could you write an essay that treats this as a genuine controversy: does hyperbolic discounting (whether taken alone or via an Ainslie-style multiple-selves picture) actually explain the philosophical phenomenon of akrasia, or does it change the subject in a way that leaves the original synchronic puzzle untouched? Please take both the rational-reconstruction worry (the hazard-rate point) and Holton's resolution-based alternative seriously, rather than treating them as throwaway complications, and don't end on a non-committal note — I need a specific, defended verdict on how much explanatory work a discounting model can and cannot do here, since it will determine how much I need to hedge the framing in my own dissertation.
