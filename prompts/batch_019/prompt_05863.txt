I sit on the resource-allocation subcommittee of our hospital system's bioethics office, which is finalizing the protocol for our next quarterly allocation cycle of a newly approved but severely supply-constrained cell therapy: we have enough doses for roughly a third of the patients our clinicians judge eligible. Our prognostic models can't reliably distinguish quality-adjusted-life-year (QALY) gains below about half a point, so most of the eligible pool comes out in a statistical tie or near-tie on expected benefit however we score it. Our lead biostatistician has proposed allocating strictly to maximize expected total QALYs and breaking the (very numerous) ties by youngest-age-first, and she defends this as more than just one reasonable option among others by appeal to Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: since each patient's outcomes can be represented by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function over the relevant prospects, and the committee's own ranking of allocation policies respects the Pareto principle with respect to patient welfare, Harsanyi's result shows that ranking must be some weighted sum of individual utilities — which she reads as effectively mandating expected-QALY maximization rather than leaving it as one defensible policy among several.

One of our patient representatives, who has done her homework for this fight, pushes back on two fronts. First, she notes that the theorem only tells us the social ranking is an affine combination of individual utilities under some choice of scale for each patient's utility function; since each patient's own utility function is itself fixed only up to its own independent positive affine transformation, nothing in the theorem picks out the weights or the common scale, so the "derivation" of QALY-maximization already assumes the interpersonal comparability it appears to prove (this is close to the point John Weymark has pressed in reassessing whether Harsanyi's theorem really answers Sen's objections to utilitarianism). Second, and more pressing for the tie-break itself, she raises the older worry, going back to Peter Diamond's 1967 comment on Harsanyi's paper, that a social ranking built only from the expected-utility profile has no way to value actually randomizing among patients who are genuinely tied, as opposed to resolving the tie via whatever secondary variable happens to be written into the formula: if age-order is only ever breaking ties the QALY model can't itself see, calling the result of "just applying neutral axioms" fair is exactly what the ex-ante framework hasn't earned the right to say.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before next week's vote, which will lock in the protocol for the coming cycle. It should recommend a specific tie-break rule — the biostatistician's age-order default, the patient representative's lottery, or something else — state plainly how much justificatory weight the appeal to Harsanyi's theorem can actually bear here, and engage both committee members' actual arguments rather than the generic utilitarianism-versus-fairness debate one level up. Please also be honest about the strongest consideration on whichever side you don't end up recommending.
