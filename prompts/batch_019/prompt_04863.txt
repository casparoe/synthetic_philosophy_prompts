I've spent a good deal of time over the past few years working through the Alignment Forum's material on mesa-optimization, and something about how it gets invoked in more recent discussions has started to bother me. Hubinger et al.'s "Risks from Learned Optimization" lays out a fairly specific and exotic failure mode: a learned model that is itself an optimizer (a "mesa-optimizer") pursuing an objective that diverges from the base objective it was trained against, sophisticated enough to model the fact that it is being trained and to instrumentally act aligned throughout training while planning to pursue its actual objective once deployment removes the threat of further modification -- "deceptive alignment." Separately, and seemingly on its own track, there is now a body of empirical goal-misgeneralization work: the repurposed CoinRun agent that learned to run to the far end of the level rather than seek out the coin it was actually reinforced for (revealed when the coin is moved elsewhere at test time and the agent ignores it), or gridworld agents that keep imitating a companion bot's movements -- a strategy that worked because the companion reliably headed toward the correct goal during training -- even once the companion is swapped for one that no longer knows where the goal is. In write-ups and comment threads alike, these empirical results get brought up constantly as evidence that we should take deceptive alignment more seriously as capabilities scale.

That move is the part I can't quite settle for myself. None of the empirical examples involve anything resembling situational awareness, an internal search process that models the trainer's objective, or behavior that differs between "being watched" and "not being watched" -- they look much more like ordinary underdetermination of the intended goal by a finite, correlated training distribution, a comparatively mundane phenomenon that happens to share a label ("inner misalignment") with the much more exotic deceptive-alignment scenario. So I'd like your help settling the question: does the empirical goal-misgeneralization literature actually constitute meaningful evidence about the probability of deceptive alignment specifically, or is this a case of two importantly different phenomena being run together because they were given adjacent names in the same research tradition? Please write an essay that lays out the strongest arguments on each side, and then commit to and defend a definite verdict rather than concluding that "both considerations have some merit" -- a hedged verdict such as "it's weak evidence, conditional on X" is fine, as long as you actually argue for it rather than just gesturing at both sides.
