Jonathan Schaffer's case of trumping preemption was designed as a harder test than the standard rock-throwing examples usually invoked to motivate and refine theories of causation. Suppose a senior officer and a junior officer simultaneously shout the identical order "Advance!" to a platoon of soldiers, and suppose military protocol dictates that when more than one officer gives an order, the soldiers obey whichever one holds the higher rank. The soldiers advance. Intuitively, the senior officer's order, and not the junior officer's, causes them to advance, yet neither order stands in the ordinary relation of counterfactual dependence to that outcome: had the senior officer stayed silent, the soldiers would have advanced anyway, on the junior officer's order, and had the junior officer stayed silent, they would have advanced anyway, on the senior officer's order. Because the two orders are simultaneous and would produce one and the same advance regardless of which is operative, the usual counterfactualist repair for preemption cases -- appealing to the fine-grained, fragile character of the resulting event, or to chains of dependence running through slightly different intermediate stages -- has no purchase here, since there is no difference in the outcome for the asymmetry to hang on.

Write a dialogue between a counterfactual theorist of causation, working in the tradition of David Lewis, and an interventionist, working in the tradition of James Woodward, in which they try to settle what causes the soldiers to advance and why this particular case is difficult for their respective theories. The Lewisian should say what, short of a bare stipulation about rank, could possibly ground the needed asymmetry, and should have to face the charge that any device adequate to the case -- much like Lewis's own revision of the similarity ordering over possible worlds in "Counterfactual Dependence and Time's Arrow," made expressly to secure the temporal asymmetry of causation -- simply encodes the desired verdict rather than deriving it from anything more basic. The Woodwardian should show how to write a structural equation for the variable representing whether the soldiers advance, one that gives the senior officer's order priority over the junior officer's, explain what testing it by intervention would actually involve, and should have to face the parallel charge that building the priority ranking into the equation is no less a stipulation, and that invoking interventions at all reintroduces exactly the causal vocabulary the framework is charged with merely presupposing rather than explaining. Push each side until it can turn the other's signature objection back against that other's own theory, rather than merely fending it off, and don't let either concede more than their theory can actually afford to; the dialogue can end with the disagreement sharpened rather than resolved.
