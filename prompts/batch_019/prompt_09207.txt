For a feature I am writing on how philosophers talk about causal powers — talk that quietly underwrites everything from how scientists describe properties like fragility and solubility to debates about human abilities and free will — I have interviewed several metaphysicians who kept invoking two pieces of technical apparatus without ever quite pausing to explain them for a non-specialist: "finkish" dispositions and "masked" dispositions. Both are supposed to make trouble for the otherwise appealing idea that having a disposition simply amounts to a conditional being true — that a fragile glass, say, just is a thing that would break if it were struck. One philosopher described a wire connected to what she called a kind of sorcerer: a hidden mechanism that kills or restores the current in the instant before contact, so that the wire's behavior at the moment of touching never lines up with what it was truly disposed to do a moment earlier. Another described a glass so carefully padded in packing material that it can be dropped again and again without ever breaking, even though nobody would say its fragility has gone anywhere.

What none of them has managed to spell out for me is why these count as two genuinely different problems rather than one problem told twice. Everyone I have spoken to treats them as requiring separate technical patches to the standard analysis of dispositions, and one source pointed me toward a paper with a title something like "Masked Abilities and Compatibilism" as evidence that the very same apparatus has since been put to work in arguments about so-called Frankfurt cases — scenarios in which someone could not have done otherwise because a third party stood by, ready to intervene but never actually needing to, and yet the person seems to have acted just as freely as anyone else.

I would like a properly detailed explanation of the logic underlying all this: what exactly distinguishes a fink from a mask in terms of what each one does to an object's underlying causal basis as opposed to its route to manifesting that basis; why a repair that handles one of the two problems — for instance, requiring that the same basis persist for the duration of the test — does not thereby handle the other; and how this bears on the free-will case I mentioned, namely whether a standing-but-inactive intervener is better understood as finking or as masking a person's ability to have done otherwise, and why a dispositional compatibilist should care which description turns out to be correct.
