I'm about halfway through a novel whose narrator spends most of the book almost entirely cut off from her body: she has survived a brainstem stroke that has left her with locked-in syndrome -- fully conscious, but unable to move or to feel almost anything below the neck. In trying to imagine my way into that condition a little more precisely, I recently booked a session in a flotation tank: ninety minutes alone in a lightless, soundproofed pod of body-temperature, Epsom-salted water, with no sound, no sight, and so little tactile sensation that after a while I could no longer tell whether my arms were resting at my sides or adrift somewhere above my head. Sometime in the second half of the session, something strange happened: I lost any clear sense of having a body at all, and yet "I" was unmistakably still there -- still thinking, still faintly irritated about a plot problem I hadn't solved, still aware of myself as the one having the experience. In the moment, it felt like unexpectedly compelling evidence that whatever I fundamentally am does not depend on there being a body around to anchor it.

When I described this afterward to a friend who works in cognitive science, she was unconvinced. All I had shown, she said, was that the brain's model of the body degrades gracefully rather than switching off when you starve it of input, in much the same way a phantom limb or the rubber-hand illusion reveals the brain assembling a sense of embodiment out of whatever fragments of signal happen to be available -- and that a "self" persisting through the experience proves nothing, any more than the persistence of a dreamer proves anything about the metaphysics of waking life. I found this unsatisfying but couldn't quite articulate why it felt too quick, and I would rather not build a scene in the novel around an insight I haven't actually examined properly. So I would like your honest, considered opinion rather than a tour of the possibilities: did that hour and a half in the tank reveal something real and philosophically significant about the relationship between the self and the body, or was my friend right that neuroscience already explains the whole episode away, with nothing left over for philosophy to add?
