I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this semester, and for the past few weeks we've been working through David Lewis's papers on objective chance -- first "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance," and now "Humean Supervenience Debugged." I think I have a reasonably firm grip on the Principal Principle in its basic form: rational credence in a proposition should match its known objective chance, provided one's other evidence is "admissible." What I'm stuck on is the problem Lewis discusses, half-jokingly, under the heading "the big bad bug" -- the idea that, given Humean supervenience about chance, a chance hypothesis can assign positive chance to courses of history that would, if actual, make that very hypothesis false.

I follow this at a fairly abstract level, but I can't get an intuitive fix on why it counts as a genuine problem rather than a curiosity that dissolves on inspection, or on why ordinary conditionalization is supposed to run into trouble because of it. Could you walk me through, informally and with the help of a concrete toy example (a biased coin or a decaying atom would do), why "undermining" futures generate an apparent inconsistency when the Principal Principle is applied to a complete chance hypothesis? I'd also like to understand, at the same intuitive level rather than in full formal notation, how Lewis's move to the "New Principle" -- conditioning on history and the chance proposition jointly, rather than treating admissibility as an independent constraint on evidence -- is supposed to get around the difficulty, and why some commentators have suggested that it merely relocates the problem rather than solving it. Our seminar discussion of this got fairly heated last week without really settling anything, so I'd like a clearer picture before we return to it next class.
