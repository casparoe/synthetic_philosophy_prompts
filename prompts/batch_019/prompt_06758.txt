I'm taking a graduate seminar on epistemology this term, and I'm drafting a term paper on Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and what it implies for the KK principle. Before I bring a full draft to my professor, I'd like feedback on the core section reconstructing the argument, reproduced below. Could you tell me: (1) whether the reconstruction is valid and faithful to Williamson's actual reasoning, (2) whether the extension to KK in the third paragraph is properly set up or is missing a needed premise, and (3) whether the worry I raise in the final paragraph, along with my tentative response to it, amounts to a real objection or whether I'm chasing something with an easy answer I'm not seeing. General comments on where the prose is unclear or overreaching would help too.

Draft excerpt:

Call a condition C luminous if, whenever C obtains, one is thereby in a position to know that it obtains. Williamson's target is the thought that some non-trivial conditions — paradigmatically phenomenal or introspectible ones, such as feeling cold — are luminous in this sense. His strategy is to show that luminosity conflicts with an independently plausible margin-for-error principle governing inexact, discrimination-based knowledge.

Take a slow warming process sliced into instants t_0, ..., t_n, such that at t_0 the subject is clearly freezing and at t_n clearly not cold, where the change between any two consecutive instants is too small to be discriminated. The margin-for-error principle says that since the subject's capacity to sort cold cases from non-cold cases is not infinitely fine-grained, a belief that one feels cold at t_i counts as knowledge only if the same belief, formed on the same basis, would not have been false in a sufficiently close case — in particular, at t_{i+1}. So if the subject knows at t_i that she feels cold, she must also feel cold at t_{i+1}. Now suppose, for reductio, that feeling cold is luminous. Feeling cold at t_i then entails knowing one feels cold at t_i, which by the margin principle entails feeling cold at t_{i+1}. Chaining this from t_0 to t_n delivers the absurd conclusion that the subject feels cold at t_n, where by hypothesis she does not. Luminosity therefore fails: somewhere in the series, she feels cold without being in a position to know it.

Williamson takes the same pattern to sink the KK principle (Kp → KKp), since KK just says that knowing is itself luminous. Run an analogous series in which the evidential support for some p weakens by continuous, indiscriminable degrees: there will be a point at which the subject still knows p — her belief is still safe enough — but does not know that she knows p, since that further belief would be no different from the belief of a subject one step over, for whom it is false.

Here is where I get stuck. The argument against KK needs the second-order state — knowing that one knows — to inherit the same margin-for-error structure as a raw sensory state like feeling cold. But knowing is not phenomenal the way a temperature sensation is; there is nothing it is like to sit at the threshold of knowledge, the way there is something it is like to be borderline cold. So it isn't obvious that unreliable introspective discrimination is what drives the failure of higher-order knowledge, rather than something about the concept of knowledge itself. My tentative response is that this doesn't matter: any view that makes knowledge require safety or reliability thereby makes reliability a matter of degree, and degree properties always support series of indiscriminable neighbors, phenomenal or not. But I can't tell whether that response is doing real work or is just restating the safety condition in different words.
