I am currently taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's unit has centered on accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory. We worked through Jim Joyce's argument that every credence function which violates the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated by some probabilistically coherent alternative, according to any legitimate scoring rule, and then proved what my professor called the "immodesty" result: provided the scoring rule is strictly proper, every probabilistically coherent credence function expects itself, by its own lights, to be strictly more accurate than any other credence function it could adopt instead. In other words, no alternative assignment of degrees of belief ever looks better to you, right now, than the one you already hold.

This result has been bothering me since section, and a couple of classmates I discussed it with afterward could not talk me out of the worry either. If my current credence function is guaranteed to regard itself as more accurate than every alternative, then updating on new evidence just amounts to trading my present credence function for a different one — and by immodesty, that different function should look strictly worse to me, from where I currently stand, than simply staying put. It seems to follow that accuracy-first epistemology actually predicts that conditionalizing on new evidence is never something your prior credence gives you positive reason to do, and is in fact predicted to make you less accurate in expectation. That sits very badly with the fact that this very framework is supposed to vindicate Bayesian updating as rational in the first place, and with the plain idea that gathering evidence before settling one's opinions is epistemically valuable. Have I misunderstood what the immodesty result actually says, or is this a genuine tension in the accuracy-first program between its argument for probabilism and its treatment of updating?
