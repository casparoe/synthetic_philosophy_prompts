I spent thirty-eight years doing condensed matter physics before I retired, and quantum foundations was always the stuff we waved our hands at in journal club and left to the theorists down the hall. Now that I've got time on my hands I've been reading Bell's old papers properly for the first time -- including the 1976 one where he talks about "local beables" -- plus a stack of philosophy of physics books my daughter (the actual philosopher in the family) keeps leaving on my desk. What's been bugging me for weeks is really a question about what Bell's theorem rules out and what it doesn't: everybody agrees the inequalities are violated and something has to go, but the Bohmians and the many-worlds people both insist they get to keep locality, and I don't see how they can both be right about that in the same sense. The Bohmians admit their theory has honest-to-god instantaneous action at a distance but say thats fine because you still can't signal with it; the many-worlds crowd say there's no nonlocal influence at all, just branching, because Bell's proof quietly assumes every run of the experiment has a single outcome, which they deny ever happens.

So here's what I'd like: a dialogue between a committed Bohmian and a committed Everettian who both know the technical literature cold, hashing this out face to face. Please don't give me a rehash of Einstein-and-Bohr shadow-boxing about "spooky action" -- I want them disagreeing about something more specific: whether "you can't send a signal" is the same thing as "local," whether denying a single outcome per run is really enough to earn the word "local," and whether a wavefunction that lives on a non-separable configuration space smuggles nonlocality back in even if nothing ever "acts" on anything faster than light. Make them actually respond to each other's best move rather than each just restating their own side -- I'd like to end up less confused about what "local" means than when I started, not more.
