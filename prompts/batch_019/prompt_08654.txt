A friend and I spent last night arguing about Frederick Douglass's constitutional theory and never managed to settle it, so I would like a more disinterested judgment. After breaking with Garrison and the "no union with slaveholders" wing of the abolitionist movement, Douglass argued, especially in his 1860 speech on whether the Constitution was a pro-slavery or anti-slavery document, that the text must be read according to its plain words and the ordinary rules used to construe any legal instrument, and that whatever the framers privately wished or believed about slavery is beside the point once the document is ratified. My friend took this as evidence that Douglass, dropped into today's debates, would side with living constitutionalists, since his whole project was bending the document toward justice. I argued the opposite: that his insistence on the plain public meaning of the text over the framers' hidden intentions sounds much closer to a forerunner of the "original public meaning" originalism associated with judges like Scalia than to anything a living constitutionalist would recognize as their own method. Which of us is closer to right, and how do you think Douglass would actually have responded if confronted with the modern originalism-versus-living-constitutionalism debate?
