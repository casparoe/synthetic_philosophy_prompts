I run my high school's philosophy club, and while prepping our next session on experimental philosophy I keep getting stuck on the Knobe effect. In Knobe's original case, a chairman adopts a policy purely to raise profits, knowing it will also harm the environment as a side effect, and most people call that harm intentional; when the identical policy instead helps the environment as a side effect, most people say the help was not intentional, even though the chairman's stated indifference to the environment is exactly the same in both versions. I've come across two rival explanations for this asymmetry that I can't adjudicate between. Knobe himself argues it isn't a bias at all: our ordinary concept of intentional action is partly constituted by moral evaluation, so factoring in moral badness is just competent use of the concept. Edouard Machery argues morality is beside the point: what actually drives the judgment is whether the agent knowingly accepted a cost to get something he wanted, and he claims to reproduce the same asymmetry with vignettes that have no moral content at all, just costs and benefits. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers, one defending each side, who've just given back-to-back conference talks on this and end up arguing about it over coffee afterward? I want real back-and-forth, with each pressing the other's weakest point directly -- including whether Machery's "cost" secretly smuggles in the same evaluative content he claims to have eliminated, and whether Knobe can explain why the identical asymmetry shows up for concepts like "deciding" and "wanting," which have no obvious cost-benefit structure. Keep both philosophers equally sharp, with each line actually answering the point just made rather than restating an opening position.
