"Expressivists think moral sentences like 'lying is wrong' don't state facts, they express an attitude, like disapproval of lying. The Frege-Geach problem is that this seems to break down once the sentence gets embedded somewhere it isn't being asserted, e.g. inside a conditional:

1. Lying is wrong.
2. If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
3. So, getting your little brother to lie is wrong.

This is a valid modus ponens, but in premise 2 the speaker isn't asserting that lying is wrong, it's just the antecedent of a conditional, so its unclear what attitude is even being expressed there, and expressivists need to explain why the argument stays valid even though the sentence isn't doing the same job twice. Since this only comes up when the sentence is embedded under a connective like "if...then," the problem is really about expressivism's account of logical connectives, and it wouldn't arise for a moral sentence that's never embedded that way.

Blackburn's quasi-realism deals with this by saying moral judgements express plans for action, and that it's inconsistent to plan to do act A yourself while also disapproving of anyone else doing A in a relevantly similar situation. That inconsistency-of-plans is supposed to mirror ordinary logical inconsistency closely enough that the modus ponens comes out valid. I think this basically works as a technical fix, but it still feels like it's borrowing the shape of logic rather than explaining why moral discourse deserves it."
