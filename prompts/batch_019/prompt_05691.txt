Next month's guest is a philosopher of history who's built her whole career defending a version of Hempel's covering-law model against the narrativists: basically the view that if a historian's account of why the war broke out doesn't reduce, at least in principle, to laws plus initial conditions, it isn't a real explanation yet, just an "explanation sketch" waiting to be filled in. As someone whose entire livelihood depends on the idea that narrative itself does real cognitive work, I kind of want to lean on her, and I don't want the hour to just be her restating the thesis in different words.

Can you draft nine or ten interview questions that push on the actual pressure points instead of just having her repeat herself? I want at least one question on the objection that a "law" which only ever fires once (there was only one July Crisis) is just the singular case dressed up in general-sounding language, so its unfalsifiable and does no independent work; one on Dray's alternative that historians mostly explain an action by showing it was the reasonable thing to do given how the situation looked to the people living through it, and whether "reasonable" is smuggling in a covering law or doing something else entirely; one on the Danto-style worry that a lot of historical understanding is built out of sentences like "that was the last day of peace Europe would see for four years," which only make sense in light of things that hadn't happened yet, and so can't be part of any explanation available at the time; and one on whether her model's built-in symmetry between explanation and prediction can survive historians' constant appeals to contingency, like the archduke's driver taking a wrong turn onto the very street where the assassin happened to be standing. For each question, give me a quick follow-up keyed to whatever dodge she's most likely to reach for, so I'm not caught flat-footed live on air.
