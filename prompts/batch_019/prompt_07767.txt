As a software engineer with an amateur interest in the history of computing, I recently reread Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" and was struck by how seriously he treats one particular objection to his imitation game: extrasensory perception. He lists telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis as genuine phenomena, judges the statistical case for telepathy — built largely on J. B. Rhine's card-guessing experiments — to be compelling, and even proposes running the test in a "telepathy-proof room" so that a human contestant cannot use ESP to tip off the interrogator. Turing died in 1954, long before Rhine-style parapsychology collapsed under repeated replication failures, and long before Daryl Bem's 2011 study claiming statistical evidence for precognition became a flashpoint in the wider replication crisis in psychology. Had Turing lived to see all this unfold, would he have treated it as a clean update against ESP, or is there something specific in his own epistemic style — his deference to hard statistical evidence, or his track record of being vindicated on other counter-intuitive claims — that might have made him slower than the average scientist of his day to abandon the view? And once that empirical premise dissolves, does the "telepathy-proof room" simply drop out of his argument harmlessly, or does its presence expose something more fragile in how he reasoned about the imitation game generally?
