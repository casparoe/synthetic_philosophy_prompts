Had a wierdly heated argument with my team last week that I can't stop chewing on. We're rebuilding the order-processing backend at work, and the debate is over how to represent an "order" as it moves through its lifecycle (created, paid, shipped, delivered, refunded, whatever). Half of us want the boring normal approach: one row per order in Postgres, and whenever something happens we just UPDATE it in place, so at any given moment there's exactly one row that just IS the order, current status and nothing else. The other half want to go full event-sourcing: never mutate anything, ever, just append immutable events to a log forever (OrderCreated, OrderPaid, OrderShipped...), and "the current order" is just something you compute on the fly by folding over its whole history of events. My coworker Priya kept insisting the mutable-row version is basically lying to us, that the real order IS the entire sequence of events, and a row that only shows "shipped" is just a derived summary, not the actual thing, whereas I kept arguing the order is whatever it currently is, right now, and the event log is just bookeeping we keep on the side in case we ever need to reconstruct something. We went around in circles for like half an hour, half joking and half not, before someone finally said "ok this stopped being a tech decision a while ago" and we just picked one so we could all go home.

It's been bugging me since though, so I brought it up with a friend from college who did a philosophy minor, and she said it sounded like it was brushing up against some actual old debate about how things persist through change, but she couldn't remember what it's called or which side people who study this stuff actually tend to land on. So, genuinely, I want to know: is there a real, principled way to say whether the "true" order is the row-right-now or the full event history, or is that a fake question and we were just dressing up a style preference as something deep? I'd rather get an actual take than a "both are valid, it depends on your use case" shrug, even if the honest answer turns out to be that it's genuinely unsettled.
