A friend of mine and I spent a long evening after our seminar on possible-worlds metaphysics arguing about counterpart theory, and neither of us gave any ground. The dispute was over whether ordinary de re modal claims like "Humphrey could have won the election" are best explained by Lewis's counterpart theory, on which nothing exists in more than one world and such a claim is made true by some sufficiently similar counterpart winning elsewhere, or by genuine transworld identity, on which the very same Humphrey, wholly present, exists and wins in some other world. My friend, defending transworld identity, opened with Kripke's Humphrey objection: on Lewis's semantics, Humphrey's own hopes and regrets about the election are answered by facts about a mere resembler, and nobody who says "I could have won" thinks they are talking about somebody else's fortunes. I brought up what I take to be Lewis's actual reply — that the objection quietly presupposes some prior, non-counterpart-theoretic fact about what "Humphrey could have won" is really about, which is exactly what an analysis of de re modality is supposed to supply, so the objection begs the question against it. She wasn't having it: getting the truth-value of "Humphrey could have won" right, she said, is not the same as getting its content right, and Lewis's rejoinder shows at most that the two can come apart, not that his account has the content right. At that point I switched to offense and brought up Lewis's own argument from accidental intrinsics: if Humphrey is literally, numerically one and the same thing in a world where he is sitting and a world where he is standing, we get a single object with incompatible intrinsic properties — exactly the problem of temporary intrinsics that pushes people toward temporal parts to handle change, now showing up across worlds instead of across time — and counterpart theory is what lets you avoid this without relativizing intrinsic properties to an index. She said relativizing looked like the cheaper bullet to bite, but we ran out of the evening before settling why one bullet should count as cheaper than the other.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a committed counterpart theorist and a committed defender of transworld identity — say, two philosophers who end up at the same table at a conference bar after giving talks on opposite sides of this debate — who work through exactly these moves: the Humphrey objection, Lewis's begging-the-question rejoinder to it, my friend's meta-level response to that rejoinder, and the argument from accidental intrinsics, including whatever the best transworld-identity answer to relativized intrinsics actually is. Please don't let either character collapse into a mouthpiece for a single slogan; each should take the other's strongest point seriously and answer what was actually said rather than a weaker version of it. And I'd be curious to see, by the end, whether one of these two arguments is doing more real work than the other, or whether the dispute — as my friend and I began to suspect — ultimately comes down to which cost each side finds easier to live with.
