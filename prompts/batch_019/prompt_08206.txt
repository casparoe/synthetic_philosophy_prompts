A dispute at my workplace two weeks ago has stayed with me, and I would like help thinking through the exchange it produced, since I was not able to settle it myself at the time.

My manager, Owen, publicly reprimanded a junior colleague in a team meeting for missing a project deadline. Nobody disputed the facts; she agreed she had missed it. What made the moment land badly is that Owen had missed an equivalent deadline on another project the previous month, which the whole office knew about. Afterward, two colleagues who had watched this happen, Naomi and Deepak, got into a drawn-out disagreement that I found myself unable to referee.

(1) Naomi said Owen had no business delivering that reprimand at all: doing the very thing you condemn someone else for, while exempting yourself from it, is the essence of hypocrisy, and that hypocrisy made the reprimand illegitimate coming from him specifically, independent of whatever else was true.

(2) Deepak answered that this is just the tu quoque fallacy: Owen's own past failure doesn't make it false that missing the deadline was a real problem needing to be addressed. If pointing to a critic's own flaws could neutralize their criticism's content, nobody with an imperfect record could ever correctly call out anything, which is absurd.

(3) Naomi replied that she wasn't disputing the content; missing the deadline really was a problem. Her objection was about something else: reprimanding someone implicitly presents the reprimander as holding themselves to the same norm they are enforcing, and Owen's exempting himself from a rule he applies to others is a distinct unfairness, one that undercuts his standing to be the one delivering that message, whatever the message's truth.

(4) Deepak pushed back that this concedes too much ground while giving up too little: if any shared flaw between critic and target erases standing, then the people most overdue for correction, those who already behave badly, become exactly the people no similarly flawed person may correct, which protects bad conduct rather than exposing it. He added that Owen's never having acknowledged his own missed deadline seemed like it should matter to how much standing he had lost, rather than standing being a fixed, all-or-nothing verdict that ignores what the hypocrite does or doesn't admit about themselves.

Could you work through Naomi and Deepak's exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether there is a significant consideration that neither of them brought up. Please close with a one-line verdict in exactly this format: \answer{Naomi}, \answer{Deepak}, or \answer{neither}, indicating whose overall position you find more defensible.
