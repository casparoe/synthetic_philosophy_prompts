I spent almost four decades as an observational cosmologist, and one of the more philosophically loaded tasks of that career was model selection: deciding whether an extra free parameter in a cosmological fit was earning its keep or merely soaking up noise. We leaned on devices like the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria, which penalize needless complexity, and I always understood this as a quantitative gloss on Occam's razor without ever interrogating why simplicity should track truth rather than mere convenience. Since retiring I have been reading much more philosophy of science, and I recently fell down a rabbit hole into algorithmic information theory — Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction — where I keep encountering the confident claim, especially in the rationalist blogosphere associated with LessWrong and in a more guarded form in Marcus Hutter's work on universal artificial intelligence, that this apparatus finally puts Occam's razor, and perhaps even Hume's old problem of induction, on rigorous mathematical footing.

The more of the technical literature I work through, the less convinced I am that any philosophical problem has actually been dissolved rather than merely relocated. The invariance theorem only guarantees that Kolmogorov complexity is machine-independent up to an additive constant fixed by the reference universal Turing machine (essentially, the length of a compiler translating between machines), and that constant is not bounded in any way that helps for short or realistic strings — a still-universal but sufficiently gerrymandered machine can make almost any chosen hypothesis look cheap. Solomonoff induction itself is uncomputable, so it cannot literally describe what any working scientist, or any brain, does when reasoning inductively, and the whole framework quietly presupposes that the data-generating process is computable to begin with, which looks like exactly the sort of substantive assumption about nature's uniformity that Hume's argument should make us suspicious of accepting for free. It also seems to me that the freedom to choose a description language just re-imports Goodman's old grue problem in algorithmic clothing: a language in which "grue" is primitive would make grue-based hypotheses come out simple, and nothing in the invariance theorem tells us that our familiar machines carve nature at its joints rather than at its grue-like ones.

A former colleague from my department, now consulting on machine learning, insists that this is a closed question and that any lingering unease is just confusion about the mathematics; I am not so sure, and would rather bring him a verdict than another survey of the back-and-forth. So I would like you to write me an essay that argues for one side or the other: does the Kolmogorov-complexity/Solomonoff-induction formalization of simplicity genuinely vindicate Occam's razor as an objective, truth-conducive principle and make real progress on Hume's problem of induction, or does it merely relocate these classical difficulties into the choice of universal machine, an unearned assumption of a computable universe, and an idealization no finite reasoner can implement? Please engage directly with the invariance theorem, with Solomonoff's convergence results for computable environments, and with the grue-style worry about language-relativity, and close with a clear, defended verdict rather than a diplomatic split-the-difference.
