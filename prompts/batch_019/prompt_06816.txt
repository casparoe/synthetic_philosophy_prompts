This question grew out of a graduate seminar on contractualist ethics, where we spent a week on John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" Taurek attacks an assumption shared by utilitarians and many of their critics alike: that if you can save either a group of five strangers or a different, smaller group, and cannot possibly save both, you are simply obligated to save the larger group because more lives are at stake. His argument is not the familiar complaint about aggregation licensing harmful means to good ends; it is more unsettling than that. He denies that there is any single point of view from which the loss of five lives is "worse" than the loss of one, since no person actually experiences the combined loss of five deaths — each person loses only their own life, and losses to distinct people cannot simply be added together the way sums of money can. From this he concludes that, absent any other morally relevant difference between the people involved, the fairer response is to give everyone an equal chance of survival — for instance, by flipping a coin between the two groups — rather than automatically favoring the many. Both the argument and the proposal struck contemporaries such as Derek Parfit as deeply counterintuitive, and the resulting debate, including later contractualist attempts (T. M. Scanlon among them) to defend saving the greater number on grounds other than simply tallying heads, remains active in ethics today.

I'd like you to write a short story that dramatizes this tension rather than explaining it. Set it wherever you like, as long as it involves someone in a position of real responsibility who learns, at nearly the same moment, of two groups of people in mortal danger — a larger one and a smaller one — such that they have the means to rescue only one group, not both: a field medic with a single dose of some scarce antidote, a search-and-rescue dispatcher with fuel for one more helicopter run before a storm closes in, or a scenario of your own devising. The two groups need not be strangers to your protagonist, but I'd like the philosophical crux to stay on the question of numbers as such, rather than sliding into a story that is really about loyalty or personal attachment. Let the character's own reasoning carry the weight, without turning into a lecture: I want the piece to register both the pull of "obviously save more people" and the genuine strangeness of Taurek's suggestion that raw numbers might not settle who should live when nothing else distinguishes the two groups — perhaps by having the character seriously entertain, rather than dismiss out of hand, some kind of impartial randomizing procedure.

Aim for something closer in spirit to a compact parable — in the vein of Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas," which dramatizes a related but distinct problem about weighing one person's suffering against many people's benefit — than a plot-driven thriller: it is fine, even preferable, if the story leaves the underlying philosophical question unresolved. Something in the neighborhood of 800 to 1200 words would be ideal.
