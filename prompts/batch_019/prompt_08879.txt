So this happened at a dinner thing about two weeks ago and I'm still half-chewing on it. My friend Deepak was telling everyone about his cousin's engagement, mentioned the ring cost something like three months salary, and (having apparently just read some pop-science book on evolution) went off on this theory that expensive engagement rings actually make total sense once you think of them as a costly signal, like a peacock's tail under the handicap principle. (1) His claim was: the ring only "works" as proof of commitment because its expensive -- a guy who wasn't serious wouldn't torch that much money on someone he might leave, so the cost itself is what makes the gesture credible. (2) Rosa, who did a masters in behavioral econ and almost never argues at these dinners, jumped in and said that's a category error: the handicap principle specifically needs a cost that's harder for low-quality signalers to pay -- a sickly peacock literally can't grow as big a tail no matter how badly it wants to -- whereas any guy, serious or not, can put a ring on a credit card, so cost alone doesn't filter for commitment, it only filters for wealth or willingness to go into debt.

(3) Deepak came back with: the debt itself is the differentiator, because an uncommitted guy wouldn't rationally take on a sunk cost tied to a relationship he doesn't expect to survive, so the risk of losing that money does exactly the filtering work Rosa was asking for -- and either way, this still proves rings aren't "just marketing," which was his real point all along. (4) Rosa said the "either way" is doing all the unearned work in that sentence: what he's now describing isn't the mechanism he opened with, it's basically a Schelling-style commitment device (a cost that binds your future self, and only "pays off" if the relationship lasts) rather than a genuine Zahavian handicap (a cost that reveals a trait the signaler already has, independent of what happens next), and the two stories actually make different predictions. If it's really a commitment device, a cheap-but-humiliating-to-back-out-of gesture, like proposing loudly in front of both families, should work just as well as an expensive ring. If it's a genuine handicap, the cost itself has to be doing the work regardless of how public or reversible it is. He can't just wave at "either way" as if the two explanations support each other when they're actually in tension, she said.

Nobody at the table, including me, managed to actually settle this. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (4) -- and say who's right about what, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether they're even disagreeing about the same thing by the end or have started talking past each other, and whether there's some consideration a real signaling theorist would bring up that neither of them landed on.
