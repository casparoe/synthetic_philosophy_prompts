So this actually happened to me and I'm still chewing on it. I'm about two thirds through a novel and I finally killed off a character I've been writing for three years, Marta (she's the protagonist's aunt, if that matters), and I actually cried at my desk typing the scene. I mentioned it at my writers group as basically a throwaway anecdote, and it turned into a real argument between two people there, Denny and Oksana, that's been bugging me since.

(1) Denny's take was that what I felt can't have been real grief, because grief requires not being in control of the outcome, and I was the one who decided Marta dies -- I could rewrite the paragraph tonight and she'd be alive again. He said this makes it more like what an actor does crying on cue, a trained and willed performance, and since nobody finds an actor's tears philosophically puzzling, mine shouldn't be either, just skilled make-believe you can turn up or down at will. (2) Oksana said the control angle proves nothing, since plenty of people cry rereading books whose ending is already fixed in their memory, with zero uncertainty left anywhere, and nobody calls those tears fake for that reason -- what matters for being moved is how vividly your entertaining the situation, not who's steering it or how settled it already is, so if anything my case is the purest version of the thing, not a watered down one. (3) Denny answered that rereading isn't actually parallel, because a rereader only ever discovers a fixed outcome a second time, whereas I had, at the actual moment of writing, the live option of not killing her -- so the two cases differ in a way that matters. (4) Oksana said that's a difference in how the feeling came about, not in the feeling itself at the moment you have it, and that Denny was quietly leaning on a premise he'd never actually defended -- that real emotion requires things to still be genuinely up for grabs -- when regret over an unchangeable past seems to be a real emotion with nothing left open anywhere.
