For the past few weeks, my high school's philosophy club has been working through Plato's Meno, and at our last meeting our faculty advisor had us focus on the closing stretch of the dialogue, somewhere around Stephanus 97a–98a, where Socrates and Meno try to work out why knowledge should count for more than "right opinion" if a true belief can guide someone's actions just as successfully as knowledge can. I followed the setup fine: Socrates gets Meno to agree that a guide who merely has a correct hunch about the road to Larisa (modern spelling "Larissa") gets travelers there exactly as well, in that instance, as a guide who actually knows the way, so true opinion looks every bit as useful as knowledge. What tripped up a friend and me after the meeting was the very next move, where Socrates brings in a comparison to Daedalus's statues to explain why knowledge is still supposed to be the better thing to have. Here is the passage, in Benjamin Jowett's translation, which is the edition our club is using:

SOCRATES: I will explain. If a man knew the way to Larisa, or anywhere else, and went to the place and led others thither, would he not be a right and good guide?

MENO: Certainly.

SOCRATES: And a person who had a right opinion about the way, but had never been and did not know, might be a good guide also, might he not?

MENO: Certainly.

SOCRATES: And while he has true opinion about that which the other knows, he will be just as good a guide if he thinks the truth, as he who knows the truth?

MENO: Exactly.

SOCRATES: Then true opinion is as good a guide to correct action as knowledge; and that was the point which we omitted in our speculation about the nature of virtue, when we said that knowledge only is the guide of right action; whereas there is also right opinion.

MENO: True.

SOCRATES: Then right opinion is not less useful than knowledge?

MENO: The difference, Socrates, is only that he who has knowledge will always be right; but he who has right opinion will sometimes be right, and sometimes not.

SOCRATES: What do you mean? Can he be wrong who has right opinion, so long as he has right opinion?

MENO: I admit the cogency of your argument, and therefore, Socrates, I wonder that knowledge should be preferred to right opinion—or why they should ever differ.

SOCRATES: And shall I explain this wonder to you?

MENO: Do tell me.

SOCRATES: You would not wonder if you had ever observed the images of Daedalus; but perhaps you have not got them in your country?

MENO: What have they to do with the question?

SOCRATES: Because they require to be fastened in order to keep them, and if they are not fastened they will play truant and run away.

MENO: Well, what of that?

SOCRATES: I mean to say that they are not very valuable possessions if they are at liberty, for they will walk off like runaway slaves; but when fastened, they are of great value, for they are really beautiful works of art. Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more honourable and excellent than true opinion, because fastened by a chain.

MENO: What you are saying, Socrates, seems to be very like the truth.

My trouble is with what the statue comparison actually proves. In the guide case, the traveler's true opinion did its whole job: it got everyone to Larisa without ever "running away," so its value on that occasion looks completely realized already, chain or no chain. All Socrates seems to have shown is that knowledge is more stable across many possible occasions, not that this particular successful belief was somehow deficient compared to knowledge at the moment it succeeded. My friend, who had read ahead about this, says this is close to what's sometimes called "the swamping problem" in later epistemology, where a true belief's value is supposed to "swamp" whatever extra value a reliable method contributed to producing it, so that reliability stops mattering once the belief turns out true. Could you help me work through whether Socrates' image of tying down the statues genuinely answers his own question about why knowledge beats right opinion, or whether it quietly swaps that question for a different one, something like "why is a reliable habit of belief-formation better than an unreliable one," and, if it does swap the question, whether that's a fair move given how he set up the Larisa example in the first place?
