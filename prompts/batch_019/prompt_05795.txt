Been rereading David Lewis's 1976 paper on time travel paradoxes (the one literally titled "The Paradoxes of Time Travel") because it keeps coming up in an argument with a hard-determinist friend about free will. It's the one where Tim travels back and tries to kill his own grandfather, and Lewis's resolution turns on there being two different senses of "can": what Tim can do relative to just his personal skills and immediate circumstances, versus what he can do relative to the entire fixed course of history (since Grandfather obviously did survive long enough to have kids). That distinction feels basically identical to the compatibilist move in the free will debate, where I "could have done otherwise" in some local/hypothetical sense even though, holding fixed the past and the laws of nature, I couldnt have done otherwise in a stronger sense. Is that actually a fair parallel, and if so, what are the most important implications of Lewis's grandfather-paradox move for how compatibilists should answer the standard "but you couldn't REALLY have done otherwise" objection?
