Had a whole argument with my friend last night about whether you should always want to know. He'd gotten his bloodwork back on the online portal before his doctor even called, and it's not like there was anything he could've done differently either way before that call happened. I said obviously you look - basically paraphrasing Good's theorem, that a rational Bayesian should always want cost-free evidence before deciding, since expected utility literally cant go down if you use the info optimally. He said that's cheating, because the proof only works if all you care about is which action you end up taking - if just knowing something is good or bad for you on its own (like the dread while you wait) independent of any decision, the theorem never actually told you to look in the first place. So I said fine, fold the dread into the utility function and the theorem still holds, it's just that the info wasn't really 'free' after all. He said that move makes the theorem unfalsifiable and useless as actual advice, since you can always redefine 'free' after the fact to rescue it. We never settled who won.
