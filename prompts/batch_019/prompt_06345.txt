So this came up in a roundabout way at work: we had a dumb Slack argument about whether Friday's deploy was "basically already decided" (someone said "the deploy will fail tomorrow" is either true or false right now, so arguing about it today is pointless), and a friend who minored in philosophy told me I'd basically reinvented Aristotle's sea battle problem. I went down a rabbit hole reading about it and it's honestly a lot deeper than I expected. The setup is just: is the sentence "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" true today, false today, or neither? And apparently people have been arguing about this for something like 2300 years, because every answer seems to cost you something: if the sentence is already true or false, the future looks locked in and it's not clear what "genuine" contingency or choice could even mean; if it's neither true nor false, you're giving up bivalence, which is supposed to be a pretty basic law of logic.

From my reading there seem to be two positions that both survive contact with each other instead of one just obviously losing. One side keeps bivalence: the sentence about tomorrow's battle is true or false right now, full stop, but truth definately isn't the same thing as necessity, so nothing is settled just because its already true -- I think this is the same move medieval writers like Ockham made about God's foreknowledge, and it's apparently why the branching-time version of this view is literally called "Ockhamist" in the tense logic literature (Arthur Prior's term, versus the "Peircean" view). The other side denies bivalence for this specific case: a genuinely open future event just doesn't have a truth value yet, or gets some third "undetermined" value, which I gather is part of why Łukasiewicz invented three-valued logic in the first place. As a dev this reminds me a lot of how SQL handles NULL with three-valued logic (TRUE/FALSE/UNKNOWN), except here the "unknown" is supposed to be a fact about reality and not just missing data.

Can you write me a dialogue between one person holding each view? Set it the night before the battle, with both of them literally standing on the docks watching the fleet get loaded, each trying to talk the other out of their position. I want real engagement, not two people just repeating their opening speech louder: e.g. I'd guess the open-future side has to explain why the same worry doesn't equally apply to true statements about the past, and the bivalence side has to explain what "necessary" even means if it's not just a fancy word for "already true and unchangeable." Neither one should be a pushover, and if you can, have them try to figure out by the end whether they're actually disagreeing about the world or just about how to talk about it -- like, would they ever disagree about how to bet on anything, or is this a difference that makes no difference. Doesn't need to be super long, just long enough that they actually land some hits on each other.
