A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether there is any principled way to stop expected-value reasoning from being hijacked once the claimed stakes get large enough, and I left the table unconvinced by my own side of it -- which is inconvenient, since a version of exactly that disagreement has landed on my desk this week in a form I actually have to resolve.

I sit on the five-person allocations committee of a small philanthropic foundation (roughly $4 million a year) whose charter commits us to funding "neglected, high-uncertainty research with the greatest expected impact on human welfare," ranked each cycle by a standard cost-effectiveness model. This round we received a proposal from a credentialed physicist requesting $2.4 million -- three-fifths of our entire budget -- to fund a legal and public-advocacy campaign aimed at delaying a newly proposed particle collider experiment. The proposal accepts the experiment's own safety review, which concludes the risk is not credible, but argues that "not credible" is not the same as "zero," and assigns the possibility of a stable, Earth-consuming micro black hole a probability of one in ten billion per run of the machine -- a figure derived from a minority reading of the physics that most physicists reject but cannot entirely disprove. Run through our own cost-effectiveness model, the proposal's expected value swamps every other line item combined, including our two best-evidenced grants -- a bed-net distribution program and a lead-paint remediation program -- each with a well-documented, modest, per-dollar impact. Three committee members want to fund it, on the grounds that the whole point of binding ourselves to a model is to follow it precisely when its conclusion feels wrong, not only when it happens to agree with our intuitions. The other two call it a textbook Pascal's mugging and want to reject it, but under questioning neither could produce a rule for doing so that would not also have zeroed out, ex ante, a pandemic-early-warning grant we made years ago that looks structurally identical on paper and that most of us now count among our best decisions.

Could you draft the memo I need to bring to Monday's meeting? I need a specific recommendation on this proposal -- fund it, reject it, or fund some capped amount, and why -- a decision rule the committee could state and apply consistently to future proposals of this shape, and a fair, undefensive treatment of the strongest argument for simply trusting the number wherever it leads.
