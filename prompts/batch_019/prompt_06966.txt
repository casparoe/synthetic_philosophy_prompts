I'm putting together a collection of short stories that each circle around a piece of math or logic that people usually flatten into a one-line soundbite, and I went down a wikipedia rabbit hole last week on the continuum hypothesis that I haven't been able to shake since. My rough understanding (I'm a novelist, not a mathematician, so tell me if I'm garbling it) is that Cantor showed the real numbers are a "bigger" infinity then the whole numbers, then spent years trying to prove there's no infinite size sitting in between the two — and more or less drove himself to a breakdown over it, or so the story goes, flipping back and forth between thinking he'd proved it and finding the hole in his own proof. What I find almost unbearably poignant, and what I want the actual story to hinge on, is that decades after he died the question turned out to be formally independent of the usual axioms of set theory: Godel showed you can't disprove it, Cohen later showed you can't prove it either. His whole life's obsession was pointed at a target that was never going to resolve the way he needed it to, not because he wasn't smart enough, but because of what the axioms simply don't pin down.

So here's the brief: a short story about a fictional mathematician (any period, doesn't have to be Cantor — I don't want a biopic) who's staked years of their life on cracking the continuum hypothesis, where the emotional climax is the moment they are confronted with the independence result and have to metabolize what it actually means for everything they've done. I loved how Uncle Petros and Goldbach's Conjecture used Godel's incompleteness theorem as a late gut-punch, and I want something in that register, except I'd like this one to actually earn its technical beat instead of just gesturing at "math is unknowable, spooky." Give me enough of a feel for what forcing or the constructible universe are actually doing that an attentive reader understands why "independent of the axioms" is a stranger fate than "nobody's solved it yet." And if a little of the deeper question can flicker through too — whether the continuum hypothesis is simply true or false out there regardless of what any axiom system can prove (Godel apparently believed something like this), or whether that's a confused way to even think about it — even better, since that's the question I want humming under the whole collection.

Would you take a crack at it? Something like 900-1300 words, literary rather than hard sci-fi in tone, and it doesn't need to resolve neatly — if anything I'd be suspicious of a tidy ending here.
