So last night I was at my friend Priya's for dinner and we ended up in this two-hour argument about whether it's "rational" for her to believe in an afterlife — it started because her grandma passed away recently and she said something like "she's in a better place now," and I pushed back a little too hard, and it just spiraled from there. Neither of us moved an inch the whole night: I kept asking what evidence would even convince her either way, she kept insisting that wasn't the point, and we both went home feeling like the other person was being kind of dense, even though normally we're good at disagreeing about stuff without it turning into this.

This morning I told my other friend Marcus (he did a philosophy minor) about it, and he brought up this whole thing about Wittgenstein's lectures on religious belief that I'd never heard of. Apparently Wittgenstein thought it was basically a category error to argue about whether something like the Last Judgement is true or false the same way you'd argue about a scientific claim — believing in it isn't the same kind of "believing" as believing your train leaves at 5, it's more like a picture that regulates your whole life. He apparently even refused to call religious belief either reasonable or unreasonable, and thought a believer and a non-believer might not even be contradicting each other in the normal sense, since they're not using words like "believe" and "know" by the same rules — more like two people playing different games with the same pieces. This kind of pissed me off at first, ngl, it definately felt like a cop-out that lets people off the hook from ever justifying anything, but it also uncomfortably nailed exactly what arguing with Priya felt like.

I can't stop chewing on it, so could you write me a short story about this — two people who are close (friends, siblings, a couple, whatever, your call) get into an argument about something religious or metaphysical, maybe triggered by a death or something else heavy, and through the dialogue itself — not through anyone stepping back and explaining the philosophy out loud — the reader slowly realizes the two of them were never really disagreeing on the same plane to begin with, they're each using "believe"/"know"/"evidence" by completely different rules. Don't have either of them "win" or have it resolve into some neat lesson at the end; I want it to land on that same itchy, unresolved feeling Priya and I were stuck with, where you can't even tell if the two people actually made contact with each other's point at all.
