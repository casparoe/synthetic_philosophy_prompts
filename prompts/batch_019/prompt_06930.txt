I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a good deal of time on my own turning over the old question of what it means to say someone "could have done otherwise" than they actually did. At some point I came across the idea, which a number of philosophers apparently take seriously, that this just comes down to a conditional claim: you could have done otherwise if it's true that you would have done otherwise had you chosen to, in the same way a glass counts as fragile because it would break if struck. Trying to work out what might be wrong with that idea sent me down a rabbit hole of old forum threads and encyclopedia articles on what the sources called the "conditional analysis of dispositions" more generally, where I kept running into two terms, "finks" and "masks," that are apparently both famous counterexamples to this kind of if-then story -- but which, to my surprise, the sources treated as two genuinely separate problems rather than the same trick told twice. As best I can tell, a fink is something like a wire hooked up to a gadget that flips the current on or off the instant before anyone touches it, so that a wire which really is live never ends up shocking the person testing it, whereas a dead one shocks them anyway; a mask, on the other hand, is supposed to be more like a poison that's always taken together with its antidote, so that it never actually kills anyone even though it never stops being poison. To my untrained eye, both stories look like the same move: the object has, or lacks, the property in question while the "would happen if" conditional comes out false, or true, regardless.

What I'd like is a careful explanation of why philosophers don't just collapse these into a single objection. Specifically, could you spell out exactly what distinguishes a fink from a mask as ways of breaking the conditional analysis, and why a repair that is supposed to take care of one doesn't automatically take care of the other? I gather that David Lewis tried to mend the original analysis by tying it to whatever intrinsic "causal basis" an object has for its disposition, rather than to the disposition by name alone, and that this move is usually credited with handling finks but not masks -- I would like to actually understand why the fix works for one and not the other, rather than simply being told that it does. And since the whole inquiry started with the free-will question, I would be glad if you could close the loop at the end: does the fink/mask distinction actually threaten to sink the "could have done otherwise" analysis I began with, or can that idea be repaired well enough to survive both objections?
