I'm on the incident review board at the plant where I work -- we get convened a few times a year whenever something happens that's serious enough to need a formal writeup for corporate. We've got a case right now that's put the whole board at each others throats, and it actually turns on a wording in our own policy that I don't think anyone thought hard about until now. A pressure tank ruptured and injured a worker pretty badly. The investigation turned up two things that were both going on at once: the relief valve had been serviced badly by an outside contractor two months back and had degraded to the point that it would've failed to vent the tank that day no matter what else was happening, and separately, a floor supervisor had been letting operators run the compressor past it's rated cycle for weeks to hit shipping deadlines (against policy, but tolerated by management for years), which on its own would have overpressurized that same tank to rupture at basically the same moment. Either factor alone, given everything else that was true that day, would have produced the exact same rupture at the exact same time.

Corporate wants our board's formal written finding on causation because two separate things hang on it: whether we terminate the servicing contract for cause, and whether the supervisor gets referred for discipline. Our policy says something only counts as a root cause -- the kind that triggers those consequences -- if the incident "would not have occurred but for" it. Run that test on the valve and it fails, since the rupture happens anyway via the compressor practice; run it on the compressor practice and it fails too, since the rupture happens anyway via the valve. Taken literally, our own policy clears everybody, which strikes most of the board as obviously wrong, but the two members insisting we just apply the test as written aren't being unreasonable either -- they're worried that if we start calling things "causes" whenever they were merely sufficient, we'll end up writing reports that blame half the plant for every incident, since there's almost always some other sufficient factor lurking somewhere if you look hard enough. One member has a philosophy background from before she switched to engineering, and keeps saying this is basically a fight between "counterfactual" and "interventionist" theories of causation from a class she took years ago, though she hasn't managed to turn that into an actual recommendation. Could you draft the board's written causal finding -- the actual document I'd bring to Thursday's meeting -- that states plainly what we should conclude happened here, what it means for the contractor and the supervisor, and that deals honestly with the "everything becomes a cause of everything" worry instead of just brushing it aside?
