I'm several drafts into a novel whose protagonist is a retired logician spending her final years revisiting an unresolved question from her graduate training: whether Skolem's Paradox is a mere technical curiosity or a genuine threat to the idea that "uncountable" names an objective feature of the mathematical world. Wanting to render the argument honestly rather than in the vague way novels usually gesture at mathematics, I asked two friends of mine — Wren, who works in model theory, and Alden, who works in philosophy of mathematics — to walk me through it over dinner. They never got around to explaining it to me, because they started arguing with each other about what the paradox actually shows, and by the end of the evening I had the back of a napkin covered in their exchange rather than notes for my chapter. I've reconstructed it below as faithfully as I can, and before I decide how, or whether, to put a version of it in my characters' mouths, I'd like a philosopher's eye on who has the better of it at each stage.

(1) Wren opened with the paradox itself: if ZFC is consistent, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees it has a model with only countably many elements; yet inside that very model, Cantor's argument still goes through, so the model certifies that some set within it — its own version of the power set of the naturals — has no enumeration. Seen from outside, every element of the model can be numbered off one by one; seen from inside, using only the model's own resources, that "same" set is uncountable. Wren's conclusion, echoing a remark Skolem himself once made, was that countability isn't a property a set simply has, the way having seven elements is a property a set simply has; it's relative to which functions happen to be available in whatever model you're standing in, so calling anything "absolutely uncountable" is a category error dressed up as a discovery.

(2) Alden objected that this trades on a sleight of hand: the model fails to contain the relevant bijection not because cardinality itself is up for grabs, but because that one function, among countably many others, simply isn't in its domain. There is a difference, he said, between "the model cannot prove a bijection exists" and "no bijection exists," and once those are kept apart the paradox dissolves into an unglamorous fact about the expressive limits of countable theories, not a discovery about the nature of infinity.

(3) Wren replied: fine, but which model gets to adjudicate that distinction? Any statement of "what's really going on" has to be made in some theory, and if that theory is first-order, Löwenheim–Skolem applies to it too, so you never reach a theory-independent fact about the cardinality of anything — you only ever get one model's word against another's. "It's models all the way down," she said, "and you're just helping yourself to a floor."

(4) Alden granted that no first-order theory can pin its models down up to isomorphism — the same thing happens with "finite," courtesy of the nonstandard models of arithmetic that compactness guarantees — but denied that this forces the relativism Wren wanted. Working mathematicians aren't struck mute by the existence of countable models of ZFC; they simply distinguish "countable-in-M" from the ordinary, informal claim that M's domain is countable, and the latter isn't itself hostage to some further model. Otherwise, he said, Wren's argument would just as easily show that no mathematical statement whatever is objectively true, since every one of them is likewise only ever asserted from within some theory — and that is a much bigger bullet than the one she thinks she's dodging.

(5) Wren's last word was that Alden's "ordinary, informal" perspective on M isn't exempt from any of this at all — it is just a formal claim he hasn't bothered to formalize, and the fact that he trusts it unreflectively doesn't make it theory-independent. Alden didn't have a rejoinder ready, and the table turned to dessert before he found one.
