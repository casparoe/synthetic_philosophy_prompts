A friend of mine answers customer-service calls for a small charity that runs a weekly raffle: ten thousand numbered tickets, one prize, drawn the next morning by an outside auditor. The afternoon before a recent draw, a caller asked whether ticket #4821 was going to win. My friend, who had no information beyond the fact that it was one ticket among ten thousand, told him flatly, "It's not going to win." He mentioned this at dinner as a throwaway anecdote, but it started an argument between two other friends of mine, Nadia and Owen, that has apparently continued over text for the better part of a week.

(1) Nadia's opening claim was that the assertion was completely fine, since my friend was in fact better justified in "it won't win" (a 99.99 percent chance of being right) than he is in plenty of ordinary things people assert all day without a second thought, such as a store's posted hours or an airline app's estimated departure time. If being well justified is what licenses an assertion, she argued, this clears the bar easily. (2) Owen disagreed. He thinks there is something off about flatly asserting the outcome of a not-yet-drawn raffle in a way that doesn't apply to the sign or the app, even holding the odds of error fixed, and that this is exactly the kind of case philosophers reach for when they want to argue that being justified is not the same as actually knowing. (3) Nadia's rejoinder was that the sign and the app are themselves basically statistical devices: the posted hours could be outdated, and the app's estimate is just a pattern read off past flights. So, she said, if Owen wants a real asymmetry between the ticket case and these cases, he owes some further difference beyond "one of them is officially a lottery." (4) Owen's answer was that the further difference is something like this: change the actual fact (the store's real hours, the flight's real status), and the sign or the app would typically have changed along with it, whereas nothing about ticket #4821 in particular made it any more or less likely to lose than any of the other 9,999 tickets that did not call in. My friend's method would have produced the very same confident "it won't win" no matter which ticket-holder happened to phone, including the eventual winner. (5) Nadia conceded that this is a genuine difference but denied that it settles anything, since shrinking the prize to something trivial, such as a five-dollar gift card, makes the flat assertion sound fine again even to Owen. This, she said, suggests that what is really driving the discomfort is the cost of being wrong, not any fact about what would have happened in nearby scenarios.
