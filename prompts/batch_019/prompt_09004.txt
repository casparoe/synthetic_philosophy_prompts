So I've been reading Michael Dummett's little book "On Immigration and Refugees" for a seminar on migration ethics, and it's weirder and more specific than the usual cosmopolitanism-vs-communitarianism stuff we do in these classes. He argues that the "genuine refugee" vs. "mere economic migrant" distinction is largely bogus and gets exploited by states to justify shutting people out, that most immigration restriction in rich countries is basically racism wearing a legal disguise, and that former colonial powers owe pretty specific debts to their old subjects. What's messing with my head, though, is that he wasn't just theorizing from an armchair - I think he was one of the founding members of CARD (the Campaign Against Racial Discrimination) back in the 60s, actually out campaigning against racist treatment of Carribean immigrants to Britain, decades before he wrote any of this down.

He died right at the end of 2011, so he missed two things that feel almost custom-built for his framework. First, the "hostile environment" policy and the Windrush scandal - the government spent years denying healthcare, housing and jobs to (and even deporting) people from that exact same Caribbean generation he'd defended as a young man, because they couldn't produce old landing-card paperwork nobody had ever told them to keep. Second, the 2022 Rwanda scheme, where asylum seekers who reached the UK would just get shipped off to have their claims processed, and be resettled, in a third country instead. A friend in my seminar reckons Dummett would obviously have hated both, but "he'd have hated it" is a vibe, not a philosophical argument, and I need the actual argument for a presentation on friday.

Could you reconstruct, from the structure of his actual arguments rather than his general politics, what Dummett would most plausibly say about each case? What I'm really stuck on is whether Windrush/hostile-environment and the Rwanda scheme are testing the same strand of his thinking, so that one Dummett-style argument condemns both, or whether they pull on genuinely different parts of his view - say, one mainly about racial injustice against an already-settled community, the other mainly about the refugee/economic-migrant line and what's owed to people who haven't arrived yet. If it's the latter, where exactly would his reasoning run out of steam for one of the cases but not the other?
