And here's the classmate's answer I've been assigned to grade:

"Clark and Chalmers's example compares Inga, whose biological memory works normally, with Otto, who has Alzheimer's and instead relies on a notebook that goes everywhere with him and gets updated the moment he learns something new. When Inga hears about a show at the Museum of Modern Art she just remembers the museum is on 53rd Street and walks over; Otto hears the same thing, looks it up in his notebook, finds the same address, and walks over. The parity principle says that if a process would count as cognitive when it happens inside someone's skull, we shouldn't automatically deny it's cognitive just because it happens to run partly outside the skull instead -- the location of a process isn't what makes it mental, its functional role is. This builds on the idea, familiar from Putnam and Burge's externalism, that what's in the head isn't all that matters for the mind -- Clark and Chalmers extend that logic from the content of concepts to entire cognitive processes.

Applying that to Otto: the notebook is always with him, he consults it automatically instead of treating it like a stranger's testimony he'd want to double check, and the information got in there because he endorsed it as true at the time he wrote it down. Because it plays the same role for Otto that biological memory plays for Inga, Clark and Chalmers say the notebook is genuinely part of Otto's mind, not just a tool he happens to use a lot -- Otto believed the museum was on 53rd Street even before he opened the notebook, and in fact the notebook is doing the same job Otto's memory would be doing if it worked, so in that sense it's conscious the same way his biological memory is.

One objection, due to Jerry Fodor, is that this confuses two different things: a process being causally coupled to the brain is not the same as that process being part of the cognitive system, so pointing out that Otto's behavior depends on the notebook doesn't yet show the notebook is constitutive of his cognition rather than just a reliable prop for it. A defender of the extended mind could reply that this is exactly why Clark and Chalmers don't rest their case on mere use or reliance -- they add further conditions (the resource has to be constantly available, trusted without much scrutiny, and something the person consciously endorsed in the past), and those conditions are supposed to rule out cases of mere coupling, like looking a fact up in someone else's notebook for the first time, while still counting Otto's own notebook as part of him."
