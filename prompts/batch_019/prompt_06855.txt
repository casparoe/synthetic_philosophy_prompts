I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and a few of us in the department run a informal reading group on the decision-theoretic side of AI-safety/x-risk stuff -- Newcomb problems, updatelessness, that whole cluster of stuff. A couple sessions back we went through the "surrogate goals" idea that came out of the Center on Long-Term Risk: the proposal that an agent worried about being threatened can deliberately adopt some cheap goal it doesn't actually care about (say, a fake variable, or a harmless button that isn't allowed to be pressed) and commit to responding to threats against that fake goal exactly as it would respond to threats against its real goal. The pitch is that a threatener who only cares about extracting concessions should then be indifferent between targeting the real goal and the surrogate, and will happily settle for "destroying" the worthless surrogate instead, since that gets them the same leverage at no extra cost. Two people in the group, Marek and Iris, ended up arguing for days afterward (mostly over Slack) about whether this actually holds up as a defense against acausal threats specifically -- the case where the threatener is some distant, causally disconnected agent, like a superintelligence in another region of the multiverse, that only ever interacts with you through simulation and correlated reasoning rather than physical contact. Neither of them budged, and I've been chewing on it since, so I'd like an outside read.

(1) Marek's opening move: if it's knowable to any sufficiently capable simulator that your policy toward threats on the surrogate is behaviorally identical to your policy toward threats on the real goal, then threatening the surrogate is a strictly dominant move for a leverage-seeking threatener -- it costs you nothing when carried out, and your real goal never even enters their calculation. He called this a free lunch. (2) Iris objected that the indifference argument only goes through if the threatener can't tell genuine surrogate-adopters apart from agents that are bluffing, or that would flinch once it's actually go-time; if it can't verify that, its safest strategy is to threaten the real goal regardless, exactly as it would against an agent with no surrogate at all, so the whole defense leans on a perfect-legibility assumption that's doing all the real work. (3) Marek replied that near-perfect legibility to a simulator is basically the standing assumption of this whole literature to begin with -- the entire premise of a superintelligence simulating you is that it can inspect your dispositions directly, so "it can't verify you're genuinely indifferent" isn't a live objection in the acausal case the way it might be between two humans haggling. (4) Iris came back that granting perfect legibility just moves the fight up a level: "I install cheap surrogates for anything I get threatened over" is now itself a legible, general policy, and a threatener who anticipates it has every reason to pre-commit to a meta-policy of ignoring declared surrogates and going after the real goal regardless -- precisely so that installing surrogates stops paying off. At that point, she said, it's not a one-shot game against a fixed leverage-maximizer anymore, it's a race over who gets to fix the rules of engagement first, and Marek's whole arguement just quietly assumes his side wins that race.
