Before retiring, I spent four decades in statistical mechanics, so "ergodic" already had a precise meaning for me by the time I stumbled across the "ergodicity economics" program associated with Ole Peters. Its central claim is that expected utility theory implicitly evaluates a gamble via an ensemble average across possible worlds, whereas any actual agent only ever lives through a single trajectory in time; replacing that ensemble average with the time-average growth rate of multiplicatively compounding wealth recovers Kelly-optimal betting — behavior extensionally equivalent to log-utility maximization — now derived as a theorem about dynamics rather than posited as a fact about psychology. Advocates describe this as replacing an "arithmetic," expectation-maximizing conception of rationality with a properly "geometric" one; critics reply that expected utility theory was never wedded to linear utility, that log-optimal growth strategies were well understood long before this rebranding, and that Samuelson's old objections to treating almost-sure long-run dominance as a universal decision criterion still stand. Rather than rehearsing either side's rhetoric, I would like you to locate the actual crux of the disagreement: is there some decision problem in which a committed "geometric rationalist" and a suitably calibrated expected-utility maximizer prescribe genuinely different actions, or does the whole dispute bottom out in framing rather than substance?
