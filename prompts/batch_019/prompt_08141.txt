In my AP European History class, we recently spent a week on the Marquis de Condorcet as part of a unit on the Enlightenment, and the detail that stuck with me most was not his political activism but a mathematical result our teacher mentioned almost in passing: the Condorcet Jury Theorem, from his 1785 essay applying probability theory to majority decisions. The idea that a group of voters who are each only slightly more likely than not to be correct about some factual question will, if their judgments are independent, reach the right verdict with near certainty as the group grows large enough seems to have been, for Condorcet, just one piece of a larger project of building a genuine "social mathematics" that could ground political decisions in something more rigorous than tradition, rhetoric, or aristocratic privilege. What impressed me further is that he applied this commitment to equality with unusual consistency: he was one of the very few Enlightenment figures to argue seriously for extending full citizenship and the vote to women, and he chaired the constitutional committee that in 1793 produced a draft constitution for France, sometimes called the Girondin constitution, which was quietly set aside once his political allies fell from power.

Not long after that unit, a friend sent me a link to a blog post about "futarchy," a governance proposal from the economist Robin Hanson, whose slogan for it is roughly "vote on values, but bet on beliefs": an elected body would decide on and periodically update some measurable definition of national welfare, while the choice of which specific policy to adopt in order to raise that measure would be handed over to speculative markets, where anyone willing to risk money could bet on the outcomes of different proposed policies, with whichever policy the market currently prices as most likely to raise expected welfare being the one that actually gets implemented. It struck me as a strange descendant of exactly the kind of probability-driven, anti-rhetorical approach to collective judgment that Condorcet was reaching for, except that it replaces his equally-weighted, independent voters with self-selected traders whose influence over the outcome is proportional to how much money they are willing and able to stake.

I would like you to reconstruct, as specifically as you can from Condorcet's own arguments rather than from a generic impression of "Enlightenment rationalism," what verdict he would likely have reached about futarchy as a mechanism of governance. Would he have welcomed it as finally realizing, through real institutions and incentives, the kind of mathematically disciplined aggregation of judgment his jury theorem only gestured toward on paper? Or would his own theoretical commitments have generated principled objections to it, for instance about whether traders who can observe and imitate one another's positions still satisfy the independence condition his theorem depends on, or about whether letting wealth determine political influence betrays the same egalitarian instincts that led him to defend women's suffrage and to distrust arguments from mere privilege? I would also be curious whether you think the divide at the center of futarchy, between values settled democratically and beliefs settled by markets, corresponds to anything in Condorcet's own writing about which political questions should be left to specialized judgment and which should be decided by the broader citizenry.
