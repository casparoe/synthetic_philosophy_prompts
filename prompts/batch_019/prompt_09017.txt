For an extended essay on the philosophical background of AI risk debates, I've been reading about Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and the instrumental convergence thesis. While tracing where the orthogonality thesis' underlying picture of practical reason comes from, I found people pointing back to Hume's theory of motivation as a kind of ancestor of the idea. Here is the passage I've gotten stuck on, from Book II, Part III, Section III of Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), "Of the influencing motives of the will":

> Nothing is more usual in philosophy, and even in common life, than to talk of the combat of passion and reason, to give the preference to reason, and to assert that men are only so far virtuous as they conform themselves to its dictates. […] In order to shew the fallacy of all this philosophy, I shall endeavour to prove first, that reason alone can never be a motive to any action of the will; and secondly, that it can never oppose passion in the direction of the will. […] Since reason alone can never produce any action, or give rise to volition, I infer, that same faculty is as incapable of preventing volition, or of disputing the preference with any passion or emotion. […] We speak not strictly and philosophically when we talk of the combat of passion and of reason. Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them.

I'm a high school student, so I get the general slogan, but I'd like to actually work through the argument rather than just cite the last line, and then use it responsibly in my essay. Could you help me with three things:

1. Reconstruct, step by step, why Hume thinks reason alone can neither motivate an action nor oppose a passion. Which premise is really doing the work here?
2. Does this argument actually get you something like the orthogonality thesis (that more or less any level of intelligence could go with more or less any final goal), or is there a gap between "reason can't generate or override ends" and "intelligence and final goals can vary independently"?
3. If we took Hume's picture of reason as purely instrumental seriously for an artificial agent, would that make instrumental convergence (very different final goals leading to similar sub-goals, like self-preservation or acquiring resources) more likely, or would it actually undercut the case for it?
