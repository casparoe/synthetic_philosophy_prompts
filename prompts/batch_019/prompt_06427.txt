Every election, my brother and I have the same argument about what I have since learned is called the paradox of voting: whether it is actually rational to vote, given how unlikely it is that any single ballot decides anything. His case is straightforward: work out the odds that one vote is the vote that flips the outcome — vanishingly small in anything larger than a tiny local race — multiply that by how much he cares who wins, and the expected payoff is nowhere near worth the trip to the polls. I used to answer with the old line that "if everyone thought that way, nobody would vote," but he has a fair reply: it is not his fault what other people decide, and treating his one choice as though it controls millions of others is simply confused. I have since decided that the real disagreement is not about civic duty at all, but about whether a choice's rationality depends only on what it causes, or also on what it is evidence of. My brother is hardly the only person who reasons about voting this way, so if he stays home, that is at least some evidence that everyone else who reasons as he does stays home too — not because he is making them do it, but because they are all instances of the same reasoning reaching the same conclusion. It strikes me as essentially the same shape of disagreement as Newcomb's problem, just dressed differently.

Write a dialogue between two people on the night before an election, one who judges the rationality of voting solely by its causal effect on the outcome, and one who judges it by what the choice is evidence of. Have each directly engage with the other's strongest objection — especially the "that is not really my fault" worry — rather than talking past one another, and do not let either side come out the clear winner.
