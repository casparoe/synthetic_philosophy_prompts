Three weeks ago I was appointed to a three-member research-misconduct panel at my university, and our very first case walked us straight into what I've since learned is called the discursive dilemma (or, in its original legal guise, the doctrinal paradox). A finding of responsibility required both that the respondent had reused figures without attribution and that the reuse was not a defensible oversight; a majority of us affirmed the first premise, a different majority affirmed the second, yet a majority voting on the bottom line thought no sanction was warranted. We still haven't settled whether the panel's official position is fixed by our votes on the premises or by our vote on the conclusion itself. For our faculty-governance podcast, I'm now interviewing a philosopher who has built her career defending the premise-based procedure as the only one that lets a panel count as reasoning together, against colleagues who would rather the panel vote on the conclusion directly. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond a restatement of the dilemma. I want at least one question on how her procedure is meant to survive the fact that whoever sets the agenda of premises all but decides the case, and one on two panelists who affirm the identical premise for flatly incompatible underlying reasons. I also want one on what her view still owes an explanation for once you grant the List-Pettit-style impossibility results, and one where I simply hand her my panel's actual case and ask her to adjudicate it on the spot. For each, add a follow-up question keyed to the deflection she's most likely to reach for.
