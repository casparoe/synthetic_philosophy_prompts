I've spent a lot of my free time over the past year reading about Kurt Gödel, and one thing keeps nagging at me: his incompleteness theorems seem to already settle the question of whether we survive death. If a rigorous mathematical proof shows that the human mind can recognize truths that no formal system or machine could ever derive from within itself, doesn't that establish that consciousness cannot be reduced to a physical, mechanical process, and therefore is not simply extinguished when the body dies the way a machine is switched off? This is not just my own speculation: Gödel himself spent his later years personally convinced there was an afterlife, and he even worked out his own formal proof of God's existence. Given that one of the greatest logicians in history put his own mathematics behind the idea, why do philosophers still treat personal survival after death as a wide-open question rather than something Gödel already settled?
