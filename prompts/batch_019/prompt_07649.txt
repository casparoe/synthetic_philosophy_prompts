In 1955, less than two years before his death, John von Neumann published an essay in Fortune titled "Can We Survive Technology?" Its central claim was that the danger of modern technology lay less in any single invention than in a structural feature of the modern world: because the exploration and settlement of the globe was essentially complete, there was no longer any "outward" expansion available to absorb and dilute the disruptive consequences of new inventions, so that each new capability now pressed directly and irreversibly on a single, finite, interconnected system. Alongside nuclear weapons and the rapid automation of industry, von Neumann singled out the deliberate, large-scale modification of climate and weather as a coming danger of comparable magnitude, suggesting that whoever mastered it might gain something like a decisive strategic weapon; more generally, he argued that none of these capabilities could realistically be stopped once they became achievable, since bans and treaties tend to fail against any technology that is simultaneously dangerous and strategically valuable. His own proposed safeguard was accordingly not prohibition but the cultivation of administrative patience, tolerance, and flexibility within whatever institutions would have to manage a permanently elevated level of technological risk.

I'm curious what this specific diagnosis, rather than von Neumann's general reputation as a hard-nosed technological optimist, implies about two debates he could not have witnessed. The first is the recent push among climate scientists for an international non-use agreement or moratorium on solar geoengineering research and deployment, essentially the same kind of deliberate climate intervention he flagged as a coming danger. The second is the 2023 call for a six-month pause on training AI systems more capable than GPT-4, together with the subsequent proposals for binding international treaties on frontier AI development. Given von Neumann's stated skepticism that prohibition can durably contain a technology that is both dangerous and strategically valuable, would he have regarded these two moratorium proposals in the same light, or would he have drawn a principled distinction between them? And short of an outright pause, what concrete institutional arrangement do you think he would have proposed for each, consistent with his emphasis on adaptive management over prevention?
