While researching a piece on how contemporary societies reason about the ethics of war, I had dinner with two friends who fell into a disagreement I still haven't been able to settle in my own mind. It began when we started discussing whether an ordinary enemy soldier who fights strictly "by the rules" is doing anything wrong.

1. Marcus argued that the law has the right idea: a soldier who refrains from targeting civilians and uses only proportionate force is just as entitled to kill enemy combatants as they are entitled to kill him, regardless of which side started the war or holds the better cause. He noted that prisoners of war captured from the aggressor's ranks receive the same legal protections as those from the defending side — the law does not treat them as murderers, and he does not think we should either.

2. Elena disagreed. Legal protections, she said, are a practical convention rather than evidence of moral parity: criminal courts extend all manner of procedural safeguards to people who have done something genuinely wrong. On her view, only soldiers fighting for a just cause possess an actual moral right to kill; soldiers on the unjust side are not doing anything permissible, they are merely going unpunished for it.

3. Marcus responded with a case: an ordinary conscript, misled by his own government about why he is fighting, thrown into a firefight in which his only choice is to kill or be killed, trying only to save himself and the men beside him. To call what that soldier does morally equivalent to murder struck Marcus as absurd, whatever the law happened to say.

4. Elena granted that the conscript might be entirely blameless, but said Marcus was conflating two distinct things. An act can be wrong even when the person who commits it is excused for committing it — on grounds of ignorance, or duress, or both. On her view, the killing itself remains unjustified; the soldier is simply not culpable for it.

5. Marcus countered that if nearly every ordinary soldier on the unjust side qualifies for that excuse, then Elena's claim that they lack a genuine right to kill never actually convicts anyone in practice. So, he asked, what is the claim really doing, beyond letting her go on calling their actions "wrong" without any of the weight that word usually carries?

Neither would budge, and I did not feel qualified to settle it myself. Could you work through the exchange move by move, from 1 to 5, judging who has the stronger argument at each step rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be interested in whether some position could accommodate both of their underlying concerns, or whether they have, in fact, reached a genuine impasse.
