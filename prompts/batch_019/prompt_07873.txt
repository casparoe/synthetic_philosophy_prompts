So this happened at my ACX meetup a couple weeks back — we'd been doing a mini reading group on Dennett ("True Believers" plus bits of "Real Patterns") and it turned into a whole side argument about whether it's literally true, not just a convenient shorthand, to say things like "Claude believes the user wants a shorter answer." Two people in the group, Nadia and Alex, went back and forth for like twenty minutes over drinks and neither of them budged an inch. I've been chewing on it since because I genuinely think they're both onto something and I can't tell who's actually ahead.

(1) Nadia's opening move: Dennett's whole point is that there's no further fact beyond predictive usefulness. If treating a system as having beliefs and desires lets you predict its behavior better than the physical or design stance would, that IS what belief amounts to, full stop — same deal as the thermostat that "wants" the room at 68. So yes, the model believes things, in exactly the sense that matters.

(2) Alex pushed back that this isn't quite Dennett's criterion. The intentional stance only does predictive work because it's rationality-constrained — you attribute the beliefs a system *ought* to have given its situation and goals, and that's what makes it an explanation rather than just a redescription. Dennett's go-to examples (thermostats, chess programs) work because the rationalizing story is basically forced and unique. But LLMs flip stated "beliefs" under trivial rewordings, get talked out of correct answers by pushback that has zero evidential weight, and are hugely sensitive to prompt order in ways no rational believer would be. Worse, you can usually tell several equally good, mutually contradictory intentional stories about why a model said something. If the interpretation isn't even unique, Alex said, it's not obviously doing the job Dennett needed it to do.

(3) Nadia's comeback: humans blow rationality constraints all the time too — framing effects, anchoring, Asch-style conformity under social pressure — and nobody concludes from that that people lack beliefs, we just say human rationality is bounded. So mere inconsistency can't be the disqualifying thing, on pain of disqualifying us right along with the model.

(4) Alex's response: it's not just that inconsistency exists, it's the structure of it. Human framing effects are perturbations around some underlying state we can cross-check independently — later recall, behavior over time, in principle other channels — so there's reason to think a real signal is being perturbed. With a model, there's no independent channel like that yet; every answer is computed fresh from whatever's sitting in the context window. So whether the model's inconsistency is "noise on top of a real belief" or "no stable state, pattern-completion the whole way down" is exactly the open empirical question, and Nadia's analogy to human irrationality just assumes an answer to it instead of arguing for one.
