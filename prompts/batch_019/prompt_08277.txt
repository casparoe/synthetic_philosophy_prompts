Okay so this has turned into a weirdly big part of my life the last few weeks. I got put on my county's brand-new "algorithmic oversight" committee (three retirees, a public defender, and me) after a local reporter did basically a mini remake of that old ProPublica/COMPAS story, except on our pretrial risk-assessment tool -- she found that Black defendants who never went on to reoffend got flagged as "high risk" at something like double the rate of white defendants who also never reoffended. Kind of a big local scandal for about a week, lots of angry city council emails, etc.

So we had an emergency meeting with the company that sells us the tool, and their data scientist gave this very smooth presentation basically saying the reporter's framing (false positive rates) is a misleading way to slice the numbers, and that the metric that actually matters is calibration: if you look at everyone the tool scored, say, 7 out of 10, roughly the same fraction actually reoffend no matter their race, and that's the "real," mathematically rigorous definition of a fair score. He kept implying the false-positive-rate stuff is basically what you get when a reporter goes looking for a scary headline instead of doing real statistics. Most of the committee (me included, in the room) nodded along, partly because none of us are stats people and he had alot of slides and jargon.

I've been chewing on it since though, because it feels suspicious that the metric the company happens to look good on also happens to be the objectively correct one. I googled around that night and found references to some Kleinberg and Chouldechova papers that apparently proved something about this exact calibration-vs-false-positive-rate fight, but the papers themselves are way over my head math-wise, so I couldn't tell if they back him up or not. Before our next meeting (in two weeks, send help), can you help me actually understand this: is calibration really the more objective, rigorous fairness standard here, meaning the vendor's basically right and the reporter's stat is just misleading advocacy math? Or is there something off with how he's framing this as a "rigorous metric vs. sloppy journalism" choice? I don't want to walk in and get out-technical'd by a slide deck again.
