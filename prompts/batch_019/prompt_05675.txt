I'm a software engineer with no formal training in mathematical logic, but I've been working through some of the older MIRI papers on self-modifying AI, in particular the "tiling agents" line of work, and I keep running into what's called the Löbian obstacle. I can recite the one-line version of Löb's theorem — if a system can prove that the provability of P implies P, then it can already prove P outright — but I don't yet see, at a mechanical level, why this specifically prevents an agent from certifying a successor that verifies its own actions using an identical proof system.

Could you walk me through this with a concrete setup, such as an agent that will only approve a self-rewrite if it can first prove the general rule "whatever my successor proves about safety is true," and show exactly where the Löb-theorem step enters and forces the derivation to collapse? I'd also like to understand why the usual fix — giving the successor a strictly weaker proof system, along the lines of Herreshoff's "infinite descent" approach — only defers the problem rather than resolving it, and whether the well-known "five-and-ten" example is really a version of the same failure mode or a distinct issue that happens to invoke Löb's theorem for its own reasons.
