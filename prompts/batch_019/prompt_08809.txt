So for my formal epistemology seminar we spent a week on the surprise exam paradox (teacher tells the class "there'll be a surprise exam sometime next week, you won't be able to know which day it is in advance," students backward-induct their way to "so it literally can't happen on any day," and then the exam happens anyway and is still a surprise). Most of my study group just wanted to marvel at how weird it is, but I got kind of obsessed with actually finding the flaw in the backward induction instead of just accepting "paradox, neat" as an answer, and I think I've got an argument, I just can't tell if it's actually good or if I'm badly reinventing something that's already out there (I vaguely remember hearing that Quine wrote a paper on this back in the 50s, and that someone came up with a "blind spot" solution at some point, but I don't remember the details and can't dig up the actual texts right now).

Here's the gist of what I've got: the backward induction "proof" that no day can host a surprise exam only actually goes through if the students fully believe the proof once they reach the last day. But if they fully believe it - i.e. they believe "the announcement can't come true on any day" - then they aren't specifically expecting an exam on any particular day, last day included. So when the exam actually shows up, it genuinely surprises them relative to what they actually believed, even on the day the "proof" supposedly ruled out. Basically the proof is self-undermining: it only works on the assumption that the students end up trusting it completely, but trusting it completely is exactly what re-opens the possibility of surprise, so full belief in the proof can never be a stable state. It reminds me a bit of Moore's paradox ("it's raining but I don't believe it's raining") in that the weirdness seems to come from the belief interacting with itself rather than from any actual fact about exams or calendars.

Could you poke holes in this for me? Specifically: (1) is the "self-undermining belief" step doing genuine logical work, or have I just hidden a fallacy in there somewhere, (2) if you happen to know what Quine's actual solution or the "blind spot" idea says, does my argument basically collapse into one of those or is it a real seperate move, and (3) even if its technically fine, does this actually dissolve the paradox or does it just relocate the weirdness onto "why can't the students hold a stable belief here" without answering that question either. Genuinely not sure if this is something to be proud of or a rookie mistake, so brutal honesty welcome.
