Since I retired from the physics department a few years back I've had way too much time on my hands, and lately I've fallen down a decision theory rabbit hole -- Newcomb's problem, the prisoner's dilemma, all of it. My son-in-law (who actually has a philosophy degree, unlike me) mentioned an old puzzle called "Death in Damascus" that apparently goes back to a paper by Gibbard and Harper in the 70s, riffing on that old fable about the servant who flees Death in Baghdad only to run straight into him in Samarra. Their version goes: you're in Damascus, Death shows up and tells you he's coming for you tomorrow, and Death is a perfect (or near-perfect) predictor who has already fixed whether he'll be waiting in Damascus or in Aleppo based on where he predicts you'll actually be tomorrow. You have to decide, right now, whether to stay put or flee to Aleppo, knowing that whichever city you end up in is presumably the one Death already picked.

At first glance this just seemed like Newcomb's problem with a body count, but the more I read the more I realized theres a nasty wrinkle: apparently this case is supposed to be genuinely embarrassing for causal decision theory (CDT) specifically, something to do with there being no "ratifiable" choice -- whatever you tentatively decide to do seems to give you a reason, on reflection, to decide the opposite, so you can't settle into a stable pick the way you can with plain old Newcomb. Evidential decision theory (EDT) apparently doesn't squirm in quite the same way, though I can't tell if thats because it actually handles the case better or just because it gives you a more fatalistic answer from the get-go.

Could you write me an essay that actually resolves this for me? Walk through how a causal decision theorist and an evidential decision theorist would each reason through the Damascus-versus-Aleppo choice, get into why the ratifiability issue specifically bites CDT and not (or not as badly) EDT, and then don't leave me hanging the way half these blog posts do -- tell me what you think the rational thing to do actually is, and defend it. If the honest answer turns out to be something like "it depends on how reliable the predictor is" or "the only stable answer involves randomizing," that's a fine verdict, as long as you actually argue for it instead of just listing the positions and shrugging.
