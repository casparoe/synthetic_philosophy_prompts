I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've been reporting a story on "smart" protective packaging -- the shear-thickening gels that stay soft when you handle them normally but seize up rock-hard the instant something slams into them. For a sidebar, I interviewed a philosopher (I'll call her Nora; she works on metaphysics of science) about why this stuff is a nice real-world hook for something philosophers actually argue about: whether a disposition, like being fragile, can just be defined as "would break if struck." Apparently there are two classic ways of breaking that simple theory, called finks and masks. A fink is when something changes the disposition itself right at the moment of the test -- the textbook case is a dead wire hooked up to a gadget that makes it live the instant before anyone touches it. A mask (some people say "antidote") is supposed to be different: nothing about the disposition changes, something just stops it from ever manifesting, like a poison that's still chemically poisonous even though you swallowed the antidote right along with it. David Lewis apparently patched the original theory to survive finks by requiring the same underlying "basis" to persist through the test, and masks are the comeback that's supposed to sink even his patch, since the basis is left completely alone -- its what happens downstream that gets interfered with.

Since I didn't want to put any of this in print without sanity-checking it, I looped in an old philosopher friend, Dan, who works on related stuff. He pushed back hard on Nora's specific example -- a fragile vase shipped inside the gel -- and the two of them hashed it out over email while I mostly just watched, cc'd on the thread. Nora's opening line: the vase-in-gel is a clean mask case, exactly like the poison and the antidote -- the vase itself hasn't changed at all, the gel just stops the fragility from ever showing up. Dan's comeback: once the gel is a fixed, permanent part of what actually gets shipped, the relevant object isn't "the vase," it's "vase-in-gel," and that object was never disposed to break in the first place, so there's no disposition there being masked -- there's just no disposition, period. Nora replied that this move, generalized, would let you dissolve every mask case on the books the same way (redescribe "poisoned food" as "food-that-comes-with-its-antidote" and the poisoning disposition disappears too), and pointed out that some real abuse-deterrent pills have the blocker compounded into the very same tablet at the factory -- "built in" every bit as much as Dan's gel -- and yet pharmacologists still call the active ingredient poisonous and the blocker a mask. Dan didn't fully back down: he said "built in from the start" was too crude a test, and that the real question is whether ordinary practice treats the protective bit as a functional component of one object (his view of the gel) or as a countermeasure bolted onto an otherwise complete one (maybe what's going on with the pill) -- and that pharmacologists saying something's "still poisonous" might just be loose talk that doesn't settle the metaphysics.

Could you go through Nora and Dan's exchange and adjudicate it move by move -- who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall -- and say what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd also like your own take on the bigger question underneath it: is there a principled way to decide when a protective add-on counts as part of the disposed object (making it a genuine mask) versus evidence that the "real" object never had the disposition to begin with, or is that line hopelessly interest-relative? End with a one-line verdict on how the vase-in-gel case should actually be classified, in exactly this format: \verdict{mask}, \verdict{no-disposition}, or \verdict{underdetermined}.
