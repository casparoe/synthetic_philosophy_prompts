At work a few weeks ago, a coworker used the phrase "the frame problem" while we were complaining about why our new agentic pipeline kept doing technically-correct-but-bizarrely-myopic things, and I realized I only had a hazy, Wikipedia-level sense of where that term actually came from. Since then I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole reading old McCarthy-and-Hayes-era AI papers, and I think I've got the textbook version down: in classical logic-based planning, it's not enough to state the effects of an action, because in principle you also need some way of handling everything the action does *not* change, and naively writing an explicit "frame axiom" for every non-effect explodes combinatorially as your world model grows.

What I still can't get an intuitive feel for is why philosophers like Dennett treated this as a symptom of something much deeper than an awkward bookkeeping problem in first-order logic. As I understand it (and I might be garbling the details), his fix for the naive robot was a second robot that reasoned explicitly about all the logical consequences of its actions before acting, relevant or not, and that one just sat there deducing trivia while the clock ran out; and Fodor apparently pushed this even further, suggesting that deciding what's relevant to a given belief or plan can't be fenced off into some tractable local module, because in principle almost anything a mind believes could turn out to be relevant to almost anything else.

Could you write me a short story (a page or two) that makes this progression felt rather than explained? I'd like it built around some artificial reasoner handling a deliberately mundane, low-stakes task (domestic or logistical, say, rather than anything weapons-related) whose plot turns on two distinct failures: one version that acts too hastily and is blindsided by an obvious unrepresented side effect, and a more "sophisticated" successor that, in trying to reason its way to what is and isn't relevant before acting, gets stuck precisely because that determination turns out to be as hard as the original task. I'd rather you invent your own scenario than retell Dennett's actual robot-and-bomb parable, and I'd like it to steer well clear of the generic "the AI didn't understand human values and caused a catastrophe" trope — the story should be about this narrower, weirder puzzle of relevance itself, not a Skynet-style morality tale. If you can gesture, even obliquely, at the Fodor-style worry that this might not be an engineering problem you can ever fully close off, all the better.
