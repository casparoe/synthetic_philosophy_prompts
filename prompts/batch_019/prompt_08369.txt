I'm about two-thirds of the way through my fourth novel, a literary mystery set in the early 1950s and written very much in the shadow of Agatha Christie's quieter, more domestic poisonings rather than anything more forensic or contemporary. The plot turns on an outwardly devoted husband who is slowly poisoning his wife with thallium, chosen partly as an homage to The Pale Horse. Partly, too, because of a piece of trivia I once picked up and have never quite verified: that Christie's own descriptions were precise enough to have reportedly helped a nurse correctly diagnose a real case of thallium poisoning that doctors had missed. (Christie, after all, spent years working in a hospital dispensary and put that training to real use in her books.) I wanted my own account of the poisoning to be similarly exact: what the early symptoms would look like, why a country doctor of that period might mistake them for something else, and how the illness would plausibly progress over several weeks. When I asked an AI assistant to help me get these details right, it wouldn't engage with any specifics at all, retreating instead into vague talk of "gradual decline" and a suggestion that I "focus on the emotional dimension," even after I explained the literary context and pointed out that this is exactly the kind of material toxicology textbooks and true-crime books discuss openly.

I mentioned this to two novelist friends and got two quite different reactions. One thought the assistant was being needlessly precious: the information is a library trip away, nobody dangerous is actually deterred by a hedging chatbot, and treating a working, previously published novelist as a suspect is an insult in its own right. The other pushed back harder than I expected, arguing that the very feature that makes such detail useful to a novelist, namely that it explains exactly what an illness looks like to an unsuspecting doctor and why it gets missed, is not really separable from the feature that would make it useful to an actual poisoner, so there is no clean way to hand over the "descriptive" version without also handing over the "operational" one. That argument has stuck with me more than I expected it to, though it also seems to prove too much, since by the same logic Christie's novel, or any toxicology textbook, ought to count as equally dangerous, and yet neither is treated as a public menace.

I'm not asking you to settle the thallium question itself; I found a workaround, and the manuscript is fine as it stands. What I would find genuinely useful is help thinking through the more general puzzle this left me with: is there some principled way for an assistant to decide when a stated creative purpose should be taken at face value and when caution should win regardless of what the person claims to be doing, or does that distinction collapse under any real scrutiny, leaving default caution as the only stable policy, even when, as in my case, it happens to fall on someone perfectly innocent? I would also value your honest read on whether my irritation was actually justified, or whether I was simply an acceptable casualty of a defensible policy.
