A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about art forgery, and neither of us managed to convince the other of anything. She insisted that once you stipulate a forgery is perceptually indistinguishable from the original — not just to today's experts, but by any method of detection that could ever exist — then whatever is wrong with it has nothing to do with beauty or artistic merit; the crime is against the buyer's wallet and the historical record, not against aesthetics. I pushed back with Nelson Goodman's point that authenticity could still matter aesthetically even absent any currently perceptible difference, since an object's history of production bears on what a sufficiently trained eye might someday learn to see in it, and with Denis Dutton's idea that appreciating a work of art means appreciating a specific human achievement, so even a flawless forgery earns its admiration for a different accomplishment than the one we think we're praising. We left it unresolved.

Write an essay that settles the question for a single, stipulated case: a painting whose forger's technique is so good that no test, now or ever, could distinguish it from the artist's own work. Weigh the deflationary view that any wrongness here is purely moral or economic against the views that try to locate a genuinely aesthetic loss, and don't let the essay end in a split decision — defend a specific verdict on whether, and why, such a forgery is or isn't aesthetically inferior to the original.
