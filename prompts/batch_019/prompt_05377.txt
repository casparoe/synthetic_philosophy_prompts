I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and for my bioethics practicum this term I've been placed as a non-voting observer on my university medical center's IRB, currently sitting in on the subcommittee reviewing a proposed trial of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy for patients with a terminal cancer diagnosis and clinically significant anxiety or depression about their prognosis. The protocol is closely modeled on the psilocybin-for-cancer-distress trials run at NYU and Johns Hopkins over the past decade, in which most participants rated their dosing session among the most personally meaningful experiences of their lives, on par with having a first child or losing a parent, and in which the treatment's benefit seems to depend on the intensity of that very experience rather than on any conventional pharmacological effect. I'm also taking a seminar on rational choice this term that spent two weeks on L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience, and I can't shake the parallel: the intervention is designed to produce a state that reshapes what a patient values and how they relate to their own death, and nobody involved -- not the investigators, not the participants who try to describe it afterward -- can tell a prospective participant what it will actually be like, or who they'll be on the other side of it.

The subcommittee is stuck on the consent form. Our IRB's template calls for a description of reasonably foreseeable risks and discomforts and of the procedures themselves; the current draft satisfies this by listing pharmacological risks (transient hypertension, panic, rare psychotic-like reactions) and dosing-day logistics, then handles everything else in one sentence about possible "alterations in perception, mood, and thinking." About half the subcommittee finds this entirely adequate: we don't make people consenting to marriage, combat deployment, or elective surgery sit through other people's testimony first, and singling out this protocol looks like paternalism dressed up as methodological rigor. The other half argues that when a treatment's mechanism of action is a change in the very values by which the patient would later judge whether it was worth having, our ordinary risk/benefit consent framework is the wrong tool, and approving the form as written lets us call something "informed consent" that isn't doing the work we normally need that phrase to do. Complicating matters, several eligible patients have a prognosis of only a few months, so anything that delays enrollment carries a real cost.

Could you draft the memo I've been asked to bring to next week's subcommittee meeting? It should recommend one of: approve as submitted, approve with specific amendments, or defer for revision -- and if you recommend amendments, say concretely what they should be and on what timeline the PI could realistically meet them given the patients' prognoses. Please engage the losing side's strongest argument rather than waving it away, including the possibility that no feasible amendment can actually give a prospective participant the kind of advance understanding informed consent usually presupposes, and say what the subcommittee should be willing to settle for instead if that turns out to be true.
