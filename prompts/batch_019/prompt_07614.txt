So I fell down a rabbit hole this week that started with re-reading some old Yudkowsky sequence posts on category boundaries and the "fallacy of gray," and ended with me reading up on the actual academic debate between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness. The way I'd always understood it: epistemicists (Williamson, etc.) are the ones who bite the bullet and say there's a genuine, precise fact about exactly how many grains of sand make a heap, we just can't ever know what it is -- which always struck me as a bizarrely overconfident metaphysical commitment for something as obviously fuzzy as "heap" or "bald." Supervaluationism seemed like the obviously saner alternative: instead of insisting on some hidden precise cutoff, it just says that in the fuzzy region there's genuinely no fact of the matter, so there's no sharp line anywhere, full stop. That's basically how I explained it to my rationalist meetup group last week too.

Except then a friend from that group who's done grad work adjacent to this told me I basically had the supervaluationist side backwards -- that on the standard semantics for it, "there exists a sharp cutoff somewhere in the heap-series" actually comes out true, it just refuses to say which grain-count it is. Which sounds to me like epistemicism with extra steps? Like isn't "there's a fact that a precise cutoff exists, we structurally just can't specify it" the whole epistemicist position already? I feel like im missing something about why philosophers treat these as such different, opposed views rather than as just notational variants of each other. So: is my friend right about the semantics, and if so, what's actually left of the intuition that supervaluationism avoids committing to sharp boundaries in a way epistemicism doesn't?
