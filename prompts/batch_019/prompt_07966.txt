Here's the question, worth 10 points:

"Explain the difference between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). Then say which of the two assumptions Bostrom's 'Presumptuous Philosopher' thought experiment is meant to be an objection to, and why. Finally, explain what happens to the classic (Carter-Leslie) Doomsday Argument if one adopts SIA in addition to SSA."

And here's the answer one student turned in, copied exactly, typos and all:

"SSA and SIA are the two main rules for how you should update on the fact that your a particular observer. SSA says you should reason as if you were randomly picked from the set of all actual observers in your reference class. SIA says you should reason as if you were randomly picked from the set of all possible observers, so it gives extra credence to theories that predict more observers overall, since there were more 'slots' you could've ended up filling.

The presumptuous philosopher case is Bostrom's way of showing what's wrong with SSA. Physicists have narrowed things down to two theories, T1 and T2, which are equally likely given the ordinary evidence, except T2 says the universe contains a trillion times more observers than T1 does. Because SSA already tells you to favor whichever theory has more observers in your reference class, a philosopher can just reason from the armchair that T2 is a trillion times more probable, no experiment needed. That's the absurd part -- your bare existence shouldn't be able to settle a physics question that confidently before any data comes in, so something's wrong with a rule that lets sheer population size drive your credence like that.

As for the Doomsday Argument: the classic Carter-Leslie version already builds in SIA -- you treat your birth rank as a random sample from everyone who'll ever live, and finding yourself this early is more likely if the future population turns out small rather than huge, so you shift your credence toward doom. Adding SSA on top of this flips the picture, because SSA's own pull toward large-population theories runs the opposite way and roughly cancels the shift out, which is why people say bringing in SSA is what defuses the Doomsday Argument rather than something that makes it worse."
