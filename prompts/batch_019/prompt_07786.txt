I've been working through the literature on anthropic reasoning for a seminar on formal epistemology, and keep running into the same two canonical examples. The self-sampling assumption (SSA) holds that an observer should reason as if randomly sampled from the set of all actual observers in its reference class, while the self-indication assumption (SIA) holds that an observer should update in favor of hypotheses implying more observers like itself, weighting each hypothesis in proportion to the number of such observers it predicts. The standard illustrations of how the two come apart are the Doomsday argument, which relies on something like SSA to push credence toward an earlier human extinction, and Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher, which is raised against SIA for letting an armchair reasoner "out-argue" cosmologists simply by favoring whichever theory implies a more populous universe. At this point I suspect I could reproduce the standard verdicts on these two cases without having really internalized the mechanics of why SSA and SIA disagree.

What I'd like instead is a new thought experiment: concrete enough that actual numbers and credences can be assigned, and structurally distinct from both the Doomsday argument and the presumptuous philosopher (so not simply a relabeling of "how many humans will ever live" or "how large is the universe"). It should be a case in which SSA and SIA yield different credences for an observer reasoning about their own predicament. Set up the scenario explicitly, compute the relevant credence under each assumption, and then localize the disagreement precisely: identify which step of the calculation -- which prior, which likelihood term, which normalization -- is where SSA and SIA actually part ways, rather than just asserting that the two totals differ.
