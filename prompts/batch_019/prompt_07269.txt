I've been reporting a feature on algorithmic risk-assessment tools used in bail and sentencing decisions, and there's a piece of the story I keep failing to explain to my own satisfaction, let alone a reader's. Everyone in this space eventually recites the shape of the 2016 dispute between ProPublica and the company Northpointe over the COMPAS recidivism tool: ProPublica showed that Black defendants who did not go on to reoffend were nonetheless flagged as high-risk far more often than white defendants who did not reoffend, while Northpointe countered that the tool was equally well-calibrated across race groups (among defendants assigned any given risk score, the actual reoffense rate was about the same regardless of race). At a conference panel on this a few months ago, a data scientist waved his hands and said both sides were right, and that this was "mathematically unavoidable" whenever the two groups have different underlying base rates of reoffending -- and then the panel moved on before I could ask him to actually show me why.

That's the gap I'd like you to fill. Rather than the general algebraic proof, could you walk me through the intuition for why this is so? Specifically: why can't a risk score be simultaneously well-calibrated within each group and have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, once the two groups' underlying base rates diverge -- true, as I understand it, except in the narrow case where the base rates happen to coincide, or the tool predicts with zero error? I'd love this grounded in a small invented example -- two groups with made-up base rates and a made-up risk threshold, worked through by hand -- so that I can watch the false-positive rates pull apart as the base rates move further from each other, and actually feel why calibration alone is doing that work. I'm less concerned, for now, with adjudicating which fairness criterion courts or parole boards ought to prefer; I just want the impossibility itself to stop feeling like an asserted fact and start feeling like an obvious, almost mechanical consequence of what calibration and equalized error rates each separately demand.
