My father-in-law and I got into it again over Christmas about wealth taxes, and this time he came armed with the Wilt Chamberlain thing from Nozick — the "25 cents a ticket, a million people freely pay to watch him play, so now he's got $250,000 he didn't have before, and if you thought the original distribution was just then the new one has to be just too, so taxing it away is just interference with people's free choices" argument. I'd never heard it before (one intro political philosophy class about a decade ago is the extent of my training) and not gonna lie, it kind of got me — it's a slick argument and all I had was the usual "but who pays for roads and schools" line, which he swatted away in about two seconds. He's already texting the family group chat about "round two" at Easter, so I actually want to be prepared this time instead of just repeating vibes.

I dug up my old copy of Anarchy, State, and Utopia over the break and started skimming, and it jogged my memory that Nozick's theory has two other pieces besides the free-transfer step that the Wilt Chamberlin story is all about: there's a "justice in acquisition" principle for how you come to own the unowned stuff in the first place, and a "rectification of injustice" principle for when the history is dirty. Once you leave the toy example and look at actual property and actual fortunes, basically none of it has a clean history — stolen land, slavery, colonial extraction, plain old fraud, etc. I even have this nagging memory that Nozick himself half-admits, almost in a throwaway line, that since we don't have anything like a precise theory of how to rectify all that, something functionally like Rawls's difference principle might have to serve as our best rough guess at what rectification requires until we know better. If I'm remembering that right, it seems like a huge concession that undercuts exactly the conclusion people reach for the Wilt Chamberlain story to support.

Can you turn this into an actual, rigorous argument rather than just my half-formed hunch? Specifically: argue that the Wilt Chamberlain argument does not establish that redistributive taxation is illegitimate, because on Nozick's own three-part framework the legitimacy of any transfer step is conditional on a just starting distribution, and given both the pervasiveness of real historical injustice and Nozick's own apparent openness to a patterned principle as a rectification stand-in, his framework — applied honestly to the actual world rather than to a stipulated-clean example — doesn't deliver the anti-redistribution verdict he's usually invoked for. I don't want a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" essay; I want the strongest version of this specific argument so I'm actually ready for Easter.
