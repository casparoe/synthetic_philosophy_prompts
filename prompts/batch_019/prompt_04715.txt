I've been reading around in metaphysics on my own, and there's a puzzle I can't quite get past. Consider the dispute between mereological universalists, who hold that any collection of objects whatsoever composes some further object (so that there is a single thing made up of, say, the Eiffel Tower and my left shoe), and nihilists, who hold that only mereological simples exist and that nothing ever composes anything else. Some philosophers treat this as a genuine, worldly disagreement on which only one side can be right. Others argue it's an illusion produced by "quantifier variance": the idea that the universalist and the nihilist are simply attaching different, equally legitimate meanings to words like "exists" and "object," so that once this is pointed out, both parties turn out to be speaking truly in their own idiom, and there is nothing substantive left to fight about.

Could you write a dialogue between a defender of each view that works through this exact case rather than staying at the level of generalities? The quantifier-variance defender should have to say what, short of simply stipulating the disagreement away, would ever count as evidence that a dispute like this one is substantive after all; the realist should have to say what could make one candidate meaning of the quantifier metaphysically privileged over the others. I'd like each side to respond to the specific point the other has just made, rather than falling back on restating its opening position.
