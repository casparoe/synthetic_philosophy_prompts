I'm working on a longread about the private metaphysical beliefs of famous scientists and mathematicians -- the stuff that never makes it into the textbook version of their work -- and I've been stuck for days on Gödel. Everyone knows the incompleteness theorems and maybe the ontological proof, but way fewer people know about this argument he apparently made (to his mother, in letters, and also in a list of his own philosophical "theses" that Hao Wang later published in *A Logical Journey*) for why there has to be an afterlife. The gist, if I'm getting it right: the world is "rationally constructed" -- basically a Leibniz-flavored principle-of-sufficient-reason thing where everything happens for an intelligible reason -- and it would be a violation of that rationality for a mind to spend decades developing itself, morally and intellectually, only to be totally annihilated right at the point where its most mature. He apparently also leaned on a conservation-law-style intuition (nothing just vanishes, it transforms) and took Leibniz's monadology seriously as an actual metaphysical model, where the simple, indivisible thing doing the thinking can't gradually decompose the way a body does.

I think this deserves waaay more attention than it gets, so I want to build a section of the piece around a dialogue that stress-tests it properly instead of just gesturing at "well Gödel believed some wierd stuff." Could you write me a dialogue (800 words or so) between two contemporary philosophers -- say, meeting up after one of them gave a colloquium talk reviving Gödel's argument -- one of whom thinks it's a genuinely underrated argument (not knockdown, but not the joke people treat it as either), and one who thinks it fails, and fails in an interesting way. I don't want either of them to be a pushover: the skeptic should press on the real soft spots (why think "the world is rational" in the strong sense the argument needs, whether that premise even gets you personal, memory-preserving survival rather than some vaguer "nothing is wasted," and whether the physics analogy is doing any real work or just borrowed prestige), and the defender needs actual comebacks rather than restating the premise louder. Real back-and-forth please, where they're responding to each other's specific points, not two monologues stitched together.
