Here's the question as it was given to us: "Explain the core idea behind Lewis's (1976) triviality result for indicative conditionals. Your answer should (i) state Stalnaker's Thesis and explain why it was an attractive proposal for the semantics of indicative conditionals, (ii) sketch the derivation of the triviality result, assuming the relevant class of credence functions is closed under conditionalization, and (iii) briefly explain how Adams's and Stalnaker's own responses to the triviality result differ from each other."

And here's my classmate's answer:

(i) Stalnaker's Thesis says that our credence in an indicative conditional "if A, then B" should equal our conditional credence in B given A: P(A > B) = P(B | A), for any A with P(A) > 0. It's an attractive idea because it lines up with how we seem to actually evaluate conditionals in practice, roughly via the Ramsey test: to figure out how confident you are in "if A, then B," you provisionally suppose A and then ask how confident you'd be in B under that supposition. If indicative conditionals express ordinary propositions with truth conditions, this thesis looks like the natural bridge between the semantics of the conditional and our credence in it.

(ii) The triviality result shows that, given one more assumption, no proposition can satisfy Stalnaker's Thesis in general except in degenerate cases. The extra assumption is that the thesis has to hold not just for our actual credence function P, but for every credence function you can get from P by conditionalizing on some proposition with positive probability -- i.e. the relevant class of credence functions is closed under conditionalization. Given that, here's the derivation: assume P(A>B) = P(B|A) holds generally, for any credence function in this class. Conditionalize P on B to get P_B: since B is certain under P_B, P_B(B|A) = 1 whenever P_B(A) > 0, so applying the thesis to P_B gives P_B(A>B) = 1, i.e. P(A>B | B) = 1. Conditionalize P on ¬B to get P_¬B: since B is impossible under P_¬B, P_¬B(B|A) = 0 whenever P_¬B(A) > 0, so P(A>B | ¬B) = 0. Now use the law of total probability: P(A>B) = P(B)·P(A>B | B) + P(¬B)·P(A>B | ¬B) = P(B)·1 + P(¬B)·0 = P(B). But the thesis also says P(A>B) = P(B|A), so P(B|A) = P(B) for arbitrary A and B in the algebra. That means every proposition would have to be probabilistically independent of every other one, which no interesting credence function can satisfy across the board -- hence the triviality result.

(iii) Adams and Stalnaker draw different morals from this. Adams keeps the idea that indicative conditionals express genuine propositions, true or false at a world relative to a selection function that picks out the closest A-world, and he responds to the triviality result by restricting the scope of the thesis rather than giving up on truth conditions. Stalnaker, on the other hand, gives up truth conditions for indicative conditionals altogether: on his view, asserting "if A, then B" doesn't state a proposition that's true or false, but expresses your conditional acceptance of B given A, so the "probability" of a conditional is really tracking assertibility rather than the probability of a genuine proposition -- which is exactly what lets him hang onto something like Stalnaker's Thesis without being touched by Lewis's argument, since that argument assumes the conditional has an ordinary propositional probability in the first place.
