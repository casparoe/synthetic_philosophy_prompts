Since retiring after thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist, I have taken to auditing philosophy courses at the local university, largely for the pleasure of having comfortable assumptions overturned by people forty years younger than me. This term that means a seminar on philosophy of mind, and a few weeks ago we spent a session on Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" — not the imitation-game setup itself, which I already knew backwards and forwards from decades of popular-science writing, but the "Contrary Views on the Main Question" section near the end, where Turing works through nine objections one by one. I will admit the ninth one, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception," genuinely startled me: I had not expected a founder of computer science to state, in print and without much apparent embarrassment, that he found the statistical case for telepathy overwhelming. Having mentioned, in passing, that I spent much of my career refereeing manuscripts, the instructor asked, half as a joke, whether I would help mark a short stack of exam responses on this very passage. I agreed, but philosophical argument is graded on rather different grounds than an experimental write-up, and before I commit my own marks to paper I would like a second, disinterested read on one particular response.

The question set to the students read: "In Section 6 of 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence,' Turing considers nine objections to the claim that a suitably programmed machine might be said to think, the last of which is 'The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception.' (a) Reconstruct the objection: what exactly does the possibility of ESP threaten to undermine about the imitation game as Turing sets it up, and by what mechanism? (b) What is Turing's own stated attitude toward the empirical evidence for ESP, and how does this attitude affect how seriously he treats this objection relative to the other eight? (c) Explain the function of the 'telepathy-proof room' that Turing proposes as a remedy, and why he takes it to be sufficient to neutralise the threat. (d) Turing closes the section by admitting that he has 'no very convincing arguments of a positive nature' for his own view. Does his handling of the ESP objection nonetheless reveal something about the deeper commitments of his overall position on machine intelligence? Defend your answer in a few sentences." Each part carries five marks, for a total of twenty.

Here is the answer as submitted, verbatim:

(a) Turing's ninth objection invokes extra-sensory perception — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis — as a possible embarrassment to the imitation game. The worry is that if the human contestant happens to be, in the parapsychological jargon of the period, a "sensitive," she might answer certain guessing-type questions — about the suit of a card the interrogator is holding, say — at a rate noticeably above chance. Since nothing in Turing's setup gives the machine any comparable power, a run of lucky guesses of this kind would let the interrogator single out the human contestant on grounds that have nothing to do with intelligence, so that the game would end up measuring psychic sensitivity rather than the capacity to think.

(b) Turing's attitude here is genuinely surprising, and rather different from his treatment of most of the other eight objections. Rather than brushing ESP aside as unscientific — the sort of dismissal he gives fairly briskly to, say, the theological objection earlier in the section — he states plainly that the statistical evidence for telepathy in particular is overwhelming, adding that once one has accepted it, one is not so very far from having to accept ghosts as well. This is a remarkably candid concession in an essay otherwise devoted to defending a fairly hard-headed, operational standard for "thinking," and it signals that Turing regards the ESP objection as resting on a genuine empirical possibility rather than a mere conceptual confusion — which is presumably why it earns a practical fix rather than a dismissal.

(c) Turing's proposed fix is to place the interrogator and both contestants in separate "telepathy-proof rooms." The safeguard is aimed at the machine's side of the ledger: granted that one cannot be certain in advance that a machine has no ESP of its own, sealing the rooms forecloses the possibility that it might pick up and exploit any ambient telepathic activity, and so end up seeming more convincingly human than its programming alone would warrant. Once the rooms are sealed, whatever the interrogator learns must come from the exchange of typewritten messages, which is what makes the test a test of thinking rather than of psychic sensitivity.

(d) I think it does. The fact that Turing is willing to entertain ESP as real, and to answer it with a procedural patch rather than a flat denial, suggests that his real allegiance is to the imitation game as a fair, symmetric procedure rather than to any particular theory of what thinking is made of. He is prepared to bracket a phenomenon that "seems to deny all our usual scientific ideas" so long as its practical effects on the game can be neutralised, which fits with the generally behaviourist cast of the rest of the essay: what matters is not the machine's inner constitution but whether a level playing field of question and answer can be maintained.
