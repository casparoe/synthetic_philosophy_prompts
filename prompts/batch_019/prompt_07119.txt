For a small collection of short fiction I'm assembling, each story built around a classical paradox (my early readers keep joking that I should just call the whole thing after Zeno), I already have a piece that circles the Ship of Theseus through a violin restored one sliver at a time, and another that stages the Sorites paradox as a quarrel among grain merchants. The story I'm missing, and the one I actually want most, is something built around supertasks, with Thomson's lamp at its center — a lamp switched on, then off, then on again, each interval half the length of the one before, so that infinitely many flips are packed into a single minute.

What I don't want is the easy version, where a mad inventor's switch simply glitches reality into paradox at the sixty-second mark. A philosopher I badgered about this over dinner insisted the real puzzle is subtler, and more interesting, than that: the trouble isn't that the lamp's state at the one-minute mark is somehow contradictory, but that nothing in the setup determines what that state is at all. The instructions fix the lamp's condition at every moment before the minute is up, but the minute mark itself lies just past every one of those moments, so the "answer" is less a paradox than a silence — a question the problem was never actually built to answer. I'd like the story to sit inside that silence rather than resolve it or play it for cheap vertigo.

Beyond that, you have full latitude with plot and character. As a possible starting point: the story might follow whoever actually builds such a lamp — an engineer settling a bet, a grieving physicist, an artist, a cult — and what it is like to be in the room as the minute runs out. If it is useful texture, there is a cousin puzzle involving balls dropped into and removed from a vase, where the final count depends unnervingly on the order in which identical-seeming balls happen to be labeled, and there is also a genuinely strange suggestion out of general relativity that certain warped regions of spacetime might let a distant observer watch an infinite task actually finish. Use either, both, or neither. Mostly I want a real story — atmosphere, characters who want things, a shape that earns its ending — in which the metaphysics of infinity is doing real work without ever being lectured about on the page.
