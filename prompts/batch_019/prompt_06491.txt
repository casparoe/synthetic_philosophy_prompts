I've been a longtime lurker on LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten, and a recent thread rehashing the "coherence theorems imply expected-utility maximization" debate sent me back to the money-pump literature, where I got stuck on something the standard expositions tend to gloss over. The textbook argument is familiar enough: if your preferences over A, B, and C are cyclical, a bookie can walk you through a sequence of trades, each of which looks like a strict improvement at the moment you make it, until you've paid several small fees to end up back with the option you started with. The usual moral drawn is that cyclical preferences are irrational.

What's nagging at me is the naive/sophisticated/resolute trichotomy that's supposed to map out the possible responses to this setup. A naive agent just walks into the pump. A sophisticated agent, foreseeing the whole sequence, refuses the first trade rather than be led step by step to a predictably regrettable finish. A resolute agent, in something like McClennen's sense, commits to a plan in advance and then actually goes through with the final trade even though, taken in isolation, it wouldn't choose it -- and thereby dodges the pump too, since the eventual "loss" was priced in from the start. Crucially, both strategies leave the agent's cyclical ranking fully intact; what's being vindicated as "rational" is a diachronic choice disposition, not the synchronic shape of the preference ordering. Resoluteness in particular has always struck critics as suspicious for exactly this reason: it looks like it could rescue almost any preference pattern from almost any pump, since you can always just precommit to stomach the bad-looking final move, which is usually taken as a mark against resolute choice rather than a vindication of cyclical preferences. And yet I keep running into what looks like the very same move being redeployed, apparently uncontroversially, on the other side of recent coherence-theorem debates -- the claim that an agent with incomplete rather than cyclical preferences can dodge the analogous money pump for completeness once it's allowed to choose in a suitably farsighted, non-myopic way.

So here's what I'd like help untangling. Is there a principled, mechanics-level reason why farsighted choice legitimately blocks the money pump for incompleteness without equally rescuing the cyclic-preference case it was originally invoked for (and where it's usually considered question-begging) -- something about what each kind of agent actually has to give up or commit to in the two cases -- or does taking the "farsighted agents dodge the pump" move seriously for incompleteness commit you to thinking the classic argument against intransitivity never showed anything either? I'd like you to work through the actual structure of the two pumps and what a sophisticated or resolute agent's choices look like in each, rather than just reporting that people disagree about it.
