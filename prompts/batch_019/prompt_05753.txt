I'm a novelist, currently drafting a book narrated by a magazine book critic, and in the course of interviewing people in publishing for research I ended up witnessing an argument that has stayed with me longer than anything I've actually managed to use in the manuscript. Two friends of mine who both review fiction for a living, Simone and Aidan, fell into it over something Simone had done the week before. An editor she trusts completely had begged her, on a tight deadline, to blurb a debut novel; Simone read the first third plus the editor's detailed chapter notes, then wrote the line that ended up on the cover: 'A stunning, fully assured debut.' Aidan thought this was a minor scandal. Simone thought he was being precious. I mostly listened, because I honestly couldn't tell who was right, and I'd like your help going through it move by move.

As best I can reconstruct it: (1) Aidan opened by saying 'fully assured' isn't a hedge but a report of a firsthand impression of the whole book, and Simone never had that impression of two-thirds of it — she has the editor's impression, filtered through trust, not her own. (2) Simone answered that this is exactly how expert deference works everywhere else: a general practitioner who defers to a radiologist's reading of a scan is still credited with knowing the diagnosis, and her trust in this particular editor is extremely well-calibrated — ten years, never once oversold a book to her — so her belief that the novel is excellent is at least as justified as a rushed read of her own would have produced, maybe more. (3) Aidan granted that testimony can hand her a justified belief, but denied it can hand her the specific thing 'assured' claims to report: a sense of how the book's parts cohere, whether its tonal shifts are earned, how the pacing lands. That, he said, isn't a discrete fact you either receive secondhand or don't, the way a scan result is; it's closer to an interpretive achievement, and no summary, however faithful, substitutes for having made it. (4) Simone replied that experienced critics make exactly this kind of call reliably on partial evidence all the time — a practiced reader can often tell by page fifty, well before the ending, whether the later shifts will land — so the 'achieved understanding' Aidan was describing isn't some indivisible thing you either possess whole or lack entirely; like most judgments, it can be well estimated from good partial evidence, hers included.

(5) Aidan then reached for a comparison: plenty of people think a child who never steals only because her mother told her it's wrong has the right belief but not yet moral understanding, however reliable a source the mother is; Simone's blurb, he said, borrows the grammatical form of 'I have judged this myself, and it holds up,' and that form claims more than her actual epistemic position entitles her to, however good her source. (6) Simone's last word was that blurbs are a recognized genre with their own conventions — everyone in publishing knows a blurb is often some mix of partial reading and borrowed trust, so no false impression is created, unlike a signed, bylined review, which really does claim full firsthand engagement. If Aidan's real complaint is with the institution of blurbing, she said, fine — but that is a much narrower claim than 'testimony can't ground an aesthetic verdict.'

Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and adjudicate them? Please give your answer first as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, ..., "6": <score>}, where each score is an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right); then, in a few sentences per move, justify each score. Finally, add a short closing paragraph on whether there is a defensible position in this dispute that neither Simone nor Aidan considered, and end with one line, on its own, naming whichever of the two you think comes out ahead once all six moves are weighed together.
