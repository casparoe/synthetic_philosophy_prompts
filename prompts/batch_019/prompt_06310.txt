For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist, and one of the unexpected pleasures of taking up philosophy in retirement has been discovering how much stranger the canonical stories are than the summaries in textbooks ever let on. I have lately been making my way through Hobbes, and was struck by the anecdote, recounted in Aubrey's *Brief Lives*, of how he came to geometry at all: already close to forty years old, and leafing idly through a copy of Euclid's *Elements* left open in a gentleman's library, he landed on Book I, Proposition 47 — the Pythagorean theorem — read it, reportedly exclaimed that it was impossible, and then worked backward through the chain of demonstrations, proposition by proposition, until he was at last convinced of its truth. By his own later account, this single encounter is what made him fall in love with geometry, and with the idea that a science of politics might be built up from clear definitions with the very same certainty as Euclid's theorems.

What I find almost too good to be true is the rest of the story. The same Hobbes who wanted to found civil philosophy on geometric demonstration went on to spend roughly the last two decades of his life insisting, against the patient and entirely correct refutations of John Wallis and the rest of Oxford's mathematicians, that he had squared the circle — and he never once backed down, even after the affair became notorious enough to be the subject of an entire scholarly monograph titled *Squaring the Circle: The War Between Hobbes and Wallis*. Having spent my own career on the receiving end of peer review, I recognize the personality type, and I find something quietly comic in a man who staked his whole philosophical method on the authority of geometric proof, yet met actual geometric refutation with nothing but obstinacy.

Could you write me a short joke built around this particular irony? I would like the punchline to depend on genuinely knowing what Hobbes hoped geometry would do for philosophy, and how badly his own mathematics undercut that hope — rather than being a generic pun about circles or mathematicians that anyone could produce without knowing anything in particular about Hobbes.
