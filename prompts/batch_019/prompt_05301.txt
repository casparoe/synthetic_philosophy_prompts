Hey, I'm prepping my epistemology seminar and got stuck re-reading the Meno. Socrates's puzzle about why knowing the road to Larissa is better than just correctly guessing it always seemed intuitive to me, but the modern version -- the swamping problem -- has me second-guessing myself. The worry: once a belief is already true, it shouldnt matter how reliably the process that produced it was, since the value of reliability just gets swamped by the value of the truth itself (like how a good cup of coffee is equally good whether or not the machine that made it is reliable). Virtue epistemologists (Sosa, Greco, the "credit for a cognitive achievement" crowd) think they escape this by saying knowledge is valuable because the agent deserves credit for getting it right through skill rather than luck. Can you walk me through, at a gut level, why crediting the agent is even supposed to add value beyond the value of the true belief itself -- and then tell me stright up whether that actually solves the swamping problem or just relocates it a level up (i.e, why would credit/achievement carry value beyond the merely instrumental)? Don't just give me the encyclopedia summary, I want the actual mechanics of the argument.
