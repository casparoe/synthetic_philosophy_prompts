Exam question: Nash's bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) solution agree on Pareto-efficiency, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine transformations of utility, but they diverge on a fourth axiom, each satisfying one that the other fails.
(a) [4 points] Name the axiom each solution satisfies instead of the other, and state in your own words what each requires.
(b) [8 points] Two bargainers, A and B, have disagreement point (0,0); their feasible utility pairs (x,y) are exactly those satisfying 2x + y ≤ 10, x,y ≥ 0. Find the Nash solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, and comment on what your results show about how the two solutions relate to each other in general.
(c) [8 points] David Gauthier's "minimax relative concession" — the bargain he argues rational, unconstrained parties would agree to in Morals by Agreement — is standardly identified with the KS solution. Explain why Gauthier's principle answers to that axiom rather than Nash's, then briefly assess the objection that grounding morality in any bargaining solution built from parties' ideal points makes justice hostage to bargaining power rather than to an independent standard of fairness.

Student's answer:
(a) Nash's solution satisfies Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA): if the solution found for a bargaining problem still lies inside some smaller feasible set that is a subset of the original problem, it remains the solution for that smaller problem too. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution gives up IIA for individual monotonicity instead: if the feasible set changes so that player 1's ideal payoff (the most they could get) rises while player 2's ideal payoff stays the same, player 1 should get at least as much in the new solution as in the old one.

(b) The ideal point here is (5, 10): setting y = 0 in 2x + y = 10 gives x = 5, and setting x = 0 gives y = 10. For the Nash solution, maximize xy subject to y = 10 − 2x: this gives f(x) = 10x − 2x², f′(x) = 10 − 4x = 0, so x = 2.5 and y = 5. For the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, intersect the segment from (0,0) to (5,10) — the line y = 2x — with the frontier: 2x + 2x = 10, so again x = 2.5 and y = 5. The two solutions coincide. This happens because the underlying problem is symmetric — A and B face mirror-image bargaining opportunities — and both solutions satisfy a symmetry axiom that forces them to agree whenever a problem is symmetric in this way.

(c) Gauthier's minimax relative concession measures each party's concession as the gap between their ideal payoff and what they actually receive, and picks the outcome that minimizes the largest such gap between the two parties; since this is exactly the gap that the Kalai–Smorodinsky construction equalizes, Gauthier's principle is standardly identified with the KS solution. He wants the bargain to answer to what each party could ideally achieve — their bargaining power, in effect — rather than only to the local shape of the feasible set near the eventual agreement, which is all IIA tracks, so monotonicity is the natural axiom for his purposes. The objection that this makes justice hostage to bargaining power is a serious one, and it bites Gauthier's construction more directly than it would a Nash-based theory, precisely because the ideal point — each party's best-case leverage — is doing explicit, load-bearing work in fixing the outcome. Gauthier's own reply is to restrict the disagreement point itself with a Lockean proviso against advantages secured by force or fraud, so that the "power" the solution tracks is at least power exercised within a prior baseline of non-interference; the objection therefore lands hardest against undisciplined uses of KS-style bargaining, not obviously against Gauthier's own, more qualified, version of it.
