I cover philosophy and the history of ideas for a science-and-culture magazine, and I'm currently drafting a feature on how different philosophical traditions counsel equanimity about dying. For the Daoist material I've been reading my way through James Legge's 1891 translation of the Zhuangzi, in the Sacred Books of the East series. The most famous set-pieces—the butterfly dream, the scene of Zhuangzi drumming on a basin after his wife's death—translate cleanly enough, but I got stuck on a shorter, much less quoted episode a little further into the "Great and Most Honoured Master" chapter (Book VI of the Inner Chapters), right after the vignette in which a man named Dze-yü, whose body has been twisted into a grotesque hunchback, cheerfully imagines his own arm being transformed into a crossbow and his rump-bone into a cart-wheel. I mentioned the passage below to a sinologist friend, who dismissed it as "just a statement of fatalism," but that gloss feels too quick to me, and I'd like a more careful reading before I decide whether to use it in the piece.

Here is the passage, in Legge's translation:

"Before long Dze-lâi fell ill, and lay gasping at the point of death, while his wife and children stood around him wailing. Dze-lî went to ask for him, and said to them, 'Hush! Get out of the way! Do not disturb him as he is passing through his change.' Then, leaning against the door, he said (to the dying man), 'Great indeed is the Creator! What will He now make you to become? Where will He take you to? Will He make you the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect?'

"Dze-lâi replied, 'Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious. There is the great Mass (of nature);—I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it:—what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.

"'Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, "I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-yeh," the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, "I must become a man; I must become a man," the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking.'"

What I'd like help untangling is the actual structure of the argument. First, the passage seems to run two analogies together that pull in different directions: the filial one (a son obeys wherever a parent sends him) implies submission to something like a legitimate personal authority, while the metallurgical one implies something closer to the sheer presumption of a raw material that thinks it gets a say in its own shape at all. Is Zhuangzi entitled to both at once, or does the argument quietly trade on treating "the Creator," or the "great Mass," first as a quasi-parental will owed obedience and then as an impersonal process for which the whole vocabulary of obedience and owing no longer even applies? Second, is my friend right that this is simply fatalism, or is there a real, textually defensible distinction between the resignation Dze-lî recommends and something closer to active equanimity or acceptance? And finally, what work is the grim, almost comic image of becoming "the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect" doing philosophically—is it there to loosen our grip on the idea of a persisting self that could be harmed by such changes, or is it mostly rhetorical shock in service of a point that doesn't actually need it? I'd rather have a close reading of the logic here than another version of "the Daoists were relaxed about death."
