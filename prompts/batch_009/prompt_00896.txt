I'm drafting a blog post about historical figures who genuinely changed their minds in light of evidence, and I want to use the philosopher A. J. Ayer as my centerpiece example — though I don't have a formal philosophy background, so I'd like to sanity-check the argument first. Ayer is best known for the verification principle: the idea that a statement is meaningful only if we can specify what observation would confirm or disconfirm it. For most of his career he treated the prospect of surviving bodily death as, at best, wildly improbable, given how thoroughly mental life seems to depend on the brain. Then, in 1988, after being resuscitated from a four-minute cardiac arrest, he wrote that the experience had "slightly weakened" his conviction that death would be the end of him, while adding that he still hoped it would be. Surely a philosopher that careful wouldn't have written that sentence unless he took his own experience as genuine, if modest, empirical evidence against his prior view — which would make this a rare, well-documented case of a leading empiricist actually revising his metaphysics under the pressure of direct personal evidence. Is that a fair characterization, or am I reading too much into a single sentence from one essay?
