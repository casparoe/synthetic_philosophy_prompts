In the early modern philosophy survey I teach, we typically stop with Berkeley's immaterialism: the denial of mind-independent matter, the appeal to God as the sustaining perceiver, the running dispute with Locke over abstract general ideas. This year I added a session on a text almost none of my students had opened, The Querist (1735-37), the sequence of rhetorical questions Berkeley wrote as Bishop of Cloyne while lobbying the Irish Parliament to charter a national bank. They were startled to find the same philosopher asking, in Query 23, whether money's "true idea" is not that of a commodity with intrinsic worth but of "a ticket or counter," and later wondering whether, "the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone," trade might carry on regardless. His case for a publicly administered bank issuing paper credit unbacked by gold or silver was pitched throughout as a way to "excite and circulate the industry of mankind," in his own phrase, rather than to accumulate metal; some historians of economic thought even treat this monetary "nominalism" as continuous with his immaterialist metaphysics, though I want to set that particular connection aside for now.

I have set my students an optional essay question asking them to extend Berkeley's reasoning to developments he could not have foreseen, and before I finalize the wording I would like to see what a genuinely careful answer looks like. Bitcoin and its imitators are engineered to behave like the bullion Berkeley wanted Ireland weaned off: a fixed, algorithmically scarce token whose value is meant to owe nothing to any government's say-so. Central bank digital currencies point the opposite direction, toward a publicly administered ledger of credit not unlike the bank he proposed. And proponents of what is now called Modern Monetary Theory argue, on grounds not so far from his own, that a sovereign issuer of its own currency is constrained by real productive capacity rather than by any stock of reserves. Reasoning from the specific arguments of The Querist rather than from Berkeley's idealism in general, how do you think he would have judged each of these three developments? Would Bitcoin's engineered scarcity strike him as a relapse into the very bullionism he spends dozens of queries attacking, or would its character as a mere token, detached from any sovereign, still have appealed to him? Would he have welcomed central bank digital currencies and the reasoning behind Modern Monetary Theory as belated vindication, or worried about which authority controls the ledger, and to what civic ends?

Please do not hedge the exercise away as unanswerable. A good response should commit to a reading, tie each judgment back to specific queries or recurring themes in the text rather than to Berkeley's reputation in general, and note candidly where an eighteenth-century clergyman's categories simply run out before a twenty-first-century question does.
