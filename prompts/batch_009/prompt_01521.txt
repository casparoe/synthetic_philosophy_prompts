After a career as an experimental physicist working mostly on non-equilibrium thermodynamics, I have spent the last couple of years of retirement teaching myself philosophy, and lately I have fallen down the rabbit hole of decision theory. What drew me in was a family of "coherence arguments" for expected utility maximization: money-pump arguments that purport to show that any agent whose preferences are cyclical, or even just incomplete, can be exploited out of money by a sufficiently patient trading partner, and is therefore irrational unless her choices conform to some complete, transitive ordering representable by a utility function. The logical shape felt very familiar to me: it is structurally the same move as a no-perpetual-motion-machine argument in thermodynamics, where you show that a hypothetical device would let you extract free energy for nothing and conclude that the device cannot exist. The more of the secondary literature I work through, though, the less tidy that analogy looks.

What I keep circling back to is the exchange over so-called "resolute choice." As I understand the dialectic, Edward McClennen's proposal was that an agent can commit in advance to a plan that carries her through a whole sequence of trades without ever completing the money-losing circle, so that being exploitable when choices are made moment to moment ("myopically") shows nothing about the rationality of the preferences underlying them. I gather that mere foresight is not sufficient on its own: Rabinowicz apparently devised a subtler pump that still catches an agent who merely reasons her way through the decision tree by backward induction, while a genuinely resolute agent, who binds herself to a whole plan rather than reoptimizing at each choice node, was thought to escape even that. More recently, though, I understand that Johan Gustafsson has argued at some length, including in a chapter devoted specifically to the question, that resoluteness does not really rescue such agents after all, or does so only by covertly presupposing the very ranking that the money pump was supposed to establish independently. I cannot tell, from the papers alone, which side has the better of the exchange, and I would rather watch the argument actually happen than read another summary of it.

Could you write a dialogue between two decision theorists of equal skill and conviction: one defending the coherence argument, including its case against resolute choice, and the other defending resolute choice as a genuine, non-question-begging way for an agent with incomplete or cyclical preferences to avoid exploitation? I would like them to engage with each other's strongest moves rather than with caricatures of them. Please have the coherence theorist explain concretely why she thinks a resolute agent's commitment either fails to block exploitation after all or covertly presupposes the very ranking in dispute, and have the resolute-choice defender explain what, if not some prior ranking of outcomes, makes a plan count as rational in the first place. I would rather neither character simply concede by the end; I am more interested in seeing exactly where their disagreement actually bottoms out than in watching one side win outright.
