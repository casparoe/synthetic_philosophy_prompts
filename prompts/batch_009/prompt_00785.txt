I'm a junior who does both competition math and our school's philosophy club, and a couple of weekends ago those two activities collided into an argument I still haven't let go of. We had an all-day practice session before a proof-based tournament, and I'd stayed up basically all night finishing an unrelated problem set that was due that morning. Around noon I worked through one of the practice problems — a long chain of deductive steps where each line followed cleanly from the last — checked it twice, and felt completely confident in my answer. Right as I was about to move on, my coach mentioned, almost as an aside, that there's real research showing sleep-deprived people make careless slips in exactly this kind of multi-step deduction far more often than rested people, and that the frustrating thing about sleep deprivation is that it doesn't feel like impairment from the inside; you feel just as sharp as ever. She added that she could tell just by looking at me that I hadn't slept. Nothing she said pointed to any specific flawed step, and when I went back over the proof a third time, every line still looked airtight. Her point was purely statistical, about people in my situation generally. She thought I was rationally obligated to lower my confidence in the proof anyway, and I felt strangely resistant to that, without being able to say exactly why.

I brought this up afterward at philosophy club, where two graduate students we'd invited to speak that week ended up taking over the discussion. One of them, Priya, told me I'd basically rediscovered a real problem in epistemology involving "higher-order evidence" (evidence about how reliable your reasoning is likely to be, as opposed to evidence about the thing you're actually reasoning about) and "epistemic akrasia" (holding a belief while also believing, on reflection, that your evidence doesn't support it). She defended what she called the enkratic principle: since it's never rational to sit in that split state, once you get good evidence that you might be impaired, you're required to actually lower your confidence in the conclusion itself, not just note the possibility of error and move on. Otherwise, she said, you could run the same move over and over, using your own track record of "staying confident anyway" to eventually dismiss the possibility that you were ever impaired at all, which seems like a strange way for evidence of your unreliability to end up vindicating you. Her colleague, Teo, said this gets things backwards: if my proof really was sound, as the case stipulates, then being forced to discount it purely because of a general statistic about tired people is its own kind of irrationality. It's a way of letting a sufficiently alarming statistic about "people like me" override reasoning that, examined on its own terms, still holds up perfectly well. He'd rather bite the bullet and be what he called a "level-splitter": keep full confidence in the proof itself, while only lowering his confidence that this very confidence is rational, even though he admitted that this is exactly the akratic combination Priya says is never allowed. The two of them argued about it in the hallway for a solid twenty minutes afterward and never really landed anywhere, which is honestly why I'm still turning it over.

Could you write out that hallway argument as a short dialogue between Priya and Teo, maybe a page or so? I'd like both positions argued at full strength, with neither one a strawman, and I'd like them to actually engage each other's specific objections rather than just restate their own view: Priya should have to say something real about why Teo's level-splitting isn't just an ad hoc way of having it both ways, and Teo should have to say something real about why Priya's principle doesn't just amount to letting any sufficiently scary-sounding statistic override a chain of reasoning nobody can actually find fault with. They don't need to resolve it, but I'd like the dialogue to end with the disagreement pinned down precisely rather than the two of them talking past each other.
