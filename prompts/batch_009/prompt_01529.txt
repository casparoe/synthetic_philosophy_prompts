Maimonides, in the Guide of the Perplexed (III:32), offers a deflationary rationale for the whole institution of animal sacrifice: God did not actually want Israel to offer sacrifices, but permitted the practice as a concession to human psychology, since a people steeped for generations in Egyptian and Chaldean modes of worship could not be expected to abandon sacrificial ritual outright and leap straight to a purely cognitive service of prayer and study. Just as God led the Israelites the long way through the wilderness rather than risk the shock of a direct route through hostile territory (Exodus 13:17), He redirected an already-entrenched form of worship toward Himself, hedging it about with restrictions — a single sanctioned altar, a single eligible priestly line — that keep it firmly subordinate to the law's real aim. In III:46 Maimonides pushes the theory further: the specific animals Israel is commanded to sacrifice (cattle, sheep, goats) were chosen, he argues, precisely because they were the animals venerated by Egyptian and other idolaters, so that slaughtering them on God's altar would help uproot the superstitions attached to them.

Nahmanides, commenting on Leviticus 1:9, rejects this account in unusually blunt language. He objects, first, that it sits badly with the Torah's own recurring description of sacrifices as a food-offering of pleasing odor to God — language suggesting sacrifices have positive value to God, not merely a corrective function — and that Maimonides' theory reduces the altar to a device for humoring the wicked and the foolish. Second, he presses a sharper logical point: if idolaters already revere rams and cattle, then offering those very animals to God would, if anything, confirm rather than discredit their sanctity in idolatrous eyes; a genuine assault on the cult would instead involve eating the animals as ordinary food. Third, and most damagingly, he raises a historical objection: Abel's offering and Noah's sacrifice were both accepted by God, and described in the same terms of pleasing odor, long before any Egyptian or Chaldean cult of animal-worship existed for such offerings to be weaning anyone away from. Nahmanides' own alternative — offered tentatively, and explicitly billed as merely the more palatable of the options available short of the kabbalistic mystery he declines to spell out — treats sacrifice as vicarious substitution, in which the sinner's hand on the animal's head, his confession, and the burning of its organs and blood enact, and mercifully stand in for, the punishment he himself deserves. Matters are complicated further by the fact that Maimonides' own legal code, the Mishneh Torah, states without qualification that the Messiah will rebuild the Temple and restore the sacrificial order — an odd thing to anticipate if sacrifice really is only a temporary accommodation to a superseded stage of religious history.

Write an essay that adjudicates this dispute. Assess how much force Nahmanides' textual, logical, and historical objections really carry against Maimonides' accommodation theory, whether Maimonides has the resources to answer them (including the tension with the Mishneh Torah), and whether some intermediate position — for instance, that the general theory of III:32 survives even where its specific application in III:46 does not, or that the two thinkers are best read as answering different questions — is more defensible than simply declaring a winner. Whichever way you come down, the essay should close by committing to a definite verdict on whose account of sacrifice comes closer to the truth, rather than leaving the matter open.
