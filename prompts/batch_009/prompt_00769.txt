I recently came across a fringe idea from decision theory, discussed mostly among rationalist and AI-safety-adjacent writers, called "acausal trade," and it has stayed with me. The basic idea: two agents cooperate with each other despite having no causal connection whatsoever — no communication, no shared history, not even certainty that the other exists. They might be separated by unbridgeable cosmic distance, by different branches of a quantum multiverse, or simply by time, one living long after the other is gone. What allows them to cooperate anyway is prediction rather than contact: if each can reason accurately about how the other thinks — because they share a similar cognitive architecture, or simply because both are careful reasoners converging on the same logic — then each can act on the other's behalf right now, trusting that a counterpart elsewhere, or elsewhen, is independently reaching the same conclusion and reciprocating in kind. It's presented as an extreme extrapolation of the logic behind Newcomb's problem and Douglas Hofstadter's "superrationality": cooperation secured purely through mutual modeling, with no way for either side ever to confirm the other held up their end, or to punish them if they didn't.

I'd like you to write a short story, roughly 1000 to 1500 words, that dramatizes this idea in a way that lets a reader feel its pull, or its strangeness, rather than just having it explained. I'd rather you not default to the most obvious version of this — two remote superintelligences, or alien civilizations, reasoning about each other across the stars — unless you can make that setting feel genuinely fresh; I would be at least as interested in seeing the same logic surface somewhere smaller and more human: a promise, an inheritance, a private ritual, an act of restraint whose intended beneficiary can never learn that it happened.

Wherever you set it, I'd like the story to sit with the hardest part of the idea rather than resolve it too neatly: whatever is given can never be verified as received, never reciprocated in any way its author could trace, and never fully distinguishable from a one-sided delusion. Something with the sensibility of a Ted Chiang or Borges story, where the abstract mechanism and the emotional stakes turn out to be the same thing, is roughly what I have in mind.
