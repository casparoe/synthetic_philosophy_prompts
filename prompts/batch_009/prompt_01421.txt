Hey — I'm teaching a graduate seminar on promising this term, and we just spent two sessions on Rawls's 1955 paper "Two Concepts of Rules," working through his practice conception of promising. His central move is that a practice like promising is "logically prior to particular cases," the same way baseball is: you can't strike out or steal a base without the rules of the game already in place, and by the same logic you can't really promise at all outside the practice. Once the practice itself is justified, Rawls says, an individual promisor doesn't get to fall back on case by case utilitarian discretion about whether to keep a given promise, because foreclosing exactly that discretion is the whole point of having the practice.

What's been bugging a few of my students (and me, honestly) is that Rawls himself takes up an objection from Ross that he actually calls the more important of Ross's counterarguments: what do we say about a promise known only to the promisor and promisee, like a son's deathbed promise to his father about handling the estate? Breaking it wouldn't dent the practice's reputation or expose the son to any censure, since no one else will ever know — yet the obligation feels just as strict as any public one. Rawls's answer is basically to reapply his general distinction between justifying a practice and justifying the particular act that falls under it: the son never had standing to weigh those consequences to begin with, because that's not a defense the practice allows anyone.

I'd like you to argue that this answer doesn't actually work — that cases like this one (bring in "desert island" or dying-promisee cases from elsewhere in the literature too, if useful) show that promissory obligation can't be even partly grounded in participation in a social practice, contra Rawls. Actually engage with the "logically prior to particular cases" move rather than just asserting the intuition, and say concretely why it fails to rescue the practice theory here. Then commit to what's actually doing the binding work instead, if not the practice — expectations the promisor deliberately creates, some kind of practice-independent trust between the two of them, a normative power exercised over one's own future conduct, or something else entirely — just pick a lane rather then listing the whole menu of options.
