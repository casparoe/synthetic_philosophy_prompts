I am several chapters into a novel set in Florence and Rome between 1613 and 1616, in which a fictional young lens-grinder turned amateur mathematician drifts into Galileo's circle just as the quarrel over the earth's motion is hardening into an ecclesiastical matter. In the chapter I am drafting now, my protagonist is handed a manuscript copy of Galileo's letter to the Grand Duchess Christina and is later pressed by a skeptical but genuinely curious cardinal to explain, in his own words, what argument the letter is actually making about the relationship between scripture and natural philosophy. I want that scene to survive scrutiny rather than lean on the flattened, faintly anachronistic version of the story in which Galileo simply asserts that science overrides religion, so I have been working through Thomas Salusbury's 1661 translation of the letter — the first ever made into English — to get the reasoning straight before I let my character paraphrase it. One paragraph in particular has resisted my attempts to put it honestly into his mouth. Here it is, spelling and punctuation as printed:

"This therefore being granted, methinks that in the Discussion of Natural Problemes, we ought not to begin at the authority of places of Scripture; but at Sensible Experiments and Necessary Demonstrations: For, from the Divine Word, the Sacred Scripture and Nature did both alike proceed; the first, as the Holy Ghosts Inspiration; the second, as the most observant Executrix of Gods Commands: And moreover it being convenient in the Scriptures (by way of condescension to the understanding of all men) to speak many things different, in appearance; and so far as concernes the naked signification of the words, from absolute truth: But on the contrary, Nature being inexorable and immutable, and never passing the bounds of the Laws assigned her, as one that nothing careth whether her abstruse reasons and methods of operating be, or be not exposed to the Capacity of Men; I conceive that that, concerning Natural Effects, which either Sensible Experience sets before our eyes, or Necessary Demonstrations do prove unto us, ought not, upon any account, to be called into question, much less condemned upon the testimony of Texts of Scripture, which may, under their words, couch Senses seemingly contrary thereto; In regard that every Expression of Scripture is not tied to so strict conditions, as every Effect of Nature: Nor doth God less admirably discover himself unto us in Nature's Actions, than in the Scriptures Sacred Dictions."

What I cannot quite work out is the internal logic that lets Galileo hold this position without contradicting the claim, made elsewhere in the same letter, that scripture cannot err. He seems to want both that scripture and nature are equally God's handiwork, and therefore equally trustworthy, and that nature should nonetheless take methodological precedence whenever the two appear to conflict on a question of natural fact. I would like to understand exactly what is doing the work in that move: why the claim that scripture "condescends" to common understanding is supposed to justify demoting its authority in this one domain, rather than simply making it an equally reliable but differently worded witness alongside nature, and what precisely is implied by casting nature as the "executrix" of God's commands, as opposed to some other kind of testimony to the same divine will. My protagonist needs to be able to deliver this argument aloud, under mild interrogation, in a way that would actually have sounded rigorous to a theologically literate seventeenth-century listener, rather than like a modern person's idea of what a scientist ought to say to a priest — so I would rather not be handed a modernized gloss that quietly smuggles in that later opposition.
