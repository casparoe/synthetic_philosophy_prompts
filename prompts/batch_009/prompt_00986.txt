I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but Frankfurt-style cases in the free will literature caught my attention because they resemble a philosophical version of a fail-safe or watchdog process. In the classic setup, a neuroscientist, Black, wants Jones to vote for a particular candidate. Black has implanted a device in Jones's brain that will force him to vote that way if Jones shows any sign of deciding otherwise, but if Jones decides on his own to vote that way, the device never activates. Frankfurt uses this case to argue against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP) -- the claim that someone is morally responsible for an act only if they could have done otherwise -- since Jones seems responsible even though, given the device, he couldn't have done otherwise. Write an essay that weighs the main considerations for and against Frankfurt's conclusion. In particular, engage with (a) the 'flicker of freedom' reply, which holds that Jones retains some thin, residual alternative (such as failing to exhibit whatever prior sign would have triggered the device) that is enough to save PAP after all, and (b) the dilemma defense associated with Kane and Widerker, which argues that the prior sign is either causally sufficient for Jones's choice -- in which case the example just presupposes determinism and begs the question against incompatibilists who hold PAP -- or it isn't, in which case Jones had a genuine alternative possibility after all. Don't just survey the debate: defend a specific verdict on whether Frankfurt cases actually succeed in refuting PAP.
