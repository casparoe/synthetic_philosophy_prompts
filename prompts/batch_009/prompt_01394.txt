I spent the last two decades of my career running a small neuropsychology laboratory studying hemispheric specialization, and along the way I helped manage the care of a few patients who, years before I met them, had undergone callosotomy to control severe epilepsy. Since retiring, I have finally had time to work through the philosophical literature that grew up around such patients, rather than just the neurological literature I was trained in, and I find that I cannot settle, to my own satisfaction, the question that seems to sit at its center: whether a split brain supports one stream of consciousness or two.

The classical picture, running from Sperry and Gazzaniga through Thomas Nagel's 1971 paper on brain bisection, rests on a fairly specific dissociation: a callosotomy patient can respond to a stimulus flashed to the right visual field either verbally or with the right hand, but can respond to a stimulus flashed to the left field only with the left hand, never verbally and never with the right — a pattern also on display in Levy, Trevarthen, and Sperry's classic "chimeric figures" experiments, where a composite face stitched together from two different half-faces was named according to one half and matched to a sample according to the other. Roland Puccetti took this dissociation seriously enough to argue for outright "mental duality," while Gazzaniga complicated matters further with his notion of a left-hemisphere "interpreter" that confabulates plausible explanations for actions it did not itself initiate. What I had not appreciated until recently is how much this textbook story has been contested just in the last several years: Pinto and colleagues reported in 2017 that their callosotomy patients could respond accurately to lateralized stimuli through either hand, or verbally, largely regardless of which visual field the stimulus fell in, and concluded that a split brain houses a single conscious agent with merely "split perception," not two. Gazzaniga's group countered that this apparent unity could be nothing more than cross-cueing between the hemispheres, and the two camps have been trading rebuttals in the pages of the journal Brain ever since without, so far as I can tell, any clean resolution. Even setting that dispute aside, it is not obvious to me that unified control of action would settle the further question of whether experience itself is unified.

A friend from my old department, now also retired, tells me the whole question is simply confused and that the responsible position is to stop asking it. I would like you to do better than that. Please write me an essay that weighs the classical dissociation evidence, the newer unity-favoring data, and the cross-cueing rebuttal against one another, that addresses explicitly whether unity of agency would actually be good evidence of unity of experience, and that then commits to an answer: one stream of consciousness, two, or, if you conclude that a determinate number simply fails to apply here — the possibility Nagel himself took most seriously — a clear statement of what you think we should say in its place. I want a defended verdict, not a shrug.
