So I'm taking a philosophy of history seminar this semester, and last week we covered Hempel's covering-law paper plus a chunk of Dray's response about how historical explanation supposedly doesn't really work via subsumption under laws. As "background," the professor also assigned this bit from Book VI of Mill's *System of Logic* (1843) — the chapter on "the Inverse Deductive, or Historical Method" — since apparently Mill was already worrying about whether history could be a law-governed science almost a hundred years before Hempel. A couple of us got into an argument after class about this exact paragraph and didn't get anywhere, so now I'm just confused with company. Here's the bit:

"But while I gladly acknowledge the great services which have been rendered to historical knowledge by this school, I cannot but deem them to be mostly chargeable with a fundamental misconception of the true method of social philosophy. The misconception consists in supposing that the order of succession which we may be able to trace among the different states of society and civilization which history presents to us, even if that order were more rigidly uniform than it has yet been proved to be, could ever amount to a law of nature. It can only be an empirical law. The succession of states of the human mind and of human society cannot have an independent law of its own; it must depend on the psychological and ethological laws which govern the action of circumstances on men and of men on circumstances. It is conceivable that those laws might be such, and the general circumstances of the human race such, as to determine the successive transformations of man and society to one given and unvarying order. But even if the case were so, it cannot be the ultimate aim of science to discover an empirical law. Until that law could be connected with the psychological and ethological laws on which it must depend, and, by the consilience of deduction à priori with historical evidence, could be converted from an empirical law into a scientific one, it could not be relied on for the prediction of future events, beyond, at most, strictly adjacent cases."

What I can't pin down is what exactly Mill thinks is missing from a merely "empirical" historical law versus a properly "scientific" one. He clearly grants that history really does show regular successions, so its not like he's some proto-narrativist who thinks every period is just unique and incomparable. But the upgrade path he wants, tying everything back to "ethological" laws of character formation, is something everybody agrees never actually got built as a real science, before or since. So is his considered position basically "there are zero real laws of history, only empirical placeholders, forever," which would make him sound weirdly close to the narrative side despite obviously writing this whole chapter as an attack on people who deny a science of society is possible? Or is there a way of reading "empirical law" here where it's still doing genuine explanatory work short of the full deductive grounding he says it needs? I'm also curious whether you'd say this passage basically anticipates Hempel's covering-law model a century early, or whether Mill's insistence on deducibility from underlying psychological laws is actually a much stricter (and different) requirement than anything Hempel ever asked for. Would love help untangling what Mill is really committed to here.
