hey, could use a sanity check on something for my diss (im in a philosophy program, chapter's on Nietzsche and moral agency). I keep getting stuck on the "sovereign individual" bit in the Second Essay of the Genealogy and can't tell if im overreading it or if theres actually something there.

Here's the passage (grabbed the old Horace Samuel translation off the internet since its public domain — sorry if the wording looks different from whatever Kaufmann edition you might know):

"If, however, we place ourselves at the end of this colossal process, at the point where the tree finally matures its fruits, when society and its morality of custom finally bring to light that to which it was only the means, then do we find as the ripest fruit on its tree the sovereign individual, that resembles only himself, that has got loose from the morality of custom, the autonomous 'super-moral' individual (for 'autonomous' and 'moral' are mutually-exclusive terms) — in short, the man of the personal, long, and independent will, competent to promise, and we find in him a proud consciousness... of what has been at last achieved and become vivified in him, a genuine consciousness of power and freedom, a feeling of human perfection in general... The proud knowledge of the extraordinary privilege of responsibility, the consciousness of this rare freedom, of this power over himself and over fate, has sunk right down to his innermost depths, and has become an instinct, a dominating instinct — what name will he give to it, to this dominating instinct, if he needs to have a word for it? But there is no doubt about it — the sovereign man calls it his conscience."

(GM II, section 2, if you want to locate it.)

Basically every secondary source I've read just takes this as N. finally cashing out his positive ideal — a self-legislating autonomous guy, like Kant's rational agent except won through breeding and cruelty instead of pure reason. But that always felt too easy to me, since a few sections later in the same essay he takes a swipe at Kant by name, saying flat out that "the categorical imperative reeks of cruelty," and the whole back half of the essay treats "conscience" turning inward as a straight-up illness (the bad conscience stuff). So is the "conscience" being praised in II.2 actually a different thing from the pathology he spends sections 16-22 diagnosing, or is it the same phenomenon and he just hasn't turned on it yet? Is there anything in the text or the essay's structure that actually settles whether this passage is sincere praise versus Nietzsche quietly setting up a target he demolishes later, or is it genuinely underdetermined and people are just projecting the Nietzsche they want onto it?
