The "discursive dilemma," also called the doctrinal paradox, is the fact that a group whose individual members hold perfectly consistent views on several logically connected questions can still reach a group verdict that depends on, and can differ across, how the votes are combined. On a premise-based procedure, the group votes separately on each underlying question and then applies the governing rule mechanically to whichever answers win; on a conclusion-based procedure, each member privately applies the rule to their own answers, and only the bottom-line verdicts are put to a vote. Lewis Kornhauser and Lawrence Sager first diagnosed this problem for multi-member courts, and Christian List and Philip Pettit later gave it its general, formal treatment in judgment-aggregation theory.

A case frequently invoked in that literature as the paradigmatic real-world instance is Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279 (1991). While serving a federal prison sentence for an unrelated crime, Oreste Fulminante confessed to a fellow inmate — who turned out to be a paid FBI informant — that he had murdered his stepdaughter; he was subsequently indicted in Arizona, convicted, and sentenced to death. The Supreme Court had to resolve three connected questions about that confession:

(A) Was it coerced, and so inadmissible?
(B) If coerced, can its admission nonetheless be excused as "harmless error," or does using a coerced confession automatically require a new trial?
(C) If harmless-error review does apply, was admitting this particular confession harmless on the actual facts of the case?

According to the Court's official syllabus of who joined which part of which opinion, the Justices split as follows:
- On (A): Justice White, joined by Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens and Scalia, held the confession coerced. Chief Justice Rehnquist, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy and Souter, held it voluntary.
- On (B): Rehnquist, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter and Scalia, held for the Court that harmless-error review applies even to coerced confessions, distinguishing this "trial error" from "structural defects in the constitution of the trial mechanism." White, joined by Marshall, Blackmun and Stevens, dissented, maintaining that admitting a coerced confession can never be harmless.
- On (C): White, joined by Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens and Kennedy, held for the Court that admitting the confession was not harmless here. Rehnquist, joined by O'Connor and Scalia, dissented, arguing it was harmless. Souter joined neither conclusion.

The Court accordingly affirmed, 5–4, the Arizona Supreme Court's order for a new trial. Justice Kennedy, who supplied the fifth vote for that majority, wrote separately:

"The same majority of the Court does not agree on the three issues presented by the trial court's determination to admit Fulminante's first confession: whether the confession was inadmissible because coerced; whether harmless error analysis is appropriate; and if so whether any error was harmless here. My own view that the confession was not coerced does not command a majority. In the interests of providing a clear mandate to the Arizona Supreme Court in this capital case, I deem it proper to accept in the case now before us the holding of five Justices that the confession was coerced and inadmissible." (499 U.S. 279, 313–15 (1991) (Kennedy, J., concurring in the judgment))
