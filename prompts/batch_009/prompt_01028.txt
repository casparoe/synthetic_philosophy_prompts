I don't have any academic background in philosophy or biology, but over the past year I've been reading Karl Popper on my own, mainly because I got interested in the falsifiability criterion as a way of telling real science apart from pseudoscience. In what I believe was his intellectual autobiography, Popper actually turns that very criterion on Darwinism and finds it wanting: he argues that the theory of natural selection really just amounts to "survival of the fittest," and since the only practical way to identify the fittest organisms is to see which ones survived, the claim collapses into something close to a tautology rather than a genuine, falsifiable empirical statement. He goes so far as to call Darwinism a "metaphysical research programme" rather than a proper scientific theory. Coming from the philosopher most responsible for the standard we use to separate science from non-science, this struck me as a much bigger deal than any popular treatment of evolution I've encountered ever lets on.

What I haven't been able to work out is how seriously I should take this. If Popper himself, after thinking it through with some care, concluded that evolutionary theory doesn't clear the bar he set for it, shouldn't that make biologists considerably more tentative about treating it as settled science, in the way they treat something like germ theory or plate tectonics? I brought this up with a cousin of mine who works in a genetics lab, and she waved it away almost instantly without really engaging with the reasoning, which left me more puzzled than reassured. Is there something about Popper's argument, or about how it's usually reported, that I'm not seeing, which would explain why it hasn't actually dented evolution's status as hard science?
