So this happened at the reception after our department's colloquium a few weeks back, and it's been bugging me ever since. I even brought it up with a couple of my grad students in office hours and none of us could agree on an answer, which almost never happens -- I've been teaching philosophy for twenty-some years at this point. Anyway: servers were circulating with trays of appetizers all evening, and beforehand I'd quietly decided on a rule for myself, since I knew I'd get pulled into conversations and lose track of things -- wave off the tray the first time it comes to me, take one the second time, and wave off every time after that. (Once leaves me starving before dinner, three or more ruins my appetite, but twice is exactly right.) The trouble is the trays are identical and the servers weave through the crowd from random directions, so by the time one is actually in front of me I genuinely cannot tell whether this is the first, second, or fifth time its happened to come my way that night.

I explained the whole thing to a colleague in the stats department, who said the question of "what's my best rule" is kind of moot -- since I can never tell the occasions apart, whatever fixed probability I use for "take one" each time a tray shows up is exactly as good as any other, because from where I'm standing, every one of these moments is identical. That felt too fast to me, though I couldn't say why. And when I actually tried, standing there with a tray in front of me, to work out something like "how likely is it that this is the second time," the answer seemed to depend entirely on which rule I was already following, which felt like reasoning in a circle. So, who's right here: is my colleague correct that no rule can beat any other, and is "how likely is this the second time" even a sensible question for me to be asking myself in that moment?
