For a seminar on early modern political thought, I'm drafting a short paper on the fragments in Pascal's Pensées dealing with justice and force, and I'd welcome your comments before I turn this into a full draft.

Here is the core of the argument so far. In fragment 294 (I'm using the Brunschvicg numbering throughout, since editions disagree), Pascal observes that what counts as just shifts with geography and custom alone, captured in his remark, "truth on this side of the Pyrenees, error on the other side." He concludes that custom, not reason, is what he calls "the mystical foundation" of law's authority, and adds that it would be dangerous to admit this to ordinary people, since they obey laws only because they believe them just, so rulers should instead have them obey simply because the laws are laws. Separately, in fragment 298, Pascal argues that justice and force have to be combined for a society to hold together, but since justice is "subject to dispute" while force is "not disputed," force ends up dressed up as justice rather than the other way around: "we have made what is strong just."

My working thesis is that this amounts to something different from both Thrasymachus's claim in Republic, Book I, that justice is simply the advantage of the stronger, and from Hobbes's contractarian case for absolute sovereignty. Thrasymachus offers a debunking definition of justice; Hobbes offers a rational, self-interested justification for obedience. Pascal, by contrast, seems to hold that genuine justice exists but is inaccessible to corrupted human reason, making enforced custom a necessary fiction against chaos rather than either cynicism or a contract we would rationally choose.

Could you tell me whether this three-way contrast actually holds up, or whether I'm exaggerating how far Pascal's picture really is from Hobbes's and Thrasymachus's? I would also appreciate pushback on whether I've read the two fragments correctly, and on whether this comparison is even the most interesting angle to take on them.
