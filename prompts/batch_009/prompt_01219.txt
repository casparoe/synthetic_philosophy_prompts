My father died a little over two months ago, unexpectedly, and in the time since I have found myself reading things I never anticipated needing. A friend who works on classical Chinese philosophy, aware that I was struggling, sent me the death-related passages of the Zhuangzi rather than any of the standard Western consolations, and they have stayed with me in a way that Epicurus and the Stoics, both of which I also tried, did not. Three images in particular keep recurring to me: the skull that Zhuangzi meets on the road and uses as a pillow, which tells him in a dream that the dead have no ruler above them and no subjects below, no toil through the four seasons, and a happiness no southward-facing king could match; the scene, in the section usually titled something like "Perfect Happiness" or "Supreme Enjoyment" depending on the translation, of Zhuangzi drumming on a basin and singing after his wife's death instead of wailing, on the grounds that her dying is only the latest in the same long sequence of transformations that earlier produced her vital breath, then her shape, then her birth; and the passage where a dying man likens himself to a piece of metal on a smith's forge, observing that metal which leapt up and insisted on becoming one particular famous sword would strike the smith as an ill omen, so he will not insist on remaining nothing but a man as the great forge that shaped him once goes on to recast him into whatever comes next.

What unsettles me about all this is that it is not really the comforting picture it becomes in popular summaries, along the lines of "nature is cyclical, so grief is a kind of confusion." It seems stranger and more demanding than that: it suggests that the particular person I lost, and for that matter the particular person doing the missing, may not even be the right unit to grieve over, in something like the way a single scene in a dream is not the right unit to feel nostalgic about once you have simply moved into a different scene (Zhuangzi, notably, never tells us which side of his butterfly dream is supposed to be the more real one). I would like you to write me a short story, in whatever setting, period, and voice you think suits the material, that takes this specific and rather vertiginous version of the idea seriously, rather than flattening it into the "death is natural, so be at peace" moral I keep encountering elsewhere. It doesn't need to be long. I would only ask that it not resolve the discomfort too neatly by the end: the text itself never fully resolves it, and I would rather the story stayed honest about that than made me feel better than the philosophy actually warrants.
