A friend and I spent much of a dinner last week arguing about the ethics of the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings, a debate that eventually turned to G.E.M. Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," which we had both read for a seminar on just war theory. My friend's position was that Anscombe's outrage at Truman ultimately reduces to arithmetic: that she thought the bombs killed an indefensibly large number of innocent people, and that her argument is therefore just a more eloquently stated version of the ordinary consequentialist objection to the bombings, the one that weighs the death toll against the contested alternative of a land invasion. On this reading, since some historians now argue that an invasion would have cost more lives on both sides than the bombs did, Anscombe's case against Truman would, if she were being consistent, have to soften or even collapse once the casualty figures are recalculated in Truman's favor.

I pushed back, mostly on instinct, because I recalled that Anscombe is closely associated with some principle distinguishing effects that are intended from effects that are merely foreseen, and it seemed strange that this would matter to her at all if her objection were really just a matter of counting bodies. I could not reconstruct the point precisely enough to settle things, though, and we more or less ended the evening agreeing to disagree. Could you help me work out what Anscombe's actual argument against Truman was, and whether recalculating the casualty figures in his favor would do anything to undermine it?
