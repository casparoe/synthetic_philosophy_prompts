In the Autobiography, Franklin describes his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection": thirteen virtues, each given a week of undivided attention in fixed rotation, tracked in a little book where a black spot marked every daily lapse. He grounds the whole scheme not in religious duty but in the claim that vicious acts are "forbidden because they are hurtful" rather than hurtful because forbidden. Yet he also confesses that his eventual acceptance of failure — his conclusion that "a speckled ax was best" — might itself be a rationalization, since the extreme self-scrutiny he had imposed on himself could look like "a kind of foppery in morals." Suppose Franklin were shown a present-day habit-tracking app that keeps his ledger automatically, replaces his private weekly self-examination with algorithmic reminders, and sustains use through streaks, badges, and comparison with other users. Reasoning strictly from his own stated grounds for designing the project as he did, would he welcome such an app as fulfilling his scheme, or see it as quietly abandoning what made the scheme a moral exercise rather than a merely behavioral one? Justify your answer with reference to specific features of his account.
