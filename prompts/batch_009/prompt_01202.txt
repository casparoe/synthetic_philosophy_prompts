hey -- teaching a seminar on phil of AI this term and there's one question I can't get my students (or myself tbh) to stop being sloppy about: do neural nets actually have representations, or are we just borrowing intentional vocabulary because it's convenient?

here's what makes it hard to just wave away: the mechanistic interpretability crowd (Olah's old "features are directions, circuits are how they compose" picture, plus the later superposition/polysemanticity work once that group had moved to Anthropic) talk like they're literally discovering representational content sitting in the wieghts. but Ramsey's old "job description challenge" for representation-talk in connectionism, plus stuff from the probing literature (Hewitt & Liang's control tasks, showing high probe accuracy can come from the probe memorizing rather than the representation containing anything) both suggest a lot of this is less principled than it looks -- decodable isn't the same as represented, and it's not even obvious a superposed, polysemantic direction CAN be a representation of any one thing in particular.

so: write me an essay that actually takes a stand on whether real, trained neural nets (not idealized formal models) have representations in some philosophically serious sense -- not a "more research is needed" cop-out -- and defends it against the strongest version of the other side. bonus points if you address whether a Dennett-style "real patterns" instrumentalism is a genuine third option here or just realism and deflationism wearing a trench coat. if it's good I'm definately stealing a paragraph or two for my lecture slides.
