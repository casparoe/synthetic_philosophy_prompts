I'm a novelist currently at work on a novel partly set in the world of opera, and I've been re-reading Bernard Williams's On Opera for background. In his essay "Manifest Artifice: The Ingenuity of Puccini," Williams asks why Puccini's operas move audiences so powerfully even though their emotional effects are so nakedly engineered, at times almost sadistically so — pointing, among other things, to the torture and death of Liù in Turandot. Williams died in 2003, three years before the Metropolitan Opera began its "Live in HD" cinema broadcasts, which bring opera to audiences worldwide through the close-up eye of a film camera rather than across the distance of a theatre. Reasoning from the actual logic of his Puccini essay rather than general enthusiasm for the art form, how do you think Williams would have judged this way of watching opera? Does the close-up, by making the artifice even more manifest than it already is on stage, extend the ingenuity he admired in Puccini, or does it push things past some threshold where the illusion simply collapses?
