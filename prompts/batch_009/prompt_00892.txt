I'm writing a historical novel set among Cambridge logicians shortly after Turing's 1936 paper appeared. In a pivotal scene, I want my protagonist, a young mathematician, to declare the question settled for good: Church's lambda calculus, Gödel's recursive functions, and Turing's machines had all turned out, quite independently, to define exactly the same class of functions, and even Gödel, who had once dismissed Church's proposal as "thoroughly unsatisfactory," came around once he saw Turing's version. I want her to say, flatly and without hedging, that this convergence amounts to a genuine mathematical proof that "effectively calculable" simply means "computable by a Turing machine." Before I commit her to that line: could a logician of the period really have claimed this outright, or is there a substantive, still-relevant reason the result is called a thesis rather than a theorem?
