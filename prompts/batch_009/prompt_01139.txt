A friend of mine — who takes her horoscope rather more seriously than I'm comfortable with — and I spent most of a long car ride last week arguing about Johannes Kepler. She had read that Kepler cast horoscopes for money and took this as a kind of vindication: proof, in her eyes, that even the greatest astronomer of his age privately respected astrology. I pushed back that the real story is stranger and less flattering to her view than she assumed. Kepler was scathing about the popular astrology of almanac-makers and sign-readers, and even described astrology as a "foolish daughter" living off the reputation of her mother, astronomy. But he was not simply a debunker moonlighting for cash. In the Tertius Interveniens (1610) and in Book IV of the Harmonices Mundi (1619), he laid out a highly idiosyncratic reformed astrology: the twelve zodiac signs and the astrological houses were, in his view, baseless conventions, yet certain aspects — the angular relationships between planets — really did stamp an imprint on a newborn's character, because the soul is innately attuned to the same handful of geometrical ratios that also underlie musical consonance. He even had a principled, geometrical reason for including new aspects of his own devising, like the quintile, while ruling out others, such as the septile, as impossible in principle — tied to which regular polygons can actually be constructed with a compass and straightedge. My friend accused me of splitting hairs to protect a scientific hero from an embarrassing hobby, and I could not, in the moment, quite articulate why the distinction mattered so much to me.

Here is what I would like help thinking through instead. Suppose Kepler could be confronted with two pieces of the subsequent empirical record on astrology. First, Shawn Carlson's 1985 double-blind test, published in Nature, in which professional astrologers failed to match natal charts to their owners' personality profiles at a rate better than chance — a result that bears directly on his claim that a birth configuration leaves a distinctive, legible imprint on character. Second, Michel Gauquelin's much more contested "Mars effect": the claim that eminent athletes were disproportionately born with Mars near the horizon or the meridian, a pattern that, if genuine, would vindicate precisely the kind of house-like, angular-position framework Kepler dismissed as groundless, while having nothing to do with the inter-planetary aspects he actually spent his effort defending. Given how specific and how unusually principled Kepler's own commitments were, how do you think he would sort through this mixed and often contradictory evidence? Would Carlson's result read to him as a threat to his theory, or as vindication of his lifelong contempt for chart-reading as it was actually practiced? Would the Mars effect strike him as an embarrassing confirmation of exactly the framework he rejected, or would his own reasoning about constructible polygons give him principled grounds to wave it away as well? I would rather have a genuine reconstruction of his specific intellectual commitments than a general verdict on whether he would "believe in astrology" today.
