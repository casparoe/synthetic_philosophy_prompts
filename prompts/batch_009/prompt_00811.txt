I've been reading about the history of astronomy as a hobby, and something about Kepler keeps nagging at me. He was scathing about much of astrology: he dismissed the twelve houses as superstitious guesswork, and treated the zodiac's twelve signs, with their fixed characters and planetary rulers, as a human convention with no basis in nature. Yet he spent decades defending another piece of the same discipline, insisting that the geometric angles ("aspects") formed between planets really do affect the weather and human temperament, because he believed the cosmos runs on the same harmonic ratios that make musical intervals pleasing, and that the earth, and even the human soul, resonate with those ratios. This wasn't a passing thought: he wrote an entire book, Tertius Interveniens, staking out a middle position between the astrologers and the critics who wanted to discard astrology altogether, and he kept refining the idea for the rest of his life.

Was Kepler drawing a real, principled line between the astrology worth keeping and the astrology worth discarding, or was this an inconsistent compromise that let him keep calling himself an astrologer without applying his own standards evenly? Write an essay that weighs both readings and then reaches and defends a clear verdict, rather than leaving the matter unresolved.
