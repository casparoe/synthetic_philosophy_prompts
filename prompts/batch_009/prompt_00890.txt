Quick one from a rabbit hole I fell into while researching a piece on the weirder corners of early analytic philosophy. I keep running into "Bradley's regress" doing what feel like two different jobs and I can't tell if that's just sloppy conflation on people's part or if there's a genuinely deep connection. On one hand there's Bradley's own argument in Appearance and Reality, where he says relations are basically unintelligible without some further relation tying them to the things they relate, and that further relation needs its own tie, forever -- which he uses to conclude relations aren't ultimately real, all in service of his big Absolute-idealism picture. On the other hand there's this seperate-seeming headache Russell has in the Principles of Mathematics about the "unity of the proposition" -- why "Socrates is wise" is one unified claim instead of just a list with Socrates and wisdom sitting in it side by side, and whether the relation in something like "A differs from B" actually relates A and B or is just another inert term along for the ride if the proposition happens to be false.

So, genuinely: are these the same regress showing up in two different contexts, or two different problems (one about whether relations are real, one about the logical unity of propositions/judgments) that got historically tangled together because Russell was directly arguing against Bradley in that book? And while you're at it, could you get into whether Frege's move of making concepts "unsaturated" actually escapes the regress or just relocates it somewhere less obvious, and whether Russell's later multiple-relation theory of judgment -- the one Wittgenstein supposedly torpedoed by pointing out it can't rule out judging nonsense -- was basically Russell quietly running face-first into his own version of Bradley's problem. Not trying to write a dissertation here, I'd just like to actually understand the moving parts well enough that I don't butcher it in print.
