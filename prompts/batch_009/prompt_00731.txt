A friend of mine, who is finishing a PhD in economics, and I spent the better part of a long dinner arguing about whether a recession is likely within the next two years. Neither of us managed to talk the other one down, even after we'd each laid out our reasons in detail, and at some point she remarked, half as a joke, that this was a little embarrassing for both of us: two Bayesians who trust each other's honesty and competence are supposedly not able to remain in disagreement once each knows what the other thinks. That offhand comment sent us down a much more interesting side-argument about Aumann's agreement theorem and the assumption usually taken to underwrite it.

She invoked Aumann's 1976 result that if two people share a common prior, it cannot be common knowledge between them that their posterior probabilities for the same event differ; and she brought up the Cowen and Hanson paper that pushes this further, arguing that persistent, knowing disagreement between two people who each consider the other honest and reasonably competent is very hard to reconcile with both of them actually being rational. I objected that all of this leans on the common prior assumption (sometimes called the Harsanyi doctrine) doing far more work than it usually gets credit for. Showing that two people updating on the same evidence from the same starting point must end up agreeing is one thing; insisting that any two reasonable people must have had the same starting point to begin with is quite another. I pointed out that Savage's own subjectivist account of probability explicitly allowed two reasonable people to hold different credences given the very same evidence, and that even Aumann has conceded that the common prior assumption is not simply forced on us by the logic of being a Bayesian. She wasn't moved: on her view, any disagreement supposedly explained by differing priors is really just a polite name for someone reasoning badly, or for a self-favoring bias in how each of us arrived at our starting point.

We had to leave it there, so I would like you to help me make the case properly. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that the common prior assumption is not a requirement of ideal rationality, and that two fully rational, mutually honest Bayesian agents can therefore knowingly and persistently disagree, even under common knowledge of each other's opinions, without either one being guilty of any epistemic failure. Engage directly with Aumann's theorem and with the Cowen and Hanson argument rather than sidestepping them, and try to say something substantive about what, if anything, could justify two such agents having started out with different priors in the first place.
