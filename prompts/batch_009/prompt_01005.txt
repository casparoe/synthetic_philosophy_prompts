I'm a novelist working on a book set at a small New England college in the autumn of 1897, and I keep getting stuck on a chapter I can't yet write to my satisfaction. Two of my recurring characters, a precise young mathematician who has taken W. K. Clifford's evidentialism as something close to a personal creed, and an older philosopher who has more or less adopted William James's new essay on the will to believe as his own, stay behind late one night after a meeting of the college's small philosophical discussion society and end up arguing on the library steps about whether one is ever entitled to let, in James's phrase, one's "passional nature" decide a question the intellect cannot settle on its own. I know the historical shape of this dispute reasonably well: Clifford's principle that it is wrong "always, everywhere, and for anyone" to believe on insufficient evidence, dramatized by his parable of the shipowner who talks himself into believing his unsound ship is seaworthy, against James's claim that some options are "living," "forced," and "momentous," so that declining to decide is itself already a decision. But every draft of the scene I write collapses into the same faith-versus-reason shorthand you get in survey articles, with each man simply restating his side.

What I'd like from you is a real dialogue between these two men, long enough to have genuine back-and-forth, that gets past that shorthand. I'd like the mathematician to press what seems to me the sharpest difficulty in James's position: that his best examples, the mountaineer who must leap a chasm and needs faith in his own success to make the leap, or the case where simply trusting that someone cares for you is part of what makes them come to care for you in return, are all instances where the very act of believing helps bring about the thing believed, and that religious faith, which is what James actually wants to defend, is nothing like this, since a man's believing in God does not do anything to make God exist. I want to see whether the Jamesian character has a real reply to that, rather than just restating the doctrine of the will to believe, and I want the mathematician's own position tested just as hard in return, since it seems only fair to ask whether Clifford's rule that belief always requires sufficient evidence is itself a claim anyone has sufficient evidence for. Let each man answer the other's actual arguments in turn, and please don't let it resolve too neatly; I would rather it end the way these conversations tend to end in life, with both of them having scored real points and neither one persuaded.
