Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through the free-will literature, and I have become stuck on one point in the debate over Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. I can follow the basic case without difficulty, and I see why Fischer's robustness requirement disposes of the simple 'flicker of freedom' reply. What I cannot reconstruct on my own is the dilemma defense that Kane, Widerker, and Ginet developed independently against the indeterministic, prior-sign versions of these cases: the charge that the sign triggering the counterfactual intervener is either causally sufficient for the agent's action, in which case the example covertly imports determinism, or it is not, in which case the agent could, after all, have done otherwise at the decisive moment. Could you lay out this dilemma, explain how defenders of Frankfurt-style cases have tried to escape between its horns (for instance, via the 'buffer' cases devised by Mele and Robb), and tell me whether you think the maneuver succeeds?
