Below is a short draft essay on a tension I believe runs through Jeff McMahan's revisionist critique of the moral equality of combatants. I would like comments on it, focused on the three questions at the end.

Traditional just war theory treats the justice of resorting to war (jus ad bellum) as independent of the justice of conduct within it (jus in bello): whatever the merits of their respective causes, combatants on both sides face the same permissions and prohibitions, including the permission to kill enemy combatants. Call this the moral equality of combatants (MEC). Jeff McMahan's revisionist critique in Killing in War (2009) denies MEC: on his view, liability to be killed in war, as in ordinary self-defense, tracks moral responsibility for contributing to an unjustified threat, not mere combatant status. Combatants fighting for an unjust cause are typically liable to attack because they contribute to such a threat; combatants fighting for a just cause typically are not.

The most familiar objection to this picture is epistemic: ordinary soldiers are rarely in a position to know whether their side's cause is just, so it seems unreasonable to treat them, rather than their justly fighting counterparts, as legitimate targets. McMahan's central reply distinguishes an act's being blameless from its being permissible: not knowing that a drink is poisoned excuses the person who serves it, but does not make serving poisoned drinks any less wrong. Excuse, on this view, defeats blame but not liability, so an unjust combatant who reasonably, but wrongly, believes his cause to be just can still be a legitimate target. Together with the further, empirical claim that few unjust combatants are in fact fully excused, this leads McMahan to conclude that an "overwhelming majority" of them remain liable to attack.

But McMahan's own treatment of what he calls "nonresponsible threats" sits awkwardly with the conceptual half of this reply. Discussing a person whose lethal action results entirely from having been drugged, he argues that a threat generated without any morally responsible agency cannot be liable to defensive killing at all; at most, a weaker lesser-evil justification might permit force against him. In a separate paper on the moral status of child soldiers, he considers two routes by which a combatant could end up in this category: lacking the capacity for moral agency altogether, as with an abducted and indoctrinated child, or being epistemically justified in a false belief that one's war is just. He explicitly allows that a combatant who is fully justified in that second way — who suffers, in the old phrase, from "invincible ignorance" — is a plausible candidate for nonresponsible-threat status, not merely an excused agent.

That looks like it undoes the earlier reply exactly where the epistemic objection needs it to hold. The objection concerns precisely the combatants whose false belief in their cause is most fully justified — the closest real-world approximation to invincible ignorance. But those are the very combatants McMahan's other discussion assigns to the nonresponsible-threat category, where excuse does not merely fail to remove blame but apparently takes liability with it. It is unclear what principled line separates the ordinary conscript — propagandized, drilled, operating under lethal military discipline — from the drugged aggressor or the abducted child soldier, if reasonable conviction of one's cause can put someone in the same boat as the latter two. Absent such a line, the distinction between "excused but still liable" and "nonresponsible and therefore not liable" starts to look drawn wherever it needs to be drawn to preserve the verdict McMahan wants, rather than derived from any independent account of responsible agency.

I would appreciate comments on the following:

1. Have I represented McMahan's position accurately, particularly the relationship between his blameworthiness/permissibility reply to the epistemic objection and his separate discussion of nonresponsible threats?
2. Is the tension I'm describing a genuine problem for the view, or is there a natural, principled way of distinguishing "excused but liable" combatants from "nonresponsible" ones that I've overlooked?
3. Does this line of criticism already have a home in the secondary literature under some other name, and if so, whose?
