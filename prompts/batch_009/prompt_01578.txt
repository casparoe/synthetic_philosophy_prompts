In my philosophy elective we've spent the last couple of weeks on the disagreement between Newton and Leibniz over whether space is a real thing in its own right or just a way of describing relations between objects, and we went through the Scholium in the Principia in a lot of detail: not just the famous rotating bucket, where the water climbs the sides once it's truly spinning even though by then it isn't moving relative to the bucket at all, but also the less famous two-globes variant, where two globes tied together by a cord and set spinning in otherwise empty space are supposed to show tension in the cord that reveals their rotation even with nothing else around to rotate relative to. Our teacher spent a whole class period on Mach's objection to this: that Newton never actually got to run either experiment with all the other matter in the universe taken away, so neither one really proves that the water or the cord would behave the same way if the fixed stars and everything else weren't there.

Weirdly, an astronomy elective I'm taking made this feel a lot less hypothetical. We learned that because the expansion of the universe is accelerating, on the order of a hundred billion years from now essentially every galaxy outside our own Local Group will have redshifted past the cosmic horizon and become permanently cut off from us causally, even though it's presumably all still out there somewhere. Any observer around at that point would be looking out at a dark, static-seeming sky with nothing gravitationally unbound left in it, which sounds a lot like the situation Mach said Newton could never actually test.

For my English class's creative writing unit, we have to submit a short story (800-1,500 words) built around a scientific or philosophical idea for the class anthology, and I'd like to use this. Could you write a story set in that far future, centered on someone (or something) who still has some kind of rotating apparatus -- an actual bucket, a spinning station, the two-globes-and-cord setup, whatever works dramatically -- and who has to work out, mainly through what they notice and reason through rather than through a narrator explaining the philosophy, whether they should expect it to behave the way a substantivalist would predict, or whether the rotation should become impossible or meaningless the way a relationist would expect once there's no longer any wider universe of matter for it to be rotating relative to? I don't want the story to just end on a pretty description of an empty sky -- I'd like the character to actually land on a considered position by the end, in a way that's honest about the fact that physicists and philosophers still haven't settled this.
