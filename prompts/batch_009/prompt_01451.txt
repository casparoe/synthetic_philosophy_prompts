I spent my working life as a chemist, and in retirement I have been reading my way through the philosophy of biology, most recently the long-running dispute over Gould and Lewontin's 1979 'Spandrels of San Marco' paper. Setting aside the well-worn complaint that the structures at San Marco are technically pendentives rather than spandrels, I was struck by the engineer and architectural historian Robert Mark's analysis showing that these pendentives actually bear substantial structural load, carrying the weight of the dome down into the piers, rather than being mere incidental left-over space. That strikes me as a genuine, not merely verbal, problem: if the one concrete case Gould and Lewontin chose to anchor their entire argument turns out, under real engineering analysis, to be functionally load-bearing rather than a non-adaptive by-product, doesn't that undermine the 'spandrel' concept at its foundation rather than merely around its edges? I would value your judgment on how much damage this really does to their case against adaptationism.
