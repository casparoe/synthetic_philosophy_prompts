I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on the causal exclusion problem and want to stress-test an objection to Stephen Yablo's determinable/determinate solution before committing to it. Yablo blocks Kim's exclusion argument by arguing that a mental property can be the proportionate cause of behavior even when a physical determinate of it is also causally sufficient, since proportionality requires that a cause be neither more specific nor more generic than its effect calls for. His illustration is Sophie the pigeon, trained to peck at red things generally: when she pecks at a scarlet triangle, 'red' (the determinable) rather than 'scarlet' (its determinate) counts as the proportionate cause, since she would have pecked at any shade of red. But this seems to depend entirely on individuating the effect coarsely, as 'pecking at a red triangle'; at the fine grain of the exact motor output produced by that specific retinal stimulation, the fully determinate physical cause will always be at least as proportionate as any determinable of it. Please argue for the position that Yablo's proportionality constraint therefore gives us no principled, non-question-begging way to fix the grain of description at which mental causes are proportionate and their physical realizers are not, and so cannot by itself rescue mental causation from exclusion.
