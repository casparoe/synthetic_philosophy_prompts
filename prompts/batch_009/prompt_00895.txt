Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through the history and philosophy of statistical mechanics, partly because I kept running into pop-science references to the "Boltzmann brain" problem and wanted to see where the idea actually comes from. It turns out the ancestor of the idea is a short passage Boltzmann tacked onto the end of his 1895 note in Nature (vol. 51, pp. 413–415), "On Certain Questions of the Theory of Gases," written in the middle of his exchange with Culverwell and others over whether the H-theorem's predicted march toward equilibrium could be squared with a universe that still contains a structured, living world like ours. The passage reads:

"I will conclude this paper with an idea of my old assistant, Dr. Schuetz. We assume that the whole universe is, and rests for ever, in thermal equilibrium. The probability that one (only one) part of the universe is in a certain state, is the smaller the further this state is from thermal equilibrium; but this probability is greater, the greater is the universe itself. If we assume the universe great enough, we can make the probability of one relatively small part being in any given state (however far from the state of thermal equilibrium), as great as we please. We can also make the probability great that, though the whole universe is in thermal equilibrium, our world is in its present state. It may be said that the world is so far from thermal equilibrium that we cannot imagine the improbability of such a state. But can we imagine, on the other side, how small a part of the whole universe this world is? Assuming the universe great enough, the probability that such a small part of it as our world should be in its present state, is no longer small.

If this assumption were correct, our world would return more and more to thermal equilibrium; but because the whole universe is so great, it might be probable that at some future time some other world might deviate as far from thermal equilibrium as our world does at present. Then the aforementioned H-curve would form a representation of what takes place in the universe. The summits of the curve would represent the worlds where visible motion and life exist."

I follow the basic statistical trade-off Boltzmann and Schuetz are proposing: fluctuations away from equilibrium become rarer as they grow larger, so a sufficiently vast universe can make even a fluctuation as large as our observable world tolerably probable somewhere within it. What I cannot reconstruct is why the argument is allowed to stop at the scale of a "world." If sheer size of the sample space is what buys down the improbability, the very same reasoning, pressed harder, should overwhelmingly favor much smaller and shorter-lived fluctuations — down to a single momentary observer furnished with false memories of an orderly cosmos — over an entire fluctuated galaxy that behaves lawfully for eons. Boltzmann and Schuetz simply help themselves to "worlds" as the relevant unit of selection, without asking whether their own logic undercuts that choice. I would be grateful for help seeing whether there is a tacit assumption in the passage that actually blocks this slide toward the more radical, single-observer version of the puzzle, or whether this is simply a gap that had to wait for later work on typicality and self-locating belief to close.
