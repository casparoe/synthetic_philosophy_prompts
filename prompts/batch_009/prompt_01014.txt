I am a philosophy PhD student working on decision theory, and I have been thinking about a puzzle from the literature on program equilibrium. In an ordinary one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, mutual defection is the only Nash equilibrium, however much each side might prefer to cooperate, since nothing binds either party to an announced intention. Program equilibrium changes the setup: instead of choosing actions, each player submits a program that can inspect the other player's source code before acting, which opens up equilibria that sustain cooperation without any outside enforcer. The obvious way to write such a program, "cooperate only once I can prove you will cooperate with me," looks hopelessly circular, since each side's proof seems to depend on the other's. Yet a peculiarity of self-referential provability, formalized through Löb's theorem, lets two such programs each secure the proof they need about the other without either one ever simulating the other's reasoning step by step.

Write a short story in which two parties, not necessarily human or even singular, face a one-off, high-stakes impasse that no ordinary promise could resolve, where the only path to safety is disclosing their actual decision procedures to each other rather than their intentions. Let the story's resolution, successful or not, turn on that specific bootstrapping problem rather than on trust or goodwill in the ordinary sense.
