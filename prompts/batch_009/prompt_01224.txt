I'm working on a feature about how stakes and pressure warp the way we think, and I fell down a rabbit hole this week reading old epistemology papers on the "bank cases" -- the thought experiment where a couple is happy to say "I know the bank's open Saturday" when it barely matters, but the moment a mortgage payment is riding on it and being wrong would be a disaster, they suddenly hedge and say they don't actually know that after all, even though not a single fact about their evidence has changed. Every source I read uses that exact case to introduce two different named positions, "epistemic contextualism" and "pragmatic encroachment," like they're two rival schools of thought.

Here's my problem: as far as I can tell they're just the same insight wearing two different labels -- i.e. how much is on the line for you changes whether you count as knowing something. I mentioned this to a philosopher friend at a party and he got kind of insistent that they're actually substantively different theories that people have spent entire careers arguing past each other about, but he couldn't really explain it in a way that survived a second glass of wine, lol. Since I don't want to either flatten a real distinction into nothing, or invent a fake distinction that isn't really there, when I write this up -- can you lay out what, if anything, actually seperates contextualism from pragmatic encroachment, given that they seem to agree on literally every case anyone throws at them?
