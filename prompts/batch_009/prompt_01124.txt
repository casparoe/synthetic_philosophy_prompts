I'm a software engineer at a small fintech company that offers short-term personal loans, often to people with limited or patchy credit history. For the past couple of months I've owned the risk model that decides who gets approved, and it was a fairly routine project until someone on the compliance side asked whether the model treats our two main customer segments fairly. These two groups, for reasons tied to income volatility and credit history length rather than anything about our company, have historically defaulted on loans at noticeably different rates. Once that question was on the table, three different people on the project each proposed a different definition of what "fair" should mean here, and I've spent weeks unable to satisfy all three at once with a single model — a fact that now worries me more than the underlying modeling work itself.

Our compliance lead wants both groups approved for loans at roughly the same overall rate. Our data science lead insists that whatever risk score the model outputs has to mean the same thing for both groups — if it tells us someone has a 15 percent chance of defaulting, that should be true no matter which group they belong to, or the number is meaningless. Our general counsel wants creditworthy applicants from both groups wrongly turned down at the same rate, and non-creditworthy applicants from both groups wrongly approved at the same rate, since she's worried about how an uneven error pattern would look to a regulator. Each of these, taken on its own, sounds like a bare minimum requirement for decency rather than an ambitious ask. Yet every version of the model I build satisfies at most two of the three, and deliberately improving one of these numbers reliably makes one of the others worse. I raised this over dinner with some friends outside tech, and one of them said, "just pick whichever one is fairest," which didn't really help, since I don't know how you'd even weigh a gap in approval rates against a gap in wrongful-denial rates in the first place.

I have no formal background in philosophy or ethics, only computer science, and everyone else on the project keeps treating this as an engineering shortfall that more data or a cleverer model would eventually fix. Is that actually right, or is there a deeper reason these three notions of fairness can't all hold at once, given that our two groups genuinely do default at different rates? And if this is a hard limit rather than a solvable bug, how should someone in my position think about which of the three to give up on?
