As part of a column I'm writing on logical fatalism, I keep returning to Aristotle's sea-battle puzzle in De Interpretatione 9: if it is already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, the battle's occurrence seems to have been fixed all along; if it is already false, its non-occurrence is just as fixed. Either way, real contingency starts to look illusory. Rather than explain the puzzle to me in the abstract, I would like a short story that puts a character inside it — a commander, a diviner, a logician traveling with the fleet, whoever you choose — who takes the argument seriously enough, on the eve of an actual engagement, that it begins to bear on what anyone thinks is worth doing. I would like the story's turn, whether through action, dialogue, or event, to embody one genuine philosophical way of blocking the fatalist conclusion — Aristotle's own move of denying that such statements are yet true or false, or a modern alternative such as Ockhamist or supervaluationist branching-time semantics — rather than simply leaving the paradox unresolved.
