I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of language this term, and we recently spent a session on Frege's "On Sense and Reference," where the students got a solid grip on the basic puzzle: why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" seems to convey real information, while "Hesperus is Hesperus" is empty, even though Hesperus and Phosphorus are the very same planet. Frege's proposed solution, that names carry a sense in addition to a reference and that co-referring names can present their common referent by way of different senses, was easy enough for everyone to follow. This week we moved on to Russell's "On Denoting," where Russell restates a version of the same puzzle using Scott and the author of Waverley, and then mounts an argument that Frege's meaning/denotation distinction cannot even be coherently formulated in the first place. I can sketch the general shape of Russell's complaint for my students, but the actual argument he gives for it — what commentators call the "Gray's Elegy argument" — keeps slipping out of my grasp whenever I try to state it as a sequence of valid steps rather than just gesture at its conclusion. I've looked at Blackburn and Code's old paper on it and at Salmon's "On Designating," and they do not reconstruct the steps in quite the same way, which has left me more rather than less confused. Here is the passage in full, as it appears in Mind, New Series, vol. 14, no. 56 (October 1905), pp. 485-487 (throughout, Russell uses a bare "C" for the denotation of a denoting phrase and "C" in inverted commas for its meaning):

The relation of the meaning to the denotation involves certain rather curious difficulties, which seem in themselves sufficient to prove that the theory which leads to such difficulties must be wrong.

When we wish to speak about the meaning of a denoting phrase, as opposed to its denotation, the natural mode of doing so is by inverted commas. Thus we say: The first line of Gray's Elegy states a proposition. "The first line of Gray's Elegy" does not state a proposition. Thus taking any denoting phrase, say C, we wish to consider the relation between C and "C," where the difference of the two is of the kind exemplified in this instance. We say, to begin with, that when C occurs it is the denotation that we are speaking about; but when "C" occurs, it is the meaning.

The one phrase C was to have both meaning and denotation. But if we speak of "the meaning of C," that gives us the meaning (if any) of the denotation. "The meaning of the first line of Gray's Elegy" is the same as "The meaning of 'The curfew tolls the knell of parting day,'" and is not the same as "The meaning of 'the first line of Gray's Elegy'". Thus in order to get the meaning we want, we must speak not of "the meaning of C," but of "the meaning of 'C'," which is the same as "'C'" by itself.

This leads us to say that, when we distinguish meaning and denotation, we must be dealing with the meaning: the meaning has denotation and is a complex, and there is not something other than the meaning, which can be called the complex, and be said to have both meaning and denotation. The right phrase, on the view in question, is that some meanings have denotations.

But this only makes our difficulty in speaking of meanings more evident. For suppose C is our complex; then we are to say that C is the meaning of the complex. Nevertheless, whenever C occurs without inverted commas, what is said is not true of the meaning, but only of the denotation, as when we say: The centre of mass of the Solar System is a point. Thus to speak of C itself, i.e., to make a proposition about the meaning, our subject must not be C, but something which denotes C. Thus "C," which is what we use when we want to speak of the meaning, must be not the meaning, but something which denotes the meaning. And C must not be a constituent of this complex (as it is of "the meaning of C"); for if C occurs in the complex, it will be its denotation, not its meaning, that will occur, and there is no backward road from denotations to meanings, because every object can be denoted by an infinite number of different denoting phrases.

Thus it would seem that "C" and C are different entities, such that "C" denotes C; but this cannot be an explanation, because the relation of "C" to C remains wholly mysterious; and where are we to find the denoting complex "C" which is to denote C? This is an inextricable tangle, and seems to prove that the whole distinction of meaning and denotation has been wrongly conceived.

That the meaning is relevant when a denoting phrase occurs in a proposition is formally proved by the puzzle about the author of Waverley. The proposition "Scott was the author of Waverley" has a property not possessed by "Scott was Scott," namely the property that George IV wished to know whether it was true. Thus the two are not identical propositions; hence the meaning of "the author of Waverley" must be relevant as well as the denotation, if we adhere to the point of view to which this distinction belongs.

Could you reconstruct this argument for me premise by premise, in a way that makes each inferential move explicit rather than paraphrasing the rhetorical flow? I would especially like to understand exactly what is supposed to go wrong, on Russell's view, when we try to use "the meaning of C" to talk about the meaning of the phrase C itself, and why he concludes that the relation between "C" and C is left "wholly mysterious" rather than simply accepted as a primitive semantic relation, which is more or less what a Fregean picture needs anyway. And, standing back from the details: once reconstructed, does this argument actually damage Frege's own solution to the identity-statement puzzle that motivated the whole discussion, or does it only show that Russell's particular way of setting up a sense/reference-style distinction, in terms of "meanings" that "denote" their denotations, is unworkable, leaving Frege's notion of Sinn comparatively untouched?
