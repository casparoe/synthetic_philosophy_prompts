I'm plotting a novel with a long framing device: through a conceit I haven't fully worked out yet (a discovered diary, or perhaps something stranger), Gottfried Leibniz becomes aware, centuries after his death, of John Searle's Chinese Room argument — the scenario in which a person with no Chinese, sealed in a room and following only a rulebook, manipulates Chinese symbols well enough to produce fluent answers, despite never understanding a word of what is said. I want to give Leibniz a full chapter of rebuttal, spoken entirely in his own philosophical idiom, and I'm wary of it reading as modern talking points draped over a historical costume.

What convinces me Leibniz belongs in this story at all is section 17 of the Monadology, where he already asks us to imagine a machine built so as to think and perceive, enlarged to the size of a mill so that we could walk through its workings, and observes that we would find only parts pushing against one another, nothing resembling perception. Searle's room is, structurally, a descendant of that same thought experiment, right down to the "step inside and look for the mind" move, and I'd like the chapter to earn that continuity rather than merely gesture at it.

Could you help me work out, in real philosophical detail, how Leibniz would actually respond if walked through Searle's argument and its "Systems Reply" (the rejoinder that it's the room as a whole — man, rulebook, symbols together — that understands Chinese, even if the man inside doesn't)? I'm especially interested in any tension between Leibniz's own commitments: his distinction between mere perception and reflective apperception, his doctrine that genuine unity — and, on his view, the capacity for perception — belongs only to simple substances and never to aggregates like a room-plus-rulebook-plus-man, his pre-established harmony as an alternative to causal interaction between symbol-manipulation and meaning, and his long dispute with the Cartesians over whether animals have souls at all. Does his own mill argument, followed through consistently, let him agree with Searle's verdict on the room while rejecting Searle's reasons for it — or does it commit him to a conclusion that would make him just as uneasy about brains? I'd rather have that reasoned through carefully than a chapter where he simply marvels at computers.
