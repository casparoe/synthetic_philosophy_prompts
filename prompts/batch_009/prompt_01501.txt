A colleague sent me Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "Torture vs. Dust Specks" post a few weeks ago, and it's been rattling around in my head ever since, usually at inconvenient moments like standup. The setup, for anyone who hasn't run into it: would you rather one person be tortured horribly for fifty years, or an incomprehensibly large number of people — 3^^^3, in Knuth's up-arrow notation, a number that would overflow any data type I've ever worked with and that you couldn't write out digit by digit using every atom in the observable universe — each get a single dust speck in their eye and nothing more? Yudkowsky bites the bullet for torture on "shut up and multiply" grounds: if disutility aggregates additively across people, the sum of 3^^^3 trivial harms has to exceed fifty years of agony eventually, and refusing to accept that conclusion once you've done the math is, on his view, just scope insensitivity rather than principle. I did some digging afterward and learned this isn't only an internet-forum thought experiment — Alastair Norcross made structurally the same move a decade earlier, in a 1997 Philosophy & Public Affairs paper called "Comparing Harms: Headaches and Human Lives," arguing that enough headaches could in principle outweigh a death, against what's sometimes called the "lives over headaches" intuition. What I haven't been able to settle for myself is whether the natural resistance to this conclusion — that some harms are simply too different in kind to sit on one scale — can be made precise rather than just asserted. If a non-arbitrary cutoff exists somewhere between "dust speck" and "fifty years of torture" where aggregation stops working, a sorites-style chain of intermediate cases (a headache, a migraine, a broken bone, a week of severe pain...) seems to make that cutoff impossible to locate without it looking arbitrary; but if no such cutoff exists, the resistance seems to collapse all the way into accepting torture.

Could you write me a dialogue that actually grapples with this instead of just re-staging the original dilemma? I want two characters, both fully fluent in the literature and neither one a pushover: TALLY, who defends the aggregative verdict along Yudkowsky/Norcross lines, and THRESHOLD, who thinks aggregation can be blocked in a principled way somewhere along the continuum. TALLY's job is to press THRESHOLD precisely on the sorites problem above — demanding either a specific, defensible location for the discontinuity or an honest account of why demanding one is itself the wrong move. THRESHOLD should have a real, technically serious answer available rather than a bare appeal to intuition — for instance, something in the spirit of Scanlon's contractualist idea that justifiability runs through separate individual complaints rather than a summed total, so that no number of headache-sufferers can outweigh the one person in agony, or Larry Temkin's willingness to simply give up transitivity of "all things considered better than" for spectrum cases like this one — or a different strong line of your own choosing if you think it serves THRESHOLD better. I'd like each side to genuinely engage with the other's strongest objection rather than talk past each other, and if you conclude the disagreement is a genuine stalemate given the current state of the art, let the dialogue end without a manufactured resolution.
