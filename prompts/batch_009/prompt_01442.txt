A friend and I spent an evening arguing about Kant on animals and ended up more confused than when we started. We'd both been repeating the familiar line from the Lectures on Ethics — animals lack self-consciousness and exist merely as a means to the end of man — until we went back and reread what Kant actually published on the subject, in the Doctrine of Virtue (Metaphysics of Morals, §17), which reaches the same conclusion by a rather different route and left us less sure we understood the argument.

As Kant lays it out there (I'm working from Mary Gregor's translation): cruel or violent treatment of an animal wrongs no one but the agent himself, because it dulls his fellow feeling for the creature's suffering and so weakens "a natural predisposition that is very serviceable to morality in one's relations with other people." He permits quick, painless killing and ordinary labor, condemns painful experiments undertaken from mere curiosity, and treats even gratitude toward a long-serving old horse or dog as, strictly, a duty owed to oneself rather than to the animal. (A few lines earlier he runs an almost identical argument for why we should love natural beauty for its own sake.)

Here's where the argument split us. Kant is elsewhere hostile to grounding obligation in contingent facts about human sentiment — that's the whole point of his case against Hutcheson-style moral-sense theories. But this particular argument seems to rest entirely on an empirical, causal claim about what cruelty does to our sympathies. My friend says that makes the "indirect duty" a sham: in a world where cruelty to animals happened to sharpen rather than dull our sympathy for other people, Kant would have to bless it, maybe even require it, which shows reason was never really doing the work. I tried arguing that the empirical claim only identifies which acts frustrate an independently rational duty of self-cultivation, so the contingency matters less than it looks.

Who's got the better reading? Is there anything in the text or in Kant's wider ethics that tells us whether the wrongness here is really grounded in reason, or smuggled in through empirical psychology?
