Odd rabbit hole I fell into this week while researching a piece on the deep prehistory of stuff like MMT and chartalism: turns out Bishop Berkeley -- yes, the "to be is to be perceived" guy, the one who denied matter exists -- also wrote this sprawling pamphlet on economics called The Querist back in the 1730s. Its a strange read, just hundreds of numbered rhetorical questions about the Irish economy, and a good chunk of them are about money specifically. He keeps hammering on the idea that gold and silver aren't wealth in themselves, that the "true idea of money" is really just a ticket or counter for recording and transferring power and credit, and that you could in principle swap the metal out for paper, or even just entries in a bank's ledger, without losing anything essential, so long as the social conventions around it stay intact.

Here's what's been bugging me, and what a philosopher friend and I couldn't fully untangle over drinks: doesn't this sit awkwardly next to the earlier, much more famous Berkeley? The same guy spends the whole Introduction to the Principles of Human Knowledge arguing that the mind literally cannot form abstract general ideas -- no idea of "triangle in general" that's neither equilateral nor scalene, no idea of motion apart from some particular fast or slow motion, no color that's neither red nor blue nor any determinate shade. But calling a coin or a banknote a "ticket" for some general quantity of purchasing power sure sounds like it needs exactly that forbidden move: an idea of "value" or "power" held in common by a side of beef, a bolt of linen, and a day's labor, without being identical to any of them. My friend thought Berkeley's fallback theory of general terms -- where one particular idea gets used as a stand-in for a whole class of resembling particulars -- might rescue him here, but neither of us was sure it actually does the job once you look closely at what treating money as a ticket requires.

Could you write me an essay working through this? I want an actual verdict, not a survey: does Berkeley's monetary theory in The Querist amount to a consistent extension of his attack on abstraction (maybe because a coin or a numeral can do the same job he thinks a general word or a drawn triangle does, without any abstract idea behind it), or does it quietly smuggle in the very sort of abstraction he spent his career insisting was impossible? Weigh the strongest version of each reading before you commit, but please actually commit.
