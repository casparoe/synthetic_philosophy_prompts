I'm taking an introductory logic and philosophy elective at my school, and last week we spent a class period on conditional ("if... then") statements. My teacher walked us through the material conditional and its truth table, and then mentioned, almost as an aside, that conditionals about hypothetical situations — what she called "counterfactuals," things like "if X had happened, Y would have happened" — need an entirely different kind of semantics involving "possible worlds," which we didn't have time to get into properly. That offhand comment has been bothering me ever since, because when I tried to work out for myself why the distinction should really matter, I ended up convincing myself that it probably doesn't, at least not in any deep sense.

Here's the example I came up with to test my own thinking. Suppose someone in my class posted an anonymous, insulting comment about a teacher on our school's online forum, and because only two students happened to be logged into the site at that exact moment, it's known for certain that the comment came from either Alex or Priya, though nobody can tell which one. It seems obviously true to say, "If Alex didn't post it, Priya did" — that's just elementary logic, since we're given that it was one of the two. Now compare the counterfactual version of the very same situation: "If Alex hadn't posted it, Priya would have." As far as I can tell, these two sentences describe exactly the same underlying facts, and the only real difference between them is that one uses ordinary past tense ("didn't"/"did") while the other uses the hypothetical "had"/"would have" construction we use for counterfactuals. A friend on my debate team told me counterfactuals just "feel" different, but that isn't a real explanation. So my tentative conclusion is that all this talk of "possible worlds" for counterfactuals is really just a more technical-sounding way of describing an ordinary shift in verb mood, and that in a perfectly precise language, both sentences should end up true together or false together, since they're built out of exactly the same information.

Is there a flaw in this reasoning? I'd like to understand whether indicative and counterfactual conditionals are really doing two fundamentally different logical jobs, or whether my instinct that this is "just grammar" is basically right and philosophers are overcomplicating a fairly simple distinction.
