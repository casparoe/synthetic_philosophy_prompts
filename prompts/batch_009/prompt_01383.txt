I've been reading through some ancient material on logical fatalism, and I keep coming back to how much philosophical weight has been hung on one throwaway example of Aristotle's. In De Interpretatione 9, he worries that if the sentence "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true today (or its negation already is), then the battle's happening or not happening seems to have been settled all along, so that nothing done in the meantime could make any difference. What strikes me is that later thinkers made the puzzle worse rather than better: the ancient logician Diodorus Cronus distilled it into his so-called Master Argument, built from three claims about necessity and possibility that he showed cannot all be true together, and the Stoics had to fend off a related worry known as the "lazy argument" — if your recovery from an illness is already fated, why bother calling a doctor at all? Chrysippus's answer was that the action itself is "co-fated" with the outcome, woven into it rather than powerless before it. Transplant that same worry onto a crew facing a fight whose outcome is supposedly already settled, and you get roughly the situation I'd like a short story to inhabit.

Write a short story that makes this dilemma feel lived-in rather than merely explained. Set it among people facing a sea battle that has not yet happened — a classical fleet on the eve of an engagement is the obvious choice, but you're free to relocate the premise to any period, culture, or even a non-human crew, so long as the underlying puzzle stays recognizable. At least one character should draw something like the lazy argument's conclusion and act on it — refusing to prepare, giving an order no one expects, standing down entirely — and at least one other should answer with something that does real philosophical work rather than mere insistence: perhaps someone who denies that the necessity of "there will be a battle, or there won't" transfers to either half of that claim on its own, or someone who denies that either half is yet true or false at all, or someone who tries a version of Chrysippus's move and argues that preparing is bound up with what happens rather than idle before it. Let this exchange carry real consequences for the plot — a decision, a betrayal, a failure of nerve, or a sudden act of courage — rather than letting it sit to one side as a debate. You don't need to adjudicate who is right; the story just needs to earn its stakes honestly. Something in the range of 1,000 to 1,800 words would suit it well.
