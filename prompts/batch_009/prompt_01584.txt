I have no formal training in philosophy — I mostly just chase down ideas that catch my interest and try to reason them through on my own — but I recently spent a few evenings reading about medieval Islamic philosophy and became rather fixated on Avicenna's "flying man" argument (sometimes called the "floating man"). As I understand the setup, we are asked to imagine a person created instantaneously, fully grown, suspended in empty air or a void so that he feels no resistance against his skin, blindfolded, hearing nothing, and with his limbs held apart so that no part of him touches any other part, or anything else at all — in short, a person with zero sensory contact with either his own body or the external world. It's a strange image, and it reminded me a little of what people say floating in a sensory-deprivation tank feels like, which is probably part of why it has stuck with me. Avicenna's claim is that such a person, despite affirming nothing about any single limb or organ, would still be certain of his own existence. From this he concludes that the soul must be something distinct from the body.

What I cannot settle for myself is how much that conclusion is actually owed to the thought experiment itself. It seems plausible enough that the flying man would retain some bare awareness of himself even while sensing nothing of his body — fine. But the argument is usually presented as establishing something considerably stronger than that: not just that self-awareness does not require body-awareness, but that the self or soul is a genuinely separate substance from the body, i.e., real mind-body dualism. Those look like two different claims to me, and I do not see why establishing the first hands you the second for free — being able to notice yourself without noticing your body seems like a fact about the structure of awareness, not a fact about what you are ultimately made of. I mentioned this to a friend who took a couple of philosophy courses years ago, and she said the argument gets compared constantly to Descartes' cogito, but that she had always found the comparison a little too quick without being able to say exactly why. That comment has stuck with me and made me want to work through the question properly rather than just repeat the comparison secondhand.

Could you write me a proper essay on this? I would like you to work out, and defend, what you think Avicenna's argument is best interpreted as actually entitled to conclude — the modest self-awareness claim, the full dualist claim, or something in between — weighing the strongest considerations on each side, and addressing along the way whether the Descartes comparison holds up or obscures more than it reveals. Please do not leave the question hanging at the end: commit to a specific verdict about whether, and in what sense, the flying man argument succeeds, and justify why that verdict is the most defensible one.
