I am several drafts into a novel whose central conceit is a deity, referred to only as the Widow, who is both genuinely dead and genuinely alive. This isn't meant as "dead to some, alive to others," and she isn't a ghost or a metaphor: both predicates are meant to be true of her at once, as a fixed feature of how the book's world works rather than a mystery the final chapter quietly resolves. A friend who studied philosophy tells me that what I'm after is close to what logicians call dialetheism, the thesis that some contradictions are actually true, and that dialetheists typically pair the view with a paraconsistent logic, since in ordinary logic a single true contradiction lets you validly prove absolutely anything, which would be fatal to a novel that still wants its other stakes and causal rules to mean something.

That's the part I can't work out on my own, and the part I most need from you: what are the most important implications of dialetheism and paraconsistent logic for a project like mine, where I want exactly one load-bearing contradiction and nothing else to go haywire because of it? I'd like you to explain, in terms a non-logician can actually use, how someone working in this tradition (Graham Priest's name came up) would try to keep a contradiction contained rather than explosive; what distinguishes that containment from simple vagueness or equivocation about the Widow's nature; and what would distinguish genuine dialetheism from the far stranger, and presumably unwanted, position that everything whatsoever is true. I'd also welcome your honest assessment of whether a genuinely paraconsistent fictional world like this is achievable at all, or whether any workable version of it inevitably smuggles in an ordinary, non-contradictory distinction, between contexts, times, or senses of "alive," that quietly does all the real work while the official contradiction stays purely decorative.
