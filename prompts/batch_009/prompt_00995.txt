I'm a biostatistician working on oncology trials, and a recent industry development has been occupying more of my attention than it probably should. This past summer, Kalshi and Polymarket both opened real-money prediction markets where anyone can bet on clinical-trial outcomes and FDA approval decisions, and several colleagues of mine have started treating this as a natural extension of our day jobs: our familiarity with trial design, comparator drugs' track records, and publicly posted interim data, they reason, should give us a genuine edge over the casual traders on these platforms.

Curious, I tried it myself a few weeks ago, on a trial run by a company I have no connection to, based entirely on public filings. I placed a fairly large bet on an outcome I felt genuinely confident about, at odds that struck me as unusually generous, and the position was filled in full almost instantly. Pleased with myself, I mentioned this to a friend from graduate school who now trades derivatives for a living. He told me that the ease and speed with which I'd found a counterparty should make me trust my judgment less, not more, and then declined to explain why. The remark has stayed with me, not least because one of the news write-ups on these new markets quoted the platforms' own rationale for existing: that they pull together everyone's scattered private information into a single, continuously updated, accurate probability.

If that rationale is correct, and someone was nonetheless eager to bet against me, I don't see how my supposed edge survives contact with the market at all; but if my friend's principle held in general, I don't understand why any trade should ever happen on these platforms, when clearly a great many do, constantly, between people who each think they know better than the other side. I'd like help working out what's actually going on here: is there a legitimate idea behind my friend's warning, and if so, does it mean that specialized knowledge like mine becomes worthless the moment a liquid enough market exists to bet in — or is there some way of reconciling his warning with the fact that trading obviously keeps happening anyway?
