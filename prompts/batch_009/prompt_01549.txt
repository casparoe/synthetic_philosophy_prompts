I recently came across an episode from Thomas Hobbes's life that has stayed with me. By one well-known account, he discovered geometry by accident in his forties, was captivated by the certainty of Euclid's proof of the Pythagorean theorem, and later invoked that same idea, that we can have full certainty about anything we construct ourselves, to argue that his political philosophy could be made just as rigorous as geometry. Yet for roughly the last twenty-five years of his life, this same man insisted, against repeated and detailed refutations from the mathematician John Wallis, that he had squared the circle, and he never conceded a single point, still claiming victory in the autobiography he wrote shortly before he died. As I understand it, his errors were bound up with his deeper materialist views on the nature of magnitude, so that giving up the proof may have felt like giving up on far more than one result. Please describe the most important implications of this episode for how much weight I should give my own confidence in a belief once specialists keep finding concrete errors in it, especially when I suspect I am resisting partly because the belief is load-bearing for a larger picture I do not want to dismantle.
