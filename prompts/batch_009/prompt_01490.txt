For a seminar on Victorian science and religion, I've been reading Sidgwick's presidential addresses to the Society for Psychical Research alongside the concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics, and his psychical research doesn't look like a hobby separate from his ethics: absent some cosmic guarantee that virtue and self-interest coincide, the "dualism of practical reason" he identifies there threatens to leave morality without a fully rational foundation, which seems to be part of why he spent decades chasing evidence for survival after death. At the same time, he held himself and his colleagues to an unusually strict evidentiary standard — in his fifth presidential address he explicitly grants that a continuing dearth of high-quality evidence would itself count as a growing argument against the Society's conclusions — and he died still disappointed at how little had actually been established. Given that combination of existential stakes and methodological severity, how do you think Sidgwick would have reacted to Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, published in a mainstream psychology journal, and to the replication crisis and preregistration reforms they helped provoke in experimental psychology? Would he have welcomed preregistration as finally supplying the rigor he had demanded, or would the whole episode have struck him as confirming his late-life pessimism about ever settling such questions?
