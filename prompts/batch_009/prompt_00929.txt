I've been working through G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903) for a reading group on early twentieth-century value theory, and I've hit a passage in Chapter VI, "The Ideal," that I can't quite square with the methodology Moore sets out for himself just one section earlier. Here is the relevant stretch, from §§112–114 (lightly trimmed, with omissions marked by ellipses):

"§112. The method which must be employed in order to decide the question 'What things have intrinsic value, and in what degrees?' has already been explained in Chap. III. (§§ 55, 57). In order to arrive at a correct decision on the first part of this question, it is necessary to consider what things are such that, if they existed by themselves, in absolute isolation, we should yet judge their existence to be good; and, in order to decide upon the relative degrees of value of different things, we must similarly consider what comparative value seems to attach to the isolated existence of each. By employing this method, we shall guard against two errors, which seem to have been the chief causes which have vitiated previous conclusions on the subject... And, secondly, there is the more subtle error which consists in neglecting the principle of organic unities. This error is committed, when it is supposed, that, if one part of a whole has no intrinsic value, the value of the whole must reside entirely in the other parts.

§113. If, now, we use this method of absolute isolation, and guard against these errors, it appears that the question we have to answer is far less difficult than the controversies of Ethics might have led us to expect... By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. No one, probably, who has asked himself the question, has ever doubted that personal affection and the appreciation of what is beautiful in Art or Nature, are good in themselves... All the things, which I have meant to include under the above descriptions, are highly complex organic unities.

§114. I propose to begin by examining what I have called aesthetic enjoyments, since the case of personal affections presents some additional complications... It is plain that in those instances of aesthetic appreciation, which we think most valuable, there is included, not merely a bare cognition of what is beautiful in the object, but also some kind of feeling or emotion... [I]f we consider what value this emotional element would have, existing by itself, we can hardly think that it has any great value, even if it has any at all. Whereas, if the same emotion be directed to a different object... the whole state of consciousness is certainly often positively bad in a high degree."

What puzzles me is this. In §112, Moore says his "method of absolute isolation" is meant to guard against two errors, the second of which is neglecting "the principle of organic unities" — that is, wrongly inferring that if one part of a valuable whole seems worthless on its own, the whole's value must be located entirely in whatever other part remains. Yet in §114 he immediately proceeds to isolate the emotional component of aesthetic appreciation from its cognitive component, observes that the emotion "existing by itself" has little or no value, and treats this as evidence for the organic character of the appreciation as a whole — which looks, on the face of it, like exactly the inferential move he has just warned against. He does something structurally similar a few sections later, in §122, when he isolates admiration of a person's mental qualities from appreciation of their bodily "expression of character" to argue that neither carries much weight on its own compared to the combination — a case that bears directly on friendship and personal affection, not just on beauty.

So: is there a genuine inconsistency in Moore's procedure, or is he really operating with two distinct notions of "isolation" — one applied to entire states of affairs (as in his comparison, back in Chapter III, of an uninhabited beautiful world with an uninhabited ugly one), and another applied to the constituents internal to a single state of consciousness — such that the organic-unities warning was never meant to rule out the second use in the first place? I'd like a careful reconstruction of what Moore is actually doing methodologically across these sections, and your own verdict on whether his practice survives scrutiny on its own terms.
