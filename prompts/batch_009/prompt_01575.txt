Longtime reader of the decision-theory corner of the rationalist blogosphere here. I've internalized the standard picture: an agent with cyclic preferences can be walked around the cycle and drained of money, violating what's sometimes called the diachronic dominance principle. The standard rebuttal is that a sufficiently sophisticated agent avoids the monetary loss by reasoning backward and refusing to complete the loop -- though, tellingly, it still ends up violating that same dominance principle, just without paying for the privilege. But I recently ran into a subtler point, which I believe traces to Johan Gustafsson's work on money pumps, that I can't fully reconstruct myself.

Take the usual cycle: the agent prefers z to y, y to x, and x to z. Now weaken that last link into a mere preferential gap -- neither x nor z ranked above the other. Having traded up from x through y to z, the agent can simply decline the final trade back to x, and this requires no foresight or precommitment at all, since nothing in its preferences favors that last trade over sitting still. Genuine incompleteness, on this argument, defangs the pump outright -- avoiding the dominance violation entirely, which mere sophistication never managed.

What I can't work out is why the same move doesn't rescue an agent whose ranking of x and z is merely vague or indeterminate rather than determinately absent: introspectively, "no comparison here" and "a comparison here I can't discern" seem to leave the agent in the same bind when the trade is offered. Could you lay out the structural reason the pump keeps its grip on indeterminacy but loses it on incompleteness, and say whether that distinction is doing real philosophical work or just papering over identical choice behavior? I ask partly because this same move -- incompleteness as a shield against coherence arguments -- has resurfaced in recent AI-safety debates about whether advanced agents must converge on complete preferences.
