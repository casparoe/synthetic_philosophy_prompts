I'm putting together a handout for my "Philosophy of AI" seminar and could use a sanity check before I use it with students. Last week we went through the usual suspects in the "do LLMs actually understand anything" fight -- Searle's Chinese room, Bender & Koller's octopus paper, a bit of the stochastic parrots stuff, some symbol-grounding-problem background. Afterwards one of my students, who is a bit of a Leibniz nerd, emailed me to say that basically all of this is just a modern remix of the "mill" argument from the Monadology (1714), which kind of blew my mind a little since I hadn't opened that text since grad school. I dug up my old copy -- this looks like it's the Duncan translation from some 1890s collection, though don't quote me on that -- and here's the bit she meant, section 17:

"We must confess, moreover, that perception and that which depends on it are inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is, by figures and motions. And, supposing that there were a machine so constructed as to cause thought, feeling and perception, we could conceive of it as enlarged and yet preserving the same proportions, so that we might enter it like a mill. And this granted, we should only find on visiting it, pieces which push one against another, but never any thing by which to explain a perception. It must be sought for, therefore, in the simple substance and not in the compound or machine."

Could you help me think through this for the handout? Two things I'm stuck on. First, in plain english: what's the actual argument here, and what work is the "walking around inside a giant mill" image doing that a bare statement of the conclusion wouldn't already do on its own? Second, my students claim is basically "this is the Chinese room, but with gears instead of a guy with a rulebook" -- I'm not sure thats quite right, since Leibniz's target seems to be mechanical explanations of perception in general, which would presumably include brains and not just symbol-shufflers like a transformer. So does the mill argument, if it works at all, end up proving way more than either Leibniz or a modern LLM-skeptic actually wants, or is there a real disanalogy in there that saves the comparison? I keep going back and forth on it.
