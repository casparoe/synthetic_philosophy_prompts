I'm several drafts into a novel about the Scottish Enlightenment, set in Edinburgh in the early 1770s, in which David Hume appears as a recurring, lightly fictionalized character who enjoys cornering his younger dinner guests with puzzles about the mind. There's a scene I keep rewriting because I can't get the philosophy right: I want Hume, reasoning only with concepts and arguments available to him in his own lifetime, to work through a problem that in actual intellectual history wasn't posed until roughly two centuries after his death.

Here is the scenario as I've sketched it. At a dinner in his New Town townhouse, a steward has secretly rigged the claret decanter so that if a young advocate at the table reaches instead for the water jug, a hidden mechanism will tip the decanter and pour claret into his glass regardless. As it happens, the advocate reaches for the claret entirely on his own, never once inclining toward the water, so the mechanism sits idle all evening and never has to intervene. The advocate has an important appearance in court the next morning and drinks just enough to blunt his performance. For the sake of getting Hume's dialogue right, I need to know whether he would hold the advocate responsible for that lapse, given that the advocate could not, in fact, have done otherwise once the steward set his trap.

I know enough of Hume's actual position to be dangerous rather than confident: in the Treatise, and again in the chapter on liberty and necessity in the first Enquiry, he defines liberty simply as the power to act according to the determinations of one's own will, ties moral evaluation to a "constant union" between settled character and action, and insists that pure indifference or chance, not necessity, is what would really destroy morality. What I can't work out on my own is whether that framework already has an answer to my decanter case built into it, so that Hume could wave the whole thing away as no threat at all, or whether conceding that the advocate "could not have done otherwise" would corner him into a difficulty his own arguments don't anticipate. What I'm really describing, of course, is a version of what philosophers now call a Frankfurt-style case against the claim that moral responsibility requires the ability to have done otherwise, and since Hume died before anyone framed the puzzle this way, there is no passage of his I can simply consult for the answer. Based on what he actually argued, how do you think he would have responded, and would his compatibilism emerge from the exercise intact, revised, or genuinely undermined?
