I've been turning a problem over in my mind for a while now and would like to see it worked through properly. Imagine someone reasons through a difficult question as carefully as they know how and ends up fully convinced of the answer. Now imagine they receive trustworthy information that people reasoning under their exact circumstances — exhausted, say, or emotionally invested, or simply prone to some well-documented bias — get conclusions of this kind wrong far more often than they would expect, and that there is no way for this person to independently check, on this particular occasion, whether they belong to the reliable group or the unreliable one. Crucially, the information points to no specific flaw in the reasoning itself: the argument still strikes the person who worked through it as entirely sound.

Here is the question that interests me. Can this person rationally go on believing the conclusion with full confidence while also believing that this very belief was probably produced by an unreliable process? Or does rationality forbid holding both attitudes together, so that something must eventually give — either the confidence in the conclusion, or the weight placed on the warning?

Write an essay that takes a definite stand on this question. Set out the strongest case for thinking such a combination of attitudes can be fully rational, then the strongest case for thinking it never can be, and then defend your own verdict. A qualified answer is fine — for instance, concluding that it is rational only when the warning is purely statistical and offers no way of examining this particular case further — but simply concluding that both sides have a point is not.
