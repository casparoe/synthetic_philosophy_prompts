I'm taking a course on early modern philosophy this semester, and we just spent two sessions on Hobbes's philosophy of geometry, which turns out to be a much stranger and more interesting position than "he thought he could square the circle." Hobbes holds that geometry is the one branch of knowledge we can grasp with full demonstrative certainty, and the reason is his so-called "maker's knowledge" doctrine: since it is we ourselves who draw the lines and generate the figures geometry studies (a circle, on his definition, is simply whatever figure results from circumducting a straight line about a fixed endpoint), we can know their causes in a way we never could for natural bodies we merely observe rather than construct. He says much the same, around De Homine 10.5, to explain why geometry yields real knowledge while natural philosophy at best yields conjecture, and he even extends the argument to civil philosophy, on the grounds that the commonwealth, too, is something we made. This is also, of course, the Hobbes who spent roughly the last quarter-century of his life insisting, against John Wallis and the rest of the Royal Society, that he really had squared the circle, and who never once backed down.

What I'd like is your best reconstruction of how Hobbes might have reacted, taken one at a time, to three things that happened to his favorite science well after his death: first, the discovery by Bolyai, Lobachevsky, and later Riemann of internally consistent non-Euclidean geometries, which raised the live possibility that physical space itself might not be Euclidean, in tension with what his physics quietly assumes; second, Lindemann's 1882 proof that pi is transcendental, which finally showed with full rigor that no straightedge-and-compass quadrature of the circle can exist, thereby settling the Wallis dispute for good; and third, Hilbert's later axiomatic treatment of geometry, on which "point," "line," and "plane" are simply whatever satisfy the relevant axioms, so that, as Hilbert reportedly put it, one should be able to say "tables, chairs, and beer mugs" instead without any loss of rigor.

My real question concerns the first and third items: are they good news or bad news for Hobbes? Do they vindicate the conventionalist, we-make-it-so-we-can-know-it strand of his epistemology, or do they instead pull apart two things his system needed to keep together — the maker's-knowledge argument for certainty, and his insistence that geometry is really about actual bodies in actual motion, not about whatever happens to satisfy some set of axioms? And on the second item: once pi's transcendence is on the table, is there any dignified way for Hobbes to retreat, or does Lindemann leave him nowhere left to hide? I'd rather you work through his actual arguments in De Corpore than just default to his reputation as a stubborn crank about mathematics.
