So I'm taking this early modern philosophy seminar and we spent a week on Hobbes's weirdly intense feud with the mathematician John Wallis, which turned into a whole argument with my friend (who's doing a math PhD, so she came into this with Opinions). Background in case it matters: starting in the 1650s Hobbes kept insisting he'd squared the circle and solved a handful of other classical construction problems, and Wallis spent something like 25 years wrecking him in pamphlet after pamphlet -- there's an entire book about it (Jesseph's "Squaring the Circle"), and at one point Hobbes fired back with a pamphlet literally titled "Marks of the Absurd Geometry, Rural Language, Scottish Church Politics and Barbarisms of John Wallis." Apparently part of Wallis's strategy was going after the geometry on purpose, on the theory that if he could show it was garbage, Hobbes's whole political philosophy (which leans on geometry as its model for what certainty even looks like) would go down with it.

My friend thinks Wallis was onto something. Hobbes's big epistemological claim is that geometry -- and, weirdly, civil philosophy/politics too -- is basically the only place we can get totally certain knowledge, precisely because we construct the objects ourselves out of our own definitions, instead of just guessing at hidden causes the way we have to in physics (I think this is literally called "maker's knowledge" in the secondary lit, though don't quote me on the term). Her point is that if Hobbes was this reliably, humiliatingly wrong about actual constructions for 25 straight years and basically never accepted a refutation, that has to count as real evidence against his theory of why geometry is supposed to be uniquely certain, not just embarassing trivia we get to set aside. I said that's basically an ad hominem -- whether Hobbes personally was any good at doing geometry seems like a totally seperate question from whether his argument about WHY geometrical knowledge is certain actually holds up, and plenty of perfectly good philosophers of math have been mediocre mathematicians. We went back and forth on this for like an hour and never landed anywhere. Was either of us actually right, or is there a better way of thinking about whether (and how) his mathematical failures should count against his epistemology?
