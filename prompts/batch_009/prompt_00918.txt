Since retiring after a career in experimental physics, I have been working through the philosophy of religion literature, and one particular move in the cosmological argument from contingency has left me stuck. Proponents invoke the Principle of Sufficient Reason to demand an explanation for the conjunction of all contingent truths, sometimes called the "Big Conjunctive Contingent Fact," and conclude that only a necessary being can supply it. Peter van Inwagen, however, offers a dilemma meant to show that no proposition could actually fill that explanatory role. The explainer cannot itself be a contingent truth, since it would then be one of the very truths making up the fact it is meant to explain; yet it cannot be a necessary truth either, because a necessary proposition can entail only further necessary propositions and so cannot entail a contingent one. I can follow each horn of the argument formally, but I would like a more intuitive sense of why an explanation is assumed to entail what it explains in the first place, since that assumption seems to be doing all the work, and it sits oddly with how causal explanation actually functions in the sciences. Could you unpack the intuition behind van Inwagen's dilemma in fairly plain terms, and then lay out the most credible strategy that defenders of the contingency argument use to resist it?
