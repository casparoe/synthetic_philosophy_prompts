I've been thinking about the 'surprise exam' paradox, and it strikes me as a case where a straightforward induction argument — the same kind of reasoning I'd use to verify a loop invariant — leads to a conclusion that real-world experience clearly falsifies. The setup is as follows: a teacher announces on Friday that there will be exactly one surprise exam next week (Monday through Friday), and that students won't be able to deduce the day in advance. Backward induction seems to rule out every candidate. It can't be Friday, since if the exam hasn't occurred by Thursday afternoon, students could deduce by elimination that it must be Friday, defeating the surprise; but once Friday is eliminated on those grounds, the identical argument eliminates Thursday, then Wednesday, and so on back to Monday, leaving no day that can satisfy the condition. Yet teachers spring unannounced quizzes on students all the time, and the students really are caught off guard. Given that we apparently have a valid inductive proof whose conclusion is directly contradicted by ordinary experience, doesn't this show that deductive logic simply breaks down once self-reference and knowledge claims are involved — meaning there's no real mistake to find in the argument, just an inherent limit on what formal reasoning can tell us about situations like this?
