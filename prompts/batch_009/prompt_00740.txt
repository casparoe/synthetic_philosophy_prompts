I have no academic background in philosophy or decision theory, but I have spent a good deal of spare time on the 'coherence arguments' found in AI-safety writing: the claim that an agent with incomplete preferences (not just intransitive ones) can be money-pumped, and that this is why sufficiently capable agents should end up maximizing expected utility.

What troubles me is the completeness step specifically. Every money-pump against incomplete preferences I have been able to reconstruct needs the agent to accept a trade whenever the new option is merely incomparable to the old one — what I've seen called a 'preferential gap' — rather than something strictly worse. It seems an agent could block this with a simple rule: give up your current option only for something you strictly prefer to it, and otherwise keep what you have. That rule never forces the incomparable options to be ranked, so the pump seems to fail without the agent's preferences ever becoming complete.

There is, however, a complication I have not been able to get around. Suppose the agent is first offered a trade of its current option for something merely incomparable to it, and only afterward, conditional on taking that trade, offered a further trade for something genuinely better than where it started. The rule makes the agent refuse the first trade, so it never reaches the second, and it misses a guaranteed improvement that a less cautious agent would have secured. No single choice is a strict loss, yet the overall policy still looks irrational.

Please argue for the position that some version of this 'strict-improvements-only' rule nonetheless defeats the money-pump case for completeness, either by showing that forgoing the improvement is not really a mark of irrationality, or by repairing the rule so that it captures such improvements without quietly reintroducing full completeness.
