I'm taking a seminar this semester on nineteenth-century aesthetics, and for the past couple of weeks we've been working through Schopenhauer using the Cambridge edition of The World as Will and Representation. Book Three gave me a reasonably firm grip on his general theory of aesthetic experience, but his separate treatment of music — first in section 52 of Book Three, then expanded in the supplementary chapter on the metaphysics of music in the second volume — has left me stuck on a tension that a few of us argued about after class without really resolving. I would appreciate your help thinking it through more carefully.

Schopenhauer's general story is that ordinary cognition is enslaved to the will: we perceive things only in terms of how they serve or threaten our own striving, and this is inherently a source of suffering, since willing is essentially a matter of lack and craving. Aesthetic contemplation is supposed to interrupt this arrangement: the subject becomes what he calls a will-less subject of knowing, who apprehends the Platonic Idea embodied in an object rather than the object's relation to her own wants, and it is precisely this that makes aesthetic experience genuinely painless and tranquil. His account of music, however, breaks this pattern in a striking way: music, he argues, is the one art that bypasses the Ideas entirely, since rather than representing some grade of the will's objectification the way a landscape or a sculpture does, it is a direct copy of the will itself — which is how he is able to trace such a precise correspondence between, say, the bass voice and inert, inorganic nature, or the melody and the conscious strivings of a human being.

What I cannot work out is how these two commitments are meant to fit together. If music gives us the will's own restless movement from craving to momentary satisfaction and back again in an unusually direct way, rather than a representation of it once removed, shouldn't listening to music amount to a more intense encounter with the very thing that constitutes suffering in his system, and so be harder to experience will-lessly, not easier? Yet Schopenhauer treats music as fully compatible with painless aesthetic tranquility, and indeed as the most profound and moving of the arts precisely because of that directness. Does his system actually contain the resources to explain why unmediated access to the will is compatible with, or perhaps even especially conducive to, will-less contemplation in this one case — or is this simply a point at which his aesthetics fails to hang together?
