Been meaning to ask this somewhere for a while. So a few of us from an old SSC-meetup group that turned into a Discord have been going back and forth for like two weeks on whether it even makes sense, epistemically, to slap a credence on literally everything -- which is sort of the working assumption behind a lot of the Sequences-style "you should have a calibrated probability for every proposition, including your priors, and update via Bayes" stuff, plus the whole point of the "0 and 1 are not probabilities" post. Someone dropped Wittgenstein's On Certainty into the chat as a counter and now im kind of stuck on it. The part that got me is around OC 341-343, where he says our doubts only make sense against a background of stuff that's exempt from doubt in the first place -- not because it's been extra-well verified, but because it functions more like the hinges a door swings on than like an ordinary empirical claim. There's also the river-bed bit around OC 96-99, where the line between "hinge" and "ordinary belief" can itself shift over time, even though at any given moment you need something to hold still for inquiry to happen at all. I know there's a whole academic cottage industry (Pritchard, Wright, Coliva, etc) arguing about whether these hinge propositions are totally "groundless" or just non-evidentially "entitled," and im sure im mangling the finer points, but the basic picture feels like a real problem for the whole rationalist "everything gets a number" project, not just a semantic quibble.

So: can you lay out what you think are the most important implications of hinge epistemology (in the Wittgenstein/On Certainty sense) for someone who's actually trying to practice rationalist-style epistemics day to day? Specifically I care about (1) what it does to the regress problem of justifying your priors in the first place, (2) whether techniques like double crux still make sense when a persistent disagreement turns out to be over something hinge-like rather than a normal shared-background empirical claim, and (3) whether there's a principled difference between "I assign this a credence of 0.9999" and "this just isn't the kind of thing that gets a credence at all." Dont need a full lit review, just whatever you think actually matters if I want to keep doing Bayesian-flavored reasoning without kidding myself about what it's resting on.
