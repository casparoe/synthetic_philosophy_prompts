So I'm prepping the decision theory unit of my grad seminar this week and I keep tripping over the same spot every time I teach Death in Damascus. Everyone's fine granting that the guy fleeing Death from Damascus to Aleppo is a case where causal decision theory has no self-ratifying pure option -- ok, fine, so the standard move (Harper's ratifiability stuff from the 80s) is that the actually rational thing is a mixed strategy, flip a coin, since that option is self-ratifying even though neither "stay" nor "flee" on its own is. I can get through that part of the lecture just fine.

Where I always start mumbling is when some sharp student (there's always one, this year its a first-year named Priya) points out that Egan's Psychopath Button case has basically the identical "no pure option is ratifiable" structure -- and yet nobody thinks "so the rational thing is to flip a coin" is remotely the right answer there. Everyone has a stable, confident intuition that Paul should just refrain, full stop, even though refraining isn't ratifiable either. So now I'm second-guessing whether ratifiability was ever doing real explanatory work in Death in Damascus, or whether it just happened to spit out a palatable answer there because the case is so symmetric, and Psychopath Button is what exposes that. Could you walk me through, at more of a gut level than a formal one, why these two "nothing is ratifiable" cases pull our intuitions in such different directions -- and whether that should worry someone who wants ratifiability to be a genuine general fix for causal decision theory, rather than something that just got lucky on a symmetric case? Happy for you to push back on my framing -- I mostly want to know if I'm even diagnosing the puzzle right before I say any of this out loud in front of 15 students tomorrow.
