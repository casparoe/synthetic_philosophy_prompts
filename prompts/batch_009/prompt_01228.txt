I'm teaching a philosophy of math seminar this term, and we just spent a session on Godel's second incompleteness theorem and what it does (and doesn't) license us to believe about the consistency of systems like PA or ZFC -- short version, not much, from the inside. That led into a tangent about the Ed Nelson thing from 2011, where he announced he'd found a proof that Peano arithmetic (actually an even weaker fragment of it) was inconsistent, after reportedly working on it for 25 years, and then Terence Tao and Daniel Tausk independently spotted the fatal gap within days. A couple students got genuinely rattled by the idea that someone that good could be that convinced of something so wrong for that long, and it got me wondering about the scarier version of that story.

So here's what I want: write me a short story where the gap DOESN'T get found. A mathematician (or a small group of them) derives an actual contradiction from the axioms of Peano arithmetic or ZFC -- your call, though the stakes are pretty different so pick on purpose -- and unlike Nelson's hand-sketched argument, suppose its fully formalized and machine-checked in something like Lean, so the usual "eh, somebody will find the error in a week" move isn't available to anyone this time. I don't want a cheesy reality-glitches-and-buildings-start-floating disaster story -- I want it to actually sit with the logical and psychological weirdness of the situation: the explosion principle meaning literally anything is now "provable," the fact that most ordinary math doesn't obviously go anywhere near whatever weird corner the contradiction lives in so it's not clear how much practically changes, the Frege/Russell precedent of just patching the axioms and moving on, and what it's actually like, as a working mathematician, to sit with the possibility that the ground you built your whole career on was never solid to begin with. Go wherever you want with tone, characters, length -- just don't dodge the actual technical substance to make the narrative tidier.
