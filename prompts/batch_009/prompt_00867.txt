I spent the better part of thirty-five years as an observational astrophysicist, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly, rather than in the stolen hours between observing runs. Partly by accident, this new reading looped back into my own field's history: I came across J. Richard Gott's old *Nature* paper applying the Copernican principle to the future lifespan of arbitrary ongoing phenomena, from the Berlin Wall (which he visited in 1969 and used to derive a confidence interval for how much longer it would stand) to, more ambitiously, the human species itself. What struck me was how spare the reasoning was, resting on nothing beyond the assumption that the moment at which one happens to observe an ongoing process is not special, and how well it performed when Gott later tested it, half in jest, against the running times of Broadway shows.

Curiosity took me further into the philosophical literature on the Doomsday Argument proper, through Carter, Leslie, and above all Bostrom, where the same conclusion -- that we should shift our credence toward an earlier human extinction -- is reached quite differently, via the Self-Sampling Assumption: the principle that one should reason as though randomly drawn from the set of actual observers within some reference class. This is the version people try to defuse with the rival Self-Indication Assumption, which weights hypotheses by how many observers they imply and, in the simplest models, cancels the doomsday shift exactly. Yet taking the Self-Indication Assumption seriously elsewhere lands one in Bostrom's own "Presumptuous Philosopher" scenario, in which one ends up favoring a physical theory purely because it entails a far larger universe of observers -- a conclusion that strikes me as at least as suspect as the one it was meant to block.

What I cannot work out, and would value an intuitive rather than formal treatment of, is this: are Gott's reference-class-free version and the Carter-Leslie/Bostrom version really the same underlying inference dressed in different notation, or is there a genuine structural difference that explains why the choice of reference class (humans? all observers? observer-moments?) does so much work in one and seems almost beside the point in the other? And is there any principled ground for preferring the Self-Sampling Assumption over the Self-Indication Assumption, or does the choice ultimately reduce to which absurd conclusion -- doom soon, or the Presumptuous Philosopher -- one finds easier to live with? I can follow the underlying algebra well enough on my own; it is the intuition behind what is actually driving the disagreement that keeps escaping me.
