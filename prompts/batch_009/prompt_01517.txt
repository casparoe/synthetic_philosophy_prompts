So we had a whole blow-up at work last week during a postmortem and I havent been able to stop turning it over in my head since. We had an outage because a cron job that renews an internal cert had been silently failing for like three weeks, and in the retro everyone pretty quickly converged on "the root cause is that Dave never set up an alert for failures on that job." Dave pushed back though - he said something like "sure, if I'd added that alert this wouldn't have happened, but I also didn't add alerts for a thousand other things that could've failed, and neither did anyone else in this meeting, so why is my not-doing-it 'the cause' and everyone else's not-doing-it isn't?" We're supposed to run "blameless" postmortems but this didn't feel very blameless, and it also kind of broke my brain a little. Part of me wants to say obviously Dave's not-adding-the-alert is the cause, since it was literally his job to own alerting for that service. But another part of me thinks that's just us picking on the guy whose job description happens to mention it, and that if your being honest about what "caused" this in some deeper sense, basically infinite people caused it - me, our CEO, the intern, anyone on earth who also didn't add that alert and also didn't do anything to stop the outage.

So I guess what I'm actually asking is: is there some principled way of picking out which non-actions really count as causes of something, or is the whole idea that a person "not doing something" can cause an event just a polite way of dressing up who we feel like blaming? Like, is what Dave did (or rather, didn't do) genuinely different, in some real causal sense, from what I didn't do, or what my cat didn't do, or are we just kidding ourselves that there's a fact of the matter here at all once you get past "yeah technically if he had done it, it wouldn't have happened"? I'd love to actually get a handle on the structure of this instead of just going with whatever the loudest person in the retro thinks.
