I'm a retired geologist, only got into philosophy the last couple years since I retired, so bare with me, total amateur here.

Something from my fieldwork days has been bugging me since I started reading this stuff. Decades ago my truck slid off a remote mountain road in an early snowstorm, no radio, no idea when anyone would pass by. A logging truck eventually came along, but before the driver would take me into town he sized me up for a minute and said something like, "I've had guys promise to pay me for the trouble and skip out the second they're safe - you look good for it, so get in, but you better pay." I agreed, and the moment we reached town I paid him cash before he'd even said goodbye.

But thinking it over since: once we got to town I was already safe, he had no way to track me down, and we were never going to see each other again - so paying him didn't get me anything going forward, it was pure loss. And yet it felt obviously like the rational thing to do, not just the polite thing, like skipping out would've been an actual mistake, not just rudeness. Why should handing over money for zero future benefit count as the smart move instead of just the nice one? Is there an actual answer here, or am I overthinking plain old good manners?
