ok so I'm outlining a novel that opens with a drunk-driving accident, and instead of actually writing it I've fallen down a moral philosophy rabbit hole (as one does). Here's the itch I can't scratch: two guys leave the same party equally wasted, drive home the exact same reckless way -- one makes it home fine, the other hits a kid crossing the street. Same choices, same drunkeness, wildly different outcomes. Is the unlucky one actually more blameworthy, or just unluckier?

Could you write me a short dialogue between two people hashing this out -- one taking the hardline "control principle" stance, that blame can only track what's within your control, so the two drivers are equally guilty and any difference in how we treat them afterward is just us reacting irrationally to something that isn't a real moral difference. The other should push back hard: the outcome really does change what he did, morally speaking, and whatever the unlucky driver feels afterward -- guilt, or something like Williams' "agent-regret" thing, if that even still applies once there was already fault involved -- is tracking something real, not just a confused feeling.

Don't let either one fold just for a tidy ending, I want actual back and forth where each one deals with the other's strongest point instead of talking past each other. And try not to let it drift into a general free-will-vs-determinism debate -- keep it on whether outcomes themselves change blameworthiness.
