I'm writing a historical novel set in Rome during the 1616 inquiry into Copernicanism, and one of my characters, a Dominican theologian assigned to assess Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, needs an objection with genuine intellectual weight rather than a caricature of dogmatic obstruction. Galileo bases his defense on Augustine's exegetical caution, in De Genesi ad litteram, against reading scripture literally where it conflicts with what is soundly demonstrated in nature, reinforced by a line he credits to an unnamed "eminent ecclesiastic": that the Holy Spirit meant to teach us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go. But Augustine's caution concerned conclusions already secured beyond dispute, while in 1615 heliocentrism was itself the thing in dispute. Compare Augustine's original principle of accommodation with Galileo's use of it, and pinpoint where the two quietly diverge—especially on who decides a conclusion counts as "demonstrated," and on Galileo's move to shift that burden onto his critics. I want my theologian's objection to target Galileo's real weak point, not one history has already discredited.
