I'm a graduate student wrapping up a thesis on Peirce's theory of truth, and something my advisor mentioned almost in passing a couple of weeks ago has been nagging at me ever since. We were going over the well-known passage from "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" where Peirce writes that "the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real." My advisor casually remarked that this is exactly the kind of view that Fitch's paradox of knowability was later used to make trouble for, then moved on to another topic before I could ask her to say more. I've since read up on the paradox myself, and now I can't shake the worry that it might undercut a central plank of the reading I've spent the last year defending.

For context, in case it's useful: the paradox, due to the logician Frederic Fitch in 1963, shows that the seemingly modest thesis that every truth is at least possibly knowable actually entails the far less modest thesis that every truth is already known. The proof works by taking any true-but-presently-unknown proposition p and considering the conjunction "p, and no one knows that p." That conjunction is itself true, so the knowability thesis says it should be possible to know it as well; but knowing it would require knowing both p and that p is unknown, which collapses into contradiction the instant it is supposedly known, since knowledge is factive. Something in the setup has to give, and the natural place to lay the blame is the assumption that truth simply is, or guarantees, knowability.

Peirce, of course, died in 1914, decades before Fitch ever wrote this down, so he never had occasion to respond to it directly. But because his account ties truth so tightly to what would eventually be settled by inquiry, it looks like exactly the kind of position the paradox was designed to threaten. What I'd like help with is reconstructing how Peirce himself might have reacted, using the rest of his philosophy as a guide rather than just his one famous slogan: things like his fallibilism, his insistence that inquiry is a self-correcting process open to an indefinitely extended community, and his treatment of real generals and "would-be's" as his own distinctive way of handling possibility. Is there a plausible, Peirce-flavored way to resist the paradox's conclusion without simply abandoning the link between truth and the ultimate fate of inquiry, or does the argument expose a real fault line in his account that he lacked the resources to fix?
