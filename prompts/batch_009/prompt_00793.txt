During my career as an experimental physicist, I became well acquainted, in a practical rather than a scholarly way, with something very like the Duhem–Quine thesis: whenever an experiment produced a result that seemed to embarrass the theory under test, there was always some auxiliary assumption about the apparatus, the calibration, or a stray background process that could be revised instead, and deciding which strand of the whole web of belief to blame was as much a matter of judgment as of logic. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy discussion group, and a few sessions ago, prompted by all the current talk about what large language models can and cannot do, we worked through some of the older AI literature on the so-called "frame problem." I was struck to find Fodor explicitly connecting the broad, epistemological version of that problem to Quine's holism about confirmation, arguing that genuine central cognition would have to be "isotropic" and "Quinean" in his technical sense of those terms — roughly, that in principle almost any belief could bear on the confirmation of almost any other — and that this is precisely what makes the problem so intractable for any system built out of narrowly encapsulated modules.

This has left me wanting a clearer picture of how the two ideas actually relate. Could you compare the frame problem — in the broad sense given to it by Dennett and Fodor, as distinct from the narrower, formal problem McCarthy and Hayes originally posed about efficiently representing the non-effects of actions — with Quine's confirmation holism and the associated Duhem–Quine thesis about the underdetermination of theory by evidence? I am particularly interested in which respects the frame problem is simply a special, real-time, action-guiding instance of the more general holism thesis, and in which respects it raises a genuinely distinct difficulty that a philosopher of science concerned only with abstract theory confirmation would never have had to face. If it helps sharpen the comparison, feel free to comment on whether any of the classical proposed remedies for the narrower AI frame problem — circumscription, default logic, closed-world assumptions in STRIPS-style planners, and so on — shed genuine light on the broader, holism-flavored problem, or whether they merely legislate it out of existence for practical purposes without actually answering it.
