I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a good deal of time lately reading discussions of large language models, and one framing that keeps coming up is that they are best understood not as "agents" pursuing goals of their own, but as "simulators": systems with no fixed identity that can generate all sorts of different characters or voices on demand, depending on how they are prompted. This reminded me strongly of Hume's treatment of personal identity — his claim that introspection never reveals a persisting self, only a bundle of perceptions succeeding one another, and his own later admission, in the Appendix to the Treatise, that he could not actually explain what unites these perceptions into something we are willing to call one mind. If Hume were confronted with a modern LLM, do you think he would treat it as vindicating the bundle theory — especially given how much his psychology already leaned on the association of ideas rather than on any executive self directing them — or would he insist that something essential to being a "self," let alone an "agent," is missing, so the analogy breaks down? And does the difficulty he confessed to, of explaining what ties a bundle of perceptions together into anything at all, suggest that "simulator" wouldn't strike him as a coherent category either, but merely his own unsolved problem in new clothes?
