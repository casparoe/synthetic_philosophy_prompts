hey, random research question that's been living in my head rent free. i'm drafting a scene in a historical novel where a woman is sheltering a man from soldiers hunting him down, and i want her to be the kind of person who genuinely will not lie, even here, even though it obviously costs her something. so i went down a rabbit hole into Augustine's "On Lying" to see how a real strict no-lying position would actually hold up under interrogation, and i got stuck on this bit:

"But one sometimes comes to a case of this kind, that we are not interrogated where the person is who is sought, nor forced to betray him, if he is hidden in such manner, that he cannot easily be found unless betrayed: but we are asked, whether he be in such a place or not. If we know him to be there, by holding our peace we betray him, or even by saying that we will in no wise tell whether he be there or not: for from this the questioner gathers that he is there... Wherefore if you know not where he is, there is no cause for hiding the truth, but you must confess that you know not. But, if you know where he is, whether he be in the place which is named in the question or elsewhere; you must not say, when it is asked whether he be there or not, 'I will not tell you what you ask,' but you must say, 'I know where he is, but I will never show.' For if, touching one place in particular you answer not and profess that you will not betray, it is just as if you should point to that same place with your finger: for a sure suspicion is thereby excited. But if at the first you confess that you know where he is, but will not tell, haply the inquisitor may be diverted from that place... For which good faith and humanity whatever you shall bravely bear, is judged to be not only not culpable, but even laudable."

i've reread this like five times. i get that hes trying to find a way for her to avoid both lying AND giving the guy up, but the actual mechanics of the move arent clicking for me - why does he think refusing to answer about "that place" specifically gives it away, while announcing up front "i know but i wont tell you" doesnt give anything away? is there a real logical difference there, or is he just finding a technicality so his absolute rule doesn't collapse the moment it meets a hard case? also, isn't deliberately phrasing a true statement to steer someone's inference away from the truth basically the "intent to deceive" he defines lying by earlier in the same book? feels like he's sneaking deception in through the side door. would love a real walk-through of the logic, not just "Augustine thought lying was always wrong" - trying to figure out if i can actually put this reasoning in my character's mouth and have it hold together.
