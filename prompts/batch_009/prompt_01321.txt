My school offers a small philosophy elective, and this week our teacher handed out a packet of primary-source excerpts on conditional ("if...then") statements for us to read and annotate before our next class discussion. Most of the packet was manageable, but one entry has had me stuck for the past two evenings: a single footnote from a 1929 essay called "General Propositions and Causality" by Frank Ramsey, a Cambridge philosopher and mathematician who, as our teacher mentioned almost as an aside, died at twenty-six, barely a year after writing it. The footnote, published posthumously in 1931, reads:

"If two people are arguing 'If p will q?' and are both in doubt as to p, they are adding p hypothetically to their stock of knowledge and arguing on that basis about q; so that in a sense 'If p, q' and 'If p, not-q' are contradictories. We can say they are fixing their degrees of belief in q given p. If p turns out false, these degrees of belief are rendered void. If either party believes not-p for certain, the question ceases to mean anything to him except as a question about what follows from certain laws or hypotheses."

I think I follow the main idea: to evaluate "if p, then q," you provisionally add p to what you already believe, adjust everything else accordingly, and see how confident that leaves you in q. What I can't get past is the very last sentence. Ramsey seems to be saying that his own procedure stops meaning anything once someone is already certain that p is false. But isn't that exactly the situation every time someone says something like "if I had studied harder, I would have passed," where both people already know perfectly well that the person didn't study harder? Our teacher mentioned in passing that this footnote is the origin of something called "the Ramsey test," which people still cite today as a basis for reasoning about hypotheticals in general, and that it connects somehow to a distinction philosophers draw between two different kinds of conditional sentences, but we ran out of time before she could get into it. Could you help me work out what Ramsey is actually claiming in that last sentence, why it seems to contradict how the "test" named after him gets used, and what that has to do with the bigger distinction she mentioned?
