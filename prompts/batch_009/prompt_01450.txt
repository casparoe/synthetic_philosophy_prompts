I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm currently working on a feature about the ethics of hypocrisy in public life — the recurring spectacle of politicians, executives, and activists accused of failing to practice what they preach, from legislators who denounce deficit spending while voting for it to climate advocates who fly private jets to summits on emissions. While researching the philosophical background, I came across the concept of "standing to blame," a notion explored in one of the earliest and most influential treatments of the subject, G.A. Cohen's essay on who does and doesn't have the right to condemn terrorism, and later developed by R. Jay Wallace and others into a broader theory linking hypocrisy to the equal moral standing of persons.

As I understand the argument, these philosophers have shown that when someone blames another person for a fault they share themselves, the hypocrisy does more than make the accuser look bad — it actually negates the truth of the accusation itself, since a claim like "it's wrong to fly a private jet to a climate summit" can only be true when the person asserting it meets some threshold of consistency. On this reading, the standing-to-blame literature amounts to a rigorous philosophical vindication of the "look who's talking" defense that pundits deploy constantly on op-ed pages and cable news. Given that this now appears to be a serious, well-established result in academic ethics, why do most logic and critical-thinking textbooks still insist that responding to a moral accusation by pointing out the accuser's own hypocrisy is a fallacious deflection — a version of the ad hominem tu quoque — rather than acknowledging it as the legitimate rebuttal that the standing-to-blame literature seems to have established?
