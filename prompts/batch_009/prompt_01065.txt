I came across a passage while reading about the debate between scientific realists and their critics, specifically about the "pessimistic meta-induction" associated with Larry Laudan: the argument that since so many past scientific theories that were once well-confirmed and empirically successful (phlogiston theory, caloric theory, the theory of the luminiferous ether) were later judged false, we have little reason to think our own best current theories are true either, no matter how successful they currently seem. While tracing the history of this worry, I found an older passage that seems to be circling around the same problem decades before Laudan gave it a name. It's from Henri Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* (1902), from the chapter "The Theories of Modern Physics," where he discusses the transition from Fresnel's ether-based theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. The passage reads:

"The ephemeral nature of scientific theories takes by surprise the man of the world. Their brief period of prosperity ended, he sees them abandoned one after another; he sees ruins piled upon ruins; he predicts that the theories in fashion to-day will in a short time succumb in their turn, and he concludes that they are absolutely in vain. This is what he calls the bankruptcy of science. His scepticism is superficial; he does not take into account the object of scientific theories and the part they play, or he would understand that the ruins may be still good for something. No theory seemed established on firmer ground than Fresnel's, which attributed light to the movements of the ether. Then if Maxwell's theory is to-day preferred, does that mean that Fresnel's work was in vain? No; for Fresnel's object was not to know whether there really is an ether, if it is or is not formed of atoms, if these atoms really move in this way or that; his object was to predict optical phenomena. This Fresnel's theory enables us to do to-day as well as it did before Maxwell's time. The differential equations are always true, they may be always integrated by the same methods, and the results of this integration still preserve their value. It cannot be said that this is reducing physical theories to simple practical recipes; these equations express relations, and if the equations remain true, it is because the relations preserve their reality. They teach us now, as they did then, that there is such and such a relation between this thing and that; only, the something which we then called motion, we now call electric current. But these are merely names of the images we substituted for the real objects which Nature will hide for ever from our eyes. The true relations between these real objects are the only reality we can attain, and the sole condition is that the same relations shall exist between these objects as between the images we are forced to put in their place. If the relations are known to us, what does it matter if we think it convenient to replace one image by another?"

I've read this several times, but I can't quite pin down what Poincaré thinks actually survives when one theory replaces another. At some points he seems to say that only abstract "relations" persist while the "images" we use to picture them (an ether, an electric current, a mechanical model) are essentially disposable, which sounds like it might collapse into a kind of instrumentalism on which we were never entitled to believe in the ether or its mechanisms to begin with, only in their predictive success. But elsewhere he explicitly resists this reading, insisting that these relations "preserve their reality" and denying that this amounts to "reducing physical theories to simple practical recipes." Is there a way of reading this passage on which Poincaré is defending a genuinely realist position, one on which we get something objectively right about the world and not merely something instrumentally useful, while still fully accommodating the abandonment of Fresnel's ether? And if such a position can be extracted from the passage, does it actually supply a principled way of resisting the pessimistic meta-induction, or does it just relocate the goalposts so that whatever happens to survive theory change gets relabeled "the real content" after the fact, which would make the resulting realism true by definition rather than a substantive thesis?
