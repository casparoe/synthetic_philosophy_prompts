I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student, and next week I'm presenting on fitting-attitude ("buck-passing") accounts of value for our metaethics seminar, with a focus on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. I've spent the last few weeks working through the Rabinowicz-and-Rønnow-Rasmussen literature and its aftermath, and after watching one proposed fix after another get refined and then punctured by a new demon case, I've started to suspect that the recurring pattern is itself the interesting result, rather than any particular patch. My advisor thinks I'm over-reading a run of bad luck in the secondary literature, and before I build a talk around a stronger claim than she's comfortable with, I'd like an independent, maximally forceful attempt at the argument from someone with no stake in how the presentation goes.

Here is the thesis. Recall that on a buck-passing view, something is valuable (admirable, desirable, etc.) just in case there is reason for a suitable pro-attitude toward it, and the standard demon case — you're threatened with harm unless you admire a saucer of mud — shows that not every such reason will do, so buck-passers need a principled way of excluding "wrong-kind" reasons like the demon's threat. Two broad strategies are on offer: formal ones in the spirit of Parfit's object-given/state-given contrast, which try to specify in general, structural terms which reasons are eligible to fix value; and strategies like D'Arms and Jacobson's appeal to the "opacity of normative force," which deny that any clean formal rule is available and instead treat the line between a reason's bearing on fit and its bearing on propriety as something to be judged case by case. I want you to argue that neither strategy, nor any natural descendant of either, can succeed, because both face a common dilemma: any criterion sharp enough to actually exclude the demon's reason either (a) smuggles into the criterion itself the very evaluative fact — that the mud isn't really admirable — that the analysis was supposed to explain rather than presuppose, or (b) avoids that circularity only by being purely structural, in which case a sufficiently inventive demon case can be constructed one level up (targeting the grounds of the attitude, a higher-order attitude, or whatever feature was meant to make the reason "object-given" in the first place) to regenerate the original problem in a new guise.

Please make this argument in its strongest form, including at least one demon-style case of your own devising to show concretely how the regress horn works against a formal proposal, and engage directly with D'Arms and Jacobson's opacity strategy as the hard case for the dilemma, since denying that a formal rule is available looks like the most promising way of dodging horn (b). If, having pushed the argument as far as it goes, you think one horn is actually avoidable, or that the right conclusion is something more modest than abandoning buck-passing altogether — treating fittingness as a primitive normative notion, say — I would genuinely rather hear that than a one-sided defense.
