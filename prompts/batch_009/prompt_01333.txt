I never had the chance to study history or philosophy in any formal way, but for the last couple of years I've been working through the abolitionist movement on my own time, mostly library books and whatever primary sources I can track down. Frederick Douglass is the figure I keep coming back to, partly because he so dramatically changed his mind on one specific question: for years he agreed with William Lloyd Garrison that the Constitution was rotten at its core on slavery, and then around 1851 he switched sides and spent the rest of his life arguing that it was, properly read, an anti-slavery document. I recently found the text of the 1860 speech he gave in Glasgow where he lays out that mature position in full, and most of it I can follow well enough, but there is one stretch, where he explains why the private beliefs of the men who wrote the Constitution shouldn't matter to how we read it now, that I keep re-reading without quite landing on what he's doing. Here is the passage:

"Again, it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States. It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution. It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say. Bear in mind, also, and the fact is an important one, that the framers of the Constitution sat with doors closed, and that this was done purposely, that nothing but the result of their labours should be seen, and that that result should be judged of by the people free from any of the bias shown in the debates. It should also be borne in mind, and the fact is still more important, that the debates in the convention that framed the Constitution, and by means of which a pro-slavery interpretation is now attempted to be forced upon that instrument, were not published till more than a quarter of a century after the presentation and the adoption of the Constitution."

What trips me up is the last third of this, where Douglass brings in the closed doors of the convention and the delayed publication of the debates. I can't tell whether these are meant as two separate points or one continuous piece of reasoning, and I can't tell whether they are just a rhetorical jab at his opponents for building their case on material nobody could have read at the time, or whether they are doing real work in his larger argument, something like: the people who ratified the Constitution could only have agreed to what was in front of them, so the written document has to be the sole legitimate object of interpretation, whatever any individual delegate may privately have wanted. If it is the second thing, I would love help spelling out the reasoning a bit more carefully than Douglass does, since on the page it seems to move very quickly from a historical fact about secrecy to a conclusion about what later interpreters are and are not allowed to take into account. I am also curious whether this is essentially the same move I have heard attributed to judges who say they care only about the words of a law and not about what its authors privately intended, and if so, whether Douglass's version of the argument manages to avoid the obvious complaint that "just stick to the text" doesn't actually resolve much, given that people who disagree about what a law should mean usually disagree about what its text says in the first place.
