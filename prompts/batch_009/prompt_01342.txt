Friedrich Hayek's case for the price system rests on a specific epistemic claim: that prices aggregate the dispersed, tacit "knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place" that no planner could ever collect and centrally process. Decades after Hayek's death, Robin Hanson proposed futarchy in "Shall We Vote on Values, But Bet on Beliefs?": citizens or their representatives would continue to vote democratically on a single scalar measure of national welfare, while speculative markets, populated by traders staking real money, would decide which policy is likeliest to raise that measure, with whichever policy the market favors adopted automatically.

Hayek never considered futarchy, but he did propose his own remedy for the pathologies he saw in majoritarian legislatures. In the closing volume of Law, Legislation and Liberty, he sketched a bicameral "Model Constitution" in which a Legislative Assembly — elected only once in each citizen's life, at age forty-five, by that year's forty-five-year-old cohort, for a single non-renewable fifteen-year term — would be insulated from electoral pressure and entrusted with laying down general, abstract rules of just conduct, leaving ordinary policy to a separate Governmental Assembly.

Reconstruct, as precisely as you can, the verdict Hayek would most plausibly have reached on futarchy, arguing strictly from commitments he actually held: his epistemics of the price mechanism; his polemic, in his Nobel lecture and elsewhere, against the "pretence of knowledge" involved in treating aggregate social outcomes as engineerable targets; his distinction between evolved and deliberately constructed institutions; and his own proposal for insulating rule-making from majorities. Would he have welcomed futarchy as the natural extension of his own economics, or dismissed it as one more piece of the "constructivist rationalism" he spent his career attacking?
