ok so I've fallen back into the epistocracy hole again (the jason brennan "against democracy" stuff that resurfaces on rationalist blogs every year or two) and this time instead of just reading takes on it I went and actually read Mill's original plural-voting proposal in Considerations on Representative Government. theres a bit in there thats bugging me and I can't tell if its a genuine crack in his argument or if I'm just being uncharitable. two passages, same chapter:

"But (though every one ought to have a voice) that every one should have an equal voice is a totally different proposition. When two persons who have a joint interest in any business differ in opinion, does justice require that both opinions should be held of exactly equal value? If with equal virtue, one is superior to the other in knowledge and intelligence—or if with equal intelligence, one excels the other in virtue—the opinion, the judgment of the higher moral or intellectual being is worth more than that of the inferior; and if the institutions of the country virtually assert that they are of the same value, they assert a thing which is not."

and then a bit later, when he gets to how you'd actually implement this:

"I hasten to say that I consider it entirely inadmissible, unless as a temporary makeshift, that the superiority of influence should be conferred in consideration of property... To connect plurality of votes with any pecuniary qualification would be not only objectionable in itself, but a sure mode of compromising the principle, and making its permanent maintenance impracticable... In the absence of [a real national education system], the nature of a person's occupation is some test. An employer of labor is on the average more intelligent than a laborer; for he must labor with his head, and not solely with his hands. A foreman is generally more intelligent than an ordinary laborer, and a laborer in the skilled trades than in the unskilled. A banker, merchant, or manufacturer is likely to be more intelligent than a tradesman, because he has larger and more complicated interests to manage."

so he explicitly rules out wealth/property as a basis for extra votes bc it'd be "supremely odious" and would corrupt the whole scheme, but then when he needs an actual workable proxy for "intelligence" he reaches for occupational categories (employer vs laborer, banker vs tradesman) that seem to track capital/class just as tightly as property would. can you help me work out whether Mill has some principled way of distinguishing "occupation as a proxy for competence" from the "property as a proxy for wealth" criterion he just spent a paragraph rejecting, or whether this is just a real inconsistency in the argument? and if it is a real one, does it sink the whole plural-voting idea or is it more of a fixable detail about which proxy you pick?
