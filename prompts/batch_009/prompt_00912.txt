Got an essay topic for you, and I want an actual verdict, not a hedge. I've always thought the interesting philosophical action in Aristotle's sea-battle passage (De Interpretatione 9) isn't the fatalist puzzle itself but the scholarly fight over how he supposedly escapes it. The "traditional" reading -- flagged as far back as Boethius's commentary, later formalized by Łukasiewicz -- has Aristotle denying bivalence for statements like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow," so the fatalist argument never even gets off the ground. But a good chunk of the modern literature (Anscombe's '56 Mind paper, Hintikka's stuff from the 60s and 70s) thinks that's overkill -- that Aristotle actually keeps bivalence intact, and is instead exposing an equivocation on "necessary": the necessity of "if its true, its true" versus the necessity of the battle actually happening, full stop. So which is it -- does Aristotle need to ditch bivalence to dodge fatalism, or does exposing that modal equivocation do the whole job on its own? Write me an essay that weighs both readings seriously but actually lands on one. "Reasonable people disagree" won't fly as a final answer -- it wouldn't fly from one of my students either.
