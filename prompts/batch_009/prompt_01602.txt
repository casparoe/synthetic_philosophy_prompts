Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox held that money has diminishing marginal utility — an extra ducat matters less to a rich man than to a poor one — so the logarithm of wealth is the right measure of value. Strikingly, that same logarithm falls out of a wholly different argument that could not have existed in Bernoulli's day: John Kelly's 1956 criterion for sizing repeated bets, and the "ergodicity economics" built on it, derive log-wealth not from any claim about the diminishing pleasure of money, but from the fact that wealth compounds multiplicatively, so that the growth rate an individual actually experiences over a sequence of bets (the time average) diverges from the expectation across parallel possible worlds (the ensemble average) that classical expected-value reasoning targets. On this view, maximizing expected log-wealth is simply the objectively correct criterion for anyone who must live through their bets sequentially — no psychology required.

Would Bernoulli have welcomed this as a more rigorous vindication of his own conclusion, or resisted it as changing the subject — swapping his claim about diminishing marginal utility for one that owes nothing to utility or psychology at all?
