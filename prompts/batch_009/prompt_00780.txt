Last night my regular havruta partner and I spent most of our session off the assigned parashah, arguing instead about Maimonides on animal sacrifice, and I have not been able to let it go. The starting point was Guide of the Perplexed III:32, where Maimonides argues that God never wanted sacrifice for its own sake: the Israelites leaving Egypt had been formed by a culture, Egyptian and Sabian alike, in which worship simply meant offering animals to consecrated images, and since it was psychologically impossible to abolish that mode of worship all at once, the Torah redirected the existing practice toward the one God as a kind of pedagogical way-station on the road to the higher worship of prayer and contemplation. In III:46 he becomes far more specific, going animal by animal to explain why sheep, goats, and cattle in particular were designated as offerings, tying each choice to a distinct idolatrous cult that revered that species.

My partner's rebuttal was Nahmanides' famous objection in his commentary on Leviticus 1:9, where he all but accuses Maimonides of trivializing the entire sacrificial order, and invokes Cain and Abel's offerings in Genesis 4, Noah's offering in Genesis 8, and Abraham's sacrifice of the ram in Genesis 22 — all of which occur long before there was an Israelite nation to be weaned off Egyptian or Sabian habits, and, in Cain and Abel's case, seemingly before organized idolatry of any kind existed. On my partner's view this is simply decisive: a theory that explains the sacrificial cult as a response to a specific, local idolatrous environment cannot also explain why sacrifice was apparently the default, unprompted response of the very first human beings to God. I fell back on the standard move of pointing out that none of the patriarchal sacrifices were commanded, unlike the legislation given at Sinai, but I could not actually say what work that distinction is doing, and my partner not unreasonably called it a dodge.

I would like you to succeed where I did not. Please construct the strongest argument you can that Maimonides' accommodation theory survives Nahmanides' objection. Take the Cain-and-Abel, Noah, and Abraham cases head-on rather than bracketing them, and make sure the argument actually cashes out what the commanded/voluntary distinction — or any other resource available within Maimonides' broader account — is doing to block the objection, rather than simply asserting the distinction and moving on.
