Quick brain-dump before I get to my actual question, since I don't think this corner of the internet is something you'd have run into a hundred times. A grad student of mine who spends way too much time on rationalist/AI-safety blogs got me hooked on this idea called a "surrogate goal." As I understand it (Caspar Oesterheld gets credit for coming up with it around 2016, Tobias Baumann wrote it up publicly the next year, both connected to the Center on Long-Term Risk suffering-focused research crowd, though I think they went by a different name back then), its meant as a defense against "acausal" threats -- the worry that some other agent, maybe a future superintelligence or something in a causally disconnected branch of the multiverse, could effectively blackmail you with no causal channel between you at all, purely because you can each correctly predict how the other reasons (Roko's basilisk is the infamous example that made the rounds). The proposed defense is that you commit ahead of time to caring about some manufactured, otherwise-worthless stand-in goal exactly as much as you care about the thing you really value, and to only ever give in to threats framed against that stand-in. Since a threatener gains nothing extra from targeting your real values over the decoy, the thought is that threats against what you actually care about just stop being worth making.

I keep getting stuck on how much this sounds like Thomas Schelling with a computer science degree. It's precommitment and deliberately rearranging your own incentives so your opponent finds it rational to back off -- basically his whole "rationality of irrationality" move, the chained-climbers-at-the-cliff-edge threat, burning your own boats so retreat isn't an option -- just applied to a situation where the other side never has to see you do any of it, since there's no causal contact to observe in the first place.

So, genuine question: if you sat Schelling down and walked him through acausal threats and surrogate goals, do you think he'd recognize the surrogate goal move as a natural extension of the commitment logic he already spent his career on, or is there something about the acausal setup -- no observation, no reputation, no history of dealing with this particular opponent, just each side's model of the other's disposition -- that would make him think his whole framework quietly stops applying? Dont just hedge and tell me "he'd find it thought-provoking" -- actually commit to an answer and defend it, ideally by pointing to something specific in how he thought about commitment and credibility.
