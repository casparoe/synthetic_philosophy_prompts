A few years ago I got into moral philosophy almost by accident, after watching "The Good Place," where one of the characters is obsessed with a book called "What We Owe to Each Other" by T. M. Scanlon. I ended up reading it myself, and one idea from it has stayed with me since: Scanlon's suggestion that calling something "good" isn't attributing some extra, mysterious property to it, but is really just a compressed way of saying that it has other, more concrete features that give us reason to want it, pursue it, or respond to it favorably. He calls this the "buck-passing" account of value, since it passes the explanatory buck from goodness itself onto whatever features are doing the actual reason-giving work. I found this quite persuasive until I went looking for objections online and ran into something called the "wrong kind of reasons problem." The version that has lodged itself in my mind involves a demon who credibly threatens to subject you to terrible suffering unless you come to admire, or desire for its own sake, an ordinary saucer of mud. That threat surely gives you an overwhelming reason to try to admire the mud, and yet the mud doesn't thereby become admirable, nor has the demon managed to inject any value into it. If "X is good" just means "there is reason to favor X," this case seems to force the theory into an absurd result.

As far as I can tell, the standard patch is to say that only reasons grounded in an object's own features count toward whether it is actually valuable, while reasons grounded merely in the payoff of adopting the attitude itself -- like avoiding the demon's wrath -- are "of the wrong kind" and get excluded by stipulation. But when I try to imagine how one would draw that line precisely, without covertly building "tracks genuine value" into the very definition of "the right kind of reason," I start to suspect this patch just relocates the problem rather than solving it. Then again, it is entirely possible I am missing some independent, non-circular way of cashing out the distinction that philosophers working on this problem have actually figured out.

I would like you to write me an essay that helps me settle this. Please lay out the strongest version of the case that the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is fatal to buck-passing and fitting-attitude theories as accounts of what goodness ultimately consists in, and the strongest version of the case that some version of the "right kind of reason" restriction -- whether the object-given/state-given distinction or some other strategy -- genuinely rescues the theory. Then do not leave me hanging: commit to, and argue for, a definite verdict on which side has the better of the argument.
