So my school added this bioethics elective this year (pretty sure it only exists so people stop complaining we don't have any philosophy classes) and this week's debate turned into something I can't stop thinking about. The topic was animal testing for new medicines. Everyone in class agreed that if a drug company grabbed 300 random unconsenting people off the street and tested an experimental vaccine on them, that would be horrifying and wrong no matter how many lives it ended up saving overall — like there's no math that makes it ok. But maybe five minutes later, that same room of people (myself included, ngl) was totally fine with testing the same vaccine on 300 rats or monkeys instead, just straight-up doing the math of suffering-caused vs lives-saved like it was a word problem. Our teacher pointed out that we'd used two completely different rulebooks back to back without even noticing, and now we have to write a response for homework on whether thats actually defensible or if we're just being inconsistent.

I keep getting stuck trying to write this. It's not like I think animals count for literally nothing — if someone tortured a dog for fun I'd say they wronged the dog, not just that they did something distasteful. But I also don't think a lab rat gets the same "you can't use me for the greater good, period" protection that a person automatically gets. Is there an actual principled reason for having one totally-off-limits rule for one group and a run-the-numbers rule for the other, or is that split just something convenient we made up? And if there IS a real reason, what happens at the edges — like a chimp that can outsmart a human toddler on some tests, or someone who's permanently unconscious with zero brain activity — does the whole line just stop making sense at that point? Kinda stressing about this since its due thursday and "it just feels different" isn't gonna cut it as a thesis, so any help actually thinking through the logic here would be great.
