I've been reporting a piece on how physicists and philosophers keep running into the same wall when they try to explain what makes two things "two" rather than "one," and it sent me back to Max Black's 1952 paper "The Identity of Indiscernibles," which is itself written as a dialogue between two unnamed interlocutors. Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two solid iron spheres, each exactly one mile in diameter, floating two miles apart in an otherwise empty and perfectly symmetrical space. Because there is nothing else around to break the symmetry, the spheres seem to share every property, including relational ones — each is exactly two miles from a sphere of the same size and composition — yet there are, by hypothesis, indisputably two of them, not one. A physicist I interviewed for the piece mentioned that almost the same argument resurfaces when people debate whether two electrons in an identical quantum state are "really" distinct particles, which is what pulled me back into this corner of the philosophy literature.

For a sidebar, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two contemporary metaphysicians who, by some conceit, find themselves actually standing at the edge of Black's two-sphere universe, arguing over what it proves. One should hold that the thought experiment genuinely refutes the identity of indiscernibles and vindicates something close to what Robert Adams called "primitive thisness" — the idea, central to haecceitism, that identity is not exhausted by qualities. The other should defend some version of the bundle theory that Black's own interlocutors were already arguing about — the view that objects are nothing over and above their qualities — whether by denying that the scenario is really coherent as described, by a move like Ian Hacking's suggestion that a perfectly symmetric two-sphere universe can always be redescribed as a single sphere in a non-Euclidean space (so that "traveling" from one sphere to the other is really just circling back to where you started), or by salvaging a weaker, relational notion of discernibility — the kind some philosophers of physics reach for with indistinguishable particles — on which a sphere bears a relation to the other that it does not bear to itself. Please have them genuinely engage each other's specific moves rather than take turns lecturing: if one side plays the relational-property card, the other should have a concrete rejoinder ready, and neither should come across as the obvious loser. I'd like a sharp reader to finish genuinely unsure who has the better of it.

Aim for something in the 800-to-1,200-word range: rigorous enough that it wouldn't embarrass me if a professional metaphysician read over my shoulder, but written so an engaged general reader could follow every move without a philosophy background. It's fine, even preferable, if the dialogue ends without a tidy resolution — that would be the honest state of this debate.
