I'm reporting on the Uniform Law Commission's now-paused effort to rewrite the Uniform Determination of Death Act, and one claim keeps coming up in interviews: that the statute's two standards for death — irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, and irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem — were never meant as rival definitions of death, just two different roads to one and the same underlying fact, since sustained loss of either one soon brings on the other. Tellingly, James Bernat, the neurologist and bioethicist most closely identified with that unifying picture, recently co-authored an argument that the two criteria are not actually consistent with each other. For the piece, construct a specific case — real or carefully worked out — in which the two standards genuinely diverge, so that a patient would count as dead under one but is clearly still alive under the other, and say exactly which assumption behind the "two roads, one destination" view the case breaks.
