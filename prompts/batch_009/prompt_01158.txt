Each year I teach a graduate seminar that puts Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy in dialogue with contemporary work on non-classical and paraconsistent logic, and there is one knot in the material that my students and I never quite manage to untie to everyone's satisfaction. Nāgārjuna's Vigrahavyāvartanī opens with an objection from a Naiyāyika-style interlocutor: if, as Nāgārjuna maintains, all things are empty of intrinsic nature (svabhāva), then his own statement to that effect must be equally empty of intrinsic nature, and so ought to have no power to negate anyone else's. His reply — that he is not advancing any thesis (pratijñā) of his own, so the objection has nothing to latch onto — became, by way of Candrakīrti's Prāsaṅgika school, close to the standard Madhyamaka answer to this whole family of worries, as against Bhāviveka's Svātantrika willingness to run independent syllogisms and simply defend positive theses.

What I'd like help thinking through is whether that "no thesis" move is doing real philosophical work, or whether it just lets Nāgārjuna have things both ways: negating all four horns of the tetralemma about causation, motion, the self, and so forth, while declining, whenever pressed, to own whatever view all that negating amounts to. I think how you answer this bears directly on a live disagreement among contemporary readers of the text. Garfield and Priest have argued that we should take the contradictions this method throws up at face value, as true contradictions occurring precisely where a certain kind of inquiry runs up against its own edge, comparable to the self-referential trouble thinkers like Russell or the early Wittgenstein got into with their own systems; others, following Tillemans, hold out for a reading on which no real contradiction is ever produced, because the four horns are quietly being denied of the same subject under different scopes rather than in the very same sense. Could you work through, with actual argumentative detail rather than a survey of the positions, what would have to be the case for the "no thesis" defense to succeed — and say which side of the dialetheism dispute you think that leaves you on?
