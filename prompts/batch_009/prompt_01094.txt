On rationalist blogs and forums, the Boltzmann brain thought experiment usually comes up in its familiar pop-cosmology form — "statistically, you're more likely to be a disembodied fluctuation than a real observer" — which tends to gloss over a more interesting dispute among physicists about why a Boltzmann-brain-dominated cosmology should actually be rejected. In "Why Boltzmann Brains Are Bad," Sean Carroll argues that such theories are "cognitively unstable": if true, they imply that observers like us have no grounds for trusting the very reasoning and memories that led us to accept the theory in the first place, so the theory undercuts its own justification. In "Bayes Keeps Boltzmann Brains at Bay," Don Page argues that no such self-undermining move is needed — ordinary Bayesian updating on our data (e.g., the fact that our memories and observations are highly ordered rather than noise) already disfavors Boltzmann-brain-dominated theories on its own.

I'd like to understand what's actually at stake in this disagreement. Is Carroll's argument doing real philosophical work beyond what Page's likelihood-based argument already delivers, and if so, could you construct a case where the two would come apart in their verdicts? Relatedly, does the standard cosmological fix for the problem — adjusting the multiverse measure (e.g., the causal-patch or scale-factor-cutoff measures) so that Boltzmann brains don't dominate to begin with — actually address Carroll's worry, or only Page's?
