Here is a passage from Henry Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), Book III, Chapter XI, where he sets out conditions a proposition must meet before we are entitled to treat it as self-evident. This is the fourth condition, concerning disagreement:

"IV. Since it is implied in the very notion of Truth that it is essentially the same for all minds, the denial by another of a proposition that I have affirmed has a tendency to impair my confidence in its validity. And in fact 'universal' or 'general' consent has often been held to constitute by itself a sufficient evidence of the truth of the most important beliefs; and is practically the only evidence upon which the greater part of mankind can rely. A proposition accepted as true upon this ground alone has, of course, neither self-evidence nor demonstrative evidence for the mind that so accepts it; still, the secure acceptance that we commonly give to the generalisations of the empirical sciences rests—even in the case of experts—largely on the belief that other experts have seen for themselves the evidence for these generalisations, and do not materially disagree as to its adequacy. And it will be easily seen that the absence of such disagreement must remain an indispensable negative condition of the certainty of our beliefs. For if I find any of my judgments, intuitive or inferential, in direct conflict with a judgment of some other mind, there must be error somewhere: and if I have no more reason to suspect error in the other mind than in my own, reflective comparison between the two judgments necessarily reduces me temporarily to a state of neutrality. And though the total result in my mind is not exactly suspense of judgment, but an alternation and conflict between positive affirmation by one act of thought and the neutrality that is the result of another, it is obviously something very different from scientific certitude."

The last two sentences describe something curious: not a clean suspension of belief in response to disagreement, but an unstable back-and-forth between affirming the proposition and being neutral about it. Is Sidgwick presenting this oscillation as a rational failure -- a falling-short of the very "state of neutrality" his own argument says the situation calls for? Or is there a reading on which the persisting "positive affirmation" is doing legitimate epistemic work, so that the oscillation is closer to the fitting response than a full, clean suspension would be? And more generally, does this passage commit Sidgwick to the thesis that it is never rational to go on believing a proposition while believing that one's evidence doesn't support it, or does it leave room for exactly the kind of split state that thesis is meant to rule out?
