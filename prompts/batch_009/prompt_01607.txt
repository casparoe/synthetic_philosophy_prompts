I'm a PhD student in philosophy, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on decision theory under risk. This term I've been attending a reading group jointly run by the philosophy and economics departments, and we just spent two sessions on Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory as a rational vindication of Allais-paradox preferences. A disagreement broke out among a few of us afterward that we didn't manage to settle, and I think seeing it played out as a dialogue between two well-matched opponents would help me get clearer on where the real disagreement lies.

Could you write a dialogue between an orthodox expected-utility theorist, who accepts the independence axiom and treats the classic Allais preference — strictly preferring a certain payout to a gamble with higher expected value but a small chance of nothing — as a mistake to be explained away, and a Buchak-style REU theorist, who holds that this preference can be fully rational once "attitude toward risk" is treated as a separate ingredient in a person's outlook, formally represented by a function that nonlinearly reweights the cumulative probability of ending up with an outcome at least as good as any given result, rather than weighting each outcome by its own raw probability. Set the scene right after the reading group has broken up, with the two of them still standing at the whiteboard where the Allais gambles are written out. Have the disagreement move through two stages: first, the orthodox theorist should challenge the REU theorist to show that the risk function is doing real explanatory work rather than just relabeling whatever preferences an agent already has as "rational"; second, once that challenge has been met, the orthodox theorist should press the more famous dynamic-choice worry — that an agent with these preferences can, over a sequence of bets, apparently be maneuvered into paying money just to avoid receiving free information, which looks like a textbook case of exploitability. The REU theorist should have a real answer to this second challenge too, presumably drawing on the distinction between naive, sophisticated, and resolute approaches to choices that unfold over time, rather than simply denying that the scenario is possible.

Please keep both characters equally sharp and arguing in good faith — neither should come off as a pushover — and have each response genuinely engage with the specific point just made rather than restate a prepared position. There's no need to force a winner; if the dialogue ends by pinning down exactly where the disagreement bottoms out rather than resolving it, that's a perfectly good place to stop.
