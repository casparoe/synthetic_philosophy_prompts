I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who's spent the last few years reading philosophy for the first time since some undergrad requirement decades ago, and lately I've been slowly working through Frege, mostly because everyone describes him as the guy who dragged logic and arithmetic out of "psychologism" and into pure objectivity — numbers and thoughts as things that are just true or false independent of anybody's head, sort of the way I'd think of a physical constant as being "out there" whether or not anyone measures it. Naturally I warmed to him for that.

So it was a bit of a gut punch when I went down a Wikipedia/blog rabbit hole and learned that in 1924, near the very end of his life, Frege kept a private diary that's viciously antisemitic and anti-democratic — wanted Jews stripped of political rights, thought parliamentary democracy was a disaster, that sort of thing. What really got me is that he apparently doesn't just rant in it, he tries to reason his way there using his own technical apparatus: fretting over how "vague predicates" would cause enforcement headaches for race laws, and leaning on the same logic of ancestral/genealogical sequences he'd worked out decades earlier in the Begriffsschrift to talk about descent. I mentioned this to an old colleague, a fellow physicist, who shrugged it off with "so what, plenty of brilliant scientists held repugnant personal views, you don't throw out the physics." Normally I'd agree, but this feels different to me, because its not just that the same man kept good ideas in one compartment and monstrous ones in another — it's that he apparently used what's supposedly the very same method, careful logical analysis of concepts, to arrive at the monstrous ones.

That's the part I can't get past: if "rigorous conceptual analysis" is loose enough to be steered toward vile conclusions by the very person who supposedly perfected it, doesn't that mean the rigor was never really constraining anything, even back in the Grundlagen? In my old line of work, if an instrument gives a beautifully precise reading in one regime and total nonsense in another, you don't keep trusting the "good" readings — you assume it's miscalibrated and go find out why before you believe it again anywhere. Shouldn't the same standard apply here? Doesn't the diary basically hand us a good reason to be suspicious of just how "objective" Frege's celebrated rigor was, even in the domains where we happen to like the conclusions it produced? I don't know any actual philosphers to run this by in person, so I figured I'd try it out here.
