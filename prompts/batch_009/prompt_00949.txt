I am a novelist currently at work on the third installment of a series that follows the same handful of characters through branching timelines: each book takes a different fork from a shared point of departure, and the fourth (and, I hope, final) volume will bring several of these forks into contact with one another. For the third book's afterword, I want to gesture at some philosophical grounding for the premise, and a friend in my reading group pointed me toward David Lewis's modal realism after I described the project to her. I have since read enough of On the Plurality of Worlds to be dangerous, and have drafted the following paragraph for the afterword. Before I commit to it in print, I would very much value your comments on whether the philosophy actually holds together, since I would rather not publish something a philosophically literate reader would wince at.

The draft paragraph reads: "According to David Lewis, every way a world could possibly have been is a way some world actually is: the road not taken is not an absence but a place, as concrete and complete as Ohio, merely distant from us in space and time. My characters, then, do not merely possibly make the choices I leave unnarrated; somewhere, in the fullest sense, they make them. Each fork in this series is less an invention than a report, and I am less its author than its cartographer—the counterpart of Mira who stays in Odessa and the counterpart who leaves are equally her, living out equally real lives, indifferent to which one I happened to set down on the page."

I would be glad for your comments on three things in particular. First, accuracy: where is this a fair gloss of Lewis's actual position, and where does it quietly help itself to something he would have denied? I am especially unsure what his apparatus of counterparts, and his insistence that a genuine world is complete down to the last detail, would say against my claim that the counterpart "is equally her." Second, is there a rival view, something like fictionalism about possible worlds, that would fit what I am actually doing more honestly, letting me keep the rhetorical payoff without metaphysical commitments I may not be entitled to? Third, purely as a matter of craft rather than accuracy: does anchoring the afterword in this metaphysics make the branching-timeline premise more interesting to a reader, or does it undersell the more ordinary, non-metaphysical reasons one already has for caring about the road not taken?
