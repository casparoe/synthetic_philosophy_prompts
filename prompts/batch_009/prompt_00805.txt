I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I routinely tell readers that one theory has brought us closer to the truth than its predecessor, with relativity's supersession of Newtonian mechanics as the standard example. Popper tried to make that intuition rigorous with his account of verisimilitude, which ranks theories according to the balance of their true and false logical consequences. In 1974, however, Miller and Tichý independently proved that, on Popper's own definition, no false theory can ever have more verisimilitude than another false theory — a flaw so serious that Popper reportedly accepted it within minutes, and one that matters enormously, since virtually every real scientific theory is false in some respect. The repairs proposed since, from the Tichý–Oddie "average distance" measure to Niiniluoto's similarity-based approach, remain contested rather than settled. Given all this, I'd like to know the most important implications of this unresolved problem of verisimilitude for how I should responsibly describe theory change and compare rival theories for my readers, and whether "closer to the truth" language can be salvaged at all or ought to be abandoned for something else.
