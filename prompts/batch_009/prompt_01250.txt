My computer science class recently did a short unit on the history of artificial intelligence, and after we covered the Turing test I got curious and read Alan Turing's original 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," instead of just relying on the summary in our textbook and the explainer videos our teacher linked. Almost none of those secondary sources mention that, near the end of the paper, Turing devotes a whole section to the argument from extrasensory perception, in which he treats the statistical case for telepathy as essentially settled and then works through a scenario in which a human contestant, using telepathy or clairvoyance, correctly names the suit of 130 out of 400 hidden playing cards against a computer that can only guess at chance level (about 104 out of 400) — he even worries that if the machine relies on an internal random-number generator, the interrogator's psychokinesis might bias its guesses instead. To keep the imitation game fair against this threat, Turing proposes placing the contestants inside what he literally calls a "telepathy-proof room," sealed off from the interrogator.

I brought this section up with a friend from my philosophy club, Priya, and we ended up disagreeing about what, if anything, it shows about the imitation game as a whole. Priya thinks it is a harmless historical curiosity: Turing was simply being a careful experimentalist, ruling out one more possible channel of information leakage in exactly the way any well-designed test controls for confounds, and once you set aside the outdated science, the fix leaves his central proposal untouched. I think it shows something more damaging: the fact that Turing kept sealing off channel after channel — sight, sound, and now telepathy and psychokinesis — suggests that whatever the imitation game actually certifies was never "thinking" in any deep sense, only indistinguishability relative to whichever finite list of channels we currently happen to have ruled out, which seems like a much thinner and more contingent achievement, especially given that Turing wants the test to replace the question of thought rather than merely serve as evidence for it.

I would like it to end without a tidy resolution, since I honestly do not yet know which of us has the better argument.
