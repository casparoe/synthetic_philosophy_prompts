I've been working through Leibniz on my own, and one claim of his keeps nagging at me: he thought only simple, soul-like monads have genuine or "true" unity, what he calls a unum per se, while anything extended and composite, even an ordinary body, is instead a unum per aggregationem: a mere aggregate whose apparent oneness is projected onto it by a perceiving mind rather than found in nature. His own favorite illustration was a rainbow. This reminds me of a much later debate in metaphysics, van Inwagen's "special composition question," which asks exactly when some objects compose a further object. His own answer, organicism, holds that this happens only when the objects' activity constitutes a life, while other philosophers have defended nihilism (composition never happens) or universalism (it always does). Given what Leibniz says about unity, aggregates, and organisms, which of these modern answers do you think his own principles commit him to, and could he have held that position consistently?
