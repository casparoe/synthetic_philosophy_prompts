Ok so for like the last two weeks I've been down this rabbit hole of reading old philosophy essays for no reason other than I get like this sometimes (no class, no book club, nothing, just me and google at 1am). It started because I saw someone mention the "shipowner" story — the guy who talks himself into believing his rickety old ship is fine and sends it off anyway — which is from an 1800s essay called "The Ethics of Belief" by a mathematician named W.K. Clifford. That got me curious enough to actually read the whole essay, and then I read William James's famous reply to it, "The Will to Believe." I followed most of both fine even though the writing is pretty dense and old fashioned, but theres one bit of James that's been bugging me for days now and my friends can't really help me with it because neither of them has actually read Clifford closely enough to argue about it with me.

Here's the James passage. It comes right after he lays out his thing about "believe truth" and "shun error" being two separate commandments that can actually pull against each other:

"Clifford, in the instructive passage which I have quoted, exhorts us to the latter course. Believe nothing, he tells us, keep your mind in suspense forever, rather than by closing it on insufficient evidence incur the awful risk of believing lies. You, on the other hand, may think that the risk of being in error is a very small matter when compared with the blessings of real knowledge, and be ready to be duped many times in your investigation rather than postpone indefinitely the chance of guessing true. I myself find it impossible to go with Clifford. We must remember that these feelings of our duty about either truth or error are in any case only expressions of our passional life. Biologically considered, our minds are as ready to grind out falsehood as veracity, and he who says, 'Better go without belief forever than believe a lie!' merely shows his own preponderant private horror of becoming a dupe. He may be critical of many of his desires and fears, but this fear he slavishly obeys. He cannot imagine any one questioning its binding force. For my own part, I have also a horror of being duped; but I can believe that worse things than being duped may happen to a man in this world: so Clifford's exhortation has to my ears a thoroughly fantastic sound. It is like a general informing his soldiers that it is better to keep out of battle forever than to risk a single wound. Not so are victories either over enemies or over nature gained. Our errors are surely not such awfully solemn things."

And then here's a bit from earlier in Clifford's essay that's making me suspicious of what James is doing up there:

"No real belief, however trifling and fragmentary it may seem, is ever truly insignificant; it prepares us to receive more of its like, confirms those which resembled it before, and weakens others; and so gradually it lays a stealthy train in our inmost thoughts, which may someday explode into overt action, and leave its stamp upon our character for ever. And no one man's belief is in any case a private matter which concerns himself alone."

So basically Clifford's whole case, between the shipowner and this other example he gives about agitators who ruin some religious minority's reputation over an accusation they never bothered to check, is about how careless believing wrecks trust and honesty on a social level and warps your own character — it's not about one guy being personally scared of feeling silly. But when James responds to him he makes it sound like Clifford's entire position comes down to a single man's private "horror of becoming a dupe," which is then easy to laugh off with the cowardly-general line. Is James actually engaging with Clifford's real argument in that passage, or is he quietly swapping in a weaker, more personal version of it so his soldier metaphor lands better? And if he is dodging it there, does he ever circle back and actually deal with the social/character-forming side of Clifford's argument anywhere else in the essay, or does it just kind of stay unanswered? I want to actually understand the logic of what's happening in this bit of text, not just get told "well obviously you have to take some risks to learn anything," because that's the answer I keep getting from people and it doesn't feel like it's engaging with what I'm actually confused about.
