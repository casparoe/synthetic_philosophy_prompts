I write literary fiction, and I'm currently assembling a small cycle of short stories, each built around a single puzzle from decision theory, somewhat in the spirit of how Ted Chiang's fiction gives emotional weight to ideas from linguistics or physics. Over dinner recently, a friend who works in AI alignment described a scenario that decision theorists call "counterfactual mugging," meant to bring out what's distinctive about updateless decision theory, and it has stayed with me as possible material for the next story in the cycle.

The setup, stripped of jargon, is this: some entity whose judgment is, within the world of the story, effectively infallible tells the protagonist that it has already flipped a fair coin. On the outcome that actually occurred, it is now asking the protagonist to hand over something of real but bearable value, and it is honest that nothing the protagonist does from this point on will change anything else about the world they actually inhabit. It adds, credibly, that had the coin landed the other way, it would instead have given the protagonist something of large, life-altering value, but only because, before flipping anything, it had already predicted whether the protagonist was the sort of person who would pay up if the coin went against them, and would have acted purely on that prediction. The protagonist is left stuck on the losing side of a coin they cannot unflip, deciding whether to honor a bet whose winning branch will now never be more than hypothetical.

Could you write this story for me, somewhere in the range of 1,500 to 2,500 words, in close third person, set in a recognizably ordinary, present-day world? I'd rather you invent your own entity and mechanism than reach for a stock superintelligent-AI or "Omega" figure, so that the strangeness stays confined to the wager itself, and I'd like the protagonist to have concrete, specific reasons for the cost to sting and a personal history that makes whatever they decide feel earned rather than arbitrary. What matters most to me is that the story itself, through the protagonist's reasoning and conduct rather than anyone explaining decision theory out loud, lets the reader feel the real difference between a mind that judges each moment only by what it can still affect and a mind that judges itself by whether it is the kind that would have committed, beforehand, to paying; please don't let the words "updateless" or "counterfactual mugging" appear on the page. If a closing beat suggests itself in which the protagonist, or someone close to them, senses that the choice which looks foolish from inside the moment might look wise from further outside it, I'd welcome you taking it, but only if the plot earns it rather than the ending simply asserting it.
