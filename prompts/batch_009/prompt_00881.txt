I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term on the philosophy of space and time. We started with the classic material—Newton's bucket and the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence—and have now moved into the contemporary literature on the "hole argument" against spacetime substantivalism. We read Earman and Norton's 1987 paper "What Price Spacetime Substantivalism? The Hole Story," and I was struck by the fact that they explicitly model their argument on Leibniz's own shift argument against Newton's absolute space, even naming their central principle "Leibniz equivalence" after him. My professor mentioned this lineage only in passing, but it's been nagging at me ever since, and I ended up arguing about it over dinner with a friend from the physics department: what would Leibniz himself, with his actual seventeenth-century metaphysical commitments intact, make of the fully modern version of this argument if someone sat him down and explained it in full?

Concretely: suppose Leibniz were told that the diffeomorphism invariance of general relativity lets one construct two models that agree perfectly outside some "hole" region of spacetime yet diverge inside it, and that this seems to saddle any substantivalist with a radical indeterminism that no amount of boundary data could ever resolve. Would he recognize this as simply his own Principle of Sufficient Reason and identity-of-indiscernibles reasoning vindicated in more rigorous, secular dress—or would he think the analogy to his own shift argument (a single global, rigid displacement of the entire material universe, defended by appeal to God's rationality rather than to physical determinism) actually strains once you look closely at the disanalogies? And on "sophisticated substantivalism"—the move of accepting Leibniz equivalence while still insisting that spacetime points are substances that merely lack primitive identity across models—would Leibniz welcome this as a genuine middle path, or would his own principles commit him to saying that it has already conceded everything relationism ever asked for, just under a different name? I'm less interested in a quick verdict than in how you think his specific commitments would actually engage with the technical details here.
