I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and for the last couple of years I have finally been reading through the history of analytic philosophy, starting, inevitably, with Frege. Something has been nagging at me long enough that I wrote up the notes below rather than let it drop. I would value your comments, since I no longer have colleagues down the corridor to try ideas out on.

Frege is credited as the founder of modern logic and of analytic philosophy of language, and his technical achievements — the sense/reference distinction, the treatment of quantifiers, the whole apparatus of the Begriffsschrift — are ordinarily discussed as though they float entirely free of the man. In 1954, working through what survived of Frege's Nachlass, Michael Dummett came across a transcript of a diary (the original is lost; what survives was copied out by Frege's son) that Frege had kept for about a month in the spring of 1924, expressing hostility to parliamentary democracy and the view that Jews should be stripped of political rights. Dummett — an anti-racism campaigner who did as much as anyone to establish Frege as a central figure in the field — later wrote that he had revered Frege as "an absolutely rational man" and was badly shaken by what he found. The diary itself was not published until 1994, with an English translation by Richard Mendelsohn following in 1996. Introducing that translation, Mendelsohn offered the natural verdict: that the reprehensible views "shake neither the truth nor the inventiveness of his philosophical achievements," even if they make Frege harder to approach with the same sympathy.

I am not sure that is quite right, though I want to avoid the obviously bad argument that a repellent man cannot produce sound arguments. My worry is more specific to this case. Frege's signature commitment was to a strictly objective realm of thoughts, purged of anything psychological or merely subjective — a realm he explicitly called, in "Der Gedanke," a "third realm" (drittes Reich), a phrase that would acquire a very different resonance a few years later, almost certainly by coincidence. It strikes me that the same impulse behind that idea — a horror of contamination by the subjective, a demand for a purified and hierarchically ordered domain — is of a piece with, rather than incidental to, the impulse behind his politics, even though the two bodies of doctrine share no actual content. Compare a tidier case from my own field: Philipp Lenard's anti-Semitism fed directly into the content of his physics, since he rejected relativity itself as "Jewish physics" — there, the scientific error and the political motive are simply the same act of judgment, and no one thinks the physics can be assessed independently of it. Frege's case looks cleaner, since nothing in the Begriffsschrift mentions Jews or democrats. But perhaps temperament can transmit a kind of contamination that operates beneath the level of content, and if so, that ought to unsettle Mendelsohn's tidy separation somewhat.

I would welcome your comments on whether this "temperament versus content" distinction is doing any real philosophical work, or is just a more elaborate way of committing the genetic fallacy; on whether I have the history right, particularly the difference between Dummett's and Mendelsohn's reactions; and on whether Frege scholars have already made, or dismantled, an argument like this one.
