In my modal metaphysics seminar, we've been contrasting David Lewis's counterpart theory with the more traditional transworld identity view associated with Kripke and Plantinga, and I'd like to get clearer on how the two positions actually fare against Chisholm's Paradox. As I understand it, the puzzle exploits the transitivity of identity across a chain of worlds connected by small, cumulative property changes to derive the awkward conclusion that Adam and Noah could have swapped roles. The standard move on the transworld identity side seems to be insisting that Adam and Noah have non-trivial individual essences that block the slide partway through the chain, whereas Lewis can apparently just deny that his counterpart relation is transitive, since it tracks similarity rather than strict identity. Could you explain why that move is so much cheaper for the counterpart theorist than for the transworld identity theorist, and whether it's really as costless as it looks? My understanding is that Lewis's own attraction to counterpart theory wasn't chiefly driven by this particular puzzle, so I'm curious whether biting the non-transitivity bullet creates complications elsewhere in his semantics for modal claims -- for instance, for which modal inference patterns come out valid.
