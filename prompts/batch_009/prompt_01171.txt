A dispute in my condo building has sent me down a rabbit hole in the philosophy of convention, and I'd like your help thinking it through in the form of a dialogue. We're a six-unit building with eight uncovered parking spots, two of which are noticeably better than the rest (closer to the entrance, shaded in the afternoon). For as long as anyone can remember, the two ground-floor units have treated those two spots as theirs, while everyone else just takes whatever is open on a given day; nothing was ever voted on or written down, it was simply "how things were done." Last month a new owner in an upstairs unit started parking in the good spots whenever she got there first, on the grounds that there's no actual rule against it, and the argument that followed in our building's group chat made me notice that people were talking past each other along a fault line I recognized from some philosophy reading I'd been doing for unrelated reasons.

Some neighbors write as though the old arrangement were just a self-sustaining equilibrium in something like David Lewis's sense: a regularity that persisted only because everyone expected everyone else to keep it up, with nothing deeper backing it, so that the new owner hasn't wronged anyone, just stopped playing a game the rest of us are still playing. Others insist she has behaved unfairly, even though nothing was ever formally agreed. This tracks an actual disagreement in the literature that grew up around Lewis's Convention. His account treats a regularity as a convention when it solves a recurring coordination problem via common knowledge of mutual expectations, and, crucially, when people are otherwise indifferent between it and some alternative that would have worked just as well. Critics have pointed out that plenty of real arrangements don't fit this mold: when the options on offer aren't equally good for everyone, as with the shaded, close-in spots, you're closer to what game theorists call a Battle-of-the-Sexes problem, where everyone prefers coordinating to not coordinating but disagrees about which equilibrium to land on. Some theorists (Edna Ullmann-Margalit, for instance) treat cases like this as norms of partiality, a phenomenon distinct from pure coordination conventions that needs its own account of how an unequal settlement becomes stable, and of how, if ever, it can legitimately change. Others (Cristina Bicchieri, for instance) argue that what actually sustains a norm's grip, and makes violating it feel like a moral matter rather than a mere inconvenience, is mutual empirical and normative expectations plus the threat of social sanction -- an epistemic basis weaker than Lewis's common knowledge, yet one that seems to do more normative work than his picture allows for.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers of roughly equal ability: one who defends and, where necessary, extends the Lewisian coordination-equilibrium picture to cover our case, and one who argues that something closer to a partiality- or bargaining-based account is required. Have them use our building's situation as their running example, and make them genuinely engage each other's specific points rather than talk past one another -- whether indifference among alternatives really fails once some spots are better than others, whether common knowledge is doing indispensable work or could be dropped in favor of weaker mutual expectations backed by sanctions, whether the new owner has actually wronged her neighbors or merely disappointed them, and whether what she's doing could eventually count as the arrangement evolving into a new convention rather than a violation of the old one. Don't let either side win too easily. End with the two of them trying to work out whether their disagreement is substantive -- a real dispute about what explains compliance and would license changing the arrangement -- or ultimately just a verbal dispute about how to use the word "convention."
