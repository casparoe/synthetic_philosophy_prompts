So I'm working on a novel where a big second-act scene only really works if I've got a piece of philosophy of mind right, so I want to nail it down before I build a whole confrontation around it. One of my characters, Aldous, is a retired philosophy professor who's devoted decades to defending illusionism about consciousness — the Frankish/Dennett line that phenomenal experience, the raw "what it's like"-ness of things, isn't something we actually have, just something our own introspection convinces us we have. His estranged daughter Ruth finally confronts him about how cold and absent he was to her and her mother for her whole childhood, expecting him to grovel a bit, and instead he gives her this very calm, very devastating little lecture: since he's devoted his career to arguing that phenomenal pain doesn't exist, only a functional state that misrepresents itself as pain, there was never any actual suffering behind her childhood loneliness for him to be responsible for — just a brain producing the appearance of hurt, with nothing it was like to be on the receiving end. He's not denying he was distant, he's denying that "distant" ever cashed out as real harm.

Me and a couple of people from my writers' group argued about this scene for way to long over drinks last weekend. We ended up agreeing Ruth doesn't really have a comeback that isn't just "I don't care about your theory, you still hurt me," which is a fine human response but not an intellectual win, and I'd like at least one scene in this book where someone beats a genuinely bad man at his own game rather than just walking away hurt. So here's my actual question: given that illusionism denies phenomenal consciousness is real, doesn't it more or less have to treat moral harm grounded in suffering as a kind of category mistake — and if working illusionist philosophers accept that consequence, how do they keep insisting cruelty matters? Basically, I want to know if there's a real philosophical weapon available to Ruth here, something a well-read daughter of a philosopher could actually throw back at him, or if Aldous has, dishearteningly, got the logic of his own view exactly right.
