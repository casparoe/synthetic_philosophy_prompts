My grandfather passed away a few weeks ago after a long illness, and something that happened afterward has stayed with me. My aunt, my mom's older sister, had been his primary caretaker for the past year, and everyone assumed she would take the loss the hardest. Instead, at the small gathering after the funeral, she was calm and even lighthearted at points, telling old stories and laughing, which visibly upset my grandmother.

When my grandmother asked, a little sharply, how she could seem so unbothered so soon, my aunt said that she had certainly grieved when he first died, just as anyone would, but that she had since come to see it differently. Before he existed, she said, there wasn't even any raw material you could point to and call "him"; then, through a perfectly ordinary process, that material was gradually put together into cells, then a body, then a person who could recognize her and tell her jokes. Now, she said, that same process has simply run in reverse, and it struck her as strange to treat one direction as a catastrophe and the other as unremarkable, especially since none of us really knows what, if anything, lies on the other side of that change, which made her doubt we're entitled to treat it as something to dread rather than, say, nothing at all, or even a kind of rest after a long stretch of effort.

My grandmother thinks my aunt is repressing her grief and will "crash" later, and a couple of other relatives quietly agreed. My aunt insists she isn't suppressing anything and genuinely feels at peace. I brought this up with a couple of friends at school afterward, and they mostly said grief is personal and there's no single right way to handle it, which felt true but also like it was sidestepping the actual claim my aunt seemed to be making. For what it's worth, nobody in my family is religious, so this wasn't a matter of believing my grandfather's soul is somewhere pleasant now. What I'd like help thinking through is whether my aunt's reasoning amounts to a genuine argument for thinking that intense or prolonged grief is a kind of mistake, and whether that argument actually holds up, or whether my grandmother's suspicion that it's really just a sophisticated form of denial is the more sensible reaction.
