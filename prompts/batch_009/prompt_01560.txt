Okay so for my Algorithmic Fairness seminar we've been going over the whole COMPAS/ProPublica saga -- the recidivism risk-score tool that ProPublica accused of being biased against Black defendants because it flagged them as high-risk at a way higher false-positive rate than white defendants, while Northpointe (the company that built it) countered that the tool was properly calibrated, i.e. among everyone given a certain risk score, the actual reoffense rate ended up basically the same regardless of race. Our professor then walked us through this impossibility result (I think it's originally due to Kleinberg and some coauthors, plus a related paper by Chouldechova specifically using the COMPAS data) that shows calibration within groups and equal false-positive/false-negative rates across groups genuinely can't both hold when the two groups have different base rates for reoffending, unless your classifier is a perfect predictor. She went through the Bayes' rule derivation on the board and I could follow each step fine, but it hasnt given me an actual gut feeling for why unequal base rates force this tradeoff no matter how good the underlying model is. Part of me keeps thinking "surely with better features or a fancier model you could just get around this," even though apparently thats not right.

Could you give me a more intuitive, less algebra-heavy explanation of why this tradeoff is basically unavoidable once base rates diverge between two groups? And as a bonus question: my study group got into an argument afterward about whether assigning different score thresholds to different groups (like a lower "high risk" cutoff for one group than the other) actually gets you out of the impossibility, or whether it just swaps one kind of unfairness (error-rate disparity) for another (disparate treatment, since people are now literally being judged by different rules depending on group membership). Curious what you think about that too.
