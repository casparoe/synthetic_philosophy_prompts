I am putting together the closing session of a graduate seminar on philosophical logic, and I have decided to build it around Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument rather than the more familiar Anselmian original. Most of my students already know the folklore: that Gödel wrote out a formal proof of God's existence in modal logic and, under the mistaken impression that he was dying, showed it to Dana Scott, whose transcription of the argument eventually reached print via Jordan Howard Sobel. Far fewer of them have worked through why the argument, once you fix its axioms about "positive" properties, the God-like property, and necessary existence (defined through the notion of an essence), forces a modal collapse in any logic as strong as S5 — the result, first pressed by Sobel, that whatever happens to be true turns out to be necessarily true, so that no genuine contingency survives anywhere in the world.

Before I stand in front of the class, I would like your help thinking past the point where most discussions of this argument stop. Rather than another walk-through of the proof's four or five lines, please work out what you take to be the most important implications of the modal collapse result. I am especially interested in three things: (a) what it implies for the dialectical fate of Gödel's proof itself, given that C. Anthony Anderson and others have tried to emend the axioms to block the collapse without losing necessary existence, and given that a philosopher already committed to necessitarianism (the thesis, associated with Spinoza, that there is no real contingency in the world) might welcome the collapse as confirmation of their view rather than treat it as a reductio; (b) what the episode implies for necessitarianism as a live metaphysical position in its own right, quite apart from theism, and for the wisdom of treating S5 as the correct logic not just for logical necessity but for metaphysical necessity as well; and (c) whether there is a genuine philosophical connection, rather than a merely cute coincidence, between this result and Gödel's separate argument, built on his rotating-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations, that the objective passage of time is not real — I noticed one commentator, Srećko Kovač, remark on a structural parallel between the two, and I would like to know whether you think that parallel holds up under scrutiny. Please build your answer out of the logical structure of the argument itself rather than out of the generic objections you associate with ontological arguments as a genre.
