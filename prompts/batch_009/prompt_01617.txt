I'm researching a historical novel set during Napoleon's Russian campaign, and there's a philosophical disagreement I need to think through before I can write a pivotal scene with any confidence. Napoleon spent thirty-five days occupying a burned-out Moscow in the autumn of 1812, waiting for a peace offer from Alexander I that never came, and afterward told his aide Caulaincourt, "Everything turned out badly because I stayed too long at Moscow." Write a dialogue between two historians arguing over whether Napoleon was right to blame himself. One takes a broadly Tolstoyan view of history, of the kind elaborated in the epilogues of War and Peace: the self-reproach is itself the illusion Tolstoy diagnoses, since the delay was the necessary result of collapsing discipline, a city already gutted of supplies by fire, and an army whose momentum was already spent, so that no decision open to Napoleon at the time could have changed much, whatever he came to believe about it afterward. The other maintains that this was a genuine hinge point: Caulaincourt argued against wintering in Russia at the time, not merely in hindsight, which suggests an earlier departure was a live option by the standards of the day, and that treating the delay as inevitable risks becoming an unfalsifiable habit of mind that can "explain" any outcome after it happens. Have them respond directly to each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another, and don't let either side land a clean, final win.
