Since I retired after thirty-odd years in statistical mechanics, I've taken up philosophy as a kind of hobby, partly through a little discussion group of other retired science types that meets once a month at the library. Lately we've been chewing on decision theory, and I got stuck on the "money pump" argument for why rational preferences have to be transitive: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can trade you around that loop forever, skimming a bit of money off you each time you land back where you started. My gut reaction as an old thermo guy is that this is basically the preference-theory version of ruling out a perpetual motion machine -- a cycle that reliably yields free "profit" every time the system returns to its starting state feels like it should be forbidden on general principle, the same way you can't build an engine that extracts net work from a single heat reservoir. Tell me if that intuition is actually load-bearing here or if its just a coincidence that it feels that way.

Here's the part that's really thrown me, though: I found a couple of papers mentioning that a sufficiently forward-looking ("sophisticated," or maybe "resolute" -- the write-ups I found seem to use these two words a bit differently and I haven't fully sorted out the distinction) agent can see the pump coming and just refuse the last trade in the sequence, dodging the whole scheme while still having the very same intransitive preferences underneath. That seems to imply the money pump never really showed that transitivity itself was required, only that acting near-sightedly is bad. But then I gather at least one author (Rabinowicz, I think?) came up with a nastier version of the pump that supposedly still catches these forward-looking agents. So which is it -- does looking far enough ahead actually get an agent off the hook for having cyclic preferences, or not? And if that's genuinely unsettled, what's actually at stake in the disagreement? I'd love a walkthrough that goes a bit beyond the textbook version of the pump, since I feel like I've already got that part down.
