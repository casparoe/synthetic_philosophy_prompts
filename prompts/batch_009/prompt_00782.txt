Over the past couple of months I've been teaching myself formal epistemology outside of school, mostly working through Richard Jeffrey's *The Logic of Decision* after a rationality-themed blog I follow kept referencing his idea of "probability kinematics" for updating on evidence you're not fully certain of. In my AP Statistics class we spent some time on conditional probability, and as a side exercise I convinced myself that ordinary Bayesian conditionalization is order-independent: if you become fully certain of some fact A and then fully certain of some fact B, your final probabilities end up exactly the same as if you had learned B first and then A, since conditioning on A-and-B doesn't care which fact you write down first. Jeffrey's rule is supposed to generalize this to evidence that only shifts your confidence across a few possibilities without making you fully certain of any one of them (his own classic example is examining a piece of cloth by candlelight and becoming more confident, but not certain, that it's green rather than blue or violet), so I assumed the same order-independence would just carry over automatically.

To check this, I built a small toy model: a hypothesis about tomorrow's weather, plus two separate, not-quite-independent pieces of soft evidence bearing on it (something like a cloudy sky and a barometer reading). I calculated my final credence in the hypothesis by updating on the first piece of evidence and then the second, then redid the whole calculation updating on them in the reverse order. I've checked the arithmetic several times, and the two final credences are genuinely different, not just off by rounding error. Since every source I've read describes Jeffrey conditionalization as simply "the" rational way to incorporate uncertain evidence, this really bothers me: it seems to imply that two people who receive exactly the same total evidence could end up with different, equally rational beliefs purely because of the order in which the evidence happened to arrive, which doesn't seem like something a good theory of rational updating should permit. Did I make some kind of mistake in setting up my example, or is there a standard way of applying the rule that restores order-independence once you do it correctly?
