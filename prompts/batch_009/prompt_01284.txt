I am a PhD student in bioethics working on triage and resource-allocation policy, and I keep running into an unresolved tension between John Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" and the standard aggregative reply to it. I would find it useful to see the disagreement worked out as a dialogue.

Scenario: a hospital ethics committee is drafting standing policy for a recurring case. A drug is available in a quantity sufficient to save either one patient outright or five different patients, each of whom needs only a fifth of the dose; the six patients are otherwise medically and morally indistinguishable. Dr. Marsh, a committed Taurekian, argues the policy should specify a straight coin flip between the one and the five, since summing losses across separate lives is illegitimate and only an equal chance expresses equal concern and respect for each person. Dr. Iyer argues the policy should instead save the five outright, or at least run a lottery weighted five-to-one in favor of the larger group, on "ex ante" grounds: deliberating in advance, without knowing which of the six patients they would turn out to be, all six would themselves prefer a policy that gives the larger group a far better chance than a coin flip does.

Write their exchange (around eight turns). Have Dr. Marsh engage seriously with the ex ante argument, including the worry that appealing to what a not-yet-identified patient would want just reintroduces expected-value aggregation by the back door, rather than simply restating the coin-flip proposal. Have Dr. Iyer press on whether a 50/50 split can really be defended as more respectful of the six patients than a five-to-one split without some covert appeal to numbers. Neither should concede the point; let the disagreement end sharpened rather than resolved.
