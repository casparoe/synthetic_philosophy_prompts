I'm a calibration engineer at an industrial metrology lab, and a dispute with an external auditor last month has sent me down an unexpected philosophical detour. Part of our validation protocol for new pressure and temperature sensors involves running them through many repeated trials under reproducible conditions and confirming that the readings stay internally consistent over time, before we ever cross-check them against a certified reference standard. The auditor objected that this preliminary step was epistemically worthless — in her words, you can't use a device to vouch for itself, no matter how many times you ask it. Trying to figure out whether she was right, I discovered that epistemologists have a name for pretty much this exact structure: the "bootstrapping problem," or the problem of "easy knowledge," which is apparently a serious puzzle about whether you can legitimately conclude that a source — an instrument, a perceptual faculty, a witness — is reliable purely by using its own repeated outputs, with no independent check anywhere in the process.

The thing is, in my field this looks like a solved problem: we simply require what's called metrological traceability, meaning every calibration must connect, through an unbroken chain of comparisons, back to a primary reference standard, so that nothing is ever validated purely against itself. It seems like the philosophical puzzle should just dissolve once you import that one rule — always demand an independent check, and "bootstrapping" of the illegitimate kind becomes impossible by construction. Given that metrologists worked this out for practical purposes decades ago, I'm struggling to understand why philosophers still write papers treating this as a live, unresolved problem. Am I missing some part of the puzzle that a traceability requirement doesn't actually touch?
