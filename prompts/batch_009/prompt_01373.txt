I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and only in the last year or two have I sat down to read philosophy of science properly, rather than absorbing it secondhand through department lore. Like most working scientists of my generation, I had internalised a fairly tidy version of Karl Popper: a theory earns the label "scientific" just in case it forbids some observable state of affairs, and it is this bar it must clear, not any accumulation of positive evidence in its favour. Actually reading Popper, and then Lakatos, has made that tidiness come apart on me somewhat. The Duhem–Quine point that no single hypothesis is ever tested in isolation, only in conjunction with a whole raft of auxiliary assumptions about instruments, background conditions and so on, seems to imply that any apparent "refutation" can always be redirected onto one of those auxiliaries instead of onto the theory under test. That looks uncomfortably close to the very manoeuvre Popper wanted to condemn when he pointed at astrology or psychoanalysis.

What I haven't been able to reconstruct for myself is exactly how Lakatos's distinction between naive and "sophisticated" falsificationism, worked out in his methodology of scientific research programmes, is meant to put the demarcation line back in place once this difficulty is granted. The case I keep circling back to is Le Verrier's two uses of what looks like the identical manoeuvre: he rescued Newtonian mechanics from the anomalous motion of Uranus by positing an unseen planet, which led to the triumphant discovery of Neptune, and he later tried to rescue it from Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession by positing an equally unseen planet, Vulcan, which was never found and whose explanatory job general relativity eventually took over instead. At the moment each hypothesis was floated, the two moves seem structurally indistinguishable, so whatever is doing the real demarcating work cannot simply be "it invokes an unobserved auxiliary entity, therefore it is suspect."

Could you walk me through, with a reasonable amount of technical care, how the progressive-versus-degenerating problem-shift distinction, or in Lakatos's terms the difference between a content-increasing modification and one that is merely content-preserving, is supposed to separate the Neptune case from the Vulcan case in principle, rather than only in the retrospective glow of hindsight? I'd also value your own honest assessment of whether this machinery genuinely rescues a criterion scientists could apply in real time, or whether it mainly relocates the problem into a judgment call about how long one ought to keep waiting for the novel confirmed predictions to show up. That question matters to me beyond idle curiosity, since I recall physicist friends of mine trading versions of precisely this argument during the string-theory and multiverse controversies, without ever quite agreeing on which side of the demarcation line Popper or Lakatos would have placed those programmes.
