I spent nearly forty years running an analytical chemistry lab before I retired, and these days I fill a lot of my time going to a philosophy discussion group that meets at the local library, which has turned out to be far more addictive than I expected. Something came up at last week's meeting about what it really means to "know" something, and it dredged up an old memory from my reviewing days that I haven't been able to shake since. I'd like an outside take on it, since everyone in my group already knows how I feel about it.

Back in the early 90s I refereed a paper for a decent, if not top-tier, journal that reported a measurement contradicting a value several independent groups had already nailed down pretty consistently over the previous decade. I recommended rejecting it, and if I'm honest about my reasoning at the time, it amounted to this: we already know the established value is right, so whatever these people think they measured, there has to be an error in there somewhere, even if I couldn't say what it was. A former postdoc of mine, who runs her own lab now, told me recently that this is a bit of a cheat, since by that reasoning I'd reject any result that disagreed with the established number no matter how careful the authors had been, meaning the established number could never actually get overturned even if it happened to be wrong. I told her that's unfair, that's just ordinary scientific judgement, you can't chase down every anomoly that comes along or you'd never publish anything, and there's a reason we expect extraordinary evidence for extraordinary claims.

Thinking it over since, I genuinely can't tell anymore whether I was exercising good judgement back then or just being dogmatic and dressing it up in a respectible-sounding justification after the fact. Is there an actual difference between "this evidence must be flawed because I already know the answer" and legitimate skepticism toward a surprising result? Or does claiming to know something in the first place always come with a built-in license to wave away whatever disagrees with it later, no matter how strong the evidence gets? I'd be curious what you make of where my reasoning holds up and where it falls apart.
