I'm working on a piece about Karl Popper's uneasy relationship with evolutionary theory, and I'd like help thinking through one aspect of it rather than just narrating it. In the 1970s, Popper argued that the theory of natural selection was "almost tautological" and, in his words, not a testable scientific theory but a "metaphysical research programme" — essentially the same unfalsifiability charge he had already leveled at psychoanalysis and Marxist theories of history as part of his demarcation criterion. In 1977 he used the inaugural Darwin Lecture at Darwin College, Cambridge (published the following year as "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind") to issue what he explicitly called a "recantation" of his position on natural selection. He never issued anything comparable for Freud or Marx.

Compare the specific falsifiability problem Popper originally diagnosed in natural selection with the one he diagnosed in psychoanalysis and Marxism. Are these the same logical defect under two different names, or is the near-tautology of "survival of the fittest" a genuinely different kind of problem from the immunizing, after-the-fact explanatory maneuvers he attributed to Freudians and Marxists? I'm especially interested in whether his recantation on Darwinism, but not on the other two, reflects a principled distinction in the structure of the theories themselves, or simply an inconsistency in how rigorously he applied his own criterion.
