I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, mostly on problems in statistical mechanics, and like most people in that line of work I grew comfortable with a loose but serviceable distinction between "fundamental" laws and merely "phenomenological" ones: the ideal gas law, say, or the classical statement of the second law of thermodynamics, both being generalizations we expected, and eventually saw, reduced to the statistical behaviour of very large numbers of particles governed by simpler underlying laws. Since retiring I have been filling the gaps in my education by reading philosophy, and a friend who teaches in the field recently told me that the question of what actually distinguishes a genuine "law of nature" from a mere accidental regularity, or from a regularity that only holds because of some derivative arrangement of matter, is still very much contested territory in metaphysics, fought over by so-called Humeans (who hold that laws are, at bottom, nothing over and above an economical description of the pattern of events in the world) and anti-Humeans (who insist that a genuine law must consist in some further relation of necessitation or governing, over and above the pattern itself). She mentioned that one of the standard Humean proposals, developed by David Lewis, is often called the "Mill-Ramsey-Lewis" account, which sent me back to Mill's own 1843 "A System of Logic" to see what, if anything, he actually said on the matter. I found a passage there that I think bears directly on this dispute, but whose argument I cannot quite reconstruct on my own; it may simply be that I am not yet used to the syntax of mid-Victorian philosophical prose, with its long periodic sentences and buried qualifications.

The passage is from Book III, Chapter IV ("Of Laws of Nature"), §1:

"It is the custom in science, wherever regularity of any kind can be traced, to call the general proposition which expresses the nature of that regularity, a law […]. But the expression law of nature has generally been employed with a sort of tacit reference to the original sense of the word law, namely, the expression of the will of a superior. When, therefore, it appeared that any of the uniformities which were observed in nature, would result spontaneously from certain other uniformities, no separate act of creative will being supposed necessary for the production of the derivative uniformities, these have not usually been spoken of as laws of nature.

According to one mode of expression, the question, What are the laws of nature? may be stated thus: What are the fewest and simplest assumptions, which being granted, the whole existing order of nature would result? Another mode of stating it would be thus: What are the fewest general propositions from which all the uniformities which exist in the universe might be deductively inferred? […] On the other hand, we must not suffer ourselves to imagine that this mode of statement amounts to a real analysis, or to any thing but a mere verbal transformation of the problem; for the expression, Laws of Nature, means nothing but the uniformities which exist among natural phenomena (or, in other words, the results of induction), when reduced to their simplest expression."

What I cannot work out is the force of that final sentence, where Mill seems to take back much of what he has just offered. He spends the preceding lines characterizing a law of nature, in the strict sense, as whatever belongs to the fewest and simplest set of general propositions from which all the observed uniformities could be deductively derived, which sounds like a genuine, substantive proposal, and indeed sounds like an anticipation of exactly the "best system" idea that I gather Lewis later worked out in technical detail. Yet in the same breath Mill insists that this is "a mere verbal transformation of the problem" and not "a real analysis," since "Laws of Nature" just means the uniformities themselves, suitably economized. I would be grateful for help with two things. First, is Mill's hedge here an early, unwitting version of the standard modern objection to the Mill-Ramsey-Lewis account, namely that appeals to the "simplest" or "fewest" axioms are empty until one already has some principled, non-arbitrary way of restricting which properties are allowed to figure in those axioms, which I take to be why Lewis eventually found he needed the further apparatus of "perfectly natural properties"? Or is Mill pointing at some narrower difficulty specific to his own argument that has nothing to do with that later worry? Second, how should I read his opening remark, that "law" originally meant "the expression of the will of a superior"? Is that merely an etymological aside, or is Mill quietly conceding that our ordinary idea of a law of nature carries real anti-Humean, necessitarian freight that his own official account cannot, by his own admission, actually capture? I would welcome help reconstructing the argument of the passage and situating it properly with respect to the modern debate.
