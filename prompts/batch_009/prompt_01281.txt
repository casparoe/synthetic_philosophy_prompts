Since January I have been the reluctant one-person "elections committee" for the residents' association in the apartment building where I live, a role I inherited when the person who used to do it moved out and nobody else was willing to take it on. Last spring's election for association chair became a minor source of building drama: three people ran, two of whom appealed to a similar, easygoing set of neighbors and ended up splitting that vote, which allowed a third, considerably more polarizing candidate to win with only about a third of first-choice support. To avoid a repeat, I proposed switching from simple plurality to a ranked-choice ballot (instant runoff) for this year's election, and the residents agreed.

We used the new system for the first time last week, and the result has left me more unsettled than reassured. A block of about a dozen residents who are close to one of the two strongest candidates appears to have deliberately ranked the other strong candidate dead last on their ballots, below even a candidate nobody takes seriously, evidently to make sure she would be eliminated in an early round rather than survive to a final head-to-head where she might well have won. It worked exactly as they had hoped, and two of my neighbors are now barely on speaking terms over it. When I mentioned my unease to a friend from business school, she told me not to be surprised, since she recalled some theorem to the effect that no voting system can ever be made fully fair, and pointed me toward Arrow's impossibility theorem. Reading up on it, though, Arrow's theorem seems to be about whether individual rankings can be consistently combined into one overall social ranking, whereas what actually bothered me about our election was something narrower: a group of people getting a better outcome for themselves specifically by voting insincerely.

I have a background in software and optimization, so my instinct is that this should be a fixable engineering problem, but I am no longer sure that is right. Before I bring my neighbors yet another proposal (Borda count and approval voting have both been suggested at various points), I would like to actually understand what happened. Is the kind of strategic ballot I described an avoidable defect of ranked-choice voting specifically, or a symptom of something that any reasonably democratic voting rule will be exposed to in some form? And practically speaking, is there any principled basis for choosing among the imperfect options for next year, or any way to at least reduce our exposure to this kind of coordinated manipulation even if we cannot eliminate it outright?
