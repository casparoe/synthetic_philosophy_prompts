In Guide of the Perplexed III:32, Maimonides argues that God permitted animal sacrifice only as a concession to a people raised in a world where sacrificing to idols was the only religious idiom they knew — a temporary rung on a ladder meant to lead toward purer, more intellectual worship such as prayer. Yet in the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melachim 11:1), writing as legal codifier rather than philosopher, he rules as settled law that the Messiah will rebuild the Temple and restore the sacrificial service in full.

That scenario has recently moved from theology toward husbandry. Since 2022, red heifer calves imported from Texas have been raised in Israel toward the exacting biblical standard (Numbers 19) required for the purification rite that must precede any resumption of sacrifice, while priestly vestments and Temple vessels are already being prepared in anticipation.

Reasoning strictly from Maimonides' own premises, how do you think he would judge this project — as the fulfillment of his own halakhic ruling, or as a regression that his theory of religious development gives him reason to resist? Reconstruct his most defensible position, and say whether the tension between the philosopher of the Guide and the codifier of the Mishneh Torah on this question can genuinely be resolved, or is simply something we must live with.
