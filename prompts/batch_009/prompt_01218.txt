I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been trying to bring William Ramsey's "job description challenge" (from Representation Reconsidered) to bear on a case from mechanistic interpretability that I don't think philosophers of mind have picked apart carefully enough yet. Below is the core argument from a paper draft; I'd welcome comments before I develop it further.

Ramsey's challenge to any theory that posits internal representations is not to show that some internal state is caused by, or reliably co-varies with, some external condition — nearly any internal state clears that bar. He grants two further conditions almost for free (that the state be capable of a kind of original, non-derived content, and that it interact causally with other internal states), but adds a third condition that does most of the real work: the state's causal role must be governed by its content specifically, not merely co-present with it. A genuine representational job description requires that the system's causal economy be organized around what the state stands for, not around some incidental physical property it happens to carry.

Othello-GPT has become something of a stock example in discussions of realism about interpretability, since it appears to build an internal "world model" despite having no direct access to the board it's playing on. But I think the case is usually treated as a single result when it is really three results of increasing strength, and I want to argue that only the last is a serious candidate for meeting Ramsey's third condition.

(1) Li et al. (2023) trained a GPT-style model purely to predict legal Othello moves and found the full board state (each square as black, white, or empty) recoverable from its internal activations via a nonlinear probe, far above chance; they also showed that editing activations along directions found by that probe steers the model's subsequent move predictions accordingly. This is real evidence of something, but Nanda, in following up on it, has himself pointed out how easy it is to be fooled by probes: a sufficiently expressive nonlinear probe can sometimes extract information a network never actually uses, because the probe quietly does representational work of its own.

(2) Nanda et al. (2023) found that if the same board state is redescribed relative to the current player (MINE/YOURS/EMPTY, since whose move it is alternates every turn) rather than by absolute color, it becomes linearly decodable at over 99% accuracy from about layer four onward. Because the representation is linear, they could intervene simply, by adding or subtracting the relevant direction, rather than by chasing a separately trained nonlinear function's gradient — and this simple intervention had the predicted causal effect. This is much harder to dismiss as a probing artifact, and is the best existing evidence that the causal role really is keyed to the geometric structure of the posited content.

(3) But Hazineh et al. (2023), training shallower networks on the identical task, found the very same linear board-state direction decodable even from a 1-layer model — and yet that shallow network's move predictions turn out not to depend on it causally at all. Only once the network is deep enough does the direction become load-bearing. So the very same representational format, on the very same task, satisfies the job description at one depth and fails it at another.

My tentative moral is that this is not just a call for methodological caution. It suggests that "representation" may not carve a joint in these systems that is independent of wherever we happen to set a threshold on causal contribution, which pushes me toward reading talk of Othello-GPT's "world model" in something closer to Dennett's instrumentalist sense — a real pattern worth tracking — than in a fully realist sense.

I'd value pushback on three things in particular: whether I've understated how much work (2) alone does toward satisfying Ramsey's third condition, so that (3) is less damaging than I'm treating it; whether the depth-dependence in (3) is a genuine metaphysical problem for realism about representation, or just an unsurprising fact about shallow networks being bad at the task; and whether the move to Dennett at the end is doing real philosophical work or is just a graceful way of declining to answer the question. And naturally, flag anything above that oversimplifies or misdescribes the empirical results.
