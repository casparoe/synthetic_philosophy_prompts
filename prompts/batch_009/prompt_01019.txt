So I'm a phil PhD student doing a formal epistemology reading group this term with a mix of econ and phil people, and we just worked through Aumann's 1995 paper on backward induction and common knowledge of rationality. Something about it is bugging me and I feel like everyone else in the room already gets whatever I'm missing. As I read Aumann, he actually proves it: if what he calls "substantive" rationality is common knowledge among the players, then in a perfect-information game like the centipede game, every player defects/takes at the very first node. That's not a heuristic or a modeling convention, it's a theorem. But then the next week's readings were Pettit and Sugden's old "Backward Induction Paradox" paper, plus the Binmore/Aumann back-and-forth from the mid-90s (Binmore's "note," then Aumann's reply to it), and everybody is writing as though this is still a totally live, unresolved puzzle in decision theory.

That's what I don't get: once there's an actual mathematical proof that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome, isn't the philosophical "paradox" just solved at that point? Like, sure, you can still ask why real subjects in actual lab experiments cooperate for a few rounds before defecting, but that seems like a seperate empirical/psychology question, not a live conceptual one anymore. My advisor keeps telling me I'm missing "the whole point" of why people, including plenty of game theorists, still call this a paradox post-Aumann, but every time I reread the proof it looks airtight to me and I genuinely can't tell what the objection is supposed to be. Is there some actual gap in the argument I'm not seeing, or is this mostly just people being unwilling to accept a clean result because it happened to come out of economics instead of philosophy?
