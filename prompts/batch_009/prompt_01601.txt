I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I'm reporting a piece on game-theoretic proposals for getting advanced AI systems to cooperate rather than compete. The centerpiece is "program equilibrium" — Moshe Tennenholtz's 2004 result that when two players in a game submit programs rather than actions, and those programs can read one another's source code before acting, they can escape the grim logic of the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. What convinced me this was more than a neat but isolated theoretical result was the follow-up work out of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, where researchers (Eliezer Yudkowsky among them) used the logician Martin Löb's theorem to construct a program, nicknamed FairBot, that cooperates with any program it can prove will cooperate with it in return — and then showed that two copies of FairBot playing against each other can, remarkably, each prove exactly that about the other, so both cooperate. An AI safety researcher I interviewed summed up the upshot for me as "sunlight is the best disinfectant, but for game theory," and that line is shaping up to be my hook.

Here's the claim I want to build the piece around, and I'd like a sanity check before it goes to print: program equilibrium seems to show that cooperation becomes the rational, provable, self-enforcing outcome once two sufficiently sophisticated agents have full visibility into each other's source code. Doesn't that give AI safety advocates a solid, formal argument for pushing frontier labs toward mutual code inspection, rather than relying on the voluntary, unverifiable safety pledges that currently pass for cooperation between competing developers?
