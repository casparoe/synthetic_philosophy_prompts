Random side-project update: I've been building a dumb little prediction-market app for a few people on my team over the last few weekends (Next.js + Supabase, nothing fancy), where we bet fake points on yes/no stuff like "will the redesign ship by Friday" or "will Dave actually finish Elden Ring this year." I was telling my friend Priya about it over drinks — she use to do options trading before switching into software — and she said something that's been rattling around in my head ever since.

Her claim was basically this: if two people are both rational and both know the other one is rational, they can't actually agree to a bet in the first place. The second the other person is happy to take the other side of your bet, that eagerness is itself information, and once you factor it in, whatever edge you thought you had disappears. So if I offer even odds that the redesign ships Friday and a coworker eagerly takes "no," I'm supposed to think "wait, why's he so confident, does he know something about the backend I don't" and pull the offer — and by the same logic he should back out of taking it too. Taken seriously that seems to say nobody should ever place a bet or take the other side of a trade with anyone, which is clearly not how things work — people bet on sports, day-trade, buy options constantly. I pushed back with the obvious stuff (different risk tolerances, some people just enjoy gambling, etc), and she agreed those matter but insisted theres a "pure" version of the argument that still holds, and that it's an actual semi-famous result and not just her being clever after two cocktails.

So: was Priya right that two rational people shouldn't be willing to bet with each other just because the other one wants to? And if that's a real principle, what exactly has to be true about me and my coworker for it to actually kick in, versus what's different about something like an actual stock exchange or a Vegas sportsbook, where trading and betting obviously happen all the time between presumably rational people? I don't need the full math, just enough to actually get what's going on instead of the hand-wavy version I gave Priya.
