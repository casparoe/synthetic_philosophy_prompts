In "Criteria of Negro Art" (1926), Du Bois declared that "all art is propaganda and ever must be," insisting that Black art earns its value only by serving "the right of black folk to love and enjoy." Yet that same year he was openly scornful of Fire!!, the little magazine through which younger writers such as Hughes, Hurston, and Thurman claimed an artist's right to render Black life's grittier corners honestly, whatever the cost to white opinion — a liberty Du Bois considered reckless.

Du Bois died in 1963, a decade before hip-hop was born in the South Bronx. Suppose he had lived to see it become, arguably, the single most influential idiom in American popular art, a status capped by Kendrick Lamar's 2018 Pulitzer Prize. Setting aside hip-hop's explicitly protest-minded strains (Public Enemy, or "Alright" as a Black Lives Matter anthem), how would Du Bois likely have judged trap and drill — subgenres whose commercial success rests heavily on depictions of violence, misogyny, and conspicuous consumption? Would his theory of art-as-propaganda commit him to defending even these as instruments of racial advancement, or would the same aesthetic and moral criteria that made him recoil from Fire!! lead him to recoil from trap and drill as well? Argue from his actual reasoning, not just the slogan.
