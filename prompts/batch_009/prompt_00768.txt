I work as a backend engineer at a logistics company, and lately we've been rolling out more "autonomous" decision-making into our routing systems — nothing as dramatic as self-driving cars, but models that keep re-training on new data and making calls nobody explicitly programmed. A coworker sent around an old paper (early 2000s, I think) that coined the term "responsibility gap" for basically this situation: the argument is that to fairly blame someone for a bad outcome, they normally need to have both known enough and had enough control to have done otherwise, and once a system's behavior is genuinely unpredictable to the people who built and deployed it (which is sort of the whole point of training it instead of hand-coding it), nobody left in the chain clearly satisfies either condition anymore. So you can end up with real harm and no one who can be honestly blamed for it.

This turned into a whole back-and-forth with people on my team. Some think this is a genuinely new hole in our moral/legal framework — like we need new concepts, maybe even something like limited legal personhood for AI systems, to patch it. Others just shrug and say we already deal with "blameless harm" all the time via vicarious liability (employers for employees, owners for there pets or kids) and strict product liability, where nobody has to be personally at fault for someone to still owe damages, so we should just use those tools and call it a day.

Could you write me an essay that actually commits to one side instead of both-sidesing it? I want you to seriously engage with the strongest version of each argument — including the difference between a gap in who's legally *liable* versus a gap in who's morally *blameworthy*, which I gather people who actually work on this dont think are the same question — and then land on an actual verdict: is the responsibility gap for autonomous/learning systems a real, distinct philosophical problem, or does it dissolve once you bring in existing liability concepts? No "more research is needed" cop-outs, please.
