I spent thirty-five years as a statistical physicist working on phase transitions and critical phenomena, and only since retiring have I had time to read moral philosophy seriously, mostly through a small discussion group that meets fortnightly at our local library. At our last meeting we took up the well-known "torture versus dust specks" dilemma, which asks whether it is worse for a single person to be tortured horribly for fifty years, or for some almost unimaginably large number of people, given in the literature as 3^^^3 using Knuth's up-arrow notation, each to experience nothing worse than a single dust speck drifting into their eye for a moment. Everyone in the group treated this as a purely ethical puzzle about how small harms add up, but as someone who has spent a career thinking about how systems change behavior once they cross a critical threshold, I cannot shake the suspicion that the choice of hyperoperation is doing real mathematical work that the discussion is glossing over.

Ordinary exponentiation, tetration, and pentation have such radically different growth rates that it seems to me the argument's conclusion should be quite sensitive to which one is invoked: perhaps the aggregate unpleasantness of the dust specks only overtakes the torture once you climb high enough up this hierarchy of operations, so that with a "merely" enormous number like a googolplex, or even with 3^^3 instead of 3^^^3, the sum would fall short and the intuitive answer would win after all. A friend in the group, who trained as a statistician, insists I am overthinking the mathematics and that the specific number was never really the point, but she could not quite tell me why. Before our next meeting, I would very much appreciate your help in working out whether there really is a precise threshold, expressible in terms of hyperoperation order or tower height, at which the crossover occurs, and, if there is not, what exactly is doing the philosophical work in this example instead.
