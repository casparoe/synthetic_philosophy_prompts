In my philosophy elective, we covered Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles alongside the standard objection to it: Max Black's thought experiment about two exactly identical iron spheres, alone in an empty, symmetric universe, sharing every property, including the very relation each bears to the other. Separately, I've been reading about quantum entanglement, and it turns out two electrons in what's called a singlet state (combined spin zero) seem indistinguishable in an even stronger sense: neither has a well-defined individual spin of its own, and the two are related only by the fact that each has the opposite spin to the other, yet quantum theory still counts them as two electrons, not one. Leibniz died roughly two centuries before any of this physics existed. Arguing strictly from Leibniz's own philosophical commitments, rather than how philosophers have since responded to this kind of case, what do you think he would have said if confronted with it?
