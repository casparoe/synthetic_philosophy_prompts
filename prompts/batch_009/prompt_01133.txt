I sometimes bring up the following episode when I teach Ayer's verification principle, and I would like a short story built around it. Ayer spent his career arguing, on verificationist grounds, that talk of surviving one's own death is strictly meaningless. In June 1988, about a year before he actually died, he choked on a piece of smoked salmon while hospitalized for pneumonia; his heart stopped for roughly four minutes before he was resuscitated. The physician who revived him later reported that Ayer's first words on waking were that he had seen a "Divine Being" and would have to revise all his books. Yet the essay Ayer himself published that August in the Sunday Telegraph, titled "What I Saw When I Was Dead," described something stranger and far more hedged: a painfully bright red light "responsible for the government of the universe," overseen by negligent "ministers" who had left space out of joint, together with a careful argument that even a genuine experience of this kind would not show death to be survivable, since the heart's stopping does not entail the brain's. Weeks later, under pressure from fellow atheists, he partly retracted even that modest concession in a follow-up piece for The Spectator.

Write a short story that moves across these episodes — the blurted remark, the hedged essay, and the retraction — and lets Ayer's actual philosophical commitments, especially his verificationism and his sense that personal identity is bound up with bodily continuity, do real work in how he resists, or half-resists, the pull of his own experience.
