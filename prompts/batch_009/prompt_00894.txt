I'm a novelist putting together a collection of short fiction that tries to make abstract philosophical arguments felt rather than argued. For the next piece, I would like something built around the ethics of "pure time preference." In 1928 the economist Frank Ramsey observed that discounting future well-being simply because it lies in the future is "a practice which is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination"; decades later, Derek Parfit sharpened the point by arguing that remoteness in time has, in itself, no more significance than remoteness in space, so that neglecting someone because they have not yet been born is, structurally, the same failure as neglecting someone because they live on the far side of the world.

Please write a short story of roughly 800 to 1,200 words in which a single point-of-view character, within one continuous scene, is confronted with both kinds of remote person — one made distant by space, one made distant by time — and treats them with an inconsistency they have had no previous occasion to notice. Do not name Ramsey, Parfit, discount rates, or climate change anywhere in the story, and do not allow the character or the narration to state the moral outright; whatever crack opens in the character's imagination should be visible only in what they notice, choose, or fail to do.
