I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or decision theory, but over the past year I've gotten fairly absorbed in AI safety discussions in my spare time, mostly through blog posts and forum threads. One argument I keep running into, in various guises, is the claim that any sufficiently capable, "rational" agent will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer with a fixed goal, because any agent whose preferences can't be represented that way is exploitable — you can construct a sequence of trades it will accept one at a time that nonetheless leaves it strictly worse off overall (the classic "money pump"). The conclusion people draw from this is that increasingly capable AI systems should be expected to become more strongly goal-directed over time, with all the attendant worries about instrumental convergence, regardless of what their actual objective turns out to be.

The more I've read around this, the less sure I am what the argument actually establishes. There's a line of criticism holding that the whole thing is nearly vacuous: if the utility function is allowed to range over entire histories, or to depend on facts about the agent's own internal computation, then essentially any policy can be redescribed after the fact as "maximizing utility," so coherence stops implying anything like a simple or stable goal. But then I've also seen the argument pushed in the opposite direction: apparently you can run a money pump against an agent merely for having gaps in its preferences, not only for cyclic ones, which is used to argue that even completeness is rationally forced. And there's a further wrinkle I can't quite get my head around, where agents that deliberately keep their preferences incomplete specifically to dodge this seem to get "trammelled" back into acting as if they were complete whenever they're actually confronted with the trades — which looks like it undoes the whole escape route.

What I'd like is a dialogue between two people who are equally sharp on this material but land on opposite sides: call them a coherence optimist, who thinks these arguments really do support the conclusion that advanced AI will converge on fixed-goal expected-utility-maximizing behavior, and a coherence skeptic, who thinks this conclusion doesn't actually follow without smuggling in substantive further assumptions. I'd like them to engage directly with each other's strongest points — the money-pump arguments for transitivity and completeness, the vacuity/rationalization worry, and whether incomplete preferences offer a real way out or just collapse back into completeness under pressure — rather than talking past each other or scoring an easy win. It doesn't need to end in agreement, but neither side should come across as a strawman — both positions should feel like ones a genuinely thoughtful person could hold.
