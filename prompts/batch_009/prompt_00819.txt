In my philosophy of history seminar this term, we've been working through the classic disagreement between Carl Hempel's covering-law model of historical explanation, from "The Function of General Laws in History," and William Dray's narrativist critique in Laws and Explanation in History. I want to see how well the disagreement survives contact with a case neither of them ever discussed. Last year, in a course on the ancient Near East, I read about the Late Bronze Age collapse: within a few decades around 1200 BCE, the Hittite Empire disintegrated, the Mycenaean palaces were burned and never rebuilt, and Ugarit was destroyed so completely that its last king, Ammurapi, is best known to us today through a series of increasingly desperate letters explaining that his army and ships were away in Hittite territory, leaving his own coast undefended against raiders arriving by sea. Egypt, meanwhile, faced many of the same pressures — drought, strained trade networks, the same seaborne raiders — and fought them off under Ramesses III, entering a long decline rather than disappearing outright.

Could you write a dialogue between two contemporary philosophers of history, one a committed covering-law theorist and the other an equally committed narrativist, arguing specifically about how to explain this collapse? I'd like the covering-law theorist to try to articulate something like a genuine law — along the lines of the "systems collapse" idea some archaeologists favor, on which sufficiently complex, interdependent palace economies fail once combined stresses cross some threshold of resilience — and to insist that without such a generalization, all historians have is a chronicle rather than an explanation. I'd like the narrativist to press specifically on the Ugarit/Egypt asymmetry: if a single general law is doing the explanatory work, why did two societies exposed to overlapping stresses meet such different fates, and doesn't patching the law with further conditions just turn it into a redescription of each particular case rather than an independent generalization?

Please have the two of them respond directly to each other's use of that asymmetry rather than simply restating textbook positions — I'm especially curious whether the covering-law theorist can save the idea of a genuine law here without making it unfalsifiable, and whether the narrativist's alternative (something like what Dray and Walsh call "colligation") can do real explanatory work without quietly smuggling in generalizations of its own. It doesn't need to end in resolution; I'd mainly like to see the strongest version of each side's case under the specific pressure the other is putting on it.
