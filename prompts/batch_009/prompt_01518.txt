Since retiring from a career in evolutionary biology, I have been using some of my newfound free time to work through the philosophical literature on evolution and ethics, prompted mainly by a small philosophy discussion group I joined last year at the local university. Our most recent meeting was devoted to Sharon Street's 2006 paper "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value," which sent me back to Darwin's own Descent of Man with rather fresh eyes. As I understand Street's argument, a realist about value owes us an account of the relationship between the evolutionary forces that shaped our evaluative attitudes and the independent evaluative truths that realism posits, and neither of the available answers—that there is no such relationship, or that natural selection tracked those truths because true evaluative beliefs happened to be fitness-enhancing—leaves realism looking especially secure.

What struck me on re-reading Chapter Four of the Descent is how close Darwin came to this same thought some 135 years earlier. His discussion of how, had our species been reared under the social arrangements of hive-bees, unmarried women would regard it as a sacred duty to kill their brothers and mothers would try to kill their fertile daughters, with no one thinking to object, seems to grant exactly the kind of contingency on which Street's dilemma depends: the content of our moral sense tracks the accidents of a lineage's social evolution rather than, apparently, any independent moral order. And yet Darwin draws no skeptical or anti-realist moral from this at all. If anything, he goes on to treat the greatest-happiness principle as a legitimate standard of conduct, one that social instinct, sympathy, and reason gradually converge upon as a species' intellectual powers develop.

I would very much like your best reconstruction of how Darwin himself—not a present-day Darwinian, but Darwin as he actually argued in his own texts—would have responded to Street's dilemma, had it somehow been put to him in his own study. Would he simply have had to concede the anti-realist horn, could his remarks on the expanding circle of sympathy and the greatest-happiness standard supply the makings of a reply Street does not consider, or is there some third option that neither my reading group nor I have managed to think of? I would rather you engage closely with what Darwin actually committed himself to in the text than offer a general account of how evolutionary debunking arguments are usually supposed to work.
