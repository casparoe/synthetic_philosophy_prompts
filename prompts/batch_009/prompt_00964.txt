I'm a junior working on my IB Extended Essay in philosophy, and I've hit a wall trying to unpack a short fragment from Pascal's Pensées (no. 298 in the standard Brunschvicg numbering, from the section "Justice and the Reason of Effects," here in W. F. Trotter's translation):

"Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

I follow each sentence on its own, but I can't tell how the passage holds together as a single argument. The first paragraph treats "make what is just strong" and "make what is strong just" as two equally available strategies for uniting justice and might. But the second paragraph explains what actually happened by saying might "gainsaid" justice and simply declared itself to be justice — which sounds less like a strategy anyone chose and more like force staging a hostile takeover of justice's name. Yet Pascal still calls the result something "we have made," as if it were a considered settlement rather than an imposition. Am I missing a way these two descriptions are compatible, or is there a genuine tension there? And however that gets resolved, does Pascal seem to be endorsing this substitution of might for justice as the most reasonable arrangement available to creatures like us, or is he exposing it as a kind of fraud we've simply gotten used to?
