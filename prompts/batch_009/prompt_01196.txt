I spent my working life as an experimental physicist (mostly statistical mechanics, if that matters), and now that I'm retired I've been reading my way through decision theory -- it scratches the same itch thermodynamics used to. Last week I was arguing about "money pump" arguments with an old colleague over coffee, and we couldn't agree how seriously to take them. You know the setup: if you prefer apple to banana, banana to cherry, and cherry to apple, a clever trader can walk you round the cycle, and each time round you'll happily loose a coin to trade up -- forever, since your preferences never stop cycling back on themselves. Apparently the same trick works, with a bit more cleverness, even when your preferences are merely incomplete rather than outright cyclic. Its presented in everything I've read as basically airtight, almost like a conservation law: an agent with intransitive or incomplete preferences is a perpetual-motion machine for extracting cash, so rationality has to rule it out. I even bought the little Cambridge Element on the topic, but it's slow going for a first-timer.

Then I ran into "resolute choice" and "sophisticated choice" -- strategies where the agent supposedly dodges the pump while keeping the offending preferences fully intact, either by committing to a plan up front and refusing to be tempted off it later, or by looking ahead and simply declining to step onto the merry-go-round in the first place. That smells exactly like the kind of loophole you get in physics thought experiments, where Maxwell's demon looks like it's beating the second law right up until you notice it has to pay an entropy cost somewhere else to do its sorting. So now I genuinely can't tell whether money-pump arguments really nail down transitivity and completeness as requirements on your preferences themselves (call that the "synchronic" reading), or whether all they actually show is a "diachronic" requirement -- that you need some consistent strategy for handling yourself over time, cyclic or incomplete preferences and all.

Could you write me an essay that lays the argument out properly (the incompleteness version too, not just the classic cyclic-preference one), gives resolute choice and sophisticated choice a fair hearing as responses, and then actually settles the question instead of shrugging at the end? A hedged verdict along the lines of "it depends on X" is fine, so long as you tell me what X is and argue for it.
