I'm a graduate student in philosophy working on formal epistemology, so Newcomb's problem and the twin prisoner's dilemma have long been stock examples in my own teaching, used to illustrate where causal decision theory seems to go wrong. Until a few weeks ago, though, I had never seriously engaged with Roko's basilisk, the argument circulating in rationalist circles that a sufficiently powerful future AI would have an acausal incentive to punish anyone who understood the case for its existence but didn't help bring it about sooner. A housemate who works in AI safety brought it up half as a joke, and I've since found that working through it properly is more unsettling than I expected, because it doesn't reduce to a crude scare story once you take seriously the very premises I use in class.

As I understand it, the argument leans on exactly the idea that is supposed to justify one-boxing on Newcomb's problem, or cooperating with an identical copy of yourself in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma: that two agents who can accurately model one another can have a genuine, timeless reason to act on a correlation they have no causal control over, rather than only on what they can actually influence. Roko's basilisk applies that same idea to a future AI that wants to exist as soon as possible, and so, on the argument, has a timeless reason to precommit to punishing anyone who grasped this reasoning but didn't act to hasten its creation, since predictably being the kind of agent who would do this is exactly what gets it built faster.

What I can't work out is whether the decision theory that makes cooperation rational in the twin case gives any principled way to treat this threat differently, or whether accepting the first argument commits me, on pain of inconsistency, to taking the second at least somewhat seriously. My housemate's response is essentially to stop thinking about it, but that feels like a psychological trick rather than a rebuttal, given that the entire premise of the argument is that a sufficiently good predictor isn't fooled by willful ignorance. Is there an actual asymmetry between acausal trades and acausal threats that this style of decision theory is itself committed to, or am I just looking for an excuse not to follow a valid argument where it leads?
