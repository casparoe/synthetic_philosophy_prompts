While researching a piece on the history of technological risk, I've found myself returning again and again to a strange, under-discussed essay: John von Neumann's "Can We Survive Technology?," published in Fortune magazine in 1955, while he was dying of cancer and serving on the Atomic Energy Commission. His argument, roughly, is that industrial civilization had always relied on an expanding supply of geographical and political room to absorb the disruptions caused by new technology, and that by the mid-1950s — with nuclear weapons, and with a section on then-nascent climate and weather control that speculates industrial carbon emissions might already be measurably warming the atmosphere — the earth had simply become too small and too politically fragmented for that mechanism to keep working.

What strikes me most is where he lands. Von Neumann explicitly rules out banning dangerous technologies as a solution, on the grounds that useful and harmful applications are too entangled to separate cleanly, and that any authority capable of enforcing such a ban worldwide would already have had to solve the very problem of global political coordination it is supposed to fix. He is similarly doubtful that war can be reliably abolished as an instrument of policy. What he offers instead is almost anticlimactic: safety, to whatever degree it is attainable, will come only from "day-to-day — or perhaps year-to-year — opportunistic measures, a long sequence of small, correct decisions," underwritten by nothing sturdier than individual judgment, adaptability, and patience.

I'd like to test that argument against a live disagreement rather than just summarize it, and I suspect a dialogue would serve the material better than another essay would. Could you write one between two people who have both just read the von Neumann piece and disagree sharply about whether its conclusion still holds for a technology like advanced AI? One should defend something close to von Neumann's own position: that no clean global fix is actually on offer, that demanding one is itself a kind of category error, since it presupposes the very coordination capacity that is the unsolved problem to begin with, and that the only lever really available to us is cultivating institutions and people capable of fast, competent, continual course-correction. The other should argue that this reasoning, however sound it may have been for the dangers von Neumann had in mind, breaks down for risks that are genuinely unrecoverable, where a single wrong decision forecloses every decision that would have followed it, and that this difference justifies attempting binding, precautionary coordination now, at real cost, in a way von Neumann would probably have dismissed as naive. You're welcome to invent whatever setting and personas feel natural — a seminar room, a conference hallway, a long email thread — so long as the substance of the disagreement stays central. Please keep both characters equally sharp, and have them actually respond to each other's strongest points as the exchange develops rather than delivering parallel monologues; I'd especially like to see at least one moment where each side is forced to reckon honestly with a genuine weakness in its own position. It doesn't need to end in resolution or agreement — I'm more interested in the quality of the exchange than in a tidy conclusion.
