I'm preparing a graduate seminar on philosophy of language and have been rereading Davidson's 'A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs,' where he extends his radical-interpretation framework to argue that even communication between fluent speakers of 'the same language' has, at bottom, the structure of radical interpretation. His key evidence is that a hearer can effortlessly grasp a speaker's meaning despite a malapropism—understanding 'a nice derangement of epitaphs' as meaning a nice arrangement of epithets, though no convention licenses that usage—and from this he concludes that, strictly speaking, there is no such thing as a shared language. I can follow the individual moves, including the distinction between prior and passing theories, but I keep losing my grip on why this phenomenon is supposed to force so radical a conclusion, rather than merely showing that our grasp of a shared code tolerates some on-the-fly repair. Could you help me feel the actual argumentative pressure here: on Davidson's view, why can't a sufficiently flexible conventionalist simply absorb malapropism and novel metaphor as tolerable noise within an existing shared language, rather than treat their intelligibility as evidence that no such language exists at all?
