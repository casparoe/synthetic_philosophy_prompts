For an independent project in my philosophy elective, I've moved on from the Republic to Plato's Laws, and I keep getting stuck on what looks like a real inconsistency between the two. In the Republic, letting appetite overpower reason, as happens in drunkenness, is treated as the mark of the most disordered, tyrannical soul. Yet in the Laws, the Athenian Stranger has the ideal city legally organize supervised drinking parties, arguing that wine strips away a person's usual self-command and so offers a cheap, safe test of their underlying character. He even claims that repeated, carefully managed exposure to it can train the very self-mastery (sōphrosynē) it seems to threaten, and lays out a legislated schedule: no wine at all before eighteen, moderate wine from eighteen to thirty, and full license to drink deeply in Dionysus's honor only after forty. Write an essay that weighs whether this defense of state-regulated drunkenness can be squared with Plato's much harsher treatment of pleasure and appetite elsewhere in his psychology, or whether it amounts to a genuine departure from it. Don't just lay out both sides — reach a specific verdict on whether the Athenian's argument for the pedagogical value of controlled drunkenness actually succeeds, and defend that verdict.
