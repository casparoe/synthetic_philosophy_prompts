My friend and I have a running disagreement about free will and determinism that resurfaces every few months, usually over dinner, and this time it went further down the rabbit hole than usual. It started with him insisting that deliberation is a kind of illusion if the future is already fixed, and it ended with us pulling up Aristotle's original discussion of the sea battle in Chapter 9 of *On Interpretation*, because he claims Aristotle already defused the whole puzzle with a fairly modest logical point, while I think Aristotle is committed to something considerably stranger. We went back and forth on the actual text quite late into the night without resolving it, so I'm hoping you can help me see more clearly what is really being claimed.

Here is the passage, in the E. M. Edghill translation, that we kept circling back to:

"Now that which is must needs be when it is, and that which is not must needs not be when it is not. Yet it cannot be said without qualification that all existence and non-existence is the outcome of necessity. For there is a difference between saying that that which is, when it is, must needs be, and simply saying that all that is must needs be, and similarly in the case of that which is not. In the case, also, of two contradictory propositions this holds good. Everything must either be or not be, whether in the present or in the future, but it is not always possible to distinguish and state determinately which of these alternatives must necessarily come about.

Let me illustrate. A sea-fight must either take place to-morrow or not, but it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, neither is it necessary that it should not take place, yet it is necessary that it either should or should not take place to-morrow. Since propositions correspond with facts, it is evident that when in future events there is a real alternative, and a potentiality in contrary directions, the corresponding affirmation and denial have the same character.

This is the case with regard to that which is not always existent or not always nonexistent. One of the two propositions in such instances must be true and the other false, but we cannot say determinately that this or that is false, but must leave the alternative undecided. One may indeed be more likely to be true than the other, but it cannot be either actually true or actually false. It is therefore plain that it is not necessary that of an affirmation and a denial one should be true and the other false."

My friend's reading is that this amounts to the scholastic distinction between the necessity of the consequence and the necessity of the consequent, applied first to conditionals and then to disjunctions: Aristotle is only pointing out that "necessarily (p or not-p)" does not license "(necessarily p) or (necessarily not-p)," and once that fallacy is blocked, bivalence is left untouched — the statement "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is simply true or false right now, we just cannot know which, and no fatalist conclusion follows. I read the third paragraph as claiming something stronger: that the statement is not yet true or false at all, only "undecided," with its truth-value settling only as the day plays out — which sounds like an actual rejection of bivalence for future contingents rather than just a warning against a modal fallacy. Could you help us work out which of these readings the passage genuinely supports, or whether Aristotle's own text is torn between them? I'd also be curious whether the stronger reading commits him to something like a three-valued logic or a genuinely open future in a way the weaker one doesn't, and, relatedly, whether he's best read as an ancestor of modern open-future views or as someone who merely wanted to block a bad argument for fatalism while leaving classical bivalence intact.
