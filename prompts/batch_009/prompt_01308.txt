At work I have been asked to design a blind conversational evaluation—pitting our company's chatbot against human volunteers in text-only exchanges—to see how reliably a panel of judges can tell which participant is which. Since this is essentially a modern version of Turing's imitation game, I went back to his 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" for guidance, reading it in full for the first time rather than in excerpted form. I was startled by objection (9), "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception," which Turing does not treat as a joke: he calls the statistical evidence for telepathy "overwhelming," worries that a telepathic or clairvoyant human contestant could out-guess a machine at a hidden-card task (he imagines a human scoring 130 out of 400 against a machine's chance-level 104) and thereby give the game away to the interrogator, and even considers the interrogator's own psychokinetic powers interfering with a machine's random-number generator. His proposed fix is to house the contestants in a "telepathy-proof room."

I cannot decide whether this is a harmless period curiosity that can simply be footnoted and set aside, or whether it exposes a real structural weakness in any purely behavioral test of a mental capacity.
