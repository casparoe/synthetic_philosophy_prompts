Okay so my friend Dev and I have been down this whole AI safety rabbit hole lately (started with a Rob Miles video, then we went and read some stuff on LessWrong, probably in over our heads lol). We keep getting stuck on this "money pump" argument. From what I understand, the idea is that if an agent's preferences are intransitive or incomplete — like it prefers A over B and B over C but then also picks C over A, or it just can't rank some options against each other — then someone could in theory set up a cycle of trades where the agent keeps paying a small fee to "trade up," and it ends up back where it started but poorer, forever. And apparently this is supposed to prove that any sufficiently rational/smart agent basically has to end up with fully consistent, complete preferences (i.e., acting like its maximizing some expected utility function), since otherwise its just leaving free money on the table for anyone willing to exploit it.

If that's actually true though, doesn't it mean the whole AI alignment problem is kind of less urgent than people make it sound? Like if getting money-pumped is simply what happens to agents with messy, inconsistent goals, wouldn't any advanced enough AI just automatically notice this during training and iron its own preferences out into something fully coherent, without anyone needing to specifically engineer for that? Dev thinks this is basically THE reason coherent utility-maximization is inevitable for any sufficiently smart AI, and that it kinda makes the "what if the AI wants something weird" worry less of a big deal. I guess what I don't get is: if the argument is this airtight, why do alignment researchers still spend so much time worrying about AIs having "bad" or inconsistent goals instead of just letting the money-pump thing sort itself out? Also, does the argument even work the same way for incomplete preferences as it does for intransitive ones, or is that a different can of worms?
