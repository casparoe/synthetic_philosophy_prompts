A friend and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about distributive justice, and neither of us walked away persuaded. We're both taking a seminar on the subject this term, which is probably why the argument got more technical than our usual disagreements. It started as a friendly dispute over Sen's old question, "equality of what?", and ended well past midnight with nothing resolved.

She defended Dworkin's equality of resources: give everyone an equal start using his combination of a hypothetical auction and a hypothetical insurance market, hold people responsible for what they make of that start (his "ambition-sensitive" clause), but not for the brute luck of talent or handicap (his "endowment-insensitive" clause). She leaned hard on his standard objections to equality of welfare — that it would have to shower extra resources on anyone who deliberately cultivates expensive tastes, and that it perversely implies compensating a naturally cheerful Tiny Tim less than a miserable Scrooge. I pushed back with Sen's point that resources are only ever instrumentally valuable, and that people convert the same bundle of resources into actual functioning at very different rates depending on personal, social, and environmental "conversion factors" — so that equalizing resources can still leave someone with a disability, or unusual medical needs, capability-poor in exactly the way equality of resources was supposed to prevent. She thinks capability-talk just smuggles in an unacknowledged, paternalistic judgment about which functionings are worth having. A third friend made things messier by raising Cohen's "equality of access to advantage" as a supposed compromise, which mostly just gave the three of us a new front to disagree about.

I'd like a more rigorous version of this argument than the one we actually had. Please write an essay addressing the question "equality of what?", treating welfare, resources, and capabilities as the three main candidate answers, and taking seriously the objections raised above — expensive tastes and interpersonal comparability against welfare, conversion factors and disability cases against resourcism, indeterminacy and paternalism against capability views. Then reach and defend an actual verdict about which metric, or which principled combination of them, egalitarians should adopt. A qualified conclusion is fine (something like "resourcism is basically right but needs a capability-based floor for cases of disability" would count) — but don't let it end as an unresolved three-way tie.
