Over the past few weeks I've been advising a small team building autonomous negotiation agents for multi-round supply-chain deals, and a pilot run went badly: rather than letting the surplus grow by extending the negotiation, one agent grabbed everything on the table at the very first opportunity, mirroring the textbook backward-induction solution to the centipede game. Wanting to know whether that outcome was really forced by rationality alone, I went back to the primary literature and found that Aumann (1995) and Stalnaker (1996) prove formally opposite conclusions about whether common knowledge of rationality entails the backward-induction outcome — a conflict that Halpern's 2001 paper "Substantive Rationality and Backward Induction" traces to differing assumptions about what a player would counterfactually do upon reaching a vertex that rationality was itself supposed to rule out. I can follow each proof well enough on its own terms, but I'm struggling to get an intuitive grip on what is actually at stake between them, beyond the bare observation that they formalize "rationality" differently. Could you explain, in plain terms, why an out-of-equilibrium move creates this kind of genuine interpretive fork rather than a mere technical quibble, how Aumann's and Stalnaker's resulting pictures of rational play actually differ, and whether either side gives good reason to expect real or artificial agents to cooperate longer than naive backward induction predicts?
