I'm taking an upper-level seminar this semester on Hobbes's natural and civil philosophy, and this week's reading pulled us out of Leviathan and into some of his more technical writings on mathematics and method, including the Epistle Dedicatory to Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematiques (1656) — his polemical response to John Wallis and Seth Ward, the Savilian professors at Oxford who had challenged his work in mathematics and natural philosophy. I brought the following passage up in office hours, but my professor only had time to mention "maker's knowledge" as a label before rushing off to a meeting, and none of the secondary readings on our syllabus actually gloss it. I was hoping you could help me reconstruct the argument properly:

"Of arts, some are demonstrable, others indemonstrable; and demonstrable are those the construction of the subject whereof is in the power of the artist himself, who, in his demonstration, does no more but deduce the consequences of his own operation. The reason whereof is this, that the science of every subject is derived from a precognition of the causes, generation, and construction of the same; and consequently where the causes are known, there is place for demonstration, but not where the causes are to seek for. Geometry therefore is demonstrable, for the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves; and civil philosophy is demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves. But because of natural bodies we know not the construction, but seek it from the effects, there lies no demonstration of what the causes be we seek for, but only of what they may be." (English Works, vol. VII, p. 184)

What I'd like is less a paraphrase than an actual reconstruction of the reasoning. First, what precisely is doing the epistemic work here: is Hobbes saying that geometrical certainty comes from the fact that lines and figures are our own constructions, as opposed to, say, the self-evidence of axioms or the validity of the inferences drawn from them? Second, how is this supposed to license treating civil philosophy as equally "demonstrable" — a commonwealth seems like a far messier, more historically contingent object than a triangle, so what exactly is being preserved when Hobbes says "we make" it in the relevant sense? And finally, given the closing sentence about natural bodies, does the passage commit Hobbes to a genuinely different and weaker notion of scientific knowledge in physics than in geometry and politics, or is this just the standard early modern worry about underdetermination dressed up in his own vocabulary? I'd also appreciate it if you could flag any point where this seems to sit in tension with his broader materialism, since that's the piece I find hardest to square.
