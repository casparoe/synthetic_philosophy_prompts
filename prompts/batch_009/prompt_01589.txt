A friend and I spent most of a long car ride arguing about the extended mind thesis after she said, only half-joking, that her phone's notes app is "part of her mind" in exactly the way her hippocampus is. I had just been rereading Clark and Chalmers for a seminar, so I brought up the Otto and Inga case, and she turned it against me: by the parity principle, she argued, there is no principled basis for treating Otto's notebook as any less constitutive of his beliefs than Inga's biological memory is of hers. I countered with the Adams and Aizawa line that this trades on a coupling-constitution fallacy — that being causally coupled to a process, however reliably, is not the same as that process being partly constitutive of cognition — and that notebooks (or apps) carry only derived content, parasitic on the intentions of whoever wrote or coded them, in a way biological memory is not. She had a surprisingly good reply about why that distinction might not do the work Adams and Aizawa want it to once you look closely at how the "trust and glue" conditions on coupling are supposed to function, and from there it just kept going, exit by exit, without either of us budging.

I'd like you to write a dialogue that does more justice to both sides than we managed. Set it between two philosophers of mind who strike up the argument after a talk at a conference: one defending the extended mind thesis in the Clark and Chalmers tradition, the other objecting in the spirit of Adams and Aizawa, or, if you would rather, Rupert's more empirically-minded "embedded cognition" alternative. Rather than replaying Otto's notebook itself, have them fight it out over a present-day case of your choosing — a smartphone, a navigation app, an AI assistant used as an external memory, or whatever you think works best.

Whatever case you pick, hold both characters to the actual criteria that made the original debate substantive rather than letting them drift into generalities: the parity principle and the "trust and glue" conditions on coupling, the coupling-constitution fallacy objection, and the question of derived versus non-derived content. Neither side should be a pushover — each should land objections that force the other to genuinely refine their position rather than simply restate it — and while they do not need to end up agreeing, the exchange should make it clear exactly where, and why, their views part ways.
