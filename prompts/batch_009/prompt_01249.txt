I'm writing a dissertation chapter on Frege's theory of concepts, and a detail from the reception of his 1924 political diary has been nagging at me. In an entry from April 1924, while arguing for legal measures against Jews, Frege writes that "one must be able to specify a distinguishing mark by which one can recognize a Jew for certain." In a footnote to this line, the diary's translator, Mendelsohn, points out that Frege had already discussed, in the Grundlagen, what it takes for a definition to correctly supply a concept's "distinguishing mark" (Kennzeichen) — essentially the completeness requirement his own account of number famously runs afoul of in the Julius Caesar problem. That doesn't strike me as coincidental: if the vocabulary Frege reaches for while trying to solve the "Jewish question" is drawn from his own technical apparatus for fixing a concept's boundary, doesn't that suggest his logical demand that concepts be sharply and completely defined was itself an expression of the same exclusionary impulse visible in his politics, rather than a neutral logical requirement? I'd appreciate help thinking through how strong this connection really is, and whether it should change how I treat the completeness requirement in my chapter.
