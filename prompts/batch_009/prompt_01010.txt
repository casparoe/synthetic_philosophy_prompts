I am a novelist, and for the past several months I have been reading in analytic metaphysics as background for a book whose central character is a modal logician. In the course of that reading I worked through David Lewis's On the Plurality of Worlds, and what began as research for the character's dialogue has turned into something more personally consuming, because Lewis's modal realism seems to hand back to me, in respectable philosophical dress, something I have always half-believed about my own craft. Like many novelists who compose by discovery rather than by outline, I have long described drafting as an act of reporting on people and places that already exist somewhere, fully formed, rather than inventing them from nothing: characters routinely do things I had not planned and would not have chosen, as though I were the first witness to a world I do not control. Lewis's claim that the actual and the merely possible do not differ in kind, and that every way a world could possibly be is a way some concrete world already is, seems to cash out that old writerly superstition quite literally. On this view, the setting and cast of my novel would simply be one more world among the plurality, occupying its own region of logical space whether or not I ever set a word to paper, which would make my drafting closer to observation than to creation.

If that is right, it leaves me with a question of authorial ethics I have not been able to settle with my writing group. If the people in my novel are exactly as real, concretely and non-fictively, as my neighbours, only located elsewhere in logical space rather than elsewhere in this one, does it follow that I bear something like genuine moral responsibility for what I put them through on the page, comparable to a journalist's responsibility when reporting on real suffering rather than staging it? And does it also follow that there is, strictly speaking, no such thing as an invented character or a merely possible plot, only varying degrees of ignorance about people who were real all along? I would value your help thinking through whether this chain of reasoning actually follows from Lewis's position, and if it does not, exactly where it breaks down.
