Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is often presented as a decision-theoretic vindication of utilitarianism: if individuals and a social planner all satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and the planner's ranking of lotteries respects the Pareto principle, then social utility must be an affine (weighted-sum) function of individual utilities. Please argue that this reading is unwarranted, because the Pareto principle here is purely ex ante, so that a planner satisfying it can be indifferent between (a) running a fair lottery to decide which of two equally deserving claimants receives a single indivisible good, and (b) simply awarding that good to one of them outright, whenever the two options yield the same expected utilities. Explain why this indifference is a genuine problem for treating the theorem as grounding utilitarian ethics, rather than a mere technicality that can be explained away.
