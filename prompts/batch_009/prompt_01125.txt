I'm taking a philosophy elective at school this semester, and we just spent a week on the ethics of promising. Part of the reading was the chapter from T. M. Scanlon's What We Owe to Each Other where he lays out "Principle F," his account of exactly when it's wrong to break a promise. What struck me was how many conditions have to be satisfied before the obligation even kicks in: it's not enough that I intend to do something and thereby lead you to expect it. I also have to know that you want to be assured I'll do it and act in order to give you that assurance; you then have to come to know that I have all of these intentions; I, in turn, have to intend for you to know this and know that you do; and finally, you have to know that I know that you know. My friend, who is taking economics and had just covered common-knowledge puzzles in a game-theory unit, said the whole structure reminded her of cases where two parties can never quite be sure they both know that the other knows, no matter how many confirmations pass between them, and joked that Scanlon had smuggled a logic puzzle into the middle of ethics.

That comment stuck with me, because it seems easy to describe perfectly ordinary promises where this layered "knowing-that-you-know" chain plausibly never gets fully established, and yet our judgment that the promisor is bound doesn't waver at all. Take a promise extracted from a relative on their deathbed, who hears "I promise" and loses consciousness moments later, long before there could be any of the back-and-forth needed to confirm that they registered the promisor's own awareness of their understanding. Or take a promise made to a very young child, or to someone drifting off to sleep, who plainly takes in that a promise has been made without registering anything about whether the promisor knows that they understood. Principle F seems committed to saying the obligation depends on all of that mutual awareness genuinely being in place, and yet nobody thinks the dying relative's inability to complete the chain lets the promisor off the hook.

Could you help me build the strongest version of an argument that cases like these show Principle F cannot be the correct general, necessary condition on promissory obligation, and that we should prefer an account closer to Hume's, on which the obligation attaches to performing a recognized act within a public convention rather than to successfully producing specific mental states in a particular promisee? I'd also like you to work out the most serious way a defender of Scanlon could try to repair Principle F to handle these cases, and tell me whether you think the repair actually succeeds.
