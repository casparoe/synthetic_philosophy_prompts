I am plotting a campus novel centered on two philosophers, once close collaborators and now estranged: Ilse, who has built her career defending Quine, and Teo, who has built his attacking him. Seated beside one another at a retirement dinner for the colleague who first introduced them decades ago, they fall back into the old argument. I would like your help drafting that scene: a page or two of genuine dialogue.

Both have taught the gavagai scenario to generations of undergraduates, so neither needs to rehearse it for the other's benefit—let the exchange begin past that point. Ilse should press the mature Quinean line: that the argument still worth defending was never the rabbit anecdote, which Quine himself came to play down in favor of the inscrutability of reference secured by his proxy-function argument in "Ontological Relativity" and "Pursuit of Truth"—the claim that there is no fact of the matter about reference even once the truth value of every whole sentence is held fixed, a claim that is supposed to stand independently of the more sweeping holophrastic indeterminacy thesis. Teo should counter with something like Chomsky's objection, sharpened by the fact that Quine himself eventually downgraded that sweeping thesis to an unproven "conjecture": if even Quine could never produce two genuinely rival manuals for an actual language, why think the proxy-function maneuver shows anything more than a harmless relabeling—formally elegant, but no more unsettling than a change of coordinate systems—unless Ilse can specify what evidence would ever count against it.

Have them respond to each other's actual moves rather than talk past one another, each conceding at least one real point before pushing back further. I want two brilliant people who cannot quite let a decades-old argument go, not two encyclopedia entries taking turns.
