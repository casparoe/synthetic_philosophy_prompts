hey, so I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD (my actual work is in philosophy of mathematics, mostly formal systems stuff), and instead of revising my dissertation chapter this week like I was supposed to, I fell down a total rabbit hole. Everyone in my field knows Gödel for the incompleteness theorems, but apparently in 1961 his mother Marianne, back in Vienna, asked him straight out in a letter whether he believed they'd see each other again after death, and instead of deflecting he wrote back four long, careful letters actually laying out an argument for why he believed in an afterlife. None of this was ever published, it only survives in the correspondence, but a couple of scholars have pieced the argument together from the letters and I read a good summary of it and now I can't let it go.

Roughly it runs like this: the world is rationally organized, not chaotic or arbitrary -- you can see this in the sheer regularity and order science keeps finding everywhere, which Gödel treats as basically the same principle of sufficient reason that underwrites all of science (everything has a reason for being exactly as it is and not some other way). But if the world really is rational in that deep sense, it can't also be true that a creature with as much room to grow as a human being -- intellectually, morally, relationally -- routinely gets switched off after developing maybe a tiny sliver of that potential, which is obviously what happens to all of us. He compares it to somebody pouring the foundation for a whole house and then abandoning the site and letting it rot: exactly the kind of pointless waste a genuinely rational world shouldn't contain. So he concludes reason itself requires a further existence where each of us actually gets to finish becoming what we had it in us to be. He's very insistent to his mother that this isn't a matter of religious faith for him -- he says he reached it "through reasoning alone" -- and he even ties its plausibility to his seperate conviction that materialism is false, which he thought his own incompleteness results made harder to maintain.

What I can't decide is whether there's an actual argument in here, or whether Gödel -- a guy whose entire reputation rests on being maniacally rigorous -- just backed into a comforting conclusion using premises that sound scientific but are doing way more work than they've earned. Could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this? Lay out the argument in its strongest form, hit it with the best objections you can, and then don't wimp out at the end -- tell me straight whether this gives a rational person any real reason to update toward believing in an afterlife, or whether it just doesn't work, and why.
