A few months ago I picked up a secondhand copy of a short book called On Immigration and Refugees, written in 2001 by the philosopher Michael Dummett. I hadn't heard of him before — I gather his main reputation was built on quite technical work in logic and the philosophy of language — but what drew me in was learning that he had also spent much of his life as a grassroots anti-racism campaigner: sitting on the executive committee of a group that fought Britain's Commonwealth immigration restrictions through the 1960s, and later co-founding what is now called the Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants.

What has really stuck with me, though, is one distinction he was careful to draw. He believed that almost every actual immigration restriction passed in Britain during his lifetime was, at bottom, motivated by racism and therefore unjust. And yet he still carved out a single narrow exception: he argued that a country does have a genuine right not to be "submerged" by immigration, provided the idea is kept entirely separate from anything to do with racial or cultural purity. He immediately added, though, that given the actual population numbers involved, this right had almost no real-world application to the Britain he was writing about.

Dummett died in 2011, so he never got to see the 2015 refugee movements into Europe, the Brexit fight over freedom of movement, or how openly some parties and commentators now talk about demographic change in language that sounds, to my ear, uncomfortably close to his own "submersion" idea, minus the careful caveats. I brought this up with a couple of friends recently and we argued about it for a good hour without settling anything. So I would genuinely like your view: based on how Dummett thought and argued more generally, do you think he would have seen these later developments as vindicating his caution, or would he have been alarmed that his own exception ended up handing a respectable-sounding peg to arguments far less careful than his own? And underneath that — does his way of separating a legitimate worry about numbers from a racist one actually hold up as a general test, or was it only ever plausible in the specific, comparatively low-stakes moment in British history when he happened to be writing?
