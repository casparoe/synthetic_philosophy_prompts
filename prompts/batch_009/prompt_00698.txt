I teach a graduate seminar on patristic ethics, and this term we spent several sessions on Augustine's De Mendacio (On Lying), a text he later reaffirmed even more forcefully in Contra Mendacium (Against Lying). What struck my students most was how uncompromising his position turns out to be once you follow the argument closely: he defines lying not in terms of the falsity of what is said but in terms of the gap between what is in one's heart and what one asserts, together with the intention to deceive, and he ultimately defends an absolute prohibition that admits no exceptions for good motives. He works out a taxonomy of eight kinds of lies ranked by gravity, from lies told in defense of religious doctrine (the worst) down to lies told merely to protect someone's bodily chastity (the least grave) — yet insists that every one of them remains sin, so that a good intention can mitigate guilt but never eliminate it. Tellingly, De Mendacio explicitly stages, and then rejects, an interlocutor's argument for telling what is called a "virtuous and merciful lie" to a gravely sick person whose weak condition might be worsened by a painful truth: even there, Augustine refuses the exception.

I mentioned this to a colleague who teaches medical ethics, and she pointed out how directly it bears on a thoroughly modern practice in dementia care that Augustine plainly never had in view: what specialists now call "therapeutic lying," a practice related to the broader "validation therapy" approach to communicating with dementia patients. A now-standard example: a woman with advanced dementia repeatedly asks after her husband, who in fact died years before; rather than tell her the truth and have her relive the grief as though for the first time, possibly several times in a single day, caregivers are now widely advised to tell her that he is "at work" or "will visit later." This is not treated as an occasional, regrettable fib but as a defensible, even recommended, standing policy — endorsed by a number of dementia-care organizations and specialists on the grounds that it measurably reduces patient distress compared with insistent honesty.

Working strictly from what you take to be Augustine's own actual commitments, rather than from generic "lying is always wrong" intuitions, I'd like your help thinking through how he would rule on this. Where would he place the practice within his eightfold hierarchy, and do you think he would really insist that it remains sinful regardless of how routinely and confidently it is now recommended by people whose profession is the wellbeing of dementia patients? Does his own framework offer caregivers any way out — for instance, his distinction between an outright false assertion and merely permitting someone to remain in a false belief through silence, redirection, or non-committal speech — that would let them secure the same benefit without, by his own strict definition, lying at all? And finally, does anything in his deeper rationale for condemning lying — roughly, that truthful speech between rational minds reflects something of the divine image in us — give him any special purchase, for better or worse, on cases where the person being addressed may no longer be able to receive or evaluate speech as true or false in the first place?
