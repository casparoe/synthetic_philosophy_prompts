Over the past few months I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole into economics, set off by a popular book on behavioral economics that mentioned, almost in passing, that mainstream economic theory doesn't define what a person wants or prefers by asking them, or by anything happening inside their head, but purely by what they end up choosing when faced with a set of options. Apparently this is called revealed preference, and the idea is that it makes economics more rigorous by tying it to observable behavior instead of unverifiable claims about inner mental states. The more I turned this over, though, the more it started to look like a trick rather than a discovery: if preferring X to Y just means choosing X over Y, then the claim that people are utility maximizers seems to come out true automatically, whatever they do, since any choice at all can be redescribed after the fact as revealing what they must have wanted. That looks less like a scientific theory to me and more like a definition wearing a lab coat.

When I mentioned this to a friend of mine who works as an actuary, she pushed back a little. She said economists aren't quite that sloppy, because a person's choices are required to hang together consistently across different situations — apparently there's a whole apparatus of axioms and statistical tests built around this — and real people fail these consistency checks fairly often, with the Allais paradox being one famous example. That took some of the wind out of my first objection, but it left me with a second worry: even granting that a consistent chooser can be described as maximizing some utility function, I don't see what licenses anyone — an economist, a company, or a government designing some nudge policy — to treat that function as a measure of what's actually good for the person, given that people routinely make choices they later regret, or that look more like confusion, temptation, or clever framing by somebody else than like considered judgments about their own well-being.

What I'd like from you is an essay, not a balanced survey, that actually stakes out and defends a position on this. Is the utility that shows up in revealed-preference economics a meaningful, testable concept; an empty tautology dressed up in mathematics; or something in between, say testable as a description of behavior but unfit to do the further job of telling us what's good for someone? Please work through the strongest considerations you can find on more than one side, including the awkward possibility that setting aside some choices as "mistakes" that don't count toward a person's real preferences quietly reintroduces exactly the kind of psychological, non-choice-based judgment that revealed preference theory was invented to avoid in the first place. Then commit to a clear verdict and defend it, rather than leaving things at "reasonable people disagree."
