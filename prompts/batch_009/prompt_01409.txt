So I've been down this rabbit hole lately about the history of probability theory (got into it through prediction markets stuff, of all things), and I found this old beef between Keynes and Frank Ramsey that I can't stop thinking about. Apparently Keynes — yeah, that Keynes, the economics guy — also wrote this whole book on probability earlier in his career, where his big claim was that probabilities aren't always comparable to each other - like there's cases where you literally can't say whether event A is more or less likely than event B, or put a clean number on either one, and thats just a fact about the situation, not us being lazy or not having enough data. Then a few years later this young math prodigy named Ramsey basically dunked on him, arguing that a rational person's beliefs have to boil down to one precise number per proposition, because he could show that if your beliefs come in fuzzy ranges instead, a clever bookie can set up a series of bets against you that you're guaranteed to loose money on overall, no matter what happens. And apparently that argument (the "Dutch book" thing, I guess that's the technical name for it) is a big part of why economics and statistics went all-in on "you must assign one precise probability to everything" for basically the rest of the century.

Except - and this is the part that's messing with my head a bit - I found out there's a whole modern field called "imprecise probability" (people like Peter Walley keep coming up) that basically brings Keynes's original idea back from the dead, using ranges or whole sets of probabilities instead of single numbers, and it's apparently a real serious research area now, showing up in robust statistics and even some AI safety stuff about handling deep uncertainty. Which makes it sound like maybe Ramsey didn't actually settle the argument forever, he just won for a few decades.

So here's my actual question: if you resurrected Ramsey today and showed him all this - Ellsberg's paradox, imprecise probability theory, AI systems that reason over whole sets of distributions instead of one clean number - do you think he'd have a real comeback to defend precise probabilities again, or did the imprecise-probability people actually find a genuine hole in his Dutch book logic, one he wouldn't have been able to patch even with his own tools? Basically, was he actually right and everyone chasing "imprecise" probabilities now is fooling themselves a bit, or was the whole "your beliefs must reduce to one exact number" thing kind of a trick of the framing all along?
