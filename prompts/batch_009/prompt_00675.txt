I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, of all things) who's recently started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local library, mostly because I finally have the time and my grandkids think it's hilarious that grandpa's reading dead Romans now. This month we did Cicero's De Senectute, which I hadn't even herd of before - I'm working through an old Loeb copy with the Latin on the facing page that I can't parse at all, so I'm leaning entirely on the English half.

What's been rattling around in my head since is the long, almost rapturous passage where Cato goes on about pruning and grafting his vines and how much joy he gets watching the seasons turn on his little farm. It's beautifully written, and it seems to be offered as Cicero's answer to the charge that old age robs you of pleasure - basically, go be a farmer and you'll be fine. Which has left me second-guessing how I've actually been spending my retirement: puttering around with old papers and now Spinoza in the mornings instead of getting my hands dirty in the garden like my wife keeps suggesting I should. So here's my question - is tending vines and vegetables really the core of what Cicero (via Cato) thinks old people ought to be doing with themselves, or am I loosing the forest for the trees and missing the actual shape of his argument? Curious what you think before I go tell my wife she was right all along.
