For my EPQ I'm investigating philosophers who ended up having a real influence on public policy outside their main academic specialism, and my supervisor pointed me toward Sir Michael Dummett as a case study. Until I started reading around him, I only knew Dummett as the Oxford logician famous for his work on Frege and intuitionism, so it was strange to learn that he spent much of his life as a grassroots activist too — he co-founded the Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants back in 1967 and was eventually knighted "for services to philosophy and to racial justice" — and that he distilled his views into a short book, On Immigration and Refugees (2001). The first half of that book, titled "Principles," argues that there should be a strong presumption in favour of free entry which only substantial reasons can override, that Britain's postwar immigration law has been driven largely by racism, and, more surprisingly to me, that a country does have a narrow "right not to be submerged" by immigration — a claim he develops as a direct, if heavily qualified, response to Margaret Thatcher's 1978 remark about Britons feeling "swamped" by newcomers.

Dummett died in December 2011, so he never had to reconcile any of this with two things that happened afterwards: the ending of free movement for EU citizens once the Brexit transition period expired, and the UK government's 2022 scheme to fly asylum seekers to Rwanda rather than process their claims at home. Working only from the principles he actually defends in the book — including what he says about rich states' obligations to poorer ones and to refugees under the Geneva Convention — could you reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Dummett would most plausibly have said about each of these two policies? I'm less interested in a quick verdict than in the reasoning behind it, and I'd genuinely like you to flag any point where his own principles seem to pull in different directions or simply underdetermine the answer. A few solid paragraphs on each policy would be more useful to me than a single-line answer either way.
