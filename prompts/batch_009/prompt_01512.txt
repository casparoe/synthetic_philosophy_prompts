This semester I'm taking a seminar on formal/Bayesian epistemology, and we've just spent two sessions on Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle: the idea that my current credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that my credence in it tomorrow will be r, must itself equal r, on pain of being exploitable by a diachronic Dutch book. What's been nagging at me since our last meeting is the move that has to be made once someone raises a Ulysses-and-the-Sirens-style case: suppose I know that tomorrow, after some manipulation, sleep deprivation, or a few drinks, my credence in some proposition will shift for reasons that have nothing to do with evidence. Reflection seems to demand that I defer to that future credence now, which is clearly wrong, so the principle apparently has to be restricted to future opinions I can regard as the product of clear thinking and, if anything, greater knowledge than I currently have. Van Fraassen's own response is that this isn't a patch bolted on after the fact: he treats it as more like a promise I can make to myself now and reasonably expect to keep, while knowing I might still be derailed by circumstances beyond my control — which ties Reflection to his broader idea that stating a credence is a kind of commitment rather than a mere self-description. A classmate of mine, echoing the kind of objection David Christensen pressed, wasn't satisfied: the worry was that once you've sorted your possible future opinions into the trustworthy ones and the corrupted ones, "Reflection" just tells you to go with the ones you already decided to trust, so the formal-looking principle adds nothing beyond that prior judgment.

I'd like to see this argued out properly instead of just gestured at. Could you write me a dialogue between two formal epistemologists — one defending Reflection (soundness restriction included) as a genuine, independently motivated coherence norm on a par with ordinary probabilism, and one who thinks the restriction turns it into a substantive policy commitment that no longer does the job a coherence constraint is supposed to do? I'd like the critic to also press the disanalogy between this diachronic Dutch book and the ordinary synchronic one behind probabilism, since nothing stops an agent from simply declining, in advance, to bet on facts about her own future credences. Please have the defender give real, specific replies rather than just restating the principle, have each side engage with the other's actual moves, and don't let either one concede by the end — this doesn't strike me as a settled question even in the literature.
