I'm a novelist assembling a linked collection of short stories about characters who sabotage their own best-laid plans, and I've been stuck for weeks on a distinction a friend who teaches decision theory raised over dinner, one I still haven't managed to work into fiction to my satisfaction. The older, more familiar picture of weakness of will, running from Aristotle's discussion of incontinence down through Donald Davidson's essay on how such a thing is even possible, treats it as a puzzle: how can a person act against what they themselves judge, all things considered, to be the better course? Behavioral economics offers a less puzzling but, to me, more disquieting answer. George Ainslie's work on hyperbolic discounting suggests that we don't discount future rewards at a steady rate, so it is entirely coherent to prefer a larger reward far off over a smaller one nearby, right up until the smaller reward drifts close enough in time that the preference flips on its own, with no new information and nothing anyone would ordinarily call a change of heart. That makes the "weak-willed" person look less like someone failing to live up to their own judgment and more like a sequence of short-lived selves, each one perfectly rational within its own narrow window, negotiating uneasily with the others. I keep suspecting a story could make that distinction felt in a way no essay of mine has managed.

Would you write one entry for the collection along these lines, something in the 1,000-to-1,800-word range? I'd like the protagonist to be someone who can already see the reversal coming and so rigs up a real precommitment device against it early on — a wager, a nonrefundable deposit, a promise extracted from someone else — in the spirit of Ulysses having his own men lash him to the mast rather than trusting himself to resist the sirens unaided. Rather than narrating the reversal only once, I would like two structurally matched scenes: one in which the character weighs the same two options from far off, when patience costs nothing, and a later one in which the near option has closed to arm's length and the earlier preference simply gives way. I want the shape of that discounting curve to be something the reader feels twice rather than something explained once, and I would like the ending to stay genuinely unresolved as to whether what happens reads as a moral failure, a defeat of a better self by a worse one, or nothing of the kind — just the same person's real preference reasserting itself, exactly as consistently as before. Please don't have anyone reach for words like "akrasia" or "discounting" themselves; all of it should live in gesture, timing, and private self-justification.
