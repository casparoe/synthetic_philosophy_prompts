I'm a site reliability engineer, and a recent blameless postmortem has left me stuck on a philosophical itch. An alert fired for twenty minutes before anyone paged our on-call engineer, who had muted a related alert two hours earlier and never reopened the dashboard to recheck it; the postmortem named that lapse as the root cause of the outage. An engineer on a neighboring team had seen the same ambiguous latency numbers and hadn't looked either, yet nobody named him a cause of anything — even though, by our own paging history, engineers check dashboards outside their assigned service about as rarely as on-call engineers recheck alerts they've already muted. The difference wasn't that one omission was more unusual than the other; it was that only one of them owned that service.

This is apparently an old problem for theories of causation: mere counterfactual dependence can't explain why we don't treat everyone who could have prevented a harm as equally a cause of it, and the usual fix — restricting causes to the "abnormal" omissions — seems to split into two repairs that needn't agree: one about statistical rarity, and one about who had a duty or role-based expectation to act.

Please argue for the position that only the normative repair can actually do the job: that no non-arbitrary way exists to single out the cause among several equally counterfactually relevant omissions without invoking duties or reasonable expectations that are irreducible to statistical rarity or to any other normatively neutral fact about structure or frequency — so that causation by omission turns out to be an inherently normative phenomenon rather than the normatively neutral relation it is often assumed to be.
