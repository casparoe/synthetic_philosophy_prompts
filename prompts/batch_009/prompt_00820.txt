I'm a novelist drafting a book centered on two soldiers posted to identical sentry duty along a perimeter wire. Trained and briefed identically, each fires in panic at a shadow moving in the dark during his own watch. One shadow turns out to be an infiltrator; the other, a local boy who wandered too close. Their state of mind, information, and reflexes at the instant of firing are, by design, indistinguishable — only the outcome differs, for reasons neither man could have foreseen or controlled.

Before I decide how the novel's other characters should judge these two men, I want to settle the question for myself. Write an essay that weighs the case for treating this as genuine "resultant" moral luck — that our practices of blame really are, and perhaps must be, sensitive to outcomes agents don't control, even though this violates the intuitively compelling Control Principle and, on Nagel's own telling, threatens to spread until nothing an agent does or is can be credited to them at all — against the skeptical case that any felt difference in guilt here is a non-moral reaction being mistaken for a genuine difference in moral desert. Don't hedge: reach and defend a verdict on whether the second soldier is truly more culpable than the first.
