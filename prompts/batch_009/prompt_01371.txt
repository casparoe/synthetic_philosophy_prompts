For a political philosophy seminar this term, we've spent the last few weeks working closely through Hannah Arendt's two major essays on political deception, and I want to use my term paper to press on a specific interpretive puzzle. In "Truth and Politics," Arendt treats the traditional lie of the statesman as a fairly contained affair: it conceals some particular fact, typically from an adversary, while the liar retains a clear grip on the distinction between truth and falsehood. In "Lying in Politics," written in response to the Pentagon Papers, she describes organized "image-making" by the war's "problem-solvers," aimed at producing what she calls "defactualization"—a wholesale substitution of coherent fabrication for fact, pursued on a scale where even the fabricators can lose their own grip on what is true. Arendt clearly treats this as more than an especially large or successful version of the traditional lie—as a different phenomenon in kind—but I'm not convinced her text fully earns that distinction. Write an essay addressing this directly: is organized political lying of the sort she diagnoses in the Pentagon Papers different in kind from the traditional statesman's lie, or is the difference ultimately just one of scale and technique? Take the strongest considerations on both sides seriously, but don't end on the fence—commit to a verdict and defend it.
