This term I'm taking a seminar on the foundations of decision theory, and a few weeks ago our instructor told us about the 1952 conference in Paris at which Maurice Allais first put his now-famous choice problems directly to Leonard Savage. I found the story fascinating: Savage, one of the chief architects of subjective expected utility theory, apparently gave exactly the combination of answers that violates independence — or, as he himself would have put it, the sure-thing principle — and only afterward decided, on reflection, that it was his own preferences that had gone wrong rather than the axiom. Allais, naturally, drew the opposite lesson, and spent much of the rest of his career insisting that it was the axiom, not the ordinary chooser, that was out of step with rationality.

A few of us in my study group spent an entire evening arguing about who had the better of that disagreement without reaching any real resolution, so I'd like to put the question to you in a more productive form. Could you write a dialogue in which Allais and Savage, rather than parting ways after that brief hallway exchange, sit down that same evening to work through the disagreement properly, each trying to talk the other out of his position? I want Savage to make the real, considered case for why a general axiom like independence deserves more trust than any particular first-order preference, including one's own; and I want Allais to give his actual objection — not merely the observation that people don't choose the way the axiom demands, but his substantive complaint that elevating the axiom to a criterion of rationality begs the question, together with his own idea that rationality is better assessed at the level of a person's whole, reflectively endorsed dispositions than at the level of a single isolated choice. Please have them genuinely respond to each other's reasoning rather than restate their opening positions — I don't want either side to come off as the easy strawman — and let the dialogue end with the disagreement pinned down as precisely as possible, even if neither of them budges.
