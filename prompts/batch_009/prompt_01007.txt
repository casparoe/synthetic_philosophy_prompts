I don't have any background in political philosophy, but a few months ago the title of a slim NYRB Classics book stopped me cold in a used bookshop: Simone Weil's "On the Abolition of All Political Parties." I later learned it wasn't meant as an abstract thought experiment at all -- she wrote it as an actual policy memo on how liberated France should be governed once the war ended, which somehow makes the argument land harder for me. What struck me most is how precise she is: she claims every party, without exception, shares three features -- it is an engine for manufacturing collective passion, it leans on its own members until they stop exercising independent judgment and simply fall in line, and its one real, built-in objective is to keep growing without any natural limit. Put those three together, she argues, and every party turns out to be totalitarian in spirit, whether or not it ever gets the chance to act on it, even inside a perfectly functioning democracy -- which is why her conclusion isn't campaign finance reform or better civics education, it's abolishing parties outright. One detail I keep returning to is her aside that the parties of England and America, in her own day, still kept something of "an element of sport" about them, a friendly rivalry between roughly equal sides, while continental parties of the Jacobin type were never content being just one team among several and always aimed at total power.

Weil died in 1943, decades before cable news, targeted online political advertising, or a newsfeed algorithm that has learned exactly which outrage keeps a person scrolling. That is the part I cannot stop thinking about: so much of what she calls "collective passion" today has nothing to do with dues-paying members or party rallies -- it is generated and amplified by algorithms and anonymous online pile-ons that pressure people into picking a side without their ever joining any formal organization at all, and the old, comparatively harmless sporting rivalry she associated with Anglo-Saxon parties looks, in America at least, a lot closer these days to the all-or-nothing continental kind she feared most. So here is what I would honestly like your reasoned opinion on, working strictly from the grounds she actually gives in the essay rather than from general assumptions about her politics: would Weil regard today's algorithm-driven tribalism as simply her old diagnosis wearing a new disguise, or would she insist it is a genuinely different problem, since it lacks the deliberate organization and stated doctrine that define a "party" in her sense? And if she would count it as the same disease, does her proposed cure of outright abolition even make sense against something as diffuse and leaderless as a newsfeed, or would she have had to invent an entirely different remedy?
