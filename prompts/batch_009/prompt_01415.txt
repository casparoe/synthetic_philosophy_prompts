Hilbert put the continuum hypothesis right at the top of his famous 1900 problem list, and he was basically the poster child for math optimism back then -- the "no ignorabimus" stuff, "wir mussen wissen, wir werden wissen," every well-posed problem has a definite answer (he even explicitly counts proving something impossible as a legit solution, not just a dodge). Except CH turned out to be independent of ZFC -- Godel showed it can't be disproven in 1940, then Cohen showed it can't be proven either in 1963, so it just floats free of the standard axioms no matter which way you go. Hilbert died in '43, so he only caught the first half of that story. Curious what you think he'd make of the full picture: does "provably neither true nor false from the axioms" count as the kind of resolution he had in mind, or would that read as a real defeat for his whole outlook? And would he side with people today who go hunting for new axioms to settle it, or would his formalism just make him shrug and say the question doesn't mean much apart from whatever axioms you pick?
