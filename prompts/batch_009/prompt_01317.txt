I'm a software engineer (mostly backend and infrastructure work) without any formal training in logic or philosophy, but I've been reading about Gödel's incompleteness theorems on and off for a couple of years, since they come up tangentially in things I care about professionally, like type systems and formal verification. Recently I finally worked through the Lucas-Penrose argument properly, having only ever seen it mentioned in passing before: the claim that for any consistent formal system F strong enough to express arithmetic, there is a Gödel sentence that F cannot prove, but that a human reasoner standing outside the system can nonetheless recognize as true — and therefore the human mind cannot itself be a Turing machine or algorithm, however sophisticated it is. What struck me about this, compared to most arguments about the nature of mind, is that it isn't just philosophical intuition-mongering; it's built directly on top of a rigorously proven theorem, which made the conclusion feel much harder to dismiss than the usual armchair speculation about consciousness.

This became more than an idle curiosity last week when a few of us at work argued about whether large language models could, even in principle, ever reach genuine human-level mathematical reasoning, as opposed to just getting better at pattern-matching with more data and compute. I ended up arguing that the Lucas-Penrose result settles this question: since any given LLM is, underneath, some formalizable computational system, there will always be some true statement — its own Gödel sentence — that it can never derive, even though a human mathematician could verify that statement's truth by reasoning about the system from the outside. That seems to imply a hard ceiling on machine reasoning that no amount of scaling or architectural cleverness could remove, short of the model somehow no longer being a well-defined formal system, which seems like a strange thing for a running computer program to stop being.

Given that this argument rests on an actual proven theorem rather than speculation, I'm confused about why so many serious AI researchers and labs still talk about reaching human-level or superhuman general reasoning purely as a matter of scaling and better architectures, as though it were an open engineering problem, rather than something Gödel already ruled out decades ago. Is the field collectively overlooking a well-established mathematical result, or is there a gap in the reasoning here that I'm not seeing?
