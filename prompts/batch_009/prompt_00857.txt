A few of us at work run an informal lunchtime reading group, and we're currently making our way through Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*, which is a departure from the technical papers we usually pass around. Given that most of my day job involves building recommendation and ranking systems that have to reason about uncertain outcomes, I got interested in trying to formalize the "veil of ignorance" as an actual decision problem. As I understand the setup, you don't know which position in society you will end up occupying, so you are supposed to reason as though you had an equal chance of landing in any of them, and then pick whichever set of principles performs best in expectation across those equally likely positions. That is exactly the kind of expected-value calculation I would reach for if I had to choose a policy under a known uniform distribution over outcomes, and it is apparently more or less the route Harsanyi took with his own version of the original position to argue for average utilitarianism.

What I haven't been able to square away is why Rawls, working from what looks like the same basic setup, insists instead that the parties would reason via maximin and end up defending the difference principle over Harsanyi's expected-utility approach. If the veil of ignorance already commits you to treating every social position as equally likely, then maximizing expected value seems like the mathematically obvious move, and maximin looks like it only makes sense for someone who is either irrationally risk-averse or who does not actually trust the equiprobability assumption in the first place. I vaguely recall Rawls invoking something called the "strains of commitment" in this context, but I could not tell whether that concept is doing any real argumentative work or is just a redescription of risk aversion under a fancier name. Is there a genuine, principled argument for why maximin, rather than expected-utility reasoning over a uniform distribution, is the rational response to the original position, or is Rawls essentially building his preferred conclusion into the setup and then dressing it up as a decision-theoretic argument?
