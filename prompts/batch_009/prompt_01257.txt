Jeremy Bentham's unpublished essay "Offences Against One's Self" (written around 1785, but suppressed by him during his lifetime and only substantially published in the twentieth century) argues that the criminal punishment of homosexual acts in Britain rested on nothing but unreasoned antipathy rather than any harm traceable to the principle of utility. Around the same period he was developing the broader argument, later formalized in the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, that both "asceticism" and "sympathy and antipathy" are spurious rivals to utility as a standard of right and wrong, and that any pleasure, however socially disreputable, must be weighed in the same coin as any other, along the dimensions of intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent that make up his felicific calculus.
