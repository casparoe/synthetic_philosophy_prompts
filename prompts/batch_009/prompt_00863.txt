Okay so I've fallen down a bit of an AI interpretability rabbit hole lately (I'm not a researcher or anything, just a hobbyist who codes and reads alot about this stuff online). There's this argument going on between two camps that I can't fully wrap my head around, and I keep going back and forth on who's right. One side is pretty confident that when researchers crack open a language model and find some direction in its internal activations that lines up with a concept, that actually means the model has that concept represented inside it — like a real ingredient of its cognition, not just some correlation somebody happened to notice. The examples that got me hooked on this were that "Golden Gate Bridge" feature Anthropic found and cranked all the way up in Claude a while back, and that "refusal direction" paper where flipping one single direction on or off turns a model's refusals on or off almost like a light switch (funnily enough, even the researchers who found that direction admitted they weren't totally sure what it actually "means").

The other camp is way more skeptical about all this. They point out that if you run these exact same feature-finding techniques (sparse autoencoders and stuff like that) on a network that's totally random and was never trained on anything at all, you still pull out features that look just as clean and "interpretable" as the real ones. To them, that suggests the technique might just be imposing structure that looks meaningful rather than discovering something that was actually already in there. I tried explaining this to my roommate and he just shrugged and said "who cares, it works the same either way," which kind of bugged me because it feels like it should matter, somehow.

Both camps seem to have a real point and I don't know how to referee it. Is this basically the same old fight scientists have about whether an electron "really exists" out there versus an electron just being a useful fiction that happens to make good predictions, just transplanted onto AI now? Or is there something genuinely different going on with neural nets specifically, something about how these "features" get found in the first place, that makes this harder (or maybe easier) to settle than the usual case in science? I'd love an explanation that actually gets at the crux of the disagreement instead of just a "well, here's both sides" summary, because right now it feels like the two camps are partly just talking past each other — and I want to know if there's an actual fact of the matter here, or if "representation" is just a word people are drawing the boundaries of differently depending which camp your in.
