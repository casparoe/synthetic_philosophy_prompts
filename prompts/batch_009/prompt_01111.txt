Random thing I've been chewing on since an argument with my team about whether it's "fine" to break a promise nobody would ever find out about: turns out philosophers have actual competing theories for why a promise obligates you at all, which is funny given that in my day job "promise" just means a value you get later, not a moral commitment, lol.

Hume's take: a promise only binds becuase of a background social convention everyone benefits from buying into, kind of like how a contract only means something because a legal system backs it up, not because of some special act of will. Scanlon's take is different: the obligation comes from deliberately getting someone to form an expectation, where breaking it exploits their trust specifically -- he even formalizes it as "Principle F," with like 6 fussy conditions attached.

Can you actually compare these two, not just summarize them one after another? Mainly I want to know: is there a case where they'd disagree about whether someone's actually obligated, not just disagree about why? I vaguely remember a "deathbed promise" case -- you promise someone who's dying, and afterward it's literally impossible for anyone to ever know or be let down either way -- being a genuine problem for one of these theories. Explain why, and whether either view actually survives it.
