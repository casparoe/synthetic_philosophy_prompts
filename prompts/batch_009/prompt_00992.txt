I've been reading recent AI-safety work on the "shutdown problem" for a reading group, and there's one proposal I keep getting stuck on that I'd like to see properly argued against rather than merely doubted. The problem, briefly, is this: we want to build an agent that pursues some objective competently, never acts to prevent a shutdown button from being pressed, never acts to cause the button to be pressed, and shuts down immediately once it is pressed. The trouble is that an expected-utility maximizer with complete preferences over entire trajectories will generally have some settled view about whether continuing to operate is better or worse than being shut down at any given moment, and whenever continuing scores higher, ordinary instrumental reasoning gives it a reason to resist — this is just the familiar instrumental-convergence worry, applied specifically to the off-switch.

One recent proposal, due to the philosopher Elliott Thornley, tries to dissolve the problem rather than patch it: train the agent to have genuinely incomplete preferences, such that it has no preference whatsoever between any two trajectories in which shutdown happens after different lengths of time, however different those trajectories are in other respects. To keep such an agent useful despite this gap, it must also satisfy what Thornley calls "Timestep Dominance": when comparing two options, check, for every time at which shutdown might occur, which option would have produced at least as much value up to that point, and choose whichever option is never worse and sometimes better by this measure, all without the agent ever forming a view about which shutdown time is itself preferable. Since the agent has no stake in when shutdown happens, it seemingly has no instrumental motive to influence that.

My worry comes from the older decision-theoretic literature on incompleteness rather than from AI safety specifically. Agents with incomplete preferences are exactly the kind of agents that theorists such as Johan Gustafsson have used money-pump arguments to rule out: if an agent has no preference between two options, it can in principle be walked through a short cycle of seemingly reasonable trades — sweetening one option slightly, trading it for the other, then trading back again — that leaves it strictly worse off by its own lights, for nothing. The standard way to block this is for the agent to choose "resolutely," committing in advance to a plan and refusing to deviate even when some later trade looks locally appealing. But resolute choice of this kind seems to require the agent to already be evaluating entire plans, including how they play out depending on when shutdown occurs, which looks a great deal like having exactly the complete, shutdown-timing-sensitive preferences the proposal was designed to avoid. Thornley is aware of this sort of worry and has offered responses to it; suppose, for present purposes, that you find those responses unconvincing. Please argue that the underlying dilemma is fatal to the incomplete-preferences solution to the shutdown problem — that no agent can simultaneously (a) be a competent, resource-using pursuer of its goals, (b) have no preference whatsoever regarding the timing of its own shutdown, and (c) be invulnerable to exploitation via money-pump sequences of trades.
