During a forty-year career in condensed-matter physics, much of it spent running large-scale numerical simulations, I treated the Church-Turing thesis rather the way one treats a well-established conservation law: an unquestioned background assumption, never really an object of scrutiny in its own right. Since retiring, I have joined a small philosophy discussion group at my old university, and two recent sessions on computability left me considerably less settled on the matter than when we began.

My own prior understanding, which I suspect is the default view among working scientists who encounter the thesis at all, was that it is simply not the kind of claim that could be proved, since it asserts an identity between a precisely defined mathematical notion (Turing computability) and an informal, pre-theoretic one (effective or mechanical calculability), and no formal argument can ever fully close that gap. This seems close to Emil Post's own position: he resisted Church's preference for presenting the thesis as a definition and insisted instead on treating it as a working hypothesis in need of continual verification, rather like an empirical law awaiting refutation. Yet one member of the group, a retired logician, complicated this picture considerably. She described a line of argument, which I understand originates with Kripke, that treats computation as a species of mathematical deduction and derives something close to the thesis as a corollary of Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, on the assumption that the steps of any algorithm can always be stated in a first-order language.

I would be grateful for help thinking this through properly. First, could you lay out the main positions one might take on what kind of claim the Church-Turing thesis actually is -- unprovable empirical hypothesis, stipulative definition, successful conceptual analysis, or genuine theorem -- and explain what practical or philosophical difference it makes which of these one accepts? Second, I would like to understand how an argument like Kripke's is supposed to get any purchase at all: if the traditional obstacle is that "effectively calculable" is irreducibly informal, does casting computation as first-order deduction actually dissolve that obstacle, or does it merely relocate the same informal judgment into the premise that algorithmic steps are always first-order expressible? Finally, does any of this bear on the further, more physical question of whether some process in nature, perhaps exploiting exotic spacetimes or quantum effects, could ever be said to refute the thesis -- or is that "physical" Church-Turing thesis really a separate claim that might fail on its own terms while leaving the original, mathematical thesis completely untouched?
