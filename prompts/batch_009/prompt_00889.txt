Been down a total Kant rabbit hole while researching a piece on animal cognition, and I keep hitting the same snag. His official line (from the Lectures on Ethics) is that we have no direct duties to animals at all -- if a guy has his old dog shot because it can't work anymore, Kant says the dog isn't wronged, the only real problem is what it does to the guy's own character, since being that callous will eventually bleed into how he treats other people. So basically, don't be cruel to animals, not for their sake, but as training wheels for being decent to humans. But then you've got Korsgaard, in Fellow Creatures, arguing that if you actually follow Kant's own logic for why rational beings count as "ends in themselves," it commits you to extending that same status to animals too, not just protecting them as a side effect of guarding human character. So which is it -- can you write me an essay that actually settles this: is "indirect duties only" the correct, stable reading of Kant, or does a Korsgaard-style extension genuinely follow from his own premises whether he'd have liked it or not? Don't give me a mushy "good arguements on both sides" ending, actually pick a side and defend it.
