So this has become weirdly personal for me: my younger brother has had drug-resistant epilepsy since he was a teenager, and his neurologist is now recommending a corpus callosotomy — a full section, not the partial anterior one they sometimes start with, because apparently his seizures are severe enough to warrant going straight for it — to stop them spreading between hemispheres. Surgery's in about six weeks. Obviously I started googling "split brain surgery" the night we found out, and I fell down a rabbit hole that went way past the usual pop-science stuff (the two hands fighting over a shirt, one hemisphere naming an object the other can't) into the actual philosophy literature, and now I can't stop thinking about it late at night, which is not great for my sleep.

Here's where I've ended up. I read Nagel's old 1971 paper where he basically concludes that none of the standard answers to "how many minds does this person have" actually work. Then I found Puccetti, who went the opposite direction and argued split-brain patients — and actually all of us, even with an intact callosum, since on his view the callosum just duplicates information rather then integrating it — have two separate minds the whole time. Then there's Bayne's "switch model," which tries to preserve full unity by saying consciousness just flips back and forth between the hemispheres really fast instead of splitting. And Schechter's book has this move I still don't fully get, where she argues there really are two minds/subjects in there (she labels them L and R) but somehow still only one person. And then, to make everything worse, I found that 2017 Pinto et al. paper in Brain that apparently found unified awareness across the whole visual field even in patients with a full, medically confirmed severing of the callosum — which sounds like its in real tension with the classic Sperry/Gazzaniga picture that started this whole debate in the first place.

Could you write me an essay that actually grapples with these specific positions instead of giving me the generic "well, it's a hard problem, there are lots of views" answer I keep getting from popsci articles? Walk through the strongest version of the case for real duality (two minds/subjects/streams), the strongest version of the case for unity (whether through switching or through something like the Pinto results), and the weirder in-between options like Schechter's minds-vs-person distinction — and then actually commit to whichever picture you think is closest to right, and justify it. If you can, end with something about whether "will there be two of him after this" is even a well-formed question to be asking, given your conclusion — that's honestly the part keeping me up.
