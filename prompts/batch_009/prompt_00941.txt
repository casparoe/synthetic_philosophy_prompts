Karl Popper died in 1994, right as intelligent design was starting to coalesce into an actual organized movement -- he never got to see Behe's irreducible complexity stuff, Dembski's "specified complexity," or the whole Dover trial circus in 2005. What's been bugging me is that ID people still love quoting the 1974 Popper who dismissed Darwinism as untestable, a mere "metaphysical research programme," conveniently skipping the part where he walked a lot of that back a few years later once biologists pushed back on him about how much natural selection actually predicts and explains. I was hashing this out with a colleague from the bio department over lunch last week (I've been using the whole Popper-Darwinism saga as a case study in my philosophy of science seminar this term) and couldn't let the question go, so now I'm just gonna put it to you instead.

Here's what I actually want: imagine Popper had lived long enough to read Behe and Dembski himself and sit through Dover. Using his whole toolkit -- not just the falsifiability slogan, but also his view that a "metaphysical research programme" can be a perfectly respectable, even necessary, stage on the way to real science (which is basically the out he eventually gave Darwinism), versus his three go-to paradigm cases of pseudo-science: astrology, Marxism, and psychoanalysis -- where does ID actually land for him? Does it clear the fairly low bar he ended up setting for Darwinism, or does it look more like the pseudo-science trio? Bonus points if you can work in the irony that his whole account of how scientific knowledge grows, conjectures getting refuted, the fittest theory surviving another day, is itself basically a Darwinian selection story. Don't just hand me a verdict -- actually walk through the reasoning like your reconstructing his argument step by step, and be honest about where his own ambivalence makes this a genuinely hard call rather then an easy dunk.
