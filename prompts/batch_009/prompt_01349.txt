I have been working through Aristotle's De Interpretatione for a reading group on ancient theories of time and modality, and I have run into a real difficulty in chapter 9 – the chapter most often cited as the origin of the debate about logical fatalism, though I suspect many people who invoke it have never worked through the argument in detail. Earlier in the chapter, Aristotle states plainly that "in the case of that which is or which has taken place, propositions, whether positive or negative, must be true or false," and that for a pair of contradictories with an individual subject "one of the two must be true and the other false." A little further on, however, discussing statements about the future, he writes (I am quoting the Edghill translation, first published in 1928):

"Let me illustrate. A sea-fight must either take place to-morrow or not, but it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, neither is it necessary that it should not take place, yet it is necessary that it either should or should not take place to-morrow... This is the case with regard to that which is not always existent or not always nonexistent. One of the two propositions in such instances must be true and the other false, but we cannot say determinately that this or that is false, but must leave the alternative undecided. One may indeed be more likely to be true than the other, but it cannot be either actually true or actually false. It is therefore plain that it is not necessary that of an affirmation and a denial one should be true and the other false."

What I cannot work out is whether Aristotle is simply contradicting himself here – first insisting that one of the two propositions about tomorrow's sea-battle "must be true and the other false," then a few lines later denying that it is "necessary that of an affirmation and a denial one should be true and the other false" – or whether some genuine, coherent distinction is doing the work, for instance between the necessity of the disjunction ("it will happen or it will not") and the necessity of each disjunct taken on its own, or between a proposition's simply being true and its being now determinately or "actually" true. Could you help me work through exactly what is going on in this stretch of the argument: does the text actually support attributing a non-contradictory position to Aristotle, and if so, does that position succeed in blocking the fatalist conclusion he spends the earlier part of the chapter setting up, without quietly abandoning bivalence for ordinary statements about the present and past?
