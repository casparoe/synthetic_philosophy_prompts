I have no formal background in philosophy or logic, but I have spent a good deal of time over the years reading about Kurt Gödel, initially out of fascination with the incompleteness theorems. That eventually led me to a stranger corner of his biography: a short, undated list of fourteen private philosophical theses that he apparently wrote only for himself around 1960, later recorded by the logician Hao Wang in "A Logical Journey: From Gödel to Philosophy" (1996). Most of the fourteen are simply asserted, with no supporting argument at all. A few read:

4. "There are other worlds and rational beings of a different and higher kind."
5. "The world in which we live is not the only one in which we shall live or have lived."
10. "Materialism is false."

From what I understand, Gödel set out a fuller version of the reasoning behind thesis 5 in letters to his mother from around the same period. The argument, roughly, is that because the world is orderly and law-governed rather than chaotic, it must also be meaningful; and since a human life plainly never uses more than a small fraction of its capacity for growth and understanding, there must be some further existence in which that capacity gets used, since otherwise the whole arrangement would amount to a kind of built-in waste.

I can't work out on my own whether this argument actually holds together, or whether it simply trades on a slide between two different senses of "rational": orderly and predictable, the way physics is, versus purposeful and fitting, the way a well-made plan is. Is that a genuine weak point, or am I misreading him? And separately, is there any real logical connection between this argument and his much better-known claim that the incompleteness theorems tell against materialism, or are these just two convictions he happened to hold at the same time?
