Ever since my family and I settled in a mid-sized city in the Maryland suburbs of DC nine years ago, I have paid income and property taxes here, sent both of my children through the local public schools, and served two terms on my neighborhood's civic association -- all without ever being permitted to cast a ballot in a local election, since I hold a green card rather than citizenship. Our city council is now debating a measure that would extend the municipal franchise to long-term non-citizen residents -- the kind of measure that towns like Takoma Park, Maryland adopted decades ago, and that New York City attempted more recently, before the state's highest court struck the law down in 2022. Talking it over with a neighbor who teaches political theory, I was introduced to what she called the "boundary problem": the puzzle, laid out most influentially in Frederick Whelan's 1983 essay and later engaged by Robert Dahl, that there seems to be no democratic way to decide who counts as "the people" for a given vote, since any procedure for settling that question would itself require an electorate whose composition is exactly what's in dispute.

I've since read enough to see that the two standard ways of cutting this knot -- enfranchising everyone whose interests are materially affected by a decision (the All-Affected-Interests Principle) and enfranchising everyone who is subject to the coercive laws at issue (the All-Subjected Principle) -- each come apart in instructive ways once you press on them. The first seems to hand almost anyone, anywhere, a claim on almost any local decision; the second has to explain why being subject to a town's zoning and school-board rules is the relevant kind of subjection, while being subject to the federal immigration law that determines whether I get to stay in the country at all apparently is not. What I haven't managed to find is these tensions actually fought out between two people who each take the underlying problem seriously, rather than one side simply treating its preferred principle as self-evident.

Could you write me a dialogue between two political theorists who reach opposite conclusions about whether my city should extend the local franchise to long-term non-citizen residents, but who both grant that the boundary problem is a genuine and perhaps ultimately irresolvable puzzle rather than a rhetorical inconvenience? I would like each to press the other on the hard cases: the one favoring enfranchisement should have to say why the line belongs at "long-term resident" rather than somewhere more expansive still, and the one favoring the status quo should have to defend citizenship as the operative criterion on grounds other than tradition or majority sentiment, given how contingently, and sometimes unjustly, citizenship boundaries themselves tend to get drawn. I would also like each to be pushed on whether their own favored criterion actually solves the boundary problem or merely relocates it, since deciding who is "affected" enough, or "subject" enough, to qualify looks like exactly the kind of question a not-yet-constituted demos cannot neutrally settle. Please have them respond to each other's actual arguments as the exchange develops, rather than simply restating their opening positions.
