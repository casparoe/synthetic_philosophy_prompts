I'm a PhD student in political theory, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on mid-twentieth-century critiques of partisanship and their bearing on contemporary worries about epistemic polarization. This has brought me back to Simone Weil's "Note sur la suppression générale des partis politiques," the short memorandum she wrote in London in 1943, shortly before her death, and which appeared only posthumously in 1950. My advisor and I got into a disagreement in a recent meeting about how one particular inference in the essay is supposed to work, and since the argument turns on a fairly technical bit of vocabulary, I wanted to work from the French directly rather than rely on Simon Leys' English translation, just to make sure neither of us was inheriting a confusion introduced in translation.

The passage comes right after Weil proposes to judge parties by the criteria of truth, justice, and the public good, and sets out to identify their essential characteristics. In French, it reads: "On peut en énumérer trois : Un parti politique est une machine à fabriquer de la passion collective. Un parti politique est une organisation construite de manière à exercer une pression collective sur la pensée de chacun des êtres humains qui en sont membres. La première fin, et, en dernière analyse, l'unique fin de tout parti politique est sa propre croissance, et cela sans aucune limite. Par ce triple caractère, tout parti est totalitaire en germe et en aspiration. S'il ne l'est pas en fait, c'est seulement parce que ceux qui l'entourent ne le sont pas moins que lui. Ces trois caractères sont des vérités de fait évidentes à quiconque s'est approché de la vie des partis." My own rough rendering: "Three can be listed: a political party is a machine for manufacturing collective passion. A political party is an organization built so as to exert a collective pressure on the thinking of each human being belonging to it. The first end, and, in the last analysis, the only end, of any political party is its own growth, without any limit. By virtue of this threefold character, every party is totalitarian in seed and in aspiration; if it is not so in fact, that is only because the parties surrounding it are no less so than it is. These three characteristics are truths of fact, evident to anyone who has come close to the life of parties."

What I can't pin down is the logical status of the move being made here. Weil is explicit that the three "characteristics" are "vérités de fait" rather than "vérités de raison," which reads to me like a deliberate echo of the old rationalist (Leibnizian) distinction between contingent truths of fact and necessary truths of reason. Yet she goes on to treat these avowedly contingent, sociological observations as grounds for a verdict on parties as such that sounds much closer to a necessary or conceptual truth than to a defeasible empirical generalization admitting of exceptions or gradual reform. Is there a non-question-begging way to reconstruct this inference, or is Weil simply sliding from "this is how parties have in fact tended to behave" to "no party could possibly be otherwise"? I'm also wondering whether this should be read against her broader treatment of force and the "collective" in works like The Need for Roots and Gravity and Grace, where certain sociological regularities seem to be granted something close to the necessity of natural laws. I'd be grateful for help reconstructing the argument as charitably as possible, or for pinpointing exactly where, if anywhere, it breaks down.
