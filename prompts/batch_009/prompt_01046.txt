I'm drafting a historical novel narrated by a young amanuensis—an invented character—who spends the winter of 1827 cataloguing Jeremy Bentham's papers at his house in Queen's Square Place. Before I write the pivotal chapter, I want to make sure I've got one strand of the philosophy right, and I'd welcome your comments on where my notes below are accurate, where they oversimplify, and which part of the material you think would carry the most dramatic weight.

My understanding so far: around 1785, working mostly for his own eyes, Bentham drafted a long manuscript, later catalogued under a title like "Offences Against One's Self," arguing that English law's treatment of sodomy as a capital offense couldn't survive his own utilitarian test. If pleasure is the only good and pain the only evil, and if what happens between two consenting men in private harms no third party, then the only thing actually doing the work of condemnation is disgust wearing the mask of principle—what he elsewhere names "antipathy," one of the two counterfeit rivals to utility that he spends a whole chapter attacking. He backs this up with the standard Enlightenment appeal to Greece and Rome. He returned to the subject in a second wave of writing from the mid-1810s, where he adds a very Bentham-ish twist: given Malthus, discouraging procreation is if anything a point in "irregular" sex's favor, not against it.

He never published any of it. After his death, his own chosen literary executor, John Bowring, left the material out of the collected edition entirely—so the man who followed the argument furthest in private is also the one whose own arrangements saw it buried furthest from the public.

Two things I'd like from you. First, tell me whether I've represented the actual shape of the argument accurately—I'm specifically unsure whether "it's mere antipathy, not real harm" is really the crux of Bentham's own reasoning, or whether I'm quietly importing a later, more Millian idea of harm that doesn't quite belong to him. Second, of the three elements above—the 1785 argument itself, the Malthus twist, or the Bowring aftermath—which would you build the chapter's central scene around, if you wanted the philosophy to do real dramatic work rather than sit in the background as period detail?
