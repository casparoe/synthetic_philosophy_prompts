I've been down a rabbit hole reading the Center on Long-Term Risk's research on cooperative AI, and I keep getting stuck on this concept of "safe Pareto improvements" (SPIs), which I think originally comes from a paper by Oesterheld and Conitzer. The basic idea, as I understand it: when principals delegate playing a game to representatives, or more generally when parties bargain under uncertainty about each other's true reservation points and strategies, you can sometimes restructure the rules each side gives its delegate - like committing to care a bit more about a cheap "surrogate" goal than your actual one, or agreeing that a breakdown in talks gets settled by a cheap simulated conflict instead of the real, costly one - in a way that's guaranteed to leave everyone at least as well off as the default, no matter what everyone's private info/strategies actually were. Every example I've found is a pretty abstract toy model (two countries with representatives squabbling over an oil-rich desert, that kind of thing), and it's mostly framed around stopping AI systems, or the states/companies delegating to them, from blundering into catastrophic wars down the line.

It got me thinking about something a lot less exotic though: my brother and I inherited a chunk of our dad's business, neither of us really trusts the other's read on what it's worth or what their actual walk-away point is, and eight months and a small fortune in lawyer's fees later we're still slogging through mediation, madder at each other every month. I mentioned the SPI idea to a friend who's a corporate lawyer and she just kind of shrugged, which wasn't super reassuring. Could you spell out what you think are the most important implications of the safe-Pareto-improvement concept for a messy, lawyer-mediated human negotiation like ours, as opposed to the clean formal setups its normally illustrated with? Specifically I'm curious whether there's anything actually actionable in there - concrete commitment devices, ways of reframing the bargaining, etc - or whether the whole framework just quietly falls apart once you leave fully-specified games and step into vague real-world stuff with incomplete contracts, imperfect lawyers, and genuine hurt feelings involved.
