A friend and I got into a long argument over dinner about Leo Tolstoy's *What Is Art?*. It started because she had just watched Leni Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will* for a film class and brought up Tolstoy as a kind of test case for thinking about propaganda. Her contention was that the treatise, for all its bluster about Shakespeare, Wagner, and Beethoven being frauds, does not actually cohere as a single theory of value: she argued that Tolstoy quietly operates with two independent standards of "good art" -- one purely technical, concerning how effectively a feeling is transmitted from artist to audience, and one moral or religious, concerning whether that feeling is itself worth spreading -- and that keeping these two standards formally separate lets him have things both ways. When a work he dislikes is obviously powerful, he can concede that it is genuine art while still condemning it as "bad" on grounds of content; when a work he approves of is technically crude, he can wave that away as immaterial next to the goodness of the feeling conveyed. I was not convinced this was entirely fair to him, so afterward I went back and found the passage where the split she is describing actually gets made explicit -- it comes near the end of Chapter XV of the Aylmer Maude translation, right before he pivots to the question of subject-matter in the next chapter.

Here is the passage:

"And the degree of the infectiousness of art depends on three conditions:—(1) On the greater or lesser individuality of the feeling transmitted; (2) on the greater or lesser clearness with which the feeling is transmitted; (3) on the sincerity of the artist, i.e. on the greater or lesser force with which the artist himself feels the emotion he transmits. [...] Such are the three conditions which divide art from its counterfeits, and which also decide the quality of every work of art apart from its subject-matter. [...] Thus is art divided from not art, and thus is the quality of art, as art, decided, independently of its subject-matter, i.e. apart from whether the feelings it transmits are good or bad. But how are we to define good and bad art with reference to its subject-matter?"

Could you help me work through what is actually going on logically in this passage and its handoff into the next chapter? Specifically: is my friend right that Tolstoy is committed to two genuinely independent axes of evaluation, with no principled way of weighing one against the other when they pull apart -- or is there a way of reading "sincerity," which he calls the most important of the three conditions, that ties it back to the shared "religious perception" he leans on for judging subject-matter, so that the two supposedly separate criteria turn out to be less independent than they look? And separately: does this passage commit Tolstoy to saying that something like Riefenstahl's film -- sincere, individual, and clear in its expression, but repugnant in content -- would count as genuine, if bad, art by his own lights? If so, is that a bullet he can bite, or does it actually break the theory?
