In discussions of decision theory and long-term AI safety, one sometimes encounters the notion of an "acausal threat": a situation in which some powerful predictor -- a future superintelligence, say, or an agent in a causally disconnected part of a very large universe -- can work out in advance exactly how you (or an accurate simulation of you) would respond to various situations, and exploits that predictability to extract concessions from you now, even though no ordinary causal signal ever connects the party issuing the threat to the party it targets. The threat is "acausal" precisely because the leverage comes from a logical or predictive correlation between decision procedures, not from any physical channel of influence. Roko's basilisk is the best-known, if widely contested, instance of the idea: the worry that a future AI might "acausally blackmail" earlier humans by predicting exactly who had understood the argument for helping build it but chose not to, and then retroactively punishing detailed simulations of exactly those people.

One countermeasure discussed in the same circles, under the name "surrogate goals," asks an agent to decide in advance, before any concrete threat has appeared, to start caring -- as a matter of standing policy -- about some cheap and otherwise arbitrary state of affairs (some trivial fact about an unused block of computer memory, say) exactly as intensely as it cares about what it actually values. The hope is that a threatener who only wants concessions as cheaply as possible will find the arbitrary decoy just as attractive a target as the agent's real values, perhaps more so, so that any threat gets redirected onto something the agent never minded losing in the first place. I've seen this presented, in the relevant blogs and forum threads, as a clever and basically sound piece of precommitment engineering, and I've also seen it dismissed as a kind of sleight of hand that no sufficiently sophisticated predictor would fall for, and I haven't been able to work out for myself which reaction is closer to right.

Write a dialogue between two decision theorists who both accept acausal reasoning as a legitimate part of rational agency -- neither one should be a strawman who simply rejects the whole framework -- but who disagree sharply about whether the surrogate-goal strategy is a genuine solution or a form of self-deception. One should defend it as sound; the other should press the objection that any predictor smart enough to mount a genuine acausal threat in the first place is also smart enough to recognize a goal adopted purely for its deflective value, and that making the decoy credible seems to require the agent to actually spend real resources demonstrating that it cares -- at which point the goal stops being cheap or arbitrary at all. Have the two of them respond directly to each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another, and let the exchange end wherever it honestly leads, even if that turns out to be a standoff rather than a resolution.
