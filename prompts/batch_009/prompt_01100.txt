So we're reading Huckleberry Finn in English class right now, and we just got to the part where Huck decides to help Jim escape even though he genuinely believes (bc of how he was raised) that it's a sin and he's gonna go to hell for it. My teacher mentioned kind of offhand that philosophers actually use this exact scene as a test case for something called "moral worth," so me and my friend went down a rabbit hole about it after school. It also made me think about smaller stuff in my own life, like how I only apologized to my little brother last week so my mom would stop being mad at me, not bc I actually felt bad — does that mean the apology doesnt count morally even tho it was the right thing to do?

From what I can tell there's an actual split among philosophers here. The classic Kantian line (I kept seeing Barbara Herman's name attached to it) says an act only has genuine moral worth if you did it because you recognized it as your duty, so by that standard Huck's choice might not really be to his credit since his own conscious moral reasoning was telling him to turn Jim in. But then people like Nomy Arpaly and Julia Markovits think that's backwards — Arpaly's whole point with the Huck Finn case is that he's responding to the right reasons (caring about Jim as an actual person) even without having the "correct" moral belief in his head, and Markovits has this thing called the coincident reasons thesis that's basically a more refined version of that same idea. There's also apparently an objection to the Kantian side called "moral fetishism," something about how wanting to do the right thing just because it's "the right thing" rather than caring about the person in front of you seems kind of cold or even creepy, but I don't fully get why that's suppose to be such a big deal.
