I never studied philosophy formally, but over the past year I've been working through introductory material on epistemology on my own, using secondhand accounts and a few translated primary texts I picked up at the library. One story comes up again and again, told almost the same way every time: Plato, in the *Theaetetus*, defined knowledge as "justified true belief," and this stood as the accepted definition throughout the whole history of Western philosophy until 1963, when an American philosopher named Edmund Gettier published a short, three-page paper with two brief counterexamples that overturned it almost overnight. I mentioned this to a coworker who took a couple of philosophy courses in college, and she confirmed the same version of events.

What bothers me is that the counterexamples I've since read about don't seem very exotic. The basic idea, that someone can end up with a belief that is true and seems perfectly reasonable, yet is true only by luck, isn't hard to think up at all. I came up with something similar myself just from the old saying that a stopped clock is still right twice a day, and what that would mean if a person happened to glance at it during the exact minute it was correct. Given how simple the underlying insight seems, and how many careful thinkers worked on the theory of knowledge across the more than two thousand years separating Plato from Gettier, I find it genuinely hard to believe that nobody noticed the problem earlier. Am I wrong to be suspicious of this tidy version of the story, in which a single, unbroken definition survives untouched for over two millennia and then gets quietly demolished in a few pages? Or is that actually a fair summary of what happened?
