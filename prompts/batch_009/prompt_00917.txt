Hey — I've been reporting on dementia care for a piece I'm writing, and I keep running into this practice caregivers call "therapeutic lying" (also "compassionate deception," or apparently some nurses just say "fiblets"). Classic example: a patient with advanced dementia keeps asking for her husband, who died three years ago, so instead of re-breaking the news every time, staff will just tell her he's "at work" or "running late." Most of the bioethicists I've talked to are pretty relaxed about this as long as its reducing real suffering, which got me wondering what a true hardliner on lying would make of it.

That sent me down an Augustine rabbit hole this week. Turns out he wrote basically two entire treatises on lying (De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium), sorting lies into eight ranked categories from worst to least-bad, and then, almost perversely, concluding that every single one is still a sin — including, from what I can tell, lying to a dying person about their condition, and even lying to save someone's actual life, which plenty of other Christians at the time apparently thought was obviously fine. So here's my question: if you explained modern dementia care to Augustine, do you think he'd actually bite the bullet and say caregivers are sinning every time they do this, even though the "honest" alternative seems needlessly cruel? Or does his own hair-splitting distinction between "lying" and just staying silent/vague give him some kind of escape hatch he could point caregivers toward instead? Genuinely not sure how far his framework bends before it just snaps.
