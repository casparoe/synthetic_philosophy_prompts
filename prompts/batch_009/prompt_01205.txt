Quick one — I've been down a rabbit hole this week for a piece on the whole AI-doomer vs. techno-optimist debate, and it kept leading back to a 1955 Fortune magazine essay by von Neumann called "Can We Survive Technology?" It's a weird read because he doesn't really take either side — his move is basically that whether we want progress is moot, because, in his words, "for progress there is no cure." The actual argument isn't about any one scary invention either, it's about speed and scale: technology now moves faster than the political and legal machinery thats suppossed to manage its effects, and the systems those effects hit (economies, ecosystems, whole populations) have grown to roughly the size of the planet, so there's nowhere left outside the system for the fallout of a bad call to stay contained. He was also skeptical you could dodge the whole problem by just banning the scary technologies and keeping the nice ones. And there's this detail that gets me: he wrote all this basically on his way out, dying of a cancer he probably got from standing too close to the bomb tests his own work made possible.

Anyway, I'd love a short story out of this, maybe a page or two. Please, not the rogue-superintelligence-robots-take-over thing, that's been written a thousand times and it's not really what von Neumann is even describing. Think less Terminator, more Ted Chiang. I want the tension to come from that specific mismatch: some technology (doesn't have to be AI — biotech, geoengineering, something in finance, whatever works) outrunning the institutions meant to govern it, at the exact moment there's no outside left to displace the consequences to. And if you can, resist a clean fix at the end — von Neumann's own answer to whether we can survive this was really just: maybe, with luck, and "patience, flexibility, intelligence." Muddling through, not a last-minute save.
