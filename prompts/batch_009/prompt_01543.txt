Random thing that's been nagging me while I put together this year's early modern lecture. I always teach Hobbes right after Descartes in the unit on the geometrical method, with the framing that the two of them are basically on the same page about why geometry is the one fully certain science: its truths are grasped by reason alone, the same way Descartes needs the intellect rather than the senses to get at what the wax really is. My TA sat in on it this term and said something felt off to her about lumping Hobbes in with that story, though she couldn't quite say why, and I told her I wasn't really seeing the problem, since if anything Hobbes is even more devoted to geometry's special status then Descartes ever was -- he's the one who called it "the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind," and he spent a good chunk of his later life insisting he'd squared the circle, against Wallis and basically the whole mathematical establishment of the day.

So here's my actual question, and it's more philosophical than historical: if Hobbes needs that same reason-over-the-senses story to explain geometry's uniquely certain status, how is that consistent with him opening Leviathan by insisting there's no conception in the mind that isn't ultimately "begotten upon the organs of sense"? That looks like Hobbes flatly contradicting his own empiricism in the first few pages of his masterwork, which seems like too obvious a howler for such a careful system, so I assume I'm just missing whatever move historians of philosophy usually make to reconcile the two. What's the move?
