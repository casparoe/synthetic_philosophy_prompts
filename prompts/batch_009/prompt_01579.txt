I am currently taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's unit asks us to trace Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity back to their historical antecedents, including Newton's opening "Rules of Reasoning in Philosophy" from Book III of the Principia:

Rule I. We are to admit no more causes of natural things than such as are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances. To this purpose the philosophers say that Nature does nothing in vain, and more is in vain when less will serve; for Nature is pleased with simplicity, and affects not the pomp of superfluous causes.

Rule II. Therefore to the same natural effects we must, as far as possible, assign the same causes.

I can see why this counts as an ancestor of Occam's razor, but I am having trouble pinning down exactly how it maps onto the modern formalization. Newton's simplicity clause is subordinate to two prior demands, that the causes be true and that they be sufficient, and it reads more like a tiebreaker than a free-standing principle. Kolmogorov complexity, by contrast, ranks hypotheses purely by the length of the shortest program that reproduces the data, with no independent truth condition; fit to the evidence enters only later, through Bayesian updating on the universal prior. Is Newton's notion of simplicity actually the same property that description length is tracking, or is he after something like economy of causes that a purely syntactic complexity measure cannot represent? Put differently: does Solomonoff induction fully cash out Newton's rule, or only the "sufficient" half of it, while quietly dropping the demand for truth?
