Over the past year my brother has moved from casual religious observance into fairly hardline young-earth creationism, and it has put a real strain on our relationship: he waves away radiometric dating, plate tectonics, and the fossil record as question-begging, and no evidence I bring up seems to register. After a particularly bad argument about it at a family gathering, a friend finishing her philosophy doctorate suggested I read Wittgenstein's On Certainty, on the thought that it might at least help me understand the shape of the disagreement, even if it couldn't resolve it. I've now worked through the book fairly carefully, along with some secondary discussion, and I think I've landed on a reading of it, though it's not one I'm entirely at peace with.

As I understand him, Wittgenstein holds that our most basic certainties aren't beliefs we know or have evidence for at all; they function as "hinges" that the whole practice of doubting, testing, and giving reasons depends on, and are therefore outside that practice rather than well or badly supported within it. Tellingly, he considers a case close to mine: a king raised from childhood to believe the world began at his own birth, and a remark about how thoroughly intelligent, well-educated people can believe the Genesis creation story while others regard it as simply disproved, neither side able to do much more than talk past the other. He goes on to say that once reasons give out, what's left is closer to conversion than to proof, comparing it to how missionaries win people over to a new faith rather than argue them into one. Putting this together, it looks to me like my old-earth convictions and my brother's young-earth convictions are, on Wittgenstein's view, just two rival hinges, equally groundless and equally beyond argument, and that the intellectually honest response is to stop treating this as a disagreement with a fact of the matter and simply accept that we're reasoning inside two incommensurable frameworks.

That conclusion is convenient, since it gives me a graceful way to stop having a fight I'm tired of having, but I'd rather get Wittgenstein right than reach for whatever reading lets me off the hook. Is this really where his account of hinge propositions leads, or am I taking something he says about deep, cross-cultural confrontations between whole ways of life and misapplying it to an argument between two people who, in fact, share nearly all of their background beliefs and standards of evidence?
