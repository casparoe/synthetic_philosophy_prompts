This semester my psychology class watched a lecture segment on how neuroscience supposedly undermines the very idea of "just deserts," which sent me down a rabbit hole on free will and moral responsibility while I was prepping for my school's Ethics Bowl team. The essay I found most interesting was P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," where instead of arguing about whether determinism is true, he tries to dissolve the whole dispute: he claims that our reactive attitudes toward one another—resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt—are natural and inescapable, and he compares them to Hume's point that our commitment to induction can't be rationally justified from outside yet could never seriously be abandoned either. On Strawson's view, being morally responsible just is being an appropriate target of these attitudes within our ordinary relationships, and no metaphysical discovery about causation could get us to give up the reactive stance as a whole—at most it could lead us to adopt what he calls the "objective attitude" toward particular people in particular cases, treating them as things to be managed rather than as fellow participants in a relationship.

While looking for objections, I was genuinely surprised to learn that P.F. Strawson's sharpest opponent on this exact question is his own son, Galen Strawson. Galen's "Basic Argument" holds that true, ultimate moral responsibility is impossible for anyone at all: to really deserve blame or credit, you would have to be responsible for being the way you are in the relevant respects, but you could only be responsible for that if you had chosen your character on the basis of values you already held—and then we can ask the same question about those values, and so on forever, so that this kind of self-creation, and with it ultimate responsibility, turns out to be impossible whether determinism is true or not. What really struck me was finding out that Galen eventually wrote a chapter literally titled "On 'Freedom and Resentment,'" engaging directly with his father's essay—but it wasn't published until 2008, two years after P.F. Strawson died, so as far as I can tell the two of them never actually got to have this argument out face to face.

Could you write the conversation they never got to have? I'd like a dialogue between P.F. Strawson and Galen Strawson in which each one responds to the other's actual argument rather than just restating his own position—I don't want either side to win too easily, since both arguments are genuinely strong. In particular, I want Galen to press his father on whether "we simply can't help having these attitudes" really answers the question his Basic Argument is asking, about whether anyone deserves blame in the deepest sense, or whether it just changes the subject, and I want P.F. to have a substantive comeback to that pressure (perhaps by leaning further on his comparison with induction) rather than only repeating that the attitudes are natural. Please end the dialogue with the disagreement sharpened and clarified rather than neatly resolved.
