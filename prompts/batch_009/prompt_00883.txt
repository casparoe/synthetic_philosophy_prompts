I'm a phil PhD student and this semester I'm TAing intro ethics, and we just got through the universalizability material in the Groundwork. The way I've been explaining Kant's Formula of Universal Law to my students (and honestly how I've thought about it since undergrad) is basically: take a maxim, imagine literally everyone acting on it, and check whether the resulting state of affairs would be self-defeating or bad enough that no one could stably will it - eg false promising: if everyone lied to get out of debt whenever it was convenient, promising as an institution collapses and lying stops working as a strategy, so the maxim fails. Same deal for the "letting your talents rust" and "refusing to help others" cases later in the text - universalize it, then check whether the downstream situation is one a rational agent could actually want.

My problem is that one of my first-years called this out in office hours as basically rule-consequentialism wearing a trenchcoat - i.e., we're evaluating the maxim by the aggregate consequences of everyone following it, which is exactly the kind of consequences-first reasoning Kant spends the opening sections of the Groundwork trying to distance the categorical imperative from. I laughed it off in the moment but I dont actually have a crisp answer for why the contradiction-in-conception and contradiction-in-the-will tests aren't just consequentialism with a formalist paint job, especially since pretty much every intro-level gloss (mine included, apparently) explains it through this exact "what if everyone did this and it went badly" move. Is my student onto something real, or is there a principled reason the universalizability test isn't secretly just smuggled-in consequentialism?
