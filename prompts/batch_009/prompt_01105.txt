I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm noodling on about the modern "atheist church" scene — Sunday Assembly, the godless congregation that comedians Sanderson Jones and Pippa Evans started in a deconsecrated London church back in Jan 2013 (motto: "live better, help often, wonder more," pop songs instead of hymns, that sort of thing), plus Alain de Botton's Religion for Atheists, which is basically the same impulse dressed up more academically. De Botton even admits near the end of the book that the whole project is lifted from Auguste Comte's old "Religion of Humanity" scheme from the 1850s — chapels, a secular saints' calendar, the works.

Which got me arguing with a friend over drinks about George Eliot, of all people, since she was tangled up with Comtean positivism her whole adult life (so was Lewes) without every quite signing on the dotted line, and famously told Frederic Myers in a Cambridge garden that God was inconceivable and immortality unbelievable, but duty was "peremptory and absolute" — and later wrote that poem about wanting to live on in the "choir invisible" rather than in any personal heaven. So here's my question: if you resurrected her and sat her down at an actual Sunday Assembly meetup, jokes about not being a cult and all, what's her honest verdict? Does she recognize her own unfinished project finally getting off the ground, or does something about it strike her as thin, or even a little embarrassing, next to what she thought religious feeling was actually for? I don't want the generic "she was basically a humanist so she'd approve" answer — really dig into her actual essays and novels (the stuff on Dr. Cumming's crowd-pleasing evangelicalism seems relevant here too) to justify whatever verdict you land on.
