I've spent the last few months reporting on a string of negligence cases where the central factual dispute isn't about what someone did, but about what they failed to do — a regulator that sat on an early warning about a faulty valve, a hospital trust that never updated its protocol after a near-miss. Trying to understand the philosophical scaffolding behind a claim like "the regulator's inaction caused the harm," I stumbled on a puzzle in the metaphysics of causation that apparently still has no settled answer: philosophers call it the problem of "profligate" omissions. The classic version goes like this: if a gardener's not watering a client's plant counts as a cause of its death, because a simple counterfactual test is satisfied (water it and it lives, don't and it dies), that same test seems to force us to say that the Queen of England's not watering it is just as much a cause — and so, in principle, is the failure of every other person, and every other process anywhere, that could have kept the plant alive.

Philosophers have split into at least three camps over what to make of this. Some, like Phil Dowe, working from a physical, conserved-quantity account of causation, conclude that no absence is ever a genuine cause at all, and that what we're really tracking when we single out the gardener is a merely practical "quasi-causal" relation. Others, like Sarah McGrath, in a frequently cited 2005 paper, argue that the example shows causation itself — not just our willingness to mention it — has a normative component, so that facts about expectation or duty are what separate the gardener's omission from the Queen's. A third line of thought, rooted in David Lewis's original counterfactual theory of causation and sharpened since by contrastivists like Jonathan Schaffer, is willing to accept that the Queen's omission really is exactly as much a cause as the gardener's, treating our discomfort with saying so as a fact about the pragmatics of assertion and explanation, not about the extension of "cause" itself.

I'd like you to argue for that third position — the one willing to bite the bullet — rather than referee between all three. Make the strongest case you can that causation should be left unrestricted and non-normative here, so that literally every difference-making omission counts as a genuine cause, and that the whole burden of explaining why we'd never talk that way in ordinary speech, or in a courtroom, belongs to a separate, independently motivated theory of explanation and conversational relevance. Engage seriously with at least one of the two rival camps — show concretely where Dowe's retreat to mere quasi-causation goes wrong, or where McGrath's insistence on a normative component to causation goes wrong — and close with a definite verdict rather than a survey. I'm ultimately trying to work out, for my own reporting, whether it even makes sense to expect the metaphysics of causation to tell us who "really" caused a regulatory failure, as opposed to who is simply the most useful or fair person to blame for it, so a considered but non-committal answer won't be much use to me.
