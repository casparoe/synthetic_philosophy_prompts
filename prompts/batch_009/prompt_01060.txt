I've been reading through some of the effective-altruism-adjacent material on risk aversion in cause prioritization — there's a Rethink Priorities series that uses Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU) to formalize caution about worst-case scenarios in animal welfare, and a Global Priorities Institute paper by Richard Pettigrew asking whether a risk-averse altruist should really fund the same causes an expected-value maximizer would (existential risk reduction, say) or something safer. That sent me back to *Risk and Rationality* itself, and to the exchange it kicked off: Pettigrew and Briggs each pressed objections forceful enough that Buchak wrote a whole reply to them, and Johanna Thoma has since argued that REU doesn't really give you an alternative to orthodox expected utility once you think dynamic choice through carefully. I want to understand the core disagreement on its own terms, apart from the EA framing it arrived in.

The theory itself: keep the usual apparatus of states, acts, and a utility function over outcomes, but instead of weighting each outcome by its raw probability, order the outcomes worst-to-best and weight each increment in utility by a "risk function" r applied to the cumulative probability of doing at least that well. Set r to the identity and you recover ordinary expected utility; make r convex and you get an agent who, with no help from a concave utility-for-money function, can prefer a certain $1M to the classic 89%/10%/1% Allais gamble over $1M/$5M/nothing, while also preferring the 10%-chance-of-$5M gamble to the 11%-chance-of-$1M one once you strip the common 89%-chance-of-$1M portion out of both options and replace it with an 89% chance of nothing. Buchak's central claim is that r is doing real, independent philosophical work here — capturing a rational attitude toward risk as such, not just relabeled diminishing marginal utility. What I keep getting stuck on is the diachronic-choice objection: you can apparently construct a sequential version of the Allais case in which a "sophisticated" REU-maximizer — one who correctly foresees her own later choices — pays a strictly positive sum just to avoid learning a piece of information that a matched expected-utility maximizer would take for free without a second thought, and ends up strictly worse off for certain as a result. Buchak's defense is that this isn't incoherent, because the dominance principle doing the work in the objection is only plausible once you aggregate over states her theory treats as separately relevant, and because the ex ante and ex post perspectives from which the value of that information gets judged don't answer to any single, neutral standpoint.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed expected-utility theorist and a Buchak-style risk-weighted-expected-utility theorist that actually stages this dispute? Set it right after the two of them — co-instructors, say, for a decision theory seminar — have walked their students through the Allais paradox and started arguing at the whiteboard about whether to tell the class that the standard preference pattern is simply a mistake. I want both sides steelmanned: the EU theorist should press the paying-not-to-learn argument as hard as it goes, and the REU theorist needs a substantive answer to it, plus a real story about why r isn't just utility curvature wearing a disguise, rather than either of them leaning on bare intuition. They don't have to reach agreement, but each should have to genuinely revise or qualify something in response to the other rather than just restating their opening position.
