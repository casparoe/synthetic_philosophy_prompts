A friend of mine, who is writing a dissertation chapter on Sidgwick, and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about how to make sense of his decades of involvement in psychical research, and neither of us came away satisfied. The background, as we both understand it: in the concluding chapter of the first edition of The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick argues that practical reason, left to its own resources, cannot adjudicate between the axiom of rational egoism and the axiom of universal benevolence, and that without some further guarantee that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide, the whole edifice of rational conduct threatens to collapse -- he is frank enough to say that the "Cosmos" of Duty is thereby reduced to a "Chaos." What most intro-ethics summaries of this "dualism of practical reason" leave out is that Sidgwick did not treat it as a merely academic embarrassment: for the last eighteen years of his life he was co-founder and longest-serving president of the Society for Psychical Research, personally overseeing investigations into telepathy, apparitions, and mediums in the explicit hope of finding empirical evidence bearing on personal survival of death and a moral government of the universe -- the very guarantee his ethics seemed to require.

My friend thinks this was Sidgwick behaving exactly as his own philosophy demanded: having located a real gap that pure reasoning could not close on its own, he went and looked, with genuine scientific discipline, for evidence that might close it -- discipline strict enough that Sidgwick himself commissioned and later helped endorse the investigation that unmasked Madame Blavatsky as a fraud rather than confirming her powers. On this reading, the psychical research is not a lapse from Sidgwick the philosopher but its most admirable extension: intellectual honesty followed all the way through. I argued the opposite: that the whole project rests on a non sequitur, since no finding about telepathy, or about the survival of consciousness after bodily death, could by itself settle a normative question about whether we have most reason to favor our own good or the good of all; and that eighteen years spent not noticing this looks less like philosophy than like a very intelligent man quietly managing his own metaphysical anxiety. We got nowhere, and ended up just agreeing that Sidgwick was "complicated."

I would like this actually settled, or at least sharpened, rather than left as an amiable draw. Could you write an essay that reconstructs the strongest version of each position -- including how each side might answer the other's central objection -- and then defends a specific conclusion about whether Sidgwick's turn to psychical research was, by his own philosophical lights, rational and well-motivated, a kind of category mistake, or something more entangled than either label allows? I would rather have a precise verdict, even a heavily qualified one, than a conclusion that simply splits the difference between my friend's view and mine.
