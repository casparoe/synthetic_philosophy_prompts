I'm a software engineer who works on formal verification for safety-critical systems, so I spend a lot of my time thinking about proof assistants, model checkers, and the gap between a claim being merely plausible and a claim being machine-checked correct. A friend recently sent me some material on how Kurt Gödel's ontological proof of God's existence, the modal-logic argument he reportedly kept to himself for years and only revealed to a colleague once he thought he was dying, has since been formalized and run through automated theorem provers like Coq and Isabelle by researchers who confirmed that the derivation genuinely goes through. Since the underlying logic, S5 modal logic, is itself a well-understood, sound and complete system, and a machine has mechanically confirmed that Gödel's conclusion follows from his stated axioms using that system's own inference rules, this struck me as categorically different from an ordinary philosophical essay arguing for God's existence.

What I haven't been able to work out is why this is still treated, in most philosophical circles, as a live and controversial argument rather than something closer to a proven theorem that one can only reasonably reject by explicitly denying one of the named axioms, in the way you'd have to reject the axiom of choice to escape some of its stranger consequences. If the logic itself is beyond reproach and the proof-checking is exhaustive and mechanical, then rejecting the argument by denying a premise seems like a much weaker move than actually finding an error in the reasoning, comparable to an engineer who dislikes a formally verified program's behavior yet has no grounds to blame the verifier, only the specification that was fed into it. Am I misjudging how much weight this kind of formal verification is supposed to carry here, or are philosophers who reject the conclusion effectively doing a more sophisticated version of just denying an inconvenient premise?
