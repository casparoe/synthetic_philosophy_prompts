So I'm teaching the early modern seminar again this spring, and I got sidetracked re-reading Berkeley's Querist instead of actually prepping the syllabus, which is usually where my best ideas come from anyway. Everybody remembers the immaterialism, esse est percipi and all that, and basically nobody remembers that the same guy spent three pamphlets' worth of hundreds of these little numbered rhetorical questions arguing that gold and silver are basically beside the point for a nation's wealth — that money is, in his words, "altogether that of a ticket or counter," and that "power to command the industry of others" is the real wealth, not the metal. I'd never clocked before how close that is to his line on abstract ideas, and now I can't unsee it, so I want to run the idea by you before I embarrass myself with it in front of a room.

Here's the actual thought: in the Introduction to the Principles Berkeley goes out of his way to insist that communicating ideas isn't even the main job of language — words are just as often there to raise a passion, or push someone toward or away from some action, or (his phrase) "the putting the mind in some particular disposition." Its a use-based story about meaning, not a referential one: stop asking what a word stands for and ask what it does to the hearer. My hunch is the Querist runs the exact same move on money — stop asking what money IS (a commodity, a store of value, a thing with some worth already baked into it) and ask what it DOES (circulates credit, stirs up industry, keeps track of who owes who what). Same suspicion of "there's some substance backing this, and that's where the real value/meaning comes from" in both places, just aimed at a different target. If I'm right, the Querist isn't some weird detour from the metaphysics, its the metaphysics wearing a different hat.

Does that actually hold up, or am I forcing an analogy where there isn't one? I've got this nagging feeling somebody in the Berkeley literature has already made basically this link (something to do with his theory of spirits, maybe?) and I'm just reinventing a wheel I read about once and forgot the name of — tell me if that rings a bell for you. And separately, be honest with me about whether this is even interesting enough to build twenty minutes of a talk around, or whether it's the kind of connection that's cute for one slide and falls apart the second someone in the audience pushes on it.
