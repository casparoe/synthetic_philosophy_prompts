I've been researching a magazine feature on the recent wave of legislation extending welfare protections to cephalopods, and a remark from a philosopher on a panel I moderated last month has been nagging at me ever since. In 2021, a government-commissioned review by the London School of Economics concluded that there was strong scientific evidence that octopuses, crabs, and lobsters are sentient; the finding led the UK to fold decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs into the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022. Washington State went further still in March 2024, becoming the first jurisdiction in the world to ban octopus farming outright, with legislators explicitly invoking the animals' "advanced cognitive abilities" — tool use, apparent play, rapid problem-solving, even personality differences that keepers and researchers can reliably track.

None of this sits comfortably with the philosophical tradition I studied as an undergraduate, and Kant in particular is the one I can't stop turning over. His scattered remarks on animals, first in the Lectures on Ethics and later formalized in the Metaphysics of Morals, rest on the claim that we have no direct duties toward them at all, since they lack self-consciousness and, more importantly, the capacity to set and pursue ends through reason; on his view, cruelty toward animals is wrong only indirectly, because it coarsens the disposition we carry into our dealings with other people. He wrote all of this, of course, without any inkling of what we now know about cephalopod cognition, or of octopuses being evolutionarily about as distant from vertebrates as an animal can be while still assembling something that looks like a mind.

So I'd like your best reconstruction of how Kant would have responded if confronted with this evidence and this legislation. Does the sheer behavioral and cognitive sophistication documented in octopuses put any real pressure on his framework, or is the line he draws simply indifferent to that kind of evidence, since moral status for him was never really a matter of degree of intelligence but of a specific, rational form of autonomy he reserved for human beings alone? And if it's the latter, is there anything in Kant's own texts that would let his framework accommodate this evidence without abandoning its basic architecture, or would he have had to choose between revising the theory and simply denying that any of it matters morally?
