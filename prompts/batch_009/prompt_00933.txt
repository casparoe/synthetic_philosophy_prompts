A friend and I spent most of a long train journey last week arguing about whether trusting someone's word is ever an epistemically basic matter, or whether it always has to be underwritten by independent evidence of the speaker's reliability — essentially the reductionism/anti-reductionism dispute about testimony that runs from Hume and Reid down through Coady, Fricker, and Lackey. I was defending something like a reductionist line: that my confidence in any given piece of testimony ultimately has to answer to my accumulated, mostly inductive track record with witnesses, institutions, and the general correlation between what people say and how things turn out to be. My friend, who is writing a dissertation chapter on Reid, thought this was hopeless, and the next morning sent me the following passage from Reid's 1764 *An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense* (Chapter 6, "Of Seeing," Section XXIV, "Of the Analogy Between Perception, and the Credit We Give to Human Testimony") as, in her view, a decisive objection. The larger point of that section is that testimony resembles perception in resting on natural signs we interpret without the aid of prior experience, and it is in that context that Reid introduces his paired principles of veracity and credulity:

"Another original principle implanted in us by the Supreme Being, is a disposition to confide in the veracity of others, and to believe what they tell us. This is the counter part to the former; and as that may be called the principle of veracity, we shall, for want of a more proper name, call this the principle of credulity. It is unlimited in children, until they meet with instances of deceit and falsehood: and it retains a very considerable degree of strength through life. … And if nature had left the mind of the hearer in equilibrio, without any inclination to the side of belief more than to that of disbelief, we should take no man's word until we had positive evidence that he spoke truth. His testimony would, in this case, have no more authority than his dreams; which may be true or false, but no man is disposed to believe them, on this account, that they were dreamed. It is evident, that, in the matter of testimony, the balance of human judgment is by nature inclined to the side of belief… In a word, if credulity were the effect of reasoning and experience, it must grow up and gather strength, in the same proportion as reason and experience do. But if it is the gift of nature, it will be strongest in childhood, and limited and restrained by experience; and the most superficial view of human life shows, that the last is really the case, and not the first."

What I could not settle with my friend is what kind of argument the second half of this passage actually is. Reid seems to move directly from a claim about the psychological or developmental order in which credulity and skepticism appear — children start out maximally trusting and grow more discriminating only with experience, rather than the reverse — to a conclusion about epistemic status, namely that credulity is basic and owes nothing to induction. That inference strikes me as suspicious: even granting every developmental claim Reid makes, a committed reductionist could simply agree that the disposition to trust is unlearned and instinctive in its origin, while still maintaining that a mature, justified belief formed on the basis of testimony needs inductive backing, so that Reid has shown something interesting about the natural history of credulity without yet showing anything about what justifies it. Is that a fair diagnosis of a gap between a genetic and a normative claim in Reid's reasoning, or is there a better way of reading the passage — perhaps by taking the analogy with perception more seriously than I have — on which the developmental observation is doing genuine epistemological work rather than merely psychological work? I would also be curious whether this is a recognized line of criticism of Reid in the secondary literature, since it seems too obvious for me to be the first to have noticed it.
