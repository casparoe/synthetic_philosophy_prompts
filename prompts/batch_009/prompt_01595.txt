Last night a dinner conversation with a close friend spiraled into a full-blown argument about decision theory, of all things. The trigger was mundane: we were debating how likely a mutual friend's startup is to still be operating in two years, and after each of us had laid out our full reasoning, the evidence we'd seen, and our track records on similar predictions, we still landed on numbers nearly twenty points apart. I found this suspicious and said so — I argued that if we were both reasoning as ideal Bayesians, we ought to have started from the same prior in the first place, since nothing in rationality licenses favoring one prior over another absent some reason to do so, and that our remaining disagreement, once all of that reasoning and evidence was out in the open between us, had to be traceable to a mistake somewhere in one of our updating processes. To back this up, I brought up Aumann's agreement theorem, which I remembered as showing that two rational people cannot "agree to disagree" once their views are mutually known. My friend, who works in operations research, said I was helping myself to something the theorem doesn't actually license, though she couldn't pin down exactly what on the spot, and we ended the night no closer to resolving either the object-level or the meta-level dispute.

I'd like a more considered verdict than either of us managed over dessert. Was I right that Bayesian rationality by itself commits two agents to sharing a prior, so that persistent disagreement after a full exchange of reasoning is evidence that somebody reasoned badly? Or did I smuggle in assumptions — about priors, about what our dinner conversation actually established, or about what the theorem requires — that don't hold up? I'd like the underlying machinery spelled out clearly rather than just a ruling on who won, since I suspect I was more confident in my invocation of the theorem than I had any right to be.
