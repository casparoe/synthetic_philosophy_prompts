I'm outlining a novel where a lighthouse keeper dozes off during a storm, the lamp goes dark, and a ship wrecks with people killed. His lawyer's defense is that him not lighting the lamp couldn't have *caused* the wreck - omissions aren't events, so 'nothing' can't cause anything, at most explain it. That sent me down a rabbit hole on 'causation by omission': some philosophers think omissions can be real causes (via counterfactuals - if he'd lit it, the ship wouldn't have wrecked), others say causation needs an actual physical process from cause to effect, which 'nothing' can't supply. I even found a paper using the example of the Queen of England technically failing to water someone's dying houseplant, which apparently doesn't count as a cause of it's death - so what makes my keeper different?

Write me a dialogue between two philosophers who testified at the trial, hashing it out after, up on the lighthouse gallery - one defends the omission-as-cause view, one denies it. The first has to actually deal with the 'is the Queen a cause too' objection instead of dodging it, and the second has to explain why denying the keeper caused anything doesn't just let him off the hook morally. Real back-and-forth - both equally sharp, nobody strawmanned.
