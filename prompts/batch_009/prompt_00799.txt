A friend and I spent much of last evening arguing about the probability that a mutual friend's new restaurant will still be open in three years. We each laid out our full reasoning, conceded a handful of minor points, and revised our numbers once or twice along the way, yet ended the night almost exactly where we began: my estimate near 70 percent, hers near 30. Replaying the conversation afterward, I kept thinking about Aumann's agreement theorem, the result that two Bayesians who share a common prior cannot hold differing posteriors on an event once those posteriors are common knowledge between them.

The usual escapes are familiar enough. Perhaps our priors were never truly common, so that the "Harsanyi doctrine" — the assumption that any two rational people's beliefs can differ only because of differing information, never because of differing priors — simply doesn't apply to us. Perhaps swapping arguments over dinner is nothing like the precise, repeated exchange of numerical posteriors that Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis (1982) showed will force convergence in finite time. Or perhaps one or both of us were reasoning in some self-deceived, non-Bayesian fashion that had little to do with the evidence at all.

Write an essay that weighs these candidate explanations, and any others you think genuinely matter, for why thoughtful, well-informed people keep disagreeing even after fully disclosing their reasoning to one another. Don't end on a non-committal note: reach and defend a specific conclusion about which explanation is doing the most work in ordinary cases like this one, and about what, if anything, persistent post-disclosure disagreement of this kind should lead us to conclude about the rationality of the people involved.
