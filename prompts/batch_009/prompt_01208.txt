I'm teaching a graduate seminar on classical American pragmatism this semester, and every year I hit the same wall: students read a chapter called "pragmatism," see the line "truth is what works" attributed to both Peirce and James, and walk away thinking the two men basically agreed with each other. They didn't! Peirce got genuinely annoyed at what he thought James (and Schiller) were doing to his idea -- annoyed enough that in 1905 he rechristened his own view "pragmaticism," a name he cheerfully admitted was ugly enough that no self respecting kidnapper would bother stealing it. That's obviously a shot aimed at James, and I think theres a real, substantive disagreement buried under the joke that gets flattened every time pragmatism gets summarized in one sentence.

So here's what I want: a dialogue between Peirce and James where they actually hash this out face to face. Doesn't need to be historically photorealistic, but I like the idea of grounding it in something like the aftermath of the "pragmaticism" business -- especially given that this is a guy, James, who spent real effort getting Peirce paid lecture gigs and generally kept him afloat, even while apparently (in Peirce's eyes) mangling his philosophy. Have James push the idea that truth just is whatever proves good, concretely, for us to believe -- including on stuff like God or immortality, where he thinks a pragmatist has every right to let the "cash value" of a belief settle things. Have Peirce push back that this collapses truth into psychology, and get him to lay out his own picture: truth as the single opinion that inquiry is fated to converge on in the long run, for a whole community of inquirers, not whatever happens to satisfy one person right now.

But don't let Peirce just win by author fiat. James should land real hits too -- pressing on whether "the final opinion of the community" is something anyone could actually cash out pragmatically in the here and now, or whether Peirce is smuggling back in exactly the kind of unpragmatic metaphysics the pragmatic maxim was supposed to dissolve. A page or so feels about right. Mostly I want two guys who clearly respect each other having a real disagreement, not one of them just raising objections so the other can knock them down.
