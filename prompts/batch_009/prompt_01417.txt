I work as a backend engineer on distributed systems, and a conversation with a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD has left me fairly convinced that a classic result from my own field quietly undermines a famous result from hers. I'd like to know whether that's actually right, or whether I'm missing something.

She brought up Aumann's agreement theorem: the claim that two Bayesian agents who share the same prior cannot "agree to disagree" about some event once their posterior probabilities for it are common knowledge. But "common knowledge" is a term I already knew from a completely different context, since it's exactly the condition that the Two Generals Problem shows can never be reached when two parties communicate over a channel that might drop messages. However many acknowledgments the generals send back and forth, neither can ever be certain the other received the last one, so there is always one more unconfirmed layer of "does she know that I know that she knows" — common knowledge, in the strict infinite-regress sense, simply never gets nailed down.

Every real channel between two people, whether it's conversation, email, or a phone call, is at least a little unreliable in exactly this sense. So it seems to follow that two real people's posteriors can never actually become common knowledge, which means the antecedent of Aumann's theorem is never satisfied outside of toy models that just stipulate common knowledge by fiat. On this reasoning, two perfectly rational Bayesians who share a prior could always go on disagreeing indefinitely with a clear conscience, since the condition needed to force their posteriors together can never actually arise in practice. My friend was confident this argument goes wrong somewhere, but couldn't quite pin down where. Am I missing something, or does the impossibility result I know from distributed systems really leave Aumann's theorem with nothing to say about real, flesh-and-blood disagreement?
