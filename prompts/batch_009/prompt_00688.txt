I'm working on an article about the strange persistence of the ontological argument in contemporary philosophy. As I understand the history, Kant settled the matter back in the eighteenth century when he observed that existence is not a real predicate, undercutting the inference from "God is the most perfect conceivable being" to "God exists." Yet Kurt Gödel, hardly a careless thinker, spent real effort formalizing his own modal version of the argument in the twentieth century, showing it privately to the logician Dana Scott in 1970 and only allowing it to become public after his death. Given that Kant's objection is treated as decisive in most introductory accounts, why do logicians and philosophers of religion still take Gödel's proof, and the broader family of modal ontological arguments, seriously?
