I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forum threads on decision theory, where the Nash Bargaining Solution tends to get treated as something close to a default answer whenever the discussion turns to how two parties should split the gains from cooperation. Most recently I ran into it in a paper on "evidential cooperation in large worlds," which used the solution to model how causally disconnected civilizations with different values might still coordinate through correlated decision-making, and which mentioned, almost in passing, that it had considered the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution as an alternative but judged it less suitable for that purpose. That offhand remark sent me down a rabbit hole into the original 1975 Kalai-Smorodinsky paper and the surrounding cooperative-bargaining-theory literature, and I came out the other side able to state the formal difference between the two solutions without having much gut-level feeling for why it actually matters.

Here's where I've gotten stuck. Both solutions agree on Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine rescaling of utility, and differ only in that Nash keeps Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) while Kalai and Smorodinsky swap it out for an axiom called individual monotonicity. As far as I can piece together, the motivating worry is that IIA permits something genuinely strange: you can enlarge the feasible set in a way that raises the best payoff available to player 2 -- without changing anything about what player 1 could achieve on their own -- and the Nash solution can respond by handing player 2 *less* utility than it did before the set expanded. I take this to be the central pathology that individual monotonicity was designed to rule out, since Kalai-Smorodinsky instead pins the outcome to the point on the Pareto frontier that preserves the ratio of each player's gain relative to their own "ideal point." But I can't yet reconstruct, from first principles, why enlarging the feasible set would ever push the Nash product's maximizer in that perverse direction, and I don't have a solid feel for why anchoring on the ideal point sidesteps the problem.

Could you help me build this intuition properly rather than just restating the definitions at me, which is about as far as I've gotten on my own? Concretely, I'd find it most useful if you could (a) construct or describe a small, concrete feasible-set example where the Nash product's maximizer visibly moves the "wrong" way as the set expands, so I can see the mechanism rather than just being told it exists, (b) give a geometric or otherwise intuitive account of why the Kalai-Smorodinsky construction is immune to that specific failure mode, and (c) share your honest sense of how bargaining theorists actually weigh IIA against individual monotonicity in practice -- whether one is generally treated as more normatively compelling than the other, or whether it really is more of a genuine values trade-off with no consensus favorite.
