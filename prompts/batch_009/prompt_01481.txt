I spent about thirty-five years doing statistical mechanics before I retired, and these days I mostly fill my time reading philosophy and decision theory, which is how I fell down a rabbit hole about the Kelly criterion. The basic idea — bet a fixed fraction of your bankroll each round so as to maximize the long-run geometric growth rate — gets presented in most textbooks as just a special case of expected utility theory, one where your utility function happens to be log(wealth). But then I found the "ergodicity economics" work of Ole Peters (a physicist by training, PhD from Imperial, which is probably why it appealed to me), who argues thats not really the right way to think about it at all. His claim is that wealth grows multiplicatively, which makes the process non-ergodic, so the quantity you actually want to optimize is the time-average growth rate a single agent experiences over repeated bets, not the ensemble average across possible worlds that ordinary expected-value reasoning is quietly built on. He presents this as more "objective" than orthodox choice theory, since supposedly you don't need to posit any subjective utility function or risk preference at all — the log just falls out of the dynamics.

This has become a running argument with an old colleague of mine from my postdoc days. He thinks Peters is doing completely standard expected-log-utility maximization dressed up with physics vocabulary for effect, while I keep feeling like there's something genuinely different going on with what people call "geometric rationality" versus the usual arithmetic-expected-value kind, even if I can't quite pin down what. To make matters worse I also dug up that old Samuelson paper from 1979 attacking mean-log-of-wealth maximization as a general decision rule, "Why We Should Not Make Mean Log of Wealth Big Though Years to Act Are Long" — apparently written entirely in words of one syllable as a joke, except for the very last word.

Anyway, could you help me actually sort this out? I'd like a proper, detailed comparison of the ergodicity/time-average case for Kelly betting against the classic expected-utility case for it: is "maximize your time-average growth rate" a genuinely distinct normative principle from "maximize expected utility for some suitably risk-averse agent," or is it the same mathematical content wearing a different philosophical costume? And does this so-called geometric rationality actually escape the charge Samuelson leveled at plain old Kelly betting, namely that its a fine rule for repeated multiplicative gambles but gets illegitimately stretched into something it was never meant to be?
