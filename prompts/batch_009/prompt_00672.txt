I have no formal background in physics or philosophy, but I have spent a long time working through the "quantum immortality" argument on my own, and there is a wrinkle in it that most fiction built around the idea seems to miss. The argument only guarantees survival from the inside: conditional on still being around to notice, you keep finding yourself alive after a quantum-suicide trial, no matter how bad the odds were. Nobody else gets that guarantee: a friend watching you play quantum Russian roulette sees you die in nearly every branch, and if you really do die, there is no surviving version of them who follows you into the branch where you did not. I would like a short story, around 1,000–1,500 words, built around that asymmetry — a character who comes to believe the argument deeply enough to act on it, written so that the people around them carry the emotional weight of the branches that do not go their way, rather than the believer carrying it. Please make the actual mechanics of the argument — branching, first-person continuity, and the fact that none of this covers ordinary gradual deaths like illness or aging — do real work in the plot, rather than just supplying mood and vocabulary for a story that could be about any other flavor of immortality.
