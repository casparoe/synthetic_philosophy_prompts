So in my philosophy of math seminar we just wrapped up the unit on Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and my professor mentioned this thing in passing that I can't stop thinking about. Everyone already knows about his ontological proof of God (the modal logic one based on Leibniz and Anselm's versions), but apparently Gödel also had a whole seperate, way more personal argument for why there has to be an afterlife, which he layed out in letters to his mother back in the early 60s -- I found a few of them quoted in Hao Wang's book on him. The basic idea, if I'm getting it right: the world is "rationally constructed," meaning everything in it has a reason the same way everything has a cause, and since a person's life clearly never gets to realize more than a tiny fraction of what they were capable of before they die, a truly rational world couldn't just let all that potential go to waste -- so there has to be some further existence where the rest of it plays out.

Me and a couple people from my study group got into it about whether this actually counts as a real argument or if it's just a brilliant logician doing very elegant wishful thinking about his own mortality. I want an actual verdict though, not a "well there's something to be said on both sides" cop-out. Can you write me an essay that reconstructs Gödel's reasoning as charitably as you can, lays out the strongest objections to it (something about the jump from "the world is intelligible and law-governed" to "the world is meaningful in a way that guarantees nothing gets wasted" feels fishy to me, but I can't fully articulate why), and then actually commits to whether you think the argument holds up, and why?
