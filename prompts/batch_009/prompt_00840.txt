I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole last week after a thread on a rationalist forum about modal realism spun off into an argument about whether David Lewis's counterpart theory actually solves the problem of transworld identity or just quietly changes the subject. Someone mentioned, almost in passing, that Leibniz had wrestled with essentially the same knot three centuries earlier, while defending his doctrine of complete individual concepts against Arnauld, using Julius Caesar as the test case. That sent me to the primary text instead of trusting the paraphrase — specifically §13 of the Discourse on Metaphysics, in George Montgomery's 1902 translation — and now I'm stuck on what exactly Leibniz thinks he has established by the end of it. The relevant stretch (I've cut a couple of digressive sentences in the middle, marked with ellipses) reads:

"But before going further it is necessary to meet a difficulty which may arise regarding the principles which we have set forth in the preceding. We have said that the concept of an individual substance includes once for all everything which can ever happen to it and that in considering this concept one will be able to see everything which can truly be said concerning the individual, just as we are able to see in the nature of a circle all the properties which can be derived from it. But does it not seem that in this way the difference between contingent and necessary truths will be destroyed, that there will be no place for human liberty, and that an absolute fatality will rule as well over all our actions as over all the rest of the events of the world? [...] Let us give an example. Since Julius Caesar will become perpetual Dictator and master of the Republic and will overthrow the liberty of Rome, this action is contained in his concept, for we have supposed that it is the nature of such a perfect concept of a subject to involve everything, in fact so that the predicate may be included in the subject ut possit inesse subjecto. We may say that it is not in virtue of this concept or idea that he is obliged to perform this action, since it pertains to him only because God knows everything. But it will be insisted in reply that his nature or form responds to this concept, and since God imposes upon him this personality, he is compelled henceforth to live up to it. [...] For it would be found that this demonstration of this predicate as belonging to Caesar is not as absolute as are those of numbers or of geometry, but that this predicate supposes a sequence of things which God has shown by his free will. [...] Now every truth which is founded upon this kind of decree is contingent, although certain, for the decrees of God do not change the possibilities of things and, as I have already said, although God assuredly chooses the best, this does not prevent that which is less perfect from being possible in itself. Although it will never happen, it is not its impossibility but its imperfection which causes him to reject it. Now nothing is necessitated whose opposite is possible."

What I can't quite pin down is which side of the modern debate this actually lands on. On one hand, Leibniz clearly wants to say it is contingent, not necessary, that Caesar crossed the Rubicon — its "opposite is possible," in his phrase — which only seems to make sense if we're talking about one and the same Caesar under two different courses of history, rather like the view Kripke later builds out of treating names as rigid designators that latch onto one individual across counterfactual scenarios. That reads as a straightforward commitment to transworld identity. On the other hand, his official doctrine — that an individual's complete concept "includes once for all everything which can ever happen to him" — seems to imply that anything differing from Caesar in even one respect simply isn't Caesar, but at best something resembling him. That looks much closer to Lewis's move: rather than one individual living through alternate histories, you get numerically distinct counterparts standing in for one another across possible sequences of events.

So I'd like help sorting out whether there's a standard way of reading this passage that dissolves that tension, or whether "contingent, but still Caesar" is simply not compatible with the complete-concept doctrine as Leibniz states it — in which case he'd be helping himself to transworld identity in one breath and something like counterpart theory in the next. More generally, once the complete-concept doctrine is taken seriously, is there any principled way of distinguishing "Caesar could have not crossed the Rubicon" from "some Caesar-resembling individual in another possible sequence didn't cross it" — or does any essentialism this strong eventually have to concede the point to Lewis, whether or not it says so openly?
