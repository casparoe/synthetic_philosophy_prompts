As a software engineer, the only "conditionals" I had seriously thought about before this year were if-statements, but a detour into philosophy of language got me curious about how logicians treat ordinary indicative conditionals — things like "if the coin I just flipped landed heads, you owe me five dollars." I've since come across Stalnaker's Thesis, the claim that the probability of such a conditional is just the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, and David Lewis's 1976 result showing that this thesis, combined with the innocuous-looking Import-Export law governing nested conditionals, forces the conditional's probability to collapse onto the bare unconditional probability of the consequent whenever the antecedent doesn't settle the consequent's truth. Every source I've found lays out the short algebraic derivation and declares the conclusion unacceptable, without dwelling on why that specific combination of assumptions is what does the damage.

Could you build up the intuition for why this triviality result goes through — showing what work each assumption is really doing, rather than just re-deriving it symbolically — and then briefly walk through the main escape routes philosophers have proposed (for instance, denying that indicative conditionals have truth conditions at all, or making the semantics of "if" context-sensitive), noting what each one costs?
