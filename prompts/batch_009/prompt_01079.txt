I trained as an experimental physicist and spent most of my working life worrying about the propagation of measurement uncertainty, and it is only since retiring three years ago that I have had time to read philosophy in any systematic way. A former colleague who now teaches decision theory recently walked me through the Ellsberg paradox: the finding that people prefer betting on an urn with a known fifty-fifty split of red and black balls to betting on an otherwise identical urn whose split is unknown, even though the two bets have exactly the same objective odds. Wanting to see how far back this worry about "ambiguity," as opposed to ordinary risk, actually goes, I ended up in Chapter VI of Keynes's 1921 Treatise on Probability, titled "The Weight of Arguments," which seems to me to be groping toward much the same distinction some forty years earlier. I have read the chapter several times now, but I cannot quite settle what to make of it, and I would value your help.

The relevant passage, given here with some cuts (I have marked Keynes's own paragraph numbers in brackets), runs as follows: "[§1] The magnitude of the probability of an argument ... depends upon a balance between what may be termed the favourable and the unfavourable evidence; a new piece of evidence which leaves this balance unchanged, also leaves the probability of the argument unchanged. ... This comparison turns upon a balance, not between the favourable and the unfavourable evidence, but between the absolute amounts of relevant knowledge and of relevant ignorance respectively. As the relevant evidence at our disposal increases, the magnitude of the probability of the argument may either decrease or increase ... but something seems to have increased in either case—we have a more substantial basis upon which to rest our conclusion. I express this by saying that an accession of new evidence increases the weight of an argument. New evidence will sometimes decrease the probability of an argument, but it will always increase its 'weight.' ... [§6] ... The typical case, in which there may be a practical connection between weight and probable error, may be illustrated by the two cases following of balls drawn from an urn. In each case we require the probability of drawing a white ball; in the first case we know that the urn contains black and white in equal proportions; in the second case the proportion of each colour is unknown, and each ball is as likely to be black as white. It is evident that in either case the probability of drawing a white ball is 1/2 but that the weight of the argument in favour of this conclusion is greater in the first case. ... [§7] ... it seems plausible to suppose that we ought to take account of the weight as well as the probability of different expectations. But it is difficult to think of any clear example of this, and I do not feel sure that the theory of 'evidential weight' has much practical significance."

What I cannot work out is the gap between §6 and §7. Keynes seems to construct, almost by accident, the very case that Ellsberg would later use to demonstrate that "weight" (or what we would now call ambiguity) has a systematic and measurable effect on choice, and then he immediately loses his nerve, professing that he cannot think of a "clear example" of weight mattering to action. Could you first help me get straight, in your own words, on the distinction Keynes wants to draw between the weight and the probability of an argument, and then help me understand why he might have hesitated exactly where he did? Is there a genuine conceptual difference between Keynesian weight and what decision theorists now mean by ambiguity, such that his caution was reasonable on its own terms, or does his retreat look, in hindsight, like a step he could not quite bring himself to take, one that had to wait for Savage's axioms and Ellsberg's experiment to complete?
