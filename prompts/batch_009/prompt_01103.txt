In a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion that I teach most years, we eventually arrive at a piece of Kurt Gödel's thinking that surprises even students already familiar with his incompleteness theorems and his formal ontological proof of God. It is the private, decidedly untechnical case for an afterlife that he laid out across several letters to his mother, Marianne, in the summer and autumn of 1961. Gödel's argument, in outline, runs like this: the world displays far too much regularity and order to be considered arbitrary or chaotic, so it must be fundamentally rational; a rational world could not tolerate the waste of meaning, talent, and unsettled moral accounts that an ordinary human life leaves behind at death; and reason therefore requires some further existence in which what this life leaves unfinished gets completed — a Wiedersehen, a "seeing again," as he and his mother put it to one another.

What has always struck me is this argument's own afterlife in Gödel's biography. Sixteen years later, in the winter of 1977, his wife Adele — by then the only person he trusted to prepare or taste his food, owing to a longstanding delusion that he was being poisoned — was hospitalized for several months. Left on his own, Gödel simply stopped eating. He died in January 1978 weighing around sixty-five pounds, his death certificate citing malnutrition brought on by "personality disturbance." The most exacting logician of his century, a man who had reasoned his way to confidence in a universe too orderly to let anything ultimately go to waste, could not extend an ounce of that confidence to a meal prepared by anyone other than his wife.

I've been turning over the idea of writing a short story around this material for a piece I contribute to our department newsletter each spring, and I'd like to see what you make of it before I attempt my own draft. Could you write a short story, something in the neighborhood of 1,000 to 1,500 words, set in Princeton during those last months while Adele is away? The vantage point is yours to choose — Gödel's own consciousness, or that of a colleague, nurse, or visitor who glimpses his condition — and you should feel free to invent scenes, dialogue, and incidental detail. What I'd like preserved is the philosophical substance: the story should leave a reader with a genuine feel for why Gödel thought a rational world entailed something like a Wiedersehen, and it should sit with the irony that this conviction, reached through the very faculty he trusted most, proved powerless against a fear no proof could touch.
