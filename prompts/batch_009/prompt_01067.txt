I spent my working life as a physicist, mostly in experimental condensed-matter work, with a late-career detour into the thermodynamics of computation, back when the first experiments testing Landauer's bound were being designed. Since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy discussion group at the university near me, and Thomson's lamp occupied us for two sessions this term. I came away unsatisfied with where the group left things: with Thomson's own 1954 argument that the lamp must end up neither on nor off, and Benacerraf's 1962 rejoinder that this simply doesn't follow, since nothing about the lamp's state "at" two minutes is entailed by its states beforehand. Since then I've drafted some notes toward a short piece arguing that the puzzle can be sidestepped for a much more mundane reason, once you take the physics of switching seriously. I'd value your comments before I inflict a fuller version on the group.

The core idea: never mind what state the lamp would be in at t = 2 minutes; no physical lamp could ever arrive there. My first instinct was to invoke Landauer's principle directly and say each flick costs at least kT ln 2, but I think that move is too quick, since a bare on/off toggle is just the bijection swapping two states, and Landauer's bound is a statement about logically irreversible operations like erasure, not reversible ones like this one — so in principle a sufficiently idealized toggle might cost arbitrarily little. What I think survives that worry is a cruder, more device-level point. Driving the dissipation per operation down toward the reversible ideal is only possible by running an operation more slowly, quasi-statically, never more quickly, so a switch capable of flicking in ever-shrinking intervals is, if anything, moving further from the zero-dissipation regime, not closer to it; every non-ideal switch has some strictly positive floor on energy cost per flick. Since infinitely many such flicks are packed into intervals shrinking geometrically to zero, the power draw needed to keep up — that floor, divided by the shrinking interval — diverges without bound as t approaches two minutes. Something in the apparatus, the switch, the supply, or the arm doing the flicking, must therefore fail at some finite time strictly before two minutes, so the disputed question of the lamp's terminal state never actually arises for anything physically built.

I'd be grateful for your reaction on three fronts. First, is the physics sound as I've stated it, and in particular is my hedge about Landauer's bound not applying to a mere toggle correct, or does the "floor on dissipation" I'm relying on secretly just reintroduce Landauer's bound by another route? Second, supposing the physics holds up, does an argument of this kind actually speak to what's traditionally at stake in the Thomson's lamp literature, where the puzzle is usually posed as one about logical or mathematical possibility, or have I merely changed the subject to nomological possibility in a way the original disputants would consider beside the point? Third, someone in the group mentioned Malament-Hogarth spacetimes as a reason relativists don't treat energetic arguments like mine as decisive; I don't understand those constructions well enough to know whether they would let a switch flick at an unchanging, bounded local cost while still completing infinitely many flicks "from the outside," which would undercut my whole argument, or whether that's a misunderstanding on my part. I haven't been able to find anything that runs the argument quite this way already, but I would rather be told I've reinvented a known wheel, or missed an obvious hole, than publish something naïve.
