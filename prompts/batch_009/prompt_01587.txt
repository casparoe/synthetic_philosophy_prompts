I'm taking a seminar this term that's cross-listed between philosophy and cognitive science. We recently spent two weeks on the history of empiricism, working through the Locke-Molyneux correspondence, Leibniz's rebuttal, and the eighteenth-century fallout from Cheselden's 1728 cataract case. Molyneux's problem is presented in our course reader as a genuinely open question: whether someone born blind, having learned to distinguish a cube from a globe by touch, could tell them apart by sight alone the moment they gained vision. While researching this for my term paper, though, I came across the Project Prakash studies out of MIT, in which children with congenital cataracts in India had their sight surgically restored and were then tested on exactly this kind of cross-modal matching. The results struck me as about as decisive as empirical philosophy ever gets: right after surgery, the children performed barely above chance when asked to pick out, by sight alone, a shape they already knew by touch, even though they could easily tell the same shapes apart using touch alone or vision alone. That looks to me like a direct, real-world "no" to the exact question Locke and Molyneux debated three centuries ago.

Given this, I don't understand why my professor, and most of the secondary literature I've read since, still calls Molyneux's problem "unresolved" instead of treating it as a question that has actually gotten a real empirical answer, the way a scientific dispute gets settled by a decisive experiment. Is there some specific flaw in treating the Project Prakash results as the answer, or is this mainly a case of philosophers being slow to update on findings that a cognitive scientist would consider fairly conclusive? I'd like to open my paper with the claim that the empirical evidence has essentially closed the debate, but I wanted to sanity-check that framing before building the rest of the argument around it.
