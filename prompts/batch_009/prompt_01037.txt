I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and in retirement I have been teaching myself epistemic logic, beginning with Hintikka's possible-worlds treatment of knowledge. What stops me in my tracks is the problem of logical omniscience: on the standard semantics, anyone who knows even a handful of axioms is thereby credited with knowing every tautology and every consequence of those axioms, as if knowing Maxwell's equations meant automatically knowing every field configuration that satisfies them. I can see why this falls out of the formalism, but I would like a more intuitive feel for why it is so difficult to avoid, and for how the two main families of proposed fixes operate: one, associated with Hintikka and Rantala, admits 'impossible worlds' into the model so that logically equivalent propositions can come apart; the other, due to Fagin and Halpern, keeps ordinary possible worlds intact but overlays a syntactic 'awareness' filter on top of them. Could you explain why each move blocks the omniscience result, what conceptual price each pays for doing so, and what that suggests about what we ought to mean by 'knowledge' in the first place?
