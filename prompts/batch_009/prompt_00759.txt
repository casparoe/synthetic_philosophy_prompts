After forty years as an experimental physicist, now retired, I am entirely at home with conditional probabilities, but I've only recently turned to philosophy, and I've just worked through David Lewis's 1976 paper "Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities." There he refutes Stalnaker's thesis that the probability of an indicative conditional "if A, C" equals the conditional probability P(C|A), by showing that requiring this equation to survive updates on new evidence forces absurd consequences except in degenerate cases. I follow the shape of the argument but find it frustratingly abstract without numbers attached. Could you construct your own concrete example? Choose two substantive, evidentially connected propositions A and C (a natural cause and its effect, say, or a hypothesis and a piece of evidence for it) from an everyday or scientific setting rather than the stock election, dice, or weather cases in the literature; assign specific probabilities to every relevant combination of them and their negations; and show, step by step, which update on the evidence makes the equation collapse. Close with a sentence on what this is supposed to show about whether indicative conditionals are genuine propositions at all.
