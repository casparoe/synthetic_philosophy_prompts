After a career spent running a biochemistry lab, I have spent much of my retirement finally reading philosophy properly, and this year that has meant working slowly through Nietzsche. I have been struck by a strand of his writing that does not seem to get much attention outside the specialist literature, and I have written up my thoughts as a short set of notes rather than a polished essay. I would value comments on two things in particular: whether I am reading the primary texts fairly, and whether the tension I think I have found is a genuine problem or one Nietzsche has the resources to dissolve.

In "Why I Am So Clever" (Ecce Homo), Nietzsche argues that questions of nutrition, climate, locality, and recreation matter more than the traditional vocabulary of philosophy — "God," "soul," "virtue," and so on — and he devotes real space to his own regimen: German cuisine is blamed for giving the German mind chronic indigestion, English cooking is dismissed as a "return to... cannibalism" that leaves the intellect with "heavy feet," and he describes swinging from an early, Wagner-influenced flirtation with vegetarianism to a settled hostility toward it, along with a growing conviction that alcohol was ruinous to him personally. This is not just memoir-writing. It connects to a claim he makes elsewhere, in Beyond Good and Evil, that every great philosophy is at bottom an "involuntary and unconscious" autobiography of its author's drives and constitution. Read together, these passages suggest a genuinely naturalistic thesis: that a thinker's physiology does not merely accompany their ideas but partly explains, and perhaps discredits, them.

What strikes me, as someone trained to distrust conclusions drawn from a sample of one, is that Nietzsche himself names this exact mistake elsewhere. In "The Four Great Errors" (Twilight of the Idols), he mocks the Venetian Cornaro for recommending his own frugal diet as a universal recipe for a long life, when in fact it was Cornaro's unusually slow metabolism that made a meagre diet bearable for him, not the diet that produced his longevity — a textbook case, on Nietzsche's own account, of confusing cause and effect. Yet a migraine-prone, dyspeptic man with a marked taste for solitude and particular climates then proceeds, at some length and with real conviction, to generalize about which foods, climates, and company are good for "spirit" as such. His later notion of a "great health" — one that, he says, can coexist with, or even be produced by, serious illness — complicates this further, since it suggests that "health" in his sense was never simply a biological baseline to begin with.

So: is this a real inconsistency, or am I flattening a distinction Nietzsche means to preserve — between diagnosing other people's illegitimate generalizations and offering his own case merely as one perspective among many? And is there a serious, defensible idea buried in this "philosophy as physiology" thesis, or should it be read as a rhetorical pose that collapses under any real epistemological weight? I would welcome pushback on any part of this, including on whether I have the texts right.
