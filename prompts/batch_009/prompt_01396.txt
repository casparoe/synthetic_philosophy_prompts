I've spent a lot of quiet time turning over the same problem on my own: whatever I believe, if you keep asking "why do you believe that?", the chain of reasons either stops somewhere, loops back on itself, or goes on without end. I gather philosophers have names for these three ways out — foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism, the last being mainly associated with Peter Klein — but it's the "goes on without end" option that I can't make peace with. If a belief is only justified when it's backed by an infinite, non-repeating series of further reasons, and no human mind could ever actually hold that many reasons at once, it seems like nobody could be justified in believing anything at all. I've read that infinitists answer this by saying the further reasons only need to be "available" on request rather than actually rehearsed, but that reply bothers me: if all that ever has to show up in practice is the next reason in line, while the rest of the infinite series never actually needs to appear, how is that really different from just calling that next reason a foundation and stopping there?

Write a dialogue between an infinitist and a foundationalist, matched in skill, who work this out using an ordinary example, such as why you believe the milk in your fridge is still good. The foundationalist should press the charge that "availability" is just foundationalism wearing a disguise, and the infinitist needs to give a genuine answer rather than simply repeating the original position.
