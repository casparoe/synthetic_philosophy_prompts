I'm a philosophy PhD student currently stuck on a dissertation chapter about Sidgwick's "dualism of practical reason" (that whole mess near the end of the Methods of Ethics where he can't get egoism and utilitarianism to count as equally rational without some extra assumption propping the whole thing up). Working on this sent me down a rabbit hole into why Sidgwick spent the better part of two decades running the Society for Psychical Research - sitting in on seances, chasing down mediums, the whole bit - instead of just writing more ethics. Every secondary source just says some version of "he wanted empirical proof of an afterlife" and moves on, so I went hunting for the actual primary text everyone's paraphrasing. What I found is this note of his from 1880, reprinted in the Memoir his wife and brother put together after he died (the ellipses are in the original, not mine):

"I do not know whether I believe or merely hope that there is a moral order in this universe that we know, a supreme principle of Wisdom and Benevolence, guiding all things to good ends, and to the happiness of the good … All I can say is that no opposed explanation of the origin of the cosmos … seems to me even plausible, and that I cannot accept life on any other terms, or construct a rational system of my own conduct except on the basis of this faith."

Ive read this like five times and I dont think im parsing it right. Two things trip me up. First, what's the "believe vs. merely hope" distinction actually doing - is that just Victorian throat-clearing about not being fully certain, or is he tracking something real, like the difference between assenting to a proposition and adopting something as a practical postulate because you need it (kind of a Kantian move)? Second, and this is the one that really gets me: how is a "rational system of conduct" supposed to depend on "faith" in a moral order at all, for a guy who's basically the poster child for scrupulous, dispassionate argument in Victorian ethics? Is he just being emotionally honest in a private note in a way that has nothing to do with the official arguments of the Methods, or is this basically him confessing, off the record, to the exact gap that the dualism of practical reason names - like the private Sidgwick here is just saying outright what the published Sidgwick can only gesture at? Curious how you'd read what's actually going on in this passage.
