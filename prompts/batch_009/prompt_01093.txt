So last week we had this whole derailment during a design review because I pointed out that our new "REST" api isnt really RESTful. Like, from what I remember reading a while back, the guy who actually coined the term (Roy Fielding, in his phd dissertation back in 2000) says real REST is supposed to involve this whole hypermedia thing where the responses themselves contain links that drive what the client does next (HATEOAS), on top of statelessness, a uniform interface, etc, and apparently he's spent years on his own blog complaining that almost nobody's "REST API" actually qualifies. Our api is literally just JSON over HTTP with normal /users/123 style endpoints and zero links anywhere in the payloads. My coworker got kind of annoyed and said something like "literally everyone on this team, and honestly like 90% of the industry, calls this stuff REST, so that IS what REST means at this point -- you're just being a purist about a word nobody uses that way anymore." Honestly I don't think he's totally wrong either, since language obviously changes over time, so maybe I'm the one being dumb here.

Mostly as a joke I brought this up with an old college friend who did a philosophy minor, and she got this look on her face and said it reminded her of some thought experiment about a planet just like earth except the clear drinkable stuff everyone there calls "water" turns out to be a totally different chemical, and that the whole point was that a word doesn't just mean whatever's in a persons head when they say it. She couldn't really reconstruct the actual argument though, or say whether it even applies to something like an api design style instead of an actual substance like water, so now its just been bugging me.

Is there an actual fact of the matter about whether our api is "really" REST, or is that a confused question to even ask? And if there is a fact of the matter, what fixes it -- whatever Fielding originally meant when he coined the term, or how the word actually gets used by the people applying it today? Zooming out even further: when a technical term's everyday usage drifts really far from the original technical definition, does the original coiner just get to be right forever, or is there some point where usage overrules them? I'd genuinely like a real argued-out answer on this, not a "well it depends" -- even if the honest answer turns out messy.
