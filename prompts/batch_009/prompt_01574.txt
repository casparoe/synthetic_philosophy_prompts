For my seminar on early Chinese philosophy of language, we've been working through Gongsun Long's "On the White Horse" (Baima Lun), and I'm stuck on whether the central argument is a genuine logical point or just wordplay. Here is the opening exchange, lightly paraphrased from our course translation:

"Can one say that a white horse is not a horse?" "One can." "How so?" "'Horse' is that by which we name a shape; 'white' is that by which we name a color. What names a color is not what names a shape. Hence I say: a white horse is not a horse."

Later in the dialogue, the Advocate adds that someone who wants "a horse" will accept a yellow or black one, while someone who wants "a white horse" will not, and treats this difference in what counts as satisfying the request as further proof that "white horse" and "horse" pick out different things.

My professor mentioned in passing that commentators disagree about the sense of "is not" (非, fei) being used here: some read it as "is not a member of the class of," others as "is not identical to," and others still (following Chad Hansen's mass-noun interpretation) think that framing the problem in terms of class-membership versus identity is already the wrong lens for classical Chinese nouns. Could you help me reconstruct these competing readings, say which one the Advocate's own reasoning in the passage actually seems to rely on, and tell me whether the argument still comes out valid, rather than merely equivocal, once we're charitable about which sense of "is not" is intended?
