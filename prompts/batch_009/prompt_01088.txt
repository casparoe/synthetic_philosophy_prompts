I'm a PhD student in political theory writing a dissertation chapter on early modern accounts of the foundations of political authority, and I've gotten stuck on how to read one of Pascal's more compressed fragments. I'd value a careful outside reading of it.

The fragment (Brunschvicg 298 / Lafuma 103), in Trotter's translation, runs:

"Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

A few fragments earlier (294), Pascal writes that custom is "the mystical foundation" of law's authority -- a phrase he draws from Montaigne, and the one Derrida later borrows for the title of "Force of Law" -- and approves the claim that "the wisest of legislators" judged it "necessary to deceive men for their own good," concealing the "fact of usurpation" at law's root so it may pass for "authoritative, eternal."

What I can't settle is whether 298 does the same thing as 294 or something different. Read as continuous with it, 298 looks performative rather than merely descriptive: Pascal is demonstrating, for readers able to follow the compression, exactly the unmasking that 294 says must never reach the people, while presumably still expecting obedience to continue undisturbed. Read on its own, though, 298 has no obvious addressee or payoff -- it simply states, almost as political sociology, that might and justice get identified because only might is "easily recognised." On the first reading Pascal is knowingly practicing a two-tier strategy of the kind he elsewhere recommends; on the second he is just an analyst with no stake in what readers do with the analysis.

Could you help me adjudicate between these readings, or show me that the contrast is overdrawn? I'd also value your view on whether Pascal's theology adds anything Montaigne's version lacks: since for Pascal the unreliability of unaided reason is a consequence of the Fall rather than a brute fact about the world, does 298 carry a diagnostic, even penitential, weight that a purely skeptical, Montaignean reading would miss -- or is that a distinction without a difference once both arrive at the same practical conclusion? Please reason closely from the wording of these fragments rather than from general summaries of Pascal's politics.
