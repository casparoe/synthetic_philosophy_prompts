I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums (LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias archives, and so on) for years, and one piece of Bayesian folklore that gets invoked constantly in discussions of rationality is I.J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information — the same I.J. Good who worked with Turing at Bletchley Park. The theorem says that for an idealized rational agent, the expected utility of costlessly observing evidence before choosing an action is never lower than the expected utility of choosing without it, and is strictly higher whenever there is some chance the evidence would change what you'd do. I've always taken this as more or less settling the question of whether ignorance can be instrumentally rational: turning down free, decision-relevant evidence looks like a fairly direct violation of Bayesian rationality, whatever comfort it might buy you.

This came up concretely a few weeks ago. A close friend has a parent with Huntington's disease, which puts her at 50% risk of carrying the mutation herself. Her hospital offers the predictive genetic test free of charge as part of a research program, and it would tell her, with certainty, whether she carries it. She has already ruled out having biological children regardless of the result, and there is no treatment or preventive regimen for asymptomatic carriers whose value would depend on knowing her status — yet she has firmly declined to be tested, saying she would rather not carry around either the dread of a positive result or the strange, unearned relief of a negative one. When I brought up Good's theorem to point out that she was leaving costless expected value on the table, she said I was treating her life like a decision-theory homework problem.

I would genuinely like to know who is right here. Is declining a free, perfectly informative test in a case like hers actually in tension with Good's theorem, or am I smuggling in some assumption of the theorem — about what counts as "free," or about what the relevant decision problem even is — that does not hold once nothing hinges on the answer? I would like to understand exactly where, if anywhere, my reasoning breaks down before I raise this with her again.
