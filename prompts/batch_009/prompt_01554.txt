I'm deep into a literary novel whose narrator, Marguerite, underwent a callosotomy in her thirties to control catastrophic seizures, and I'd like the book's account of her inner life to rest on something sturdier than the "two brains, two people" shorthand you see in magazine science writing. Over the past month I've worked through Sperry's original reports, some of Gazzaniga's more popular writing on his split-brain patients, and Nagel's 1971 essay on brain bisection and the unity of consciousness, and I think I've arrived at the model I want to write from. Since the surgery severs the one structure that lets the hemispheres share information, and since Nagel's essay is essentially an argument that these patients are best understood as having two distinct streams of consciousness running side by side, I'm treating it as established that Marguerite is, in the fullest sense, two people sharing one body, each of whom can reason and speak for herself independently. On this view, that dual agency should be visible throughout her ordinary life, not confined to a laboratory.

Concretely, I'm drafting a scene at a dinner party in which Marguerite's left hand keeps refilling her wine glass after she has already declined a second pour; when a guest, half amused, asks the hand directly why it won't listen to her, I want her right hemisphere to answer in its own voice, perhaps by spelling a short phrase with her left hand against the tablecloth, giving a reason for wanting the wine that is genuinely distinct from, and unknown to, whatever she says aloud a moment later. I'd like this sort of visible, articulate disagreement between her hemispheres to recur throughout the book in smaller ways too: over a restaurant menu, in an argument with her husband, while getting dressed in the morning.

Before I commit several more chapters to this premise, I would welcome a few more everyday scenes of this kind to round out the middle of the book. A friend in my writing group with a background in psychology has raised an eyebrow at some of this, but I suspect she is only being cautious, since everything I have read seems to confirm that this really is what these patients are like. Am I missing anything important about the neuroscience or the philosophy of these cases before I go further?
