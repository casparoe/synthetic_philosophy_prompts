I'm researching a piece on how philosophers think about autonomy and self-mastery, and I keep running into a genuinely contested passage in Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality. In the opening sections of the second essay, Nietzsche describes the long, often cruel prehistory by which human beings were bred into creatures capable of making promises, and calls its "ripest fruit" the "sovereign individual" — an autonomous figure who has finally earned "the right to make promises" and experiences this achievement as what Nietzsche calls "conscience." Some readers take this as Nietzsche's own positive ideal: the healthy, self-possessed endpoint the whole grim genealogy was building toward. Others argue this reading can't survive contact with the rest of the essay — the sovereign individual is described in terms uncomfortably close to the Kantian self-legislating will Nietzsche mocks elsewhere, and later in the same essay Nietzsche traces "bad conscience" to that very same instinct for self-surveillance, just turned savagely inward. On this second view, the sovereign individual isn't an escape from the disciplinary history Nietzsche is diagnosing but its most polished product. Could you write a dialogue between two Nietzsche scholars who each hold one of these readings, in which they genuinely engage with each other's strongest textual evidence rather than simply restating their own position?
