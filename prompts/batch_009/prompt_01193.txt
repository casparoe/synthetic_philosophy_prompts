hey, kind of a niche question but it's been bugging me. I'm re-reading the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence for a paper on the identity of indiscernibles (trying to line up Leibniz's actual argument against Black's two-spheres counterexample, the symmetric universe with nothing in it but two qualitatively identical spheres) and I noticed something in Leibniz's fourth letter that doesnt sit right. Everyone quotes the "two leaves in the garden" bit on its own, but never the paragraphs right around it. Here's the relevant chunk (this is Clarke's own 1717 translation, so forgive the capitalization):

"3. 'Tis a thing indifferent, to place three Bodies, equal and perfectly alike, in any order whatsoever; and consequently they will never be placed in Any order, by Him who does nothing without Wisdom. But then, He being the Author of things, no such things will be produced by him at all; and consequently there are no such things in Nature.

4. There is no such thing as Two Individuals indiscernible from each other. An Ingenious Gentleman of my Acquaintance, discoursing with me, in the presence of Her Electoral Highness the Princess Sophia, in the Garden of Herrenhausen; thought he could find two Leaves perfectly alike. The Princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the Garden a long time to look for some; but it was to no purpose.

...

6. To suppose two things indiscernible, is to suppose the same thing under two Names. And therefore to suppose that the Universe could have had at first another position of Time and Place, than that which it actually had; and yet that all the Parts of the Universe should have had the same Situation among themselves, as that which they actually had; such a Supposition, I say, is an impossible Fiction."

So here's my confusion: §3 sounds like a contingent claim - basically "a wise god just wouldn't bother making indiscernible duplicates" - which leaves it at least conceivable that indiscernibles could've existed, just not here. But §6 sounds way stronger, like two indiscernibles isnt even a coherent description of two things, it's just one thing described twice over. Those are pretty different theses and I genuinely can't tell if Leibniz thinks he's running one argument the whole time or if he's sliding between them without noticing.

Can you help me figure out which reading is actually doing the work here, and then what it implies for Black's spheres either way? If it's the weak §3 reading, Black's thought experiment looks like a totally fine counterexample. But if §6 is what he's really committed to, does Black even get to stipulate "there are two spheres" in the setup at all, or has he basically smuggled in the conclusion he needs before the argument even starts?
