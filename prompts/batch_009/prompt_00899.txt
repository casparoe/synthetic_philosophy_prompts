As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I first encountered "the frame problem" in an AI course as a narrow representational puzzle: in a planner based on the situation calculus, one cannot afford a separate axiom stating that every unaffected fact persists after each action, so some device such as Reiter's successor-state axioms is needed to capture that "inertia" economically. I have since learned that Dennett, and later Fodor, apply the same name to something broader: the problem of how any real-time reasoner bounds which of its beliefs are even relevant to check before acting, a difficulty Dennett dramatizes with his fable of robots that get blown up either for missing a relevant side effect of their own plan or for endlessly proving irrelevant ones.

Compare these two "frame problems." Is the philosophical version simply the engineering problem restated at the scale of an entire cognitive agent, so that a technical fix like successor-state axioms counts as genuine, if partial, progress on it? Or does Fodor's use of the frame problem to argue that central cognition cannot be modular show that it is a distinct and deeper problem, one that only borrows the engineering problem's name?
