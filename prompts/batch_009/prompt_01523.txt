I'm taking a seminar this semester on the philosophy of perception, and we're currently working through the historical background to the argument from illusion before we get to sense-datum theories and, eventually, disjunctivism. As part of that background, our instructor assigned the section of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature usually titled "Of scepticism with regard to the senses" (Book 1, Part 4, Section 2), on the grounds that Hume's reasoning about why we credit our perceptions with a "continu'd and distinct existence" already anticipates a lot of what shows up later in Price and Ayer. I've read through the section twice now and can follow the general shape of the argument, but there's one paragraph, right where Hume moves from describing the imagination's tendencies to citing actual "experiments," that I keep getting stuck on. Here it is, in the original spelling:

"'Twill first be proper to observe a few of those experiments, which convince us, that our perceptions are not possest of any independent existence. When we press one eye with a finger, we immediately perceive all the objects to become double, and one half of them to be remov'd from their common and natural position. But as we do not attribute a continu'd existence to both these perceptions, and as they are both of the same nature, we clearly perceive, that all our perceptions are dependent on our organs, and the disposition of our nerves and animal spirits. This opinion is confirm'd by the seeming encrease and diminution of objects, according to their distance; by the apparent alterations in their figure; by the changes in their colour and other qualities from our sickness and distempers; and by an infinite number of other experiments of the same kind; from all which we learn, that our sensible perceptions are not possest of any distinct or independent existence."

What I can't reconstruct is the logic of the middle sentence. Hume seems to be leaning on two distinct observations—that we don't "attribute a continu'd existence to both" of the doubled finger-images, and that the two images are "both of the same nature"—and then treating them as jointly sufficient to license a conclusion about all our perceptions whatsoever, not just the two produced by pressing on the eye. I can't see how those two clauses are supposed to combine to do that work, especially since a few paragraphs earlier he'd said it's precisely the resemblance between interrupted perceptions that leads the imagination to ascribe continued existence to them in the first place—which sounds like almost the reverse of the point being made here. Could you spell out, premise by premise, what's actually doing the argumentative work in that sentence? I'd also be curious to hear whether you think this is an early version of the same move—roughly, that perceptions which are subjectively alike must get the same ontological verdict—that later shows up explicitly as something like the phenomenal principle in the standard statements of the argument from illusion, and that disjunctivists about perception are specifically committed to denying. I don't want to read something anachronistic into Hume if it isn't really there, so I'd appreciate your honest sense of how far that parallel actually holds.
