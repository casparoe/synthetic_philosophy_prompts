So a while back I was messing around building a simple minimax search for a toy chess AI, and that sent me down a wikipedia hole into the "centipede game" and backward induction, which I'd never run into before. I get the basic mechanics fine: the pot grows every round, at the very last move the player should obviously just take the money instead of passing, so working backwards the second-to-last player should take too instead of passing to someone whos gonna take anyway, all the way back to move 1, where the "rational" thing is apparently to just grab the tiny initial pot immediately instead of ever cooperating. I can follow that recursion no problem, it's just standard backward induction / minimax logic. Then I found out Aumann has an actual theorem from the 90s that formalizes this: if "common knowledge of rationality" holds in a game of perfect information, backward induction is literally the necessary outcome, not just a decision heuristic people happen to use.

Here's the bit that's been bugging me for like a week, and that a friend of mine (did a philosophy minor, loves to dunk on my takes) keeps poking at: doesnt the whole derivation kind of eat its own tail? The move at, say, node 10 is only pinned down as "rational" because we're still assuming common knowledge of rationality holds at that point. But node 10 can only ever get reached if someone passed earlier when backward induction says they shouldn't have - so hasn't common knowledge of rationality already broken down by the time we're supposedly using it to figure out what the "rational" player does next? It feels sketchy that the argument needs an assumption to hold for its later steps that its own conclusion says must already be false by then. Just calling those nodes "off-path" doesn't seem like it actually resolves that, it just relabels it and moves on.

Anyway, I don't want a wishy-washy "well, reasonable people disagree" answer - I've already skimmed enough summaries like that. What I actually want is for you to pick a side and argue it properly: make the strongest case you can that common knowledge of rationality does NOT coherently entail the backward-induction outcome in perfect-information games like the centipede game, i.e., that the standard argument is epistemically self-undermining rather than merely "people don't actually play this way in experiments" (I already know that part, it's not what I'm interested in here). Go ahead and gesture at how Aumann's side would push back if you want, but I want an actual argument for the self-undermining view, not a balanced survey of the debate.
