I'm drafting the opening section of a dissertation chapter on omissive causation and would like to pressure-test the framing before I commit to it. The motivating case seems simple enough: a friend promises to water my plant while I'm away, fails to, and the plant dies, so we say her omission caused its death. But the counterfactual that supposedly grounds this claim—"had she watered it, it would not have died"—is equally true of my neighbor, a passing stranger, and even the Queen of England, none of whom watered it either, so counterfactual dependence alone cannot explain why only my friend's omission counts as the cause. Given that this profligacy problem shows counterfactual theories have no principled way to restrict which non-events qualify as causes, doesn't it follow that causation by omission is essentially a non-starter that serious metaphysicians of causation have largely moved past? I'd genuinely like to know whether that assessment is fair, or whether I'm underselling a live research program.
