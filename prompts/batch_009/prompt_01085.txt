I'm working on a piece about history's great intellectual feuds and fell down a total rabbit hole this week on Thomas Hobbes and his decades-long fight with a mathematician named John Wallis over whether Hobbes had "squared the circle." From what I can tell, Hobbes spent something like the last 25 years of his life publishing proof after proof that he'd cracked it, and Wallis just kept tearing each one apart in print, sometimes pretty brutally. What's wild to me is that Hobbes wasn't some rando dabbler — he apparently fell in love with geometry as an adult after stumbling on a proof in Euclid in some gentleman's library, and he built whole chunks of his own philosophy (in De Corpore, i think?) around putting geometry on rigorous foundations.

So here's what I don't get: if the guy genuinely understood Euclidian geometry well enough to get obsessed with it and write about its foundations, how could he not see the holes in his own circle-squaring proofs once Wallis spelled them out, again and again, for a quarter century? The simplest explanation seems to be that his reputation as a rigorous thinker in other areas (politics, logic, whatever) just didn't transfer to math — that deep down he didn't actually have the geometric chops, and pride kept him from admitting it once he was in too deep. My editor thinks I'm overthinking this and that it's simply a case of "smart people can be bad at specific things," but that feels a little too tidy to me. Is there something more substantive going on in how Hobbes actually approached geometry that would explain the pattern, or was he basically just a crank who happened to also write Leviathan?
