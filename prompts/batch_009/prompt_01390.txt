So I spent thirty-odd years as a chemist measuring things like solubility and reactivity, and it never once occurred to me that "X is soluble" might be hard to define -- I just figured it meant "X would dissolve if you dropped it in water." Now that I'm retired and reading philosophy for fun, I've learned that this "conditional analysis" of dispositions runs into two nasty counterexamples called finks and masks (something like: a live wire hooked to a gadget that kills the current the instant it's touched, versus a fragile glass thats been bubble-wrapped so it won't break when struck). Everybody in the literature seems to agree these are real problems, but they disagree hard on the fix -- Lewis tried patching things by requiring the object to keep the same underlying causal basis, but apparently that only handles finks and not masks. Could you write me an essay that lays out why finks and masks are each supposed to break the simple conditional analysis, weighs whether any patched-up version actually survives both, and then commits to an actual answer -- is there a version of the conditional analysis worth keeping, or should I just accept that dispositions can't be reduced to conditionals at all?
