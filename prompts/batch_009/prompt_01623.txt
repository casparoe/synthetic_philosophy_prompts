I don't have any formal background in philosophy or economics, but I've been slowly working through George Berkeley's *The Querist* on my own, and there's a cluster of queries about the nature of money that I can't quite untangle. Here they are, numbered as in the text:

23. "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"

25. "Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?"

37. "Whether power to command the industry of others be not real wealth? And whether money be not in truth tickets or tokens for conveying and recording such power, and whether it be of great consequence what materials the tickets are made of?"

I can follow the basic idea that Berkeley doesn't think coins or notes are wealth in themselves, only markers for something else he calls "power." What I'm stuck on is what exactly that "power" is supposed to be — just purchasing power over goods and labor, or something broader like social or political standing — and how it's supposed to attach to a "ticket" that has no value of its own. I also can't shake the feeling that this must connect somehow to Berkeley's much more famous claim that physical objects have no existence apart from being perceived, since here too he seems to be saying that something we normally treat as a solid, real thing (money) is actually nothing but a relation. Is that a genuine connection to his broader philosophy, or am I forcing his economic writing to fit his metaphysics? I'd appreciate help working through what he's actually claiming in these passages.
