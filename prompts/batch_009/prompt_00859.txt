hey, random philosophy question wrapped inside a fiction problem. I'm drafting a novel with a pov character, Ruth, a comms director for a mid-size political party who's having a slow-motion crisis of conscience about the whole apparatus of party politics. I want her interior monologue to have actual philosophical teeth instead of generic "politics is dirty" vibes, so I've been re-reading Simone Weil's 1943 essay on abolishing political parties for material. Before I put words in her mouth I want to make sure I haven't flattened Weil into a strawman. Below are my notes, not polished prose yet.

what I think I've got:

- weil says a party is defined by three things: it's a machine for generating collective passion, it's an organization that pressures the minds of its own members into conformity, and its real, ultimate goal (once you strip the rhetoric away) is just its own unlimited growth — not truth, not justice, not the public good.
- because the party's growth gets treated as an end instead of a means, she concludes every party ends up totalitarian in nature — not just the obvious 20th-century monsters but the sleepy centrist ones too, a difference of degree rather than kind.
- her fix isn't just "ban parties" as a slogan, its oddly specific: make it illegal for any circle of people to formally organize into a membership body, but allow looser affinities (like people who write for the same journal), with guardrails so those affinities can't just function as parties in disguise.
- all of this comes out of Rousseau's general will for her — people are supposed to reason individually toward the public interest, and parties short-circuit that by getting people to feel together instead of think seperately.

the thing i can't get past, and where i actually want your help: this sounds like the photo-negative of Madison's Federalist 10. Madison says the causes of faction can't be removed without removing liberty itself, so you regulate the effects instead. Weil wants to go straight at the cause, by law. And her own diagnosis, that collective structures corrupt individual judgment, seems like it should indict her precious journal-circle exception almost as much as an actual party, just at a smaller scale. so is she actually solving the problem, or just shrinking it down to a size she can personally live with?

could you check three things for me: whether i'm misreading weil anywhere above, whether that madison-shaped objection actually lands or if she's got the resources to answer it, and whether this is rich enough for a character to be chewing on mid-novel or just reads like me smuggling a term paper into fiction. also, there's a line of hers i want to put straight into dialogue — "goodness alone is an end" — can you confirm that's actually hers and not me misremembering something.
