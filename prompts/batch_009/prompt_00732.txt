For my seminar on Henry Sidgwick this term, we've been asked to look at some of his less-discussed writing on psychical research alongside The Methods of Ethics. I've gotten stuck on a passage from his 1882 address as founding President of the Society for Psychical Research (Proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research, vol. 1), where he argues that the Society should not expect any single piece of evidence to overcome "scientific incredulity" about phenomena like thought-transference:

"...every additional witness who, as De Morgan said, has a fair stock of credit to draw upon, is an important gain. Though his credit alone is not likely to suffice for the demand that is made on it, his draft will help. For we must not expect any decisive effect in the direction at which we primarily aim — on the common sense of mankind — from any single piece of evidence, however complete it has been made. Scientific incredulity has been so long in growing, and has so many and so strong roots, that we shall only kill it, if we are able to kill it at all as regards any of these questions, by burying it alive under a heap of facts. We must keep 'pegging away,' as Lincoln said; we must accumulate fact upon fact, and add experiment upon experiment, and, I should say, not wrangle too much with incredulous outsiders about the conclusiveness of any one, but trust to the mass of evidence for conviction."

I can't quite work out what model of evidential reasoning is doing the work here. Is "burying incredulity under a heap of facts" essentially the old reply to Hume on testimony for miracles — that enough independent, individually fallible witnesses can jointly outweigh a strong prior improbability — or is Sidgwick after something else, more like a purely inductive weight-of-evidence argument? And however we characterize it, does this way of building conviction sit easily with the conditions he lays down in The Methods of Ethics for treating a proposition as properly certified, especially the requirement of a "consensus of experts"? I'd like to walk into next week's discussion with a clearer sense of whether his epistemology of psychical evidence is continuous with, or actually in tension with, his official epistemology of ethics.
