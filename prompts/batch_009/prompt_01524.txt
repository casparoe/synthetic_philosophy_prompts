As part of my research on comparative ethics, I keep returning to a tension inside Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont's account of Confucian role ethics. Their relational conception of personhood is meant to make role-based obligation intelligible without appeal to any general, role-independent principle. Yet the tradition's own paradigm cases of role conflict seem to function exactly like such principles once we ask what they license other agents to do. Mencius 7A35 is the sharpest instance: asked what Shun, as sovereign, should do if his father Gusou committed murder while Gao Yao, Shun's own minister of justice, remained bound to prosecute impartially, Mencius answers that Shun should abdicate the throne and secretly flee with his father rather than let the law take its course. Liu Qingping has used this episode to argue that Confucian ethics is at bottom a "consanguinitism" that allows kinship to override any public standard of justice, a charge Ames and Rosemont have explicitly contested.

Write a dialogue of at least six exchanges, staged as a disagreement over the Shun case specifically, between a defender of Ames and Rosemont's role-ethics reading and a proponent of the consanguinitism-style critique. The defender should explain how Mencius's verdict can be understood as situational relational discernment rather than the covert application of a general rule; the critic should press on whether treating Shun's choice as praiseworthy and instructive for others does not itself commit the tradition to a standing ranking of roles that role ethics claims to do without. Each speaker should respond to the specific point the other has just made rather than restate their opening position, and neither should concede the debate too quickly.
