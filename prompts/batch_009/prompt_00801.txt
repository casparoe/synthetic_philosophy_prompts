I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the long-running argument between causal and evidential decision theory, and one thought experiment keeps hijacking my interviews. It is Gibbard and Harper's 1978 case of "Death in Damascus" — a philosopher's reworking of the much older Mesopotamian fable about the servant who flees to Samarra to escape Death, the same tale Somerset Maugham retold in his 1933 play Sheppey. In the philosophers' version the cities become Damascus and Aleppo, and Death is given a crucial upgrade: he works from a fixed appointment book, has already settled on a city before the man in the story decides anything, and is essentially never wrong.

That single change turns a parable about the futility of outrunning fate into something stranger and more formal. Whichever city the man resolves to be in, his very act of resolving counts as evidence that Death guessed correctly, so neither staying nor fleeing ever settles into a choice he would not instantly want to reverse — and even the seemingly clever fix of flipping a coin turns out to be the one move that does not immediately undercut itself, which is an odd thing to have to tell someone trying to save his life. A philosopher I interviewed for the piece calls it the cleanest example of "decision instability" he knows; a colleague of his insists it is just bad bookkeeping dressed up as paradox. I suspect it would make a better short story than either of their papers.

I would like you to try writing that story: something in the 1,000-1,800 word range, built on this structure rather than on the fable's simpler irony. Invent whatever period, setting, and stand-in for Death you like — a diagnosis, an algorithm, a debt collector, an actual reaper — but keep the underlying architecture intact: your antagonist has to commit to where or how the protagonist will be found before the protagonist chooses anything, and be reliable enough that the protagonist's own reasoning keeps undercutting itself in real time. I want the tension to come from watching the protagonist actually think their way into the trap — noticing that every plan, including hedging, seems to dissolve the moment they commit to it — rather than from a closing twist that just restates the old moral that you cannot outrun fate.
