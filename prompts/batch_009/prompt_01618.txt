For my graduate seminar on philosophy of biology, I'd like a dialogue between an etiological (selected-effects) theorist of function, in the lineage of Wright, Millikan, and Neander, and a causal-role theorist, in the lineage of Cummins. Stage the two standing before a natural history museum case that traces feathers from simple insulatory filaments on non-flying theropods, through the vaned flight feathers of early birds, to the purely ornamental train of a peacock, and have each try to talk the other out of their theory by working through this very case. The causal-role theorist should press the etiological theorist on why, if functions require a selection history, a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a bird assembled by pure chance would have feathers with no function at all; the etiological theorist should press back on whether the causal-role theorist can distinguish a malfunctioning feather from one that has simply taken on a new function, and on what, absent any appeal to selection, could single out which of a feather's many current effects—trapping air, generating lift, catching a peahen's eye—is its function rather than a mere side effect. Have the two end by trying to work out whether they are disagreeing about the nature of biological function itself, or merely about which of two equally legitimate questions is more worth asking.
