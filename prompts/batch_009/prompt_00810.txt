I'm a retired physicist, and lately I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole now that I finally have time to read the stuff I used to wave off as "not real science." I got interested in the identity of indiscernibles partly because a friend mentioned it's supposedly relevant to why physicists insist two electrons are genuinely identical rather than just extremely similar. Not asking you to referee the quantum mechanics angle, that might be a total red herring, just explaining how I ended up here.

So I've been slowly working through the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence in the original 1717 English translation, which is not exactly built for skimming, and I'm stuck on part of Leibniz's fourth letter. He writes:

"4. There is no such thing as Two Individuals indiscernible from each other. An Ingenious Gentleman of my Acquaintance, discoursing with me, in the presence of Her Electoral Highness the Princess Sophia, in the Garden of Herrenhausen; thought he could find two Leaves perfectly alike. The Princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the Garden a long time to look for some; but it was to no purpose. Two Drops of Water, or Milk, viewed with a Microscope, will appear distinguishable from each other. This is an Argument against Atoms; which are confuted, as well as a Vacuum, by the Principles of true Metaphysicks.

5. Those great Principles of a sufficient Reason, and of the Identity of Indiscernibles, change the State of Metaphysicks. That Science becomes real and demonstrative by means of these Principles; whereas before, it did generally consist in empty Words.

6. To suppose two things indiscernible, is to suppose the same thing under two Names. And therefore to suppose that the Universe could have had at first another position of Time and Place, than that which it actually had; and yet that all the Parts of the Universe should have had the same Situation among themselves, as that which they actually had; such a Supposition, I say, is an impossible Fiction."

What I can't work out is how paragraph 4 and paragraph 6 count as the same argument. Paragraph 4 reads like an empirical wager: go look closely enough, even with a microscope, and you'll always find some difference between two "identical" things. But paragraph 6 is a much stronger claim, that its an "impossible fiction," not just a bet that happens to keep paying off, for two indiscernible setups to really be distinct. Is paragraph 5's appeal to sufficient reason supposed to carry him from the modest empirical claim to the much stronger logical one, and if so how exactly does that work? Or is he just running two seperate arguments together without quite noticing it?

There's a second thing nagging me. Clarke's reply to this very passage argues, I'm paraphrasing to keep this from getting too long, that sure, no two actual leaves or drops of water are exactly alike, but only because they're complicated compound bodies; God could easily have made two drops of water exactly alike, and they'd still be two drops, not one, just by being in different places. A friend described Max Black's famous two-spheres thought experiment to me a while back, the one where he imagines a universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical iron spheres, as though it were some clever twentieth-century move that finally broke the identity of indiscernibles for good. But now that I've actually read Clarke's reply, it sounds like he already made more or less the same move in 1716, over two centuries earlier. So did Black really contribute anything new, or did he just dress Clarke's old rebuttal up in a fancier example? And does it matter that Clarke's version of the point is explicitly limited to "simple" bits of matter, while leaves (and presumably Black's iron spheres) are compounds, which by Clarke's own admission never actually turn out perfectly alike? I'd love some help untangling this, because right now I can't tell whether Black quietly patched a real gap in Clarke's argument or just reinvented it.
