I have been thinking, on and off, about what it really means for a group of people to act or intend something together — not just each person doing their own part alongside the others, but genuinely functioning as a single unit, in the way we say "the board decided," or the way two people manage to carry a couch up a narrow staircase without ever spelling out who does what. While working through Hobbes's *Leviathan* on my own, I came across a passage that seems to bear directly on this, and I would like help understanding it. It is from Chapter XVI, "Of Persons, Authors, and Things Personated":

"A Multitude of men, are made One Person, when they are by one man, or one Person, Represented; so that it be done with the consent of every one of that Multitude in particular. For it is the Unity of the Representer, not the Unity of the Represented, that maketh the Person One. And it is the Representer that beareth the Person, and but one Person: And Unity, cannot otherwise be understood in Multitude.

And because the Multitude naturally is not One, but Many; they cannot be understood for one; but many Authors, of every thing their Representative saith, or doth in their name; Every man giving their common Representer, Authority from himselfe in particular; and owning all the actions the Representer doth, in case they give him Authority without stint."

Two things about this passage puzzle me. First, in plain language, what exactly is Hobbes saying makes a multitude into "one person"? He seems to deny that it has anything to do with the members having something in common with one another — a shared plan, a shared state of mind — and to insist instead that the oneness is manufactured entirely by a single representative plus each member's separate act of authorizing him. Second, that account sits oddly next to the everyday cases I mentioned above, where the natural thing to say is that the group's unity comes from how the members' own intentions and expectations mesh with each other, with no representative anywhere in the picture. Is Hobbes's picture actually at odds with that everyday picture, or is "authorization by each member" doing more of the same work as "interlocking individual intentions" than it looks like at first — that is, is his account secretly less top-down, and more built out of the members' own individual attitudes, than it sounds? I would like to understand the passage on its own terms first, and then see how well it holds up once set alongside that more everyday picture of acting together.
