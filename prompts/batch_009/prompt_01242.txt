I'm drafting a novel narrated by a woman who, like Vonnegut's Tralfamadorians, has stopped experiencing her life as a sequence: she treats every moment of it, her own death included, as a permanent fixture in a four-dimensional block universe, and uses this idea to console a dying friend, promising him that nothing once true of him will ever stop being true. Before I let this consolation stand, I want to know whether it amounts to sound philosophy or wishful thinking dressed up as physics.

Write an essay addressing the following question: does an eternalist, four-dimensionalist view of time actually support the Tralfamadorians' equanimity about death, or does it undercut it? One philosopher, Harry Silverstein, has invoked four-dimensionalism specifically to rebut Epicurus's argument that death cannot harm the person who dies, since no subject remains to be harmed once that person is gone — the thought being that the four-dimensional person, considered as a whole, is still there to serve as the worse-off subject that a deprivation account of death's badness requires. If that move works, the very metaphysics my narrator reaches for may commit her to finding death harder to bear, not easier. Weigh this against the case for the opposite reading, take a stand on when, if ever, death is bad for the deceased on either picture, and defend a definite verdict rather than splitting the difference.
