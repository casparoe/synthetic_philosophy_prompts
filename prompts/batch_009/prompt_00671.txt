Okay so I was up way too late last night arguing with my friend Priya about Kurt Gödel of all people, and I can't stop thinking about it. Backstory: I'd read about these letters Gödel wrote to his mom Marianne in 1961 where she straight up asked him if he believed they'd see each other again after death, and he says yes and lays out this whole rationalist argument for it — basically, the world is ordered and meaningful (he ties this to the principle of sufficient reason, very on-brand for a Leibniz fanboy), and a person's capacity for growth and understanding is so much bigger then what anyone actually manages to realize before they die that it would be totally irrational for the universe to just let all that potential go to waste, kind of like pouring the foundation for a house and then abandoning the build. So there has to be some further existence where the "learning" actually gets finished.

Priya, who's way more into transhumanist stuff than me, latched onto the "unrealized potential shouldn't go to waste" part and argued that this is basically an argument FOR things like cryonics and whole-brain emulation — that Gödel, if you explained Alcor and mind uploading to him, should've seen those as a rational, technological path to exactly the continuation he was arguing for. I said no way, because the same letters (and his other stuff, like the mind/machine disjunction he pulled out of his own incompleteness theorems) make clear he thought materialism was basically false and that the mind isn't reducible to a finite mechanical process — so scanning a brain and running it on a computer seems like exactly the kind of thing he'd say misses the point, maybe even a category error, rather than delivering the "Wiedersehen" he promised his mom.

We went back and forth for like an hour and never landed anywhere (she had to catch a train). Gödel obviously died in 1978, decades before any of this was mainstream, so this is squarely one of those "what would he actually think" questions rather than something he ever weighed in on himself. Based on what you know of his specific philosophical commitments here — not just "he was sort of a mystic" — who do you think has the stronger read: would Gödel most likely welcome cryonics/uploading as a secular vindication of his argument, dismiss it as solving the wrong problem entirely, or land somewhere messier than either of those? I'd genuinely like you to engage with the actual structure of his argument rather than give me a wishy-washy "we can't really know" answer.
