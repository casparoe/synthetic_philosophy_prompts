Since retiring after thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, I have finally been reading philosophy in earnest, and I have gotten stuck on a dispute in axiomatic bargaining theory that feels oddly familiar from my old field. Nash's original bargaining solution and the rival one Kalai and Smorodinsky proposed in 1975 agree on three conditions — Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance under positive affine rescaling of each bargainer's utility — which read like the same symmetry and invariance principles I was trained to trust. They diverge on a fourth condition: Nash retains independence of irrelevant alternatives, while Kalai and Smorodinsky give it up in favor of individual monotonicity, which guarantees that a bargainer whose own best-case prospects improve can never fare worse at the solution.

I have not been able to settle which of these two conditions has the better claim to being a genuine requirement of rational, fair bargaining. Please write an essay that lays out the strongest case for each side and then defends a definite verdict on which condition should give way when they conflict — do not leave the matter simply balanced between them.
