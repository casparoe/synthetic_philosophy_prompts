For nearly forty years I worked as a field geologist, spending most of my career reconstructing the depositional and tectonic history of sedimentary basins from what the rocks themselves preserve: cross-bedding, unconformities, isotopic signatures, and the like. In practice this meant continually weighing rival scenarios against one another and settling on whichever one best accounted for the pattern of evidence in front of me, a habit I never thought to examine closely until retirement gave me the time to work through some philosophy of science, starting with a slim book by the philosopher Peter Lipton that a former colleague pressed on me. I have since learned that the everyday move it describes has a name, "inference to the best explanation" (IBE for short), and I have been surprised at how much genuine disagreement surrounds something that, from the inside, always felt like plain common sense.

The difficulty I have gotten stuck on concerns IBE's relationship to probability theory, an area where I would have expected my scientific training to help more than it has. As far as I can tell, there are two separate worries in the literature, both traced back to Bas van Fraassen, and I cannot tell whether commentators regard them as the same complaint in different clothing or as genuinely independent problems. One, the "best of a bad lot" point, says that even the strongest hypothesis among those anyone has actually proposed could still be far inferior to some better explanation that nobody has gotten around to formulating, so favoring "the best we have" is no real assurance of truth. The other is a more formal dilemma about rules for revising belief: either inferring the best explanation always agrees with plain Bayesian conditionalization on the same evidence, in which case it does no independent work, or it can sometimes disagree, in which case it seems to expose you, in the manner of a Dutch book argument, to accepting a set of bets that is guaranteed to lose money regardless of the outcome. Lipton himself, if I am reading him correctly, tries to head off exactly this second worry by arguing that Bayesians should themselves be "explanationists," letting explanatory virtues help fix the very priors and likelihoods that feed into Bayes's theorem, rather than treating IBE as a rival procedure running alongside it.

Could you help me get a firmer grip on this? I would like to understand, at a level suited to someone at home with quantitative reasoning but rusty on formal epistemology, whether the bad-lot point and the Dutch-book dilemma are really the same underlying worry or two distinct ones, and then whether Lipton's proposal actually dissolves the second dilemma or merely relocates it to the question of where those explanatorily informed priors are supposed to come from. If a concrete illustration would help, I would welcome one drawn from something closer to my own field, such as choosing between two competing depositional histories for a rock sequence, rather than the standard textbook cases about doctors and diseases.
