Kind of a random question that came out of a work argument. My team built this dumb little internal prediction market—kind of a baby version of Manifold Markets—where people bet fake points on stuff like whether a project ships on time or whether the tests pass CI on the first try, mostly to get people used to giving actual calibrated percentages instead of just saying "probably" or "likely" in standups. One of my coworkers defended the whole idea by linking a blog post about Dutch book arguments: basically the claim that if your beliefs don't follow the probability axioms, a bookie could construct a set of bets against you thats guaranteed to loose you money no matter what happens, so rational people are supposedly forced to have coherent, probability-following beliefs.

Something about this has been bugging me ever since, though. Nobody is actually running around trying to Dutch-book anyone in real life, and even if some sneaky bookie tried, couldn't I just... decline the bet? It kinda feels like the whole argument only works if you assume I'm some kind of automaton that's contractually obligated to accept any bet offered to it at my stated odds, which is obviously not true of any real person. So is this "coherent credences" thing actually resting on anything besides a cute hypothetical about a con man who doesn't exist, or am I misunderstanding what the argument is even supposed to prove? Kind of want to know before I keep telling my team our dumb little point system is "backed by decision theory" in the readme.
