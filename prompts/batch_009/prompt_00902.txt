I am several chapters into a novel set in Cambridge in the 1880s, whose central figure — a moral philosopher I have named Josiah Aldis, modeled loosely on Henry Sidgwick — spends his mornings revising a treatise on ethics and his evenings chairing committees that test mediums and investigate haunted houses for a thinly fictionalized Society for Psychical Research. I am building toward a dinner-party scene in which a former pupil, now an unsparing skeptic of anything that smells of the séance room, corners Aldis and accuses him of a kind of intellectual bad faith: how can a man who has built his whole career on clarity, consistency, and a refusal to let wishful thinking pass for argument spend decades chasing table-rappings? I don't want Aldis to have a merely evasive answer — I want him to have the best answer that was actually available to the real Sidgwick, and I've realized I don't understand that position well enough yet to write the scene honestly.

Here is what my research has turned up. Sidgwick's ethics ends in an impasse he called the "dualism of practical reason": rational egoism and utilitarianism each strike him, on careful reflection, as equally self-evident first principles, yet they can flatly prescribe opposite actions, so that, in his own words, the "cosmos of duty" threatens to collapse into "chaos." He considered simply following Kant in postulating personal immortality as the condition under which duty and self-interest are guaranteed to coincide, but he could not, in good conscience, help himself to that postulate on faith alone — so instead he spent much of his adult life searching for actual empirical evidence of survival after death and of a benevolently governed moral order, an effort that, by his own late admission, never produced the proof he was after.

For the scene to work, I need to actually pick a side rather than let Aldis shrug. Please write an essay that weighs the two most natural readings of this: that the psychical research was a rational, even admirable, continuation of Sidgwick's philosophical rigor — taking an uncomfortable implication of one's own theory seriously enough to go and test it, rather than resting on a comfortable postulate the way Kant did — against the reading that it instead betrays a real confusion at the heart of his notion of practical reason, such that no empirical discovery could ever have settled the question he needed it to settle. Weigh both readings seriously, but don't leave the matter open at the end: reach a definite verdict that Aldis could actually deliver, in his own defense, across that dinner table, and give the argument that earns it.
