Random thing I learned recently: in Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (the one that lays out the imitation game), objection #9 is literally called "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception," and Turing isnt dismissive about it at all -- he says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," calls it "quite a strong one" as objections go, and even works out a scenario where a telepathic contestant correctly names the suit of a hidden card 130 out of 400 tries (vs. ~100/400 you'd expect from chance), beating the machine at the game. His proposed fix: run the whole test in a "telepathy-proof room."

Heres the part he couldn't have known: the single most celebrated card-guessing results from exactly that period, S.G. Soal's experiments in Britain, were shown in 1978 (24 years after Turing died) to have been faked, digit by digit, by Soal himself. And that's basically been the pattern for the whole field ever since, right down to Bem's 2011 "feeling the future" precognition studies, which also fell apart once people tried to replicate them.

So given what we actually know about how Turing thought -- the statistical rigor from his cryptanalysis background, his habit of updating hard on evidence -- what's your real best guess about how he'd react to learning all this? Would argument 9 just quietly disappear if he rewrote the paper today, or is there some version of that worry he'd still take seriously even after the fraud and decades of failed replications? I want your actual best guess here, not a "we can't know what he'd think" cop-out.
