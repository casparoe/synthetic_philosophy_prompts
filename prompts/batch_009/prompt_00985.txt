Quick gut check before I lock in tomorrow's lecture slides for my political philosophy seminar. We're on Nozick this week, and a colleague and I got into a whole thing over lunch about how far the Wilt Chamberlain argument is actually supposed to reach, so I want to make sure I've got it straight before I stand in front of 40 undergrads and say something dumb.

Here's the takeaway I'm planning to give them. In "How Liberty Upsets Patterns," the point of the Wilt Chamberlain case is that starting from any patterned distribution D1 you like - strict equality, Rawls's difference principle, whatever - a million fans freely paying an extra 25 cents each to watch Wilt play produces a new distribution D2 where Wilt ends up $250,000 richer than anyone else, and D2 is exactly as just as D1 was, since nobody's rights were violated getting from one to the other. Since this generalizes to any pattern, no patterned or end-state theory of distributive justice can be maintained without constantly interfering with people's free choices. My plan is to tell the class this basically hands Nozick a knock-down case against redistributive taxation in general: our own real-world distribution of income and wealth, however lopsided it looks, gets the same presumption of justice as D2, as long as the actual trades, wages, and gifts that produced it were themselves voluntary - so taxing it back toward some pattern is just as illegitimate as forcibly grabbing Wilts quarters back from him.

Is that a fair summary to put in front of the class, or is there something about how the entitlement theory is actually supposed to apply to a distribution with a real (and pretty messy) history behind it that I'm glossing over?
