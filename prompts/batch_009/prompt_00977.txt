"Pascal's Mugging" -- devised by Eliezer Yudkowsky and elaborated by Nick Bostrom -- imagines a stranger who claims the power to affect an astronomically large quantity of value, and who can always inflate that claim to outweigh whatever tiny probability you assign to his honesty, all in order to talk you out of a few dollars. Ordinary expected-value reasoning then seems to counsel paying up -- a conclusion almost everyone rejects, yet one that is surprisingly hard to block in a principled way.

Two standard fixes exist: a "leverage penalty," which discounts the credence assigned to a claim in proportion to the number of people or amount of value it purports to affect, and a bounded utility function, which denies that any prospect can carry more than some finite maximal value. Yet Nick Beckstead and Teruji Thomas have argued that any theory of choice under uncertainty must be either "reckless" (willing to forgo a guaranteed good, however large, for a tiny enough chance of a large enough gain) or "timid" (willing to forgo an arbitrarily large gain to avoid an arbitrarily small increase in risk) -- suggesting that no such fix fully dissolves the tension. Hayden Wilkinson has, in effect, defended the reckless horn, which he calls "fanaticism," over the timid one.

Write an essay that weighs these positions and defends a verdict on how an ideally rational agent should respond to a Pascal's mugger. A hedged verdict (e.g., pay up only below some specified, well-motivated probability threshold) is acceptable; declining to choose between recklessness and timidity is not.
