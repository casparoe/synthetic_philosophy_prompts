Simone Weil's 1943 essay on political parties, composed in the last months of her life and left unpublished at her death, argues that a party is defined by three features regardless of its programme: it operates as a machine for generating collective passion rather than sober judgment, it exerts continuous pressure on members to subordinate their own sense of truth and justice to the party line, and its own uninterrupted growth becomes, in practice, both its first objective and its ultimate purpose. From these three features she concludes that every party is totalitarian in aspiration, held in check only by the fact that its rivals are equally so, and that genuine collective deliberation about what is true, just, or good for the whole cannot coexist with a party's presence within a body.

I sit on the seven-member elected board of a small community land trust with about 150 member-households, and over the past year two informal slates have hardened ahead of board elections, each running joint candidates on a shared platform and, increasingly, voting together as a bloc on matters that have nothing to do with whatever originally divided them, from budget line items to routine bylaw language. I obviously cannot and would not want to ban people from forming alliances the way Weil proposes abolishing parties outright at the level of the state, so I am less interested in her policy conclusion than in what her underlying diagnosis, taken seriously, implies for a body this size: what it would predict about where the dynamic goes if left alone, and what it should most importantly change about how I frame agenda items, sequence votes, and conduct myself as a board member who would like to preserve whatever is genuinely useful about like-minded members coordinating, while resisting the specific pathology she describes.

Please lay out the most important implications of her argument for my situation, and also flag the respects in which an analysis built around sovereign states and mass electorates most plausibly stops transferring to a small voluntary association, so that I don't lean on the analogy past the point it can bear.
