I'm on my school's Lincoln-Douglas debate team, and the resolution at our next tournament is close enough to "the law ought to enforce a society's shared moral standards" that I decided to build my affirmative case around the actual historical Hart-Devlin debate instead of improvising from first principles. I feel solid on the basics: Devlin's argument, sometimes called the "disintegration thesis," holds that a society's shared morality functions as the cement holding it together, so that flouting it, even privately, is closer to treason than we usually assume, and he locates the boundary of that shared morality wherever an ordinary, reasonable person would feel "intolerance, indignation, and disgust." I also know Hart's classic reply, that Devlin never actually shows that moral non-conformity threatens any society's survival.

What worries me more is Ronald Dworkin's follow-up, since I suspect it's the rebuttal my cross-ex opponents are more likely to have actually read. Dworkin argues that the "reasonable man" standard can't do the work Devlin wants, because a person's stated moral view might really just be prejudice, a rationalization built on unsupported factual claims, mere personal aversion with no reasoning behind it, or "parroting" (simply repeating whatever the people around them already say) — and only convictions that survive this filtering, he argues, should count toward the "public morality" that could justify legislation. My debate partner thinks this basically wins the round for the negative before it even starts.

I'd like you to help me build the strongest available response on Devlin's behalf. Specifically, please argue that Dworkin's four-part filter is not the neutral, purely descriptive sociological test it's presented as, but already smuggles in a substantive and contestable theory of what makes a moral belief legitimate — roughly, that a legitimate moral belief must come from independent reasoning grounded in accurate premises, rather than from inherited authority or tradition. Since Devlin claims only to be describing a society's actual morality as it really is, not defending any first-order ethical theory of his own, he shouldn't have to accept that assumption just to get his argument off the ground. In short, make the case that Dworkin doesn't beat Devlin on Devlin's own terms — he relocates the fight onto ground where a broadly liberal, rationalist picture of moral belief wins automatically. Please work directly with Dworkin's specific categories rather than falling back on Hart's separate harm-principle objection, which I've already got covered elsewhere in my case.
