I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the epistemology of financial markets and keep getting stuck on the philosophical upshot of the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem: roughly, that if it is common knowledge that traders share a common prior and are rational, an offer to trade purely on private information is self-undermining, since the offer itself reveals information that a rational counterparty should use to withdraw. Actual markets, of course, see enormous trading volume, which is standardly explained away by appeal to hedging, liquidity needs, and "noise traders." Write a dialogue between two philosophers of economics, one a defender of market efficiency and the other a skeptic about it, who accept all of this but disagree about what it implies for whether we are entitled to treat a market price (in, say, a prediction market) the way we would treat a well-calibrated expert's testimony. The defender should argue that the noise-trader rescue is a principled, independently motivated part of the rational-expectations picture, so that prices remain trustworthy; the skeptic should argue that it is closer to an ad hoc patch that, once admitted, leaves us with no real assurance that any given price tracks truth rather than crowd psychology. Have the two engage directly with each other's strongest moves rather than talking past one another.
