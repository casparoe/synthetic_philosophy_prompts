We hired this ex-physics-PhD guy on my team a while back ("escaped academia" is literally how he describes it), and he brought up Bell's theorem during standup of all places, because we were blocked on a flaky test and someone made a joke about "spooky action at a distance" in our test suite. I went home and actually read up on it instead of doing something useful with my evening, and now I can't stop bringing it up at lunch.

Here's roughly what I pieced together, correct me if I butchered any of it: Einstein spent a big chunk of his later career convinced QM had to be an incomplete theory, because the alternative was "spooky action at a distance" — that's literally his phrase, from a 1947 letter to Max Born — meaning instantaneous correlations between particles with no local explanation. His famous EPR paper (1935) was basically the formal version of "there's gotta be hidden variables we're missing, this can't be it." Then Einstein dies in 1955, and in 1964 Bell proves mathematically that no local hidden-variable theory, however clever, can ever reproduce everything QM predicts — not "we haven't found them yet," but "they can't exist if locality holds." Decades of increasingly loophole-free experiments since then (there's literally a 2022 Nobel for this exact line of work) just keep siding with QM over "local realism." Oh, and the other fun fact I dug up: Bohm actually published a real, working hidden-variable theory back in 1952, while Einstein was still alive — and Einstein reportedly hated it, calling it "too cheap" in a letter to Born, presumably because Bohm only rescues determinism by making the theory blatantly non-local.

So here's my actual question: given all that, what do you think Einstein does if he lives another couple decades and someone walks him through Bell's proof plus the experiments? Does he just swallow nonlocality and give up on "realism"? Does he reach for superdeterminism instead (which as far as I can tell means giving up on experimenters having genuinely free choice over what they measure — which honestly seems like giving up even more then locality does, though I could see him being weirdly okay with that given how determinist he already was)? Does he go hunting for some totally different escape hatch, like retrocausality? Or does he just stay stubborn about it forever, the way he did with the "God doesn't play dice" stuff? I want an actual opinionated answer here, reasoned from what we know about how the guy actually thought, not a "we can't ever really know" shrug.
