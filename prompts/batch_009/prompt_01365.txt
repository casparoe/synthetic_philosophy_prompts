I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading around classical Indian epistemology as a side interest. I came across Gautama's definition of perception (pratyaksa) from the Nyaya-sutra (1.1.4), quoted in a paper I found as:

"Indriyarthasannikarsotpannam jnanam avyapadesyam avyabhicari vyavasayatmakam pratyaksam."

The gloss given alongside it runs roughly: perception is that cognition which arises from the contact of a sense organ with its object, and which is avyapadesya (not associated with words, i.e., non-verbal), avyabhicari (unerring), and vyavasayatmaka (of the nature of a determinate judgment).

As an engineer I tend to read definitions the way I'd read a spec, so the apparent tension here bothers me more than it probably should. "Avyapadesya" seems to demand that the cognition carry no verbal or conceptual overlay whatsoever, while "vyavasayatmaka" seems to demand exactly the determinate, subject-predicate structure (as in "this is a jar") that sounds inseparable from concepts and words. I gather commentators tie this to the nirvikalpaka/savikalpaka (indeterminate/determinate perception) distinction, but just naming that distinction feels like relabeling the puzzle rather than resolving it. Could you walk me through how classical Naiyayikas actually reconcile these two clauses within a single definition of one pramana, and what's philosophically at stake in insisting that perception, so defined, has to cover both?
