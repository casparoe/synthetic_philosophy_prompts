I am taking a research seminar this term on Isaac Newton and the Scientific Revolution, and this week's session set the *Principia* aside entirely to focus on Newton's private theological writings. We spent most of the class working through "An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture," the roughly 25,000-word dissertation Newton sent to John Locke in a letter dated 14 November 1690. Drawing on patristic citations and early Greek, Latin, Syriac, and Ethiopic manuscript evidence, Newton argued that two of the New Testament's clearest proof-texts for the Trinity are corruptions rather than original readings: the explicit "Father, Word, and Holy Ghost… these three are one" clause at 1 John 5:7, which he traced to a Latin marginal gloss that only later crept into a handful of very late Greek manuscripts, and the phrase "God was manifest in the flesh" at 1 Timothy 3:16, which he argued began as the Greek relative pronoun "which" (ὅ), was miscopied as "who" (ὅς), and was finally read as an abbreviation for "God" (θεός). This philology fed directly into Newton's own carefully concealed anti-Trinitarian convictions.

A few of us stayed after seminar arguing about just how cautious Newton was about all this, and how that caution was vindicated twice over, in two quite different directions. Locke had already forwarded the manuscript to Jean Le Clerc in Holland for anonymous translation and publication when Newton lost his nerve and asked him to stop; for the rest of his life he refused to revisit the question of publication, a caution only reinforced once the Blasphemy Act of 1697 made anti-Trinitarian writing a serious legal risk, and once his friend William Whiston, whom he had arranged to succeed him in the Lucasian professorship, was stripped of that very chair in 1710–11 for publicly arguing much the case Newton was making in private. The piece went unpublished until 1754, twenty-seven years after Newton's death. On the narrow philological question, Newton has since been thoroughly vindicated: standard critical editions of the Greek New Testament now omit the Johannine Comma as a late interpolation and render 1 Timothy 3:16 with "who" or "he" rather than "God," exactly as he concluded. But the wider discipline that grew out of this sort of argument is today most publicly represented not by anything resembling Newton's own project of stripping away corruptions to defend a purer, still fully authoritative Christianity, but by scholars such as Bart Ehrman, who marshal the very same kind of manuscript evidence toward a much more general skepticism about the reliability, coherence, and divine authorship of the New Testament as a whole.

Given everything we know about Newton's actual theological temperament — his decades-long, almost obsessive confidence that scripture's true providential core could be recovered once a small number of doctrinally motivated corruptions were identified and removed, and his well-documented hostility to atheism and religious skepticism more generally — how do you think he would react if confronted with this later history? Would he read it as straightforward confirmation of his own labors, or would he be alarmed to see his own method turned toward conclusions he would surely have found intolerable? I am especially curious whether, in your view, his framework actually has the resources to stop precisely at the two corruptions he identified, or whether the kind of argument he pioneered tends, once let loose, toward exactly the more corrosive skepticism that a figure like Ehrman represents.
