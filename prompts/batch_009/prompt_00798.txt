Got into it with my friend Priya last night about whether accuracy-first epistemology actually earns its keep. She's finishing a phil of science PhD and has been raving about Jim Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism -- her claim was that its basically a free, non-pragmatic proof that your credences have to obey the probability axioms, way better than the old Dutch Book argument because there's no betting or money involved, just pure "closeness to the truth." My pushback was that this smells circular to me: if you get to pick which "measure of accuracy" counts as legitimate, cant you just pick one that's rigged to make coherent credences come out on top, so the whole theorem is smuggling in the conclusion? She said no, and started sketching some square-with-a-diagonal-line picture on a bar napkin that I only half followed before the bar closed on us.

So two things I'd love help with. First, can you give me the intuitive, gut-level version of the actual argument -- like, treat credences as points in a cube, treat possible worlds as the corners, accuracy as distance to a corner -- and walk me through why stepping outside the "coherent" region always makes you simultaneously worse off no matter which corner ends up being real, not just worse off on average? I don't need the full axiomatic proof, more the "aha, of course" picture. Second, on my circularity worry: Priya says the argument only needs the accuracy measure to be a "strictly proper" scoring rule (the Brier score is the classic example), which she says is a pretty minimal, independently motivated constraint and not something rigged for the conclusion. Is that actually right, and if so, what makes propriety the "innocent" requirement here rather than just one more question-begging assumption dressed up in technical language?
