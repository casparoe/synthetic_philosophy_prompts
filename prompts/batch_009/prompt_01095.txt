I spent forty years as a physical chemist working mostly on reaction kinetics, so I carry a fairly unreflective conviction that when a spark ignites a mixture of gases, the spark is genuinely responsible for the explosion that follows, not merely its herald. Since retiring I have been working through a history of Islamic philosophy with two former colleagues, and last month we arrived at al-Ghazālī's Tahafut al-Falasifa. The seventeenth discussion, on causality and miracles, unsettled me rather more than I expected, since Ghazālī's chosen example turns out to be exactly the sort of case I spent my career on: a piece of cotton brought into contact with fire. He argues that we never actually observe the fire's power to burn anything; we observe only a habitual succession of two events, each independently created by God, who is the sole real agent involved and who could just as easily will the cotton to remain whole. What we are inclined to call a law of nature, on this account, is nothing more than God's constant custom.

I gather that the philosopher who answered him at length, Ibn Rushd, is the same figure known in the history of science (rather better than in philosophy, at least to someone with my background) as Averroes. In his own Tahafut al-Tahafut he insists that created things really do have natures and dispositional powers of their own, and that denying this does not merely contradict experience but destroys the very possibility of the demonstrative knowledge on which any science worth the name depends; miracles, on his view, are best understood as rare suspensions of a power that is nonetheless perfectly real. A commentary I consulted afterward also raised, and then complicated, the obvious comparison with Hume, suggesting that the resemblance in conclusion masks a real difference in motivation and structure that I don't yet feel equipped to evaluate on my own.

What I would like from you is an essay rather than a survey. Please reconstruct the strongest version of al-Ghazālī's argument against real causal powers and the strongest version of Ibn Rushd's defense of them, weigh in on the Humean comparison to whatever extent you think it actually bears on the dispute, and then commit to an answer rather than leaving the two sides in equilibrium: which position has the better of the argument, and why? I would also welcome, though this part is optional, a closing remark on whether anything in this centuries-old debate should unsettle the confidence of a modern working scientist in the reality of the causal powers they spend their career presupposing.
