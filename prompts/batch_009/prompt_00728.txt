Hey, I'm outlining a novel where one of the characters is a bit of a Kant obsessive — the type who whips out "what if everyone did that" on people mid-argument. I went back to the actual Groundwork instead of just trusting my memory from an intro ethics class years ago (using the old Abbott translation, it's the one that's free online), and I got stuck on a bit I want to understand properly before I put it in this guy's mouth.

Kant runs through a several examples of bad maxims (lying to get a loan, letting your talents rot, etc), then sums it up like this, which is the part that's tripping me up:

"These are a few of the many actual duties, or at least what we regard as such, which obviously fall into two classes on the one principle that we have laid down. We must be able to will that a maxim of our action should be a universal law. This is the canon of the moral appreciation of the action generally. Some actions are of such a character that their maxim cannot without contradiction be even conceived as a universal law of nature, far from it being possible that we should will that it should be so. In others this intrinsic impossibility is not found, but still it is impossible to will that their maxim should be raised to the universality of a law of nature, since such a will would contradict itself. It is easily seen that the former violate strict or rigorous (inflexible) duty; the latter only laxer (meritorious) duty."

Here's my problem. The example meant to illustrate that second kind is the guy who's doing fine for himself and decides he owes nothing to anybody else. Kant flat out admits a whole world of people like that could exist, no logical contradiction anywhere — and then says you still couldn't will it, because you might need other peoples charity yourself somewhere down the line. That feels like a totally different move from the lying-promise example a page earlier, where the promise just logically cancels itself out the moment everyone's doing it.

So which is it, is this actually one test, or has he quietly swapped in a second, much weaker one that basically comes down to "you wouldn't like it if this caught on"? And if that second "contradiction" isn't really a logical contradiction at all, has he snuck in some assumption about what people can't help wanting, right after all his talk about keeping the whole thing free of anything empirical? I want my character to be sharp about this exact soft spot, but I need to know if it's a real soft spot in Kant, or one I'd be inventing just to make the plot work.
