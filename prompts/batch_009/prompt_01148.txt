Random work rabbit hole that I can't shake. We had a resource-scheduling feature basically fall over because someone tried to brute-force the "optimal" assignment, and while I was reading up on why that's a different flavor of hard than just "hard," I somehow ended up on the wikipedia page for Leibniz, of all people. Turns out back in the 1600s the guy spent a huge chunk of his life trying to build a "characteristica universalis" - basically a universal symbolic language for every concept there is - plus a "calculus ratiocinator" so that any dispute, philosophical, legal, whatever, could get settled the way two accountants settle a bill: sit down, run the calculation, done. His actual line for this was "calculemus" - let's just compute it. Wild thing to be chasing three centuries before Turing was even born.

Here's what's bugging me though. As far as I can tell, Leibniz's whole dream treats "formalizable" and "solvable" as basically the same thing, like once you've got the symbols and the rules down, cranking out the answer is just a matter of turning the handle long enough. He doesnt seem to have any notion of a question being answerable in principle but still practically hopeless because the computation blows up exponentially, which is kind of the entire point of P vs NP. Except then I also stumbled on this other bit of Leibniz where he says necessary truths only need a finite chain of steps to prove, while contingent truths need an "infinite analysis" that only God has the horsepower to actually finish. Which sounds suspiciously like he was groping toward some kind of complexity hierarchy without having the math for it, and now I can't tell if that's a real connection or just me pattern-matching noise.

So, genuine question, no "we can't know what a 300-year-dead guy would think" cop-outs: if you sat Leibniz down today and explained P vs NP to him properly (assume he gets the technical content fine, the man was clearly sharp enough), would it wreck his whole calculus ratiocinator project, or does his own system already have the resources - maybe that necessary/contingent, finite/infinite analysis distinction - to absorb it and shrug it off? I'd like an actual argued position either way, not just a "who can say."
