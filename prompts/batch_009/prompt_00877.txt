I've been working on a term paper for a philosophy of religion seminar on Molina's theory of middle knowledge, and I've gotten stuck on a technical dispute that strikes me as more interesting than the standard "doesn't God's foreknowledge rule out free will" question. Molinists typically claim that their view secures a libertarian freedom that Calvinist-style decree cannot, because the truth of any counterfactual of creaturely freedom (e.g., "if Peter were in circumstances C, he would freely deny Christ") is explanatorily prior to, yet not caused by, God's decision to create. On this picture, it is the creature rather than God who ultimately fixes which counterfactuals are true, and God simply works with the results when deciding which world to actualize.

Robert Adams, in an argument later defended and sharpened by William Hasker, presses a more technical version of the worry: assuming explanatory priority is transitive, if the truth of the counterfactual about me is explanatorily prior to God's decision to create me, which is explanatorily prior to my existing at all, which is in turn prior to any choice I make, then that same counterfactual truth turns out to be explanatorily prior to the very choice it describes — so the fact about what I would do seems to have been settled before there was any "me" around to settle it. Defenders of Molinism, including William Lane Craig, respond that the argument trades on an equivocal or overly generic notion of "explanatory priority," and that once the relevant relation is pinned down precisely, either the transitivity premise fails or the kind of priority that does hold throughout is too weak to generate the sort of fixity that actually threatens freedom.

Write an essay that reconstructs this dispute in enough detail to be useful to someone (like me) trying to write about it, and that then takes a side: does Molinism actually secure a kind of libertarian freedom that theological determinism lacks, or does the Adams–Hasker argument show that its "freedom" is no more substantial than the determinist's? Defend a definite answer rather than concluding that both sides simply have a point.
