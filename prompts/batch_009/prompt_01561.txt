Over the past month I've been in a painful dispute with my older sister over the care of our father, who was recently diagnosed with early-stage dementia. I confronted her about repeatedly cancelling the respite shifts she had agreed to cover, and she pointed out, not unreasonably, that I moved four hours away last year in part to avoid exactly this burden, and that I now see him only on holidays myself. I won't pretend there isn't some tension in my position, and the exchange sent me on something of a research tangent into the ethics literature on what's called the "standing to blame" — starting with G. A. Cohen's well-known essay asking who has the standing to condemn terrorism (not remotely my own situation, but apparently treated as one of the founding texts of this whole debate), and on into some of the responses it provoked.

What strikes me, reading around, is how much disagreement there is about what exactly strips a critic of standing. Some writers tie the loss of standing narrowly to hypocrisy: holding others to a standard that you yourself violate without ever acknowledging as much. Others think the real work is done simply by being "involved in," or complicit in, a comparable wrong — on this view it wouldn't matter whether you're honest about your own failings, since even a candid, non-hypocritical wrongdoer would still lack standing. And others emphasize whether the wrongdoing is any of your business to begin with, i.e., whether you stand in the sort of relationship to the wrongdoer that licenses you to speak at all. To help me get some precision that my own case doesn't afford, could you construct two original scenarios that pull these strands apart cleanly? I'd like, first, a case in which the critic is unambiguously a hypocrite in the narrow sense just described, yet intuitively retains full standing to blame; and second, a case in which the critic is not a hypocrite by any reasonable definition — indeed has never done anything resembling the wrong in question — yet intuitively lacks standing all the same. Please don't reach for the standard smoking parent or the unfaithful spouse who confronts his cheating friend; I've encountered those a dozen times already. For each scenario, I'd be grateful for a short diagnosis of which consideration (hypocrisy, complicity/involvement, or relationship/business) is actually doing the real work of preserving or destroying standing, since untangling that is precisely what I'm struggling with in my own situation.
