I've read enough rationalist writing on responsibility and blame to assume disputes like this usually have a clean underlying structure, but I can't find it in the case I'm actually stuck in, which isn't hypothetical: it's a live disagreement at my job.

Three of us on a small infrastructure team each separately noticed, over the course of a week, that a certificate underlying a production system was about to expire, and none of us filed the renewal ticket, each assuming one of the others already had. The certificate expired, the system went down, and we lost a chunk of customer data before anyone caught it. In the postmortem, I argued that I didn't cause the outage, only failed to prevent it, which matters especially because even if I had filed the ticket, there's a real chance it would have sat unactioned anyway given how backlogged the other two were. One colleague calls this a dodge, on the grounds that noticing a preventable failure and doing nothing is functionally identical to causing it. Another wants to skip the counterfactual question entirely and just ask who had explicit ownership of that alert, treating that as sufficient on its own for assigning blame.

None of us is applying a consistent standard, and I'd rather understand the actual crux of the disagreement than have it settled by whoever argues loudest in the postmortem. What's really going on here, and is there a principled way to decide who's responsible?
