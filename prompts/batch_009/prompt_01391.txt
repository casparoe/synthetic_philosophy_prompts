I have been working through Søren Kierkegaard's 1846 review-essay "En literair Anmeldelse" (often translated as "Two Ages: A Literary Review"), specifically its closing section, which contrasts the age of revolution with "the present age" and is frequently excerpted in English under that latter title. I am less interested here in the existentialist themes Kierkegaard is usually read for than in what this section says, almost in passing, about "the public" as a distinct kind of social entity — a topic that gets far less attention than the leap of faith or the anxiety material, despite (or perhaps because of) how strange the underlying picture is. Since the standard English translations I have consulted tend to smooth over exactly the paradox I want to think through, I went back to the Danish text (in the standard critical edition, Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter, vol. 8) and translated the relevant stretch myself. I would like you to work from my rendering below rather than from any summary of the book you might already have encountered, and to flag anything where you suspect my translation choices have themselves distorted the argument.

Here is the passage, from the part where Kierkegaard explains how "leveling" (Nivellering) becomes possible in the modern age:

"For leveling really to come about, a phantom must first be produced — its animating spirit, a monstrous abstraction, an all-encompassing something that is nothing, a mirage — and this phantom is the public. Only in an age that is passionless yet reflective can this phantom develop, with the help of the press, once the press itself has become an abstraction. In ages of enthusiasm, in passionate and tumultuous ages, even when a people wants to realize the barren ideal of the desert by destroying and effacing everything, there is nonetheless no public: there are parties, and there is concretion. The public is the true master of leveling, for when leveling happens only approximately, it happens by means of something — but the public is a monstrous nothing.

"The public is not a people, not a generation, not an age, not a congregation, not a society, not these particular persons — for all of these are what they are only through concretion. Indeed, not one single person belonging to the public has any essential commitment: for a few hours of the day he perhaps counts as belonging to the public, namely in the hours in which he is nothing, for in the hours in which he is the determinate person that he is, he does not belong to the public. Formed of such units, of individuals in the moments when they are nothing, the public is a monstrous something, the abstract wasteland and void that is everyone and no one. The public is everything and nothing, the most dangerous of all powers, and the most vacuous."

What I cannot work out is how the two halves of this picture are meant to fit together. On the one hand, the public is explicitly a "nothing," composed only out of individuals in precisely those moments when they carry no determinate commitments at all, whereas an actual people, party, or congregation is composed of individuals in their moments of concreteness and so counts, on Kierkegaard's own terms, as a "something." On the other hand, he insists that the public levels more thoroughly, and is more dangerous, than any of these concrete "somethings," which can at best level only "approximately." Why should sheer abstraction, or the absence of determinate content, make something a more effective leveling force rather than simply rendering it powerless? And is this connected to Kierkegaard's claim that only an age which is "passionless but reflective" can generate this phantom in the first place — that is, is there some conceptual link, on his view, between reflectiveness and passionlessness on one side and the peculiar kind of force he attributes to a "nothing" on the other, as opposed to the more ordinary force wielded by concrete parties or crowds? I would like a close reconstruction of the argument here, rather than a general summary of Kierkegaard's views on the crowd or on leveling.
