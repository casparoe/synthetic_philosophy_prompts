I'm a PhD student in economics, about to finish up this year, and something that came up while I was weighing where to move for my postdoc has been bothering me. Comparing my two leading options, Ashford and Brookfield, I prefer Ashford (shorter commute to the lab I'd join); comparing Brookfield and Camden, I prefer Brookfield (much lower cost of living); but comparing Ashford and Camden directly, I actually prefer Camden (by far the strongest group of researchers in my subfield). A friend of mine who's really into game theory pointed out that, in principle, she could exploit this: offer to swap my stated choice from Ashford to Camden for a small fee, since I prefer Camden; then from Camden to Brookfield for another fee; then from Brookfield back to Ashford for a third — and by my own pairwise judgments I'd agree to every swap, ending up exactly back at Ashford, just poorer for it. She says this proves my preferences are simply inconsistent, and that the only rational fix is to sit down, rank the three cities once and for all from best to worst, and never let that ranking bend depending on which two I happen to be comparing.

I'm not sure she's wrong that the trap would work on me in the moment, but I'm not convinced it shows what she thinks it shows. Each pairwise judgment tracks something I genuinely care about, and forcing them into one fixed ranking feels like it would paper over real, differently-weighted considerations rather than resolve them. Is my friend right that this makes me irrational, and that a single fixed ranking is the only way out? Or is there a flaw in her argument?
