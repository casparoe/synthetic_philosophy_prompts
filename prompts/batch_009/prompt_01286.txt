I cover science and ideas, and one story keeps pulling me back to Maimonides: the convergence of livestock genetics and end-times expectation. In September 2022, an American group shipped five red heifers from a Texas ranch to Jerusalem's Temple Institute, hoping that a wholly unblemished animal could finally supply the ashes that Numbers 19 requires for the ritual purification of priests — a precondition, on the traditional view, for resuming sacrificial worship in a rebuilt Third Temple. All five heifers were eventually disqualified over stray hairs, but the search continues.

Maimonides is the classical philosopher most identified with a deflationary account of animal sacrifice: in the Guide of the Perplexed (III:32), he argues that God tolerated sacrificial worship only as a concession to a people too habituated to the idolatrous cults of the ancient Near East to be weaned onto pure prayer all at once. Yet in the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melachim 11:1), the same Maimonides rules, without qualification, that the Messiah will rebuild the Temple and restore the sacrifices exactly as they were — and elsewhere he insists that the messianic age will come about through ordinary political and historical causes rather than miracles, while repeatedly cautioning against trying to calculate its arrival.

Reasoning only from those commitments: how do you think Maimonides would have judged today's red-heifer breeding programs? Would his own rationale for why sacrifice arose in the first place make such projects look like backsliding into the very mentality he thought Judaism was designed to outgrow — or does his insistence that sacrifice must literally return, combined with his naturalism about how redemption unfolds, commit him to treating careful animal husbandry as exactly the unglamorous, non-miraculous groundwork the messianic project requires? Argue for a specific verdict, and use it to show how you think he would have reconciled the Guide and the Mishneh Torah on this point.
