I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the past few months I've gotten a bit obsessed with a puzzle from experimental philosophy that I first heard about on a podcast and then chased down through blog posts and a couple of the original papers: the so-called Knobe effect. The classic case is a company chairman who is told that a new policy will increase profits but, as a side effect, harm the environment; he says he doesn't care one way or the other about the environment and approves the policy anyway. Most people asked say he harmed the environment "intentionally." Yet when the same vignette is rewritten so the side effect is helping the environment instead, hardly anyone says he helped it "intentionally," even though his stated indifference is identical in both versions. What I can't settle in my own head is which of two rival explanations for this asymmetry is actually correct.

One camp traces back to Joshua Knobe's own proposal that moral or evaluative considerations are built directly into our concept of intentional action, and it has since been elaborated further, for instance in Chandra Sripada's "deep self" model, on which a side effect counts as more intentional to the extent that bringing it about expresses the agent's stable values and character, rather than being merely incidental to them. The other camp, associated with a paper by Kevin Uttich and Tania Lombrozo, denies that evaluation of the outcome is doing any real work. On their account, violating any norm whatsoever, moral or not, makes it more reasonable to infer that the agent registered and weighed that consideration before acting, and it is this inference to a "considered" mental state, not badness, that produces the asymmetry. What makes me take this second view seriously is that they ran a version of the chairman case in which the only thing violated was an arbitrary, morally neutral company convention, something like an internal rule about what color a product should be, and still found a similar split in intentionality judgments. I have tried to invent a scenario that would cleanly separate the two theories, for instance a norm violation that is entirely non-moral but statistically rare, set against one that is moralized but statistically unremarkable, but every version I sketch seems to leave a defender of either camp with a ready reply.

Rather than a balanced survey of both positions, which I feel I could piece together myself, I would like you to actually take a stand. Please argue that, despite the evidence from non-moral norm violations, there is still good reason to think something distinctively evaluative, and not merely the detection of norm violation as such, is doing the essential work in the Knobe effect. I would like you to engage with the strongest version of the opposing "it's all just norms" view rather than a weaker one, and you are welcome to bring in other considerations you think are relevant, such as the effect's extension to notions like deciding or knowing, or the moral versus conventional distinction from developmental psychology, if you think they strengthen the case.
