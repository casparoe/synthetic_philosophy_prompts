I'm a PhD student in cognitive science—my own work is on computational models of visual attention, so this is somewhat outside my usual territory—and a close friend recently brought me a dilemma I haven't been able to think through as clearly as I'd like, even though I suspect it connects to a decision theory seminar I sat in on a couple of years ago.

My friend has treatment-resistant depression and has been offered a place in a trial of subcallosal cingulate deep brain stimulation. The outcome statistics her psychiatrist showed her look good, but she has also spent a lot of time reading patient forums and case reports, both from this trial and from people who've had DBS for other conditions, describing something odder than plain symptom relief: a sense that some of their attachments, ambitions, or even their sense of humor changed afterward in ways nobody had prepared them for—changes that they and the people around them mostly read as improvement, but that occasionally struck old friends as more like a replacement. She feels caught between two bad ways of deciding. She can't just ask whether she, as she currently is, wants that outcome, since her present preferences are shaped by the very depression the procedure targets, which is precisely why they're supposed to be unreliable here. But she's also unwilling to simply defer to whatever her post-stimulation self would say about it, since we don't normally treat a change as vindicated just because the person who comes out the other side endorses having gone through it—otherwise we'd have to say the same of certain cults, or abusive relationships that produce perfectly contented converts.

I told her this reminded me of an argument about a class of decisions where ordinary cost-benefit reasoning supposedly can't get a grip, because the standpoint from which you'd need to do the weighing doesn't exist until after you've decided—but I couldn't reconstruct the argument well enough to actually help her, or rule out the possibility that I was just dressing up "hard decisions are hard" in fancier language. Is there a real, principled way to think about a decision like hers, and if so, does it actually give her more than "look at the group-level data and decide how much you trust it"?
