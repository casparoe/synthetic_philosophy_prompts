I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, where the standard line has always been that frequentism is basically a historical dead end -- something intro stats courses keep around for institutional reasons -- while "real" probability is Bayesian, i.e., subjective credence in the tradition of Ramsey and de Finetti. Recently, though, I went down a rabbit hole in the academic philosophy-of-probability literature (the SEP, plus some Popper), and I came away thinking that both sides of that dispute are fighting over the wrong territory, because Popper's propensity theory seems to just straightforwardly beat them both.

My reasoning: frequentism's central weakness is the reference class problem that Venn and Reichenbach struggled with -- a single case (this particular election, this specific radioactive atom, this one coin toss) doesn't have a well-defined long-run frequency until you've already committed to a reference class, and there's no non-arbitrary way to choose one. Subjectivism sidesteps that problem, but only by giving up on there being any objective fact about how likely this atom is to decay in the next hour, independent of what anyone happens to believe. Propensity theory looks like it gets the best of both: probabilities are grounded in the physical chance setup itself, so they're not merely "in the head" the way credences are; they attach directly to the single case rather than to a hypothetical long-run sequence, so there's no reference class to specify; and a propensity is still, formally, nothing more than an ordinary probability, obeying the same Kolmogorov axioms as a frequency or a credence, just with a different metaphysical story about what grounds the numbers. Given all that, why isn't propensity theory simply treated as having settled the debate for objective, physical chances -- demoting frequentism and subjectivism to accounts of something else (data summaries and rational belief, respectively) rather than genuine rivals? Is there some technical problem with treating propensities as garden-variety probabilities that I'm missing, or is the persistence of the three-way debate mostly philosophical path-dependence at this point?
