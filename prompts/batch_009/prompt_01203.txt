I've been part of a small reading group working through eighteenth-century moral philosophy, and our most recent session was devoted to Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness (Discours sur le bonheur). One claim of hers that generated a lot of debate is her list of the necessary conditions for happiness: freedom from prejudice, virtue, health, cultivated tastes and passions, and, most contentiously, a deliberate susceptibility to illusions, since on her view we owe most of our pleasures to illusions and whoever loses that susceptibility becomes unhappy. On this basis she defends activities like gambling, theater-going, and passionate romantic love as valuable precisely because of the intensity of feeling, illusions included, that they generate, and she explicitly rejects the advice of moralists who counsel curbing one's passions and mastering one's desires.

Several people in the group wanted to read this as basically an Epicurean position, since du Châtelet agrees with Epicurus that pleasant feeling is the good at which a life should aim, but I suspect that framing hides more than it reveals. Could you compare her account of passions and illusions as constitutive of happiness with Epicurus's own hedonism—in particular, his preference for katastematic pleasure (the stable pleasure of aponia and ataraxia) over kinetic pleasure, his program of paring desire down to what is merely natural and necessary, and his well-known wariness of erotic passion, which he thought more likely to harm than help? I'm especially interested in whether du Châtelet's embrace of strong, even destabilizing, passions and self-serving illusions amounts to a genuine rejection of core Epicurean commitments rather than a variation on them, and, if so, whether "Epicurean" is still an apt label for her overall view or whether some other characterization fits her better.
