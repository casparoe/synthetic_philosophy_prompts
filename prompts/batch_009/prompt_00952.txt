I'm working on a novel with an old-school logical positivist type character (1930s Oxford, very dismissive of religion but for "rigorous" reasons, not just vibes), and I want a scene where he lays out why belief in an afterlife is basically nonsense. I dug up this bit from Ayer's Language, Truth and Logic to base it on, but the actual move he's making kind of loses me:

"It is common to find belief in a transcendent god conjoined with belief in an after-life. But, in the form which it usually takes, the content of this belief is not a genuine hypothesis. To say that men do not ever die, or that the state of death is merely a state of prolonged insensibility, is indeed to express a significant proposition, though all the available evidence goes to show that it is false. But to say that there is something imperceptible inside a man, which is his soul or his real self, and that it goes on living after he is dead, is to make a metaphysical assertion which has no more factual content than the assertion that there is a transcendent god."

What I can't figure out is why "men never die" gets to count as a real (if obviously false) claim, but "the soul survives" doesn't even rise to the level of false, its just meaningless to him. Whats the actual principle drawing that line, and is it a fair one, or is he basically just defining soul-talk out of the running so he wins by default? Trying to get my character's logic airtight before I put a version of this speech in his mouth.
