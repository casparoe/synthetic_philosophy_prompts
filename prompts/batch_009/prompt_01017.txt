I'm a PhD student studying a primitively eusocial wasp, and my committee is split on how to frame my dissertation's key finding: within any given nest, helpers who sneak in a bit of their own reproduction tend to outreproduce purely cooperative helpers, yet the cooperative trait persists across the population as a whole. My advisor wants this written up as straightforward kin selection: helpers are maximizing shared gene copies per Hamilton's rule. My second reader thinks that framing hides what's actually going on, which is selection between nests, and wants me to call it group selection instead. Neither of them disputes my data; they disagree about which description is correct. I've since learned that almost this exact fight broke out in evolutionary biology in 2010, when a Nature paper tried to unseat kin selection as the explanation for eusociality and drew a rebuttal signed by 137 biologists. My defense is in three weeks, and I still can't tell whether my advisors hold genuinely incompatible views about my own study system, or are arguing over bookkeeping dressed up as substance. Could you write a dialogue between a committed kin selectionist and a committed multi-level selectionist arguing about a case like mine, in which each is forced to directly engage the other's strongest point rather than talk past it — so that I can finally see whether there's real daylight between the two positions?
