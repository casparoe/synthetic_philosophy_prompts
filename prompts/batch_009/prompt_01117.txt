I'm taking a seminar this term on the history of analytic philosophy, and this week's session covered Gottlob Frege's political diary: the notebook he kept in the spring of 1924, now preserved in the Frege Archive at Münster, in which he expresses support for the Deutschvölkische Freiheitspartei, argues that Jews should be stripped of political rights or expelled from Germany, and states that Bismarck's one real mistake was introducing parliamentary government. This is the same material that so unsettled Michael Dummett, who had revered Frege as an utterly rational man before he came across it.

For my term paper, I'd like your comments on an argument I'm considering before I commit to it in writing. The thesis is this: Frege's logic rests on a fierce anti-psychologism — logical laws and 'thoughts' belong to a mind-independent 'third realm', graspable through reason alone and answerable to no vote or consensus. I want to argue that this same impulse, the conviction that truth is not something the many get to decide, also underlies his contempt for parliamentarism and universal suffrage, so that the diary is not an unrelated personal failing sitting alongside his logical work but is continuous with the outlook behind his Platonism.

Could you evaluate whether this argument holds up? In particular: does it collapse into some version of the genetic fallacy, or is there a genuine conceptual link here rather than a merely biographical one? And doesn't the fact that other prominent opponents of psychologism in logic during the same period, such as Russell and Carnap, ended up on the political left count as fairly serious evidence against any necessary connection between the two commitments?
