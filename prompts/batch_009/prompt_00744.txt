One puzzle from the "Robust Delegation" section of MIRI's Embedded Agency sequence has stayed with me: the Löbian obstacle documented in Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's work on tiling agents. Because a formal system that trusts its future self merely by trusting the output of its own reasoning turns out to be inconsistent (a corollary of Löb's theorem, closely related to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem), a proof-based agent can seemingly certify only a successor running on a strictly weaker deductive system than itself -- an apparent hard limit on rational self-trust under self-modification.

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp rationalist-forum regulars hashing this out at a meetup. One argues that the obstacle points to something real and basically irreducible about formal self-trust, and that later probabilistic moves like logical induction's calibrated self-trust merely relocate the underlying tension rather than actually resolve it. The other argues that the obstacle is an artifact of insisting on deductive certainty in the first place, and that logical induction genuinely dissolves the problem for any agent willing to settle for calibrated confidence instead of proof. Have the two engage each other's strongest points directly -- including whether "your successor must be provably weaker than you" is really fatal for iterated self-improvement, and whether probabilistic self-trust quietly reintroduces a regress of its own -- and don't let either side concede without a substantive counterargument.
