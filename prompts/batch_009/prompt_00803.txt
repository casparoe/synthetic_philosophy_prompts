So I'm a backend engineer, definitely not a philosophy guy, but I got into a whole argument with an old college friend (he did a philosophy PhD, I did CS) about "the frame problem" and now it's stuck in my head. The version I know is the classic AI one: back in old-school logic-based planning, if you want a system to reason about the world you can't just write down axioms for what an action changes, you'd also technically need a seperate axiom for every single thing it *doesn't* change (painting a chair doesn't move it, moving a chair doesn't repaint it, moving the chair doesn't change the price of tea in China, etc), which blows up to something like M x N axioms for M actions and N facts. People patched around this with non-monotonic logic tricks like circumscription, ran into weird edge cases like the Yale shooting problem, and mostly just engineered their way past it. Always felt to me like a real but kind of boring optimization/representation problem, the sort of thing a good engineer eventually routes around with the right data structure.

My friend says that's not really what philosophers mean anymore when they invoke "the frame problem." According to him, Dennett and then Fodor grabbed the term and turned it into something way bigger: how does any mind at all, human or machine, ever manage to figure out which of its ~infinite beliefs are even relevant to reconsider after something happens, without literally checking everything it knows? He was going on about Fodor's claim that central cognition is "informationally unencapsulated," some argument by analogy to how confirming a scientific theory can in principle draw on any other belief you hold, and even this bizzare thought experiment of his involving something Fodor called a "fridgeon" - I think the idea being that flipping a switch on a fridge technically changes some physical property of practically every particle in the room, so "most things stay the same when you act" isn't even obviously true once you get picky about what counts as a "thing." He thinks this version is way deeper than my engineering problem, maybe even a real argument that no tractable computational theory of mind can work, not just a puzzle waiting on smarter axioms.

So, genuine question: is my friend right that these are basically two different problems that happen to share a name, or is the philosophers' version just my narrow "what doesn't change" problem taken to its logical extreme? Specifically I'd love to know what the extra ingredient in the "relevance"/epistemological version actually is that isn't present in the bookkeeping version, and whether that ingredient is really a difference in kind rather than just in degree. And is there an actual argument for why it's unsolvable in principle (versus merely very hard), or is that more just Fodor being Fodor?
