I've been listening to a few philosophy of religion debates recently — the kind where someone like William Lane Craig defends classical theism against various objections — and I got interested in a view called Molinism. As I understand it, the core idea is that, logically prior to deciding which world to create, God has "middle knowledge" of what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible set of circumstances. This is supposed to let God providentially arrange history in exhaustive detail without ever overriding anyone's free will: he simply chooses which circumstances to actualize, already knowing, via these counterfactuals, how each person would freely respond in them.

The objection that keeps coming up is the "grounding objection." The idea, roughly, is that a claim like "if Peter were placed in circumstances C, he would freely deny Christ" can't actually be true or false, because there's nothing to make it true: logically prior to God's decision to create, there is no Peter, no choice has been made, and nothing about Peter's character or circumstances necessitates one answer over the other — if it did, the choice wouldn't be free in the sense Molinists need. So allegedly there's just no fact of the matter about what he'd do.

Here's what's been bugging me. I build experimentation infrastructure for a living, and reasoning about counterfactual outcomes — what would have happened to this particular user if they'd been shown the other variant — is something my field handles routinely and rigorously. The entire potential-outcomes framework in causal inference is built on comparing counterfactual quantities that can never be jointly observed for the same unit, and nobody treats that as a metaphysical crisis; we just define the quantities, adopt some assumptions, and estimate away. So it seems to me that the grounding objection is basically an artifact of pre-statistical thinking, and that modern causal inference already shows how to make peace with unobservable counterfactuals. I mentioned this to a friend who's doing a philosophy PhD, and she just smiled and said "that's not quite the same problem," without elaborating. Is there actually a meaningful disanalogy between the two cases, or was she just being cagey?
