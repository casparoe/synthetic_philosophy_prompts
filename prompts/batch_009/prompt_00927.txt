I'm working on my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy, and I picked the theory of vagueness as my topic, focusing specifically on the sorites paradox and two of the rival technical solutions to it: epistemicism, in the style of Timothy Williamson, and supervaluationism, in the style of Kit Fine and Rosanna Keefe. I can explain each view well enough on its own, roughly that the epistemicist thinks predicates like "heap" have a perfectly sharp cutoff that we are simply constitutionally unable to know, while the supervaluationist thinks such predicates are true on every admissible way of making them precise, false on every admissible way, or neither in the borderline cases. My supervisor's comment on my last draft was that it reads like two separate encyclopedia entries stapled together rather than an actual disagreement, and I think she's right that I haven't shown where the two views would really get stuck arguing with each other.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed epistemicist and a committed supervaluationist to help me see the disagreement in action? I'd like it set somewhere mundane, maybe over a dwindling bowl of rice at dinner, since that's the example my teacher always uses in class. I specifically want the supervaluationist to press the epistemicist on how a fact as fine-grained as "exactly this many grains" could possibly be fixed by anything about our actual (obviously imprecise) use of the word "heap," and I want the epistemicist to answer with something like Williamson's margin-for-error story before turning around and pressing the supervaluationist on whether "definitely" is itself vague, so that the higher-order vagueness problem just reproduces the original sorites one level up rather than solving it. Rather than having one side clearly win, I'd like the dialogue to end with each of them having to admit, at least to themselves, which part of their own theory is hardest to live with.
