I'm a retired research chemist who has spent the last few years reading through the classical empiricists, and lately one small, well-documented episode in Ayer's life has occupied me more than the general doctrine. After his heart briefly stopped in 1988, A. J. Ayer wrote that the experience had "slightly weakened my conviction that my genuine death ... will be the end of me," though it left his atheism untouched. A few weeks later he revised even that, saying what had really shifted was "not my belief that there is no life after death, but my inflexible attitude towards that belief." This sits oddly against two commitments Ayer held on independent grounds: the verification principle, on which claims about an immortal soul are strictly meaningless rather than merely improbable, and his own account of personal identity, worked out at length in The Origins of Pragmatism, which makes bodily continuity essential and so leaves no obvious content for "survival" to have once the body is gone. Please compare Ayer the verificationist with Ayer the witness to his own experience. Can a committed verificationist coherently hold a "conviction," of any strength, about a proposition his own philosophy calls meaningless, or that his own theory of persons rules out as incoherent? And is his postscript distinction between a belief and one's attitude toward it a genuine fix, or just a relocation of the same problem?
