I'm writing a novel set during the final deliberations of a fictional literary-prize jury, and I'd like a pivotal scene to turn on a genuine philosophical disagreement rather than on personality. With only days left before the shortlist is due, one judge, Nadia, hasn't finished several of the longlisted novels and proposes to rely on a trusted colleague's detailed account of the ones she's missed. Her fellow judge, Julian, objects that no report—however careful, however trusted its source—can substitute for Nadia reading the book herself, and that a vote cast on that basis isn't really a judgment of the novel's merit at all. Nadia presses him on why aesthetic testimony should be treated so differently from the testimony he relies on everywhere else in his own critical practice. Write the scene as a dialogue between them, and have Julian eventually concede that testimony might, in principle, transmit a true belief about a book's quality, while insisting that something is still owed to the novel, or to the office of judging, that only direct encounter can satisfy. Neither should be a pushover for the other: give both of them real, escalating counters rather than a restatement of first positions, and let the scene end without a tidy resolution.
