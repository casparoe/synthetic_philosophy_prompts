A friend of mine from undergrad went off and did a philosophy PhD while the rest of us went into software, and she's been sending me stuff about Donald Davidson lately because she knows I've been arguing with people at work about whether LLMs actually "understand" language or if it's all just glorified autocomplete. The idea that got stuck in my head is his "radical interpretation" thing: basically you figure out what someone means AND what they believe at the same time, starting from zero shared language, just by watching when they assent or dissent to sentences in different situations, using a "principle of charity" that assumes they're mostly rational and right about the world to bootstrap the whole process. Apparently later in his career he added this "triangulation" requirement too, where you need two creatures both reacting to the same object in the world and to each other before there's even a fact of the matter about what either of them is responding to - thats supposedly what makes content "objective" rather than just noise. And then theres the Swampman thing, this molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Davidson created by a random lightning strike, which acts exactly like him but which Davidson himself apparently said doesn't mean or believe anything, because it has no causal history with the world or with other speakers.

Given he died in 2003, well before any of this LLM stuff, I'm curious what you think he'd actually say if you handed him GPT-4 or something similar and asked him to apply his own framework to it. Would radical interpretation even be possible on a model like that, given it's never assented or dissented to anything in a shared world the way an actual person Davidson meets would have? Does the triangulation requirement basically rule out LLMs having real beliefs or meaning by construction, or could training on tons of human-generated text plus RLHF count as some kind of weird, indirect triangulation? And does the Swampman comparison actually hold up here, or does it break down once you look closer? I'd rather you actually reconstruct his reasoning step by step than just give me a "some philosophers think X, others think Y" survey - I can get that from the Wikipedia page.
