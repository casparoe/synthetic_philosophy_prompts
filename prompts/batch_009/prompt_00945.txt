Last night a friend and I ended up in a surprisingly long argument about how much stock to put in long-range weather forecasts -- she checks the ten-day outlook religiously before making plans, whereas I think anything past three or four days is closer to guesswork than genuine prediction. We went back and forth for the better part of two hours, each of us citing our own track record of forecasts that had panned out or fallen flat, and neither of us budged. At some point she joked that, as two people who each consider ourselves reasonably rational, we technically shouldn't have been able to end the night still disagreeing -- which sent us down a half-remembered attempt to reconstruct Aumann's agreement theorem: something about two Bayesians with the same prior being unable to disagree once their posteriors on some question are common knowledge, plus a related result to the effect that if you just keep exchanging updated posteriors back and forth, rather than re-litigating the underlying evidence, you're eventually forced to converge.

I'd like you to write me a short joke built around the gap between that tidy idealized result and what it's actually like when two stubborn people argue about something like weather forecasts. For it to really land for someone who already knows the theorem, the punchline should hinge on a specific piece of the actual machinery -- the common prior assumption, common knowledge as something stronger than just both of you having heard the same arguments, or the fact that the convergence result technically requires exchanging posteriors rather than reasons -- rather than being a generic joke about two people who won't budge. A one-liner or a brief exchange is fine; it doesn't need an elaborate setup.
