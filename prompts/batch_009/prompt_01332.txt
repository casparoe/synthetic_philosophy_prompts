For my IB Extended Essay, I've been reading about rational choice theory, and I keep getting stuck on the so-called backward-induction paradox in the centipede game. The standard argument concludes that a fully rational player should end the game and take the pot on the very first move, since rationality is supposed to unravel all the way back from the final round. But that conclusion seems to depend on common knowledge of rationality holding at every node, including nodes that, by the theory's own logic, should only be reached if someone has already failed to play rationally. So if play somehow continues to round 10, it looks like a player has to keep treating their opponent as rational enough for backward induction to still be the correct way to reason, while also knowing that this same opponent must have deviated from rational play to get them both there. I haven't come across an explanation that resolves this cleanly, so could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp game theorists sitting down across from each other to actually play a several-round centipede game—one who maintains that backward induction still delivers the uniquely rational answer despite this difficulty, and one who thinks it shows the whole argument is self-undermining? I'd like them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest objections rather than simply restate their own opening position, and the disagreement doesn't need to be resolved by the end.
