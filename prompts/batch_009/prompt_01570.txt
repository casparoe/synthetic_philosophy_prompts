I'm a PhD student working on the reception of early modern rationalism in contemporary analytic metaphysics. Leibniz's theodicy rests on a specific picture: infinitely many complete, internally compossible worlds exist as objects of God's understanding, and the principle of sufficient reason requires that God's will actualize exactly one of them, namely whichever is best. Writing in the twentieth century, David Lewis defended a modal realism that keeps the infinity of concrete worlds but discards the actualizing altogether: every world is equally real and concrete, and "actual" is merely indexical, true of each world relative to its own inhabitants, much as "here" and "now" pick out different places and times for different speakers. Suppose Leibniz had to respond to Lewis directly: could the claim that ours is "the best of all possible worlds" survive as a substantive, non-vacuous thesis once there is no longer any privileged act of selection among worlds, or would his entire theodicy unravel without one? Reconstruct the strongest Leibnizian rejoinder you can, engaging concretely with Lewis's indexical analysis of actuality.
