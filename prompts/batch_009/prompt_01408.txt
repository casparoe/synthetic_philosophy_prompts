Got nerd-sniped by this a couple weeks ago after a tangent in a rationalist-adjacent discord about how you'd even formalize "fair" for a two-party negotiation. Everyone's first answer is the Nash Bargaining Solution — maximize the product of each side's utility gain over the disagreement point — and my game theory course presented it as basically the canonical answer, uniquely pinned down by four clean axioms: efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, and independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA).

Then I fell down a rabbit hole into the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, which keeps the first three axioms but swaps out IIA for something called individual monotonicity, and the motivating case against Nash kind of wrecked my confidence in it. Setup: take a bargaining problem and expand it so that my "ideal point" (the best I could possibly get while still leaving you at least your disagreement payoff) goes strictly up, while your ideal point doesn't move at all. Naively you'd think that can only help me, or at worst leave me unaffected. But under Nash's solution that's not guaranteed - depending on how the new frontier curves, I can actually end up with less then before, purely as an artifact of where the utility-product-maximizing point happens to land. Kalai-Smorodinsky is basically built to rule that out, at the cost of IIA. I brought this up with a friend who did her masters in econ and she just said "IIA's the load-bearing one, monotonicity failures are a quirk everyone lives with," which felt like a total non-answer.

So here's what I actually want from you: mount the strongest case you can that Kalai-Smorodinsky has the better claim to formalizing fairness here, i.e. that we should be willing to give up IIA to preserve individual monotonicity rather than the reverse. Don't just re-explain the two solutions to me, i basically know the setup already - i want the actual normative argument for why "your best case shouldn't get worse when only your own options improve" deserves more weight than "the outcome shouldn't depend on alternatives nobody picked", and ideally you address the strongest pushback a die-hard Nash defender would make.
