Setup everyone knows: flip a fair coin repeatedly, payout starts at $1 and doubles every time it lands tails, the game ends (and you collect) the instant it lands heads. Expected payout is infinite. Nobody actually pays more than like $10-20 to play. That's the paradox. Bernoulli's classic fix: switch from expected dollars to expected log-utility of dollars, comes out finite, done, case closed.

Except apparently Menger (1934) broke this: he built a "Super St Petersburg" version where the payout on round n is w*exp(2^n) - w instead of 2^(n-1) (note the payout formula literally has the player's own wealth baked into it, which is a weird move), and showed that even under log utility the expected utility gain is STILL infinite. Generalized, this seemed to prove no unbounded utility function can ever fully resolve St Petersburg-style games — only bounded ones can — and this apparently got waved through by people like Samuelson and Arrow for something like 77 years.

The part I want pushback on: Ole Peters' ergodicity-economics redo of this (2011, Phil Trans A) ditches utility entirely and instead computes the time-average growth rate of one person's wealth across repeated rounds, instead of the usual ensemble average across parallel copies of them. It lands on the exact same formula as Bernoulli's log utility, but Peters insists that's incidental — the log is doing compounding-arithmetic, not measuring usefulness. Then, in the same paper, he turns around and argues Menger's proof has a bug: Menger's divergent "gain" term ignores a second, hidden divergence in the "loss from paying for the ticket" term that shows up right as the ticket price approaches the player's wealth, and once you account for both, log utility resolves the modified game fine after all.

Two things I cant fully talk myself into:

1. The time-average derivation openly depends on treating repeated rounds as "equivalent lotteries" (same wealth, same price) even though real wealth updates after every round — Peters patches this by rescaling time into infinitesimal sub-intervals rather than requiring literal repetition. Is that a legit way to get a genuine time-average out of what's really one draw, or is it just relabeling the ensemble average as a time average because they happen to integrate to the same thing here?
2. Even granting Peters caught a real bug in Menger, does that actually rehabilitate unbounded utility functions in general, or just show this one specific construction fails, leaving the door open for some other divergence a Menger-style critic could cook up against the time-average criterion itself?
