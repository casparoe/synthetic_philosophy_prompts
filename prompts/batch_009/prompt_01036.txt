Okay so this is a weirdly specific rabbit hole but bear with me. Last weekend at game night my smart bulb glitched and started flickering on and off super fast before the app would even respond, and my friend Jake made a joke about "Thomson's lamp." Turns out that's an actual thought experiment: you flip a lamp on, then off after 1/2 a minute, then on after another 1/4 minute, then off after another 1/8, and so on forever, cramming infinitely many flips into one single minute. The question is: once the minute's up, is the lamp on or off? I got kind of obsessed and fell down a wiki hole about "supertasks" (apparently that's the general name for infinite-steps-in-finite-time puzzles like this), and learned the whole thing is basically a descendant of Zeno's paradoxes, which means Aristotle was already wrestling with a version of it over 2000 years before Thomson.

So I dug up the actual bit where Aristotle tries to handle it (Physics, Book VI, ch. 2, in the old Hardie & Gaye translation), and I've read it about six times and only kind of get it:

"Hence Zeno's argument makes a false assumption in asserting that it is impossible for a thing to pass over or severally to come in contact with infinite things in a finite time. For there are two senses in which length and time and generally anything continuous are called 'infinite': they are called so either in respect of divisibility or in respect of their extremities. So while a thing in a finite time cannot come in contact with things quantitatively infinite, it can come in contact with things infinite in respect of divisibility: for in this sense the time itself is also infinite: and so we find that the time occupied by the passage over the infinite is not a finite but an infinite time, and the contact with the infinites is made by means of moments not finite but infinite in number."

Can you help me actually unpack what move he's making here? My best guess is he's saying "infinite" is ambiguous between infinitely-many-parts and infinitely-large-in-extent, and Zeno's runner only ever needs the first kind, which is fine because time is divisible the same way distance is. But is that basically identical to the modern calc-101 answer (the intervals shrink geometrically so they sum to something finite), just dressed in different words, or is Aristotle actually claiming something subtly stronger or weaker than that? And seperately - does this move give him ANY resources for dealing with my lamp, or does the lamp sneak in something totally different that his solution just can't see, since a runner's position converges to one nice limit point but "on/off" doesn't converge to anything at all? I can't tell if I'm missing something in the text, or if that's genuinely the exact spot where Thomson's version escapes the ancient solution entirely.
