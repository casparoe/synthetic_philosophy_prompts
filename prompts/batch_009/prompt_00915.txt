A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about a question we couldn't resolve, and I'd like a considered outside opinion. In his 1860 Glasgow speech on the Constitution, Frederick Douglass was answering fellow abolitionists who argued that the Constitution's pro-slavery character was proven by over seventy years of pro-slavery practice under it. Douglass didn't dispute the practice; he denied that it settled the question of meaning, insisting that wherever "human liberty and justice are at stake," interpreters need "something more than history — something more than tradition": for instance, a presumption that ambiguous text be read in favor of liberty, with rights curtailed only by language of "irresistible clearness." My friend thinks this makes Douglass an unlikely forerunner of today's "history and tradition" school of constitutional interpretation, running from Glucksberg through Dobbs and Bruen, on the grounds that he too wanted judges constrained by something more fixed than their own moral convictions. I think Douglass's own words amount to a fairly direct rebuttal of that method, since he is explicitly telling us that a society's historical practice is poor evidence of what liberty actually requires. Working from Douglass's specific arguments, rather than his general reputation as an antislavery hero, how do you think he would assess the Court's current reliance on history and tradition to fix the scope of constitutional rights?
