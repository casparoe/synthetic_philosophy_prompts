I've been reporting on the long-running public-health dispute between "abstinence-only" drug and alcohol education and harm-reduction measures — supervised drug-consumption sites, drug-checking tents at festivals, campus programs that teach moderate drinking rather than demanding total abstinence — and I keep coming back to an unlikely precedent: Plato's discussion of wine in Books 1 and 2 of the Laws. There, the Athenian Stranger rejects the Spartan and Cretan policy of simply banning drink outright, on the grounds that it trains citizens to resist fear and pain while leaving their self-restraint against pleasure entirely untested. His preferred alternative is the tightly regulated drinking party: no wine at all before eighteen, wine in moderation but never to the point of drunkenness from eighteen to thirty, and freer indulgence only after forty, with every gathering overseen by a sober master of ceremonies and the wine itself serving as a cheap, low-stakes test of character.

Based specifically on those arguments, rather than on the generic idea that Plato simply favored moderation in all things, which side of today's abstinence-versus-harm-reduction debate do you think he would actually land on — and at what point would his reasoning stop mapping cleanly onto how harm-reduction advocates justify their programs today?
