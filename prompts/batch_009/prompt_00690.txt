I'm writing a novel set in the Bavarian courts of 1949, built around a case drawn from history. In 1944 a woman denounced her husband for disparaging remarks about Hitler made while he was home on leave; a military tribunal sentenced him to death, but he was never executed and survived the war. Afterward, the wife stood trial before an appellate court in Bamberg for unlawfully depriving him of his liberty, pleading that her denunciation had simply invoked the law as it then stood. This is the "grudge informer" case at the heart of H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller's famous exchange on legal positivism and natural law, and I want the judge in my novel to reach his verdict with their rigor, not a caricature of decent judges versus evil law. Hart held that the wartime statute was valid law, however odious, and that the only honest way to convict her lay in an openly retroactive statute that admitted its own cost. Fuller held that a regime so devoid of the "inner morality of law" never produced valid law to begin with, so no question of retroactivity arises. Write an essay weighing these two positions on their merits and reaching a verdict on how a rigorous judge should actually rule. I'm not looking for a survey of both sides: tell me, and him, which way the better argument points, and why.
