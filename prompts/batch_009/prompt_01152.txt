I've been reporting a longer feature on philosophers who thought music belonged to a different metaphysical category than the other arts, and I keep returning to Schopenhauer's treatment of the subject in Book Three of The World as Will and Representation (section 52, plus a whole supplementary essay on "the metaphysics of music" he added years later) because of how literal-minded it is. His broader aesthetic theory holds that architecture, sculpture, painting, and tragedy each let us contemplate a different "grade" of the Will's objectification by way of its corresponding Platonic Idea. Music, he insists, isn't in that business at all: rather than copying any Idea, it copies the Will directly, which is supposedly why it can overwhelm us in a way no painting or play manages. He even maps the mechanics of tonal music onto degrees of nature — the ground bass standing in for inert, inorganic matter, the inner harmonic voices for plant and animal life, the melody line for the restless, deliberating human intellect — and argues this is why music can render something like joy or grief in the abstract, detached from any particular object or backstory, in a way words and images never quite achieve.

For a companion piece, I'd like to try dramatizing the theory rather than explaining it to readers. Would you write a short story, no more than a page or two, centered on a character — a composer finishing a score, an amateur cellist, a sound engineer doing a late-night mix, or anyone else you find more alive — who undergoes something like what Schopenhauer describes: an experience in which music seems to strip away the ordinary world of objects, stories, and motives, and put the listener in contact with something like the Will itself, underneath it all? I'd like the story to actually put his specific machinery to work — the bass/harmony/melody correspondence, the business about emotion "in the abstract," the claim that music is metaphysically prior to representation — rather than gesturing vaguely at "the power of music," and I'd rather it not resolve into a tidy epiphany. I'm more interested in whether the metaphysics survives being embodied in a scene than in a moral.
