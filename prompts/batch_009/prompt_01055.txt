I'm taking an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology this term, and the last couple of weeks have focused on higher-order evidence and its connection to epistemic akrasia. We spent a lot of time on two standard cases: the hypoxic pilot (often called "Aisha" in the literature) who has strong instrument-based evidence that she has enough fuel to reach her destination, but who also knows that pilots performing exactly this kind of calculation at her altitude get the right answer only about half the time; and Timothy Williamson's unmarked-clock case, where the setup itself guarantees that a perfectly rational observer will end up doubting whether her own credence is properly calibrated. Both are meant to introduce epistemic akrasia: believing some proposition P while also believing, on the basis of separate evidence about your own reliability, that your evidence doesn't really support P. About half the seminar defended a "level-splitting" view, on which this mismatch can be perfectly rational because first-order and higher-order evidence answer different questions; the rest thought any such mismatch is, by definition, a failure of rationality that something like an "enkratic principle" should rule out. What has stuck with me more than either position, honestly, is just how strange it would be to actually be the person caught in the middle of it.

For our final assignment, we've been given the option of submitting a short story instead of a paper, provided it engages seriously with a concept from the syllabus. I want to write mine on epistemic akrasia, but without simply re-dressing the pilot or the clock in new clothes, since my professor would see through that immediately. Could you write me a self-contained short story, somewhere around 800 to 1,200 words, whose protagonist ends up genuinely epistemically akratic? Over the course of the story, they should acquire specific, concrete evidence that some faculty they have been relying on - memory, perception, inference under stress, or anything else you think would work dramatically - is compromised in a way that would ordinarily undercut a particular belief of theirs, even as the original grounds for that belief remain just as compelling to them as before. The character should have to decide what to believe and what to do without the tension resolving itself neatly. I would like the scenario and the specific nature of the compromising evidence to be entirely your own invention rather than an adaptation of an existing thought experiment, and I would prefer that the story leave open whether the character's persistent confidence was actually justified - much like the real disagreement we have been having in seminar.
