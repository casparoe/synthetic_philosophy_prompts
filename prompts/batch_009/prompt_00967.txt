I've spent the past several months slowly working through James Legge's 1891 translation of the Zhuangzi in the evenings. I have no background in philosophy or in classical Chinese — I just found a free copy online after my grandmother died last year and found myself wanting something older and steadier than the usual self-help material to think about death with. Most of it I can puzzle through well enough on my own, but I've been stuck for a couple of weeks now on a passage in the chapter called "The Great and Most Honoured Master." Just before it, one of four friends, Zi-li, comes to the bedside of another, Zi-lai, who is dying while his family wails around him, and asks him almost teasingly what the Creator is going to turn him into next — perhaps "the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect." Zi-lai answers:

'Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious. There is the great Mass (of nature);-- I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it: what has made my life a good will make my death also a good. Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, "I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-ye," the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, "I must become a man; I must become a man," the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking.'

What I can't quite work out is what exactly the metal-in-the-furnace image is condemning. Is Zi-lai saying it is foolish to want to go on existing at all once your allotted span is up, or is the target narrower than that — something more like wanting to control the particular shape your existence takes, as though insisting on becoming this sword rather than that one, or a person rather than something else entirely? Those feel like two different claims to me, and the passage seems to lean on both without ever quite distinguishing them. I'm also curious how "the great Mass (of nature)" is supposed to relate to "the Creator" two sentences later — are these just two names Legge is using for one and the same impersonal process, or is there a real conceptual difference in the original that an old-fashioned translation like this one might be smoothing over? And, standing back a bit, is there an actual argument in this passage for meeting death with equanimity, or is it doing something more like talking the reader into a feeling rather than reasoning them into a conclusion? I would like to work through what the passage itself is doing, on its own terms, before I go looking at what anyone else has made of it.
