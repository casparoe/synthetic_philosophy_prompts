He sat with the pen an hour before writing anything. Outside, Princeton was thick with summer, indifferent to him. Dear Mother, he began, then crossed it out four times before he let himself say what he actually thought: that the world was not, could not be, an accident of unreason -- that if it had a rational structure at all (and everything he'd spent his life proving suggested it did) then it could not also permit the kind of waste a purely mortal life would represent. A man does not spend sixty years assembling a mind, a set of loves, a way of seeing, only to have the whole apparatus deleted the moment it becomes any good. That would be a world lying about its own premises. So there had to be a continuation -- not, he was careful to write, because he wanted comfort, but because the alternative was logically worse then any doctrine of heaven he'd ever been asked to swallow.
