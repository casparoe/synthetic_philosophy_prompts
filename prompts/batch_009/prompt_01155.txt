I spent nearly forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have been filling my time with philosophy, currently working through a local reading group's syllabus on epistemology — a humbling change of pace after a career spent mostly with instruments and error bars. We have just finished a unit on Descartes and Hume, and next month the group moves on to more contemporary material, but an old episode from my own career keeps resurfacing in my mind, and I do not think it will wait that long, so I would be grateful for some outside perspective.

Early in my career, my research group had collected data pointing fairly strongly toward a new effect, but we had not yet ruled out a subtle systematic error in our apparatus that, if present, would have accounted for the entire signal; ruling it out properly would have taken the better part of a year. My co-investigator wanted our paper's abstract to state flatly that we had "observed" the effect, whereas I preferred something more hedged, along the lines of "our data are consistent with" or "we find preliminary evidence for." He argued, not unreasonably, that we were both better than 90 percent confident and that hedging was just false modesty. As it happens, the effect was confirmed a few years later by other groups using quite different apparatus, and he has cited this ever since as proof that his instinct was the right one: "it was true, and I said so, so what exactly was your objection?"

That reply has never sat right with me, though I have struggled to say precisely why, especially given that he turned out to be correct. Was I simply being too cautious? Or is there a principled sense in which what he was entitled to say at the time should have depended only on the evidence we actually possessed then, rather than on a fact about the world that neither of us was yet in a position to know? I would value the group's judgment on who had the better of this argument, and why.
