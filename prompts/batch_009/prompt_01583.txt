I've been rereading Rousseau's 1758 "Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre" — his broadside against d'Alembert's proposal, in the Encyclopédie, to give Geneva a public playhouse — for a piece on whether popular entertainment shapes a society's morals or merely reflects them back to it. One passage has me stuck. I've translated it myself below, to be sure I'm working from what Rousseau actually wrote rather than any one translator's gloss:

"The stage, generally speaking, offers a portrait of human passions, whose originals exist in every heart; but had the painter failed to flatter those passions, audiences would quickly have been put off, unwilling to see themselves cast in a light that bred self-contempt. When he does paint a passion in ugly colors, it is only one that is uncommon and already an object of natural dislike — so that even here the author is merely following public feeling. Such discarded, unflattering passions are invariably used only to throw others into relief: passions that may not be more legitimate, but are certainly more to the audience's taste. Reason alone is worthless on the stage. A man without passions, or one who had fully mastered them, could hold no one's interest there, and — as has been observed before — a Stoic would make an insufferable figure in tragedy, and in comedy would at best raise a laugh. Let no one, then, credit the theatre with any power to change sentiments or morals; it can only follow and embellish them. Any author who set himself against the prevailing taste would soon be writing for himself alone."

Read in isolation, this sounds like a strong, almost sociological claim: because playwrights depend on pleasing audiences who already hold certain passions and tastes, drama is causally inert with respect to morals — it can only flatter and entrench sentiments that precede it. Yet the entire practical argument of the letter is that installing a theatre in Geneva would corrupt the city by importing Parisian sentiments foreign to it, gradually reshaping what Genevans find admirable or shameful. Those two claims seem to be in real tension: either the theatre is powerless to alter a community's sentiments, or it isn't.

Can this passage be reconciled with Rousseau's broader case against a Genevan theatre, or does it expose a genuine fault line in his argument? I'm especially curious whether he has some implicit distinction in mind — between what a single play can do to an audience in one sitting and what the institution of theatre does to a city over decades — that would let both claims stand.
