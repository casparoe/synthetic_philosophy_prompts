I spent thirty-five years working as an experimental physiologist before I retired, and for the past year or so I have been using my newfound free time to work through some of the philosophy I never had space for during my research career. A friend from a philosophy discussion group I recently joined pointed me toward Robert Nozick's treatment of Newcomb's Problem, and I confess I have not been able to leave it alone since. I follow the two competing intuitions well enough on their own terms: causal decision theory tells you to take both boxes, since by the time you choose, the million is already there or it is not, and nothing you do now can reach back and change that; evidential decision theory tells you to take only the opaque box, since one-boxing is such overwhelming evidence of being the sort of person who ends up rich. What I have not been able to settle for myself is what, if anything, this is supposed to teach someone who is never going to meet a reliable predictor of this kind.

The variant that has really got under my skin is the "smoking lesion" case, in which a hidden gene causes both a taste for smoking and a susceptibility to lung cancer, so that smoking itself has no causal effect on health, and yet a naive evidentialist seems committed to giving it up anyway, purely because abstaining would be good news about one's genotype. This struck uncomfortably close to home, since it revived memories of the "constitutional hypothesis" episode from my student days, when Fisher himself seriously argued that the smoking-cancer correlation might be a statistical artifact of shared genetic causes rather than a genuinely causal relationship, and more generally of how much of observational science has always had to live with exactly this ambiguity between correlation and common cause.

Now that I no longer design my own experiments and mostly just read the scientific literature as an interested outsider, I keep encountering studies of the form "people who do X enjoy better outcome Y," in domains where a randomized trial is impractical or unethical. I would very much like you to set out what you take to be the most important implications of Newcomb's Problem, and of the causal/evidential decision theory dispute it gave rise to, for how someone in my position ought to reason about, and act on, correlational findings of that kind. In particular, I would be grateful if you could address whether committing to causal decision theory really does commit one to a general wariness about acting on unexplained correlations, or whether there is some important disanalogy between the predictor's relationship to my choice and an epidemiological correlation's relationship to my behavior that would make drawing that lesson too quick.
