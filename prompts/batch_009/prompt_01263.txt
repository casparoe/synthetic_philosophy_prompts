I'm several drafts into a novel, working title The Census of Hallucinations, built around the real circle of Cambridge philosophers and classicists who founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 — Henry and Eleanor Sidgwick, Frederic Myers, Edmund Gurney — and I've reached the point where I owe the reader an author's note explaining what in the book is documented and what I've invented. I keep circling a line of Keynes's, quoted in Bart Schultz's biography of Sidgwick, that he "never did anything but wonder whether Christianity was true and prove it wasn't and hope that it was," because it names exactly the tension the novel is built on: a philosopher who resigned his fellowship rather than keep professing a creed he doubted, then spent the back half of his life on committees, census forms, and cross-examined mediums, trying to obtain, by other means, the reassurance his own ethics had failed to supply. My editor's worry, which I share on my more honest days, is that this is a novelist's tidy pattern imposed on a life that was probably messier and less thematically obliging. Here is the note as it currently stands; what I want from you is not a copyedit but three honest reactions to it.

"Henry Sidgwick, Eleanor Sidgwick, Frederic Myers, and Edmund Gurney were real people; the scenes in which they appear here are invented, though I have tried not to give them opinions they could not plausibly have held. In 1869 Henry resigned his Trinity fellowship rather than go on subscribing to a creed he no longer believed. Five years later he published The Methods of Ethics, which ends not in resolution but in an admission that reason can defend both a life of impartial benevolence and a life of self-interest with equal force, and offers no way to choose between them — what he called, in words his commentators still quote back at him, the cosmos of duty reduced to chaos. In 1882 he became the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, and gave much of the rest of his life to census forms, sealed test-letters, and the patient statistical labor behind Phantasms of the Living. I have chosen to treat this not as a second, separable enthusiasm but as the same problem pursued by other means: if reason alone could not show him that virtue and self-interest must ultimately coincide, perhaps some fact about the world — a life after death, a universe with a mind already in it — could do the work reason had failed to do. He never got his fact. He died in 1900 still unconvinced; the following year, after Myers died too, mediums on different continents who had never met one another began producing fragments of automatic writing that some of Sidgwick's former colleagues believed, once assembled, formed a single design — as if the inquiry that had failed to satisfy him living were somehow still being carried on posthumously."

So: first, is the note's central claim — that the psychical research continues the ethical problem rather than escaping it — a reading that historians of philosophy would recognize as fair, or am I imposing a shape on Sidgwick's life that the record doesn't really support? Second, flag anything that is simply wrong, or true but compressed to the point of being misleading. Third, be honest about the prose itself: does the paragraph earn its ideas, or is it handsome Victorian fog dressed up as thought?
