I'm writing a novel centered on a Faustian bargain: a mysterious patron will pay my protagonist, a painter, a fortune the instant she can sincerely declare her intention to give up art forever, though the renunciation itself is not due to occur for another year, on the night of her greatest exhibition. The patron can somehow discern a genuine intention from a feigned one, pays out the moment sincerity is confirmed, and asks nothing further of her, so once the money has cleared she will have no remaining reason to actually stop painting. I recognize this is, structurally, Kavka's toxin puzzle transposed into fiction, and before drafting the scene I want to work through its logic more carefully than the usual pop-philosophy summary allows. Could you compare the toxin puzzle to Newcomb's Problem? Both are often invoked in the same breath, since each seems to reward a state anterior to action rather than the action itself, but I would like to know precisely where they diverge—in particular, whether the toxin puzzle really requires anything like Newcomb's predictor, and how that difference determines which decision-theoretic solutions (causal versus evidential reasoning, resolute choice, and so on) are even applicable to each puzzle.
