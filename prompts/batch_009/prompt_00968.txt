Had a long, kind of heated argument with my friend Priya last night about those criminal-justice risk-assessment algorithms (like COMPAS, the recidivism-scoring tool judges use for bail and sentencing decisions) — she's in a public-defense clinic this semester so she's neck deep in this stuff right now. She brought up the ProPublica investigation that found the tool was flagging Black defendants as "high risk" way more often than white defendants who, it turned out, didn't actually reoffend, and then said there's an honest-to-god mathematical impossibility proof (she name-dropped Kleinberg and someone else, plus a seperate paper by a woman named Chouldechova I think?) showing that you literally cannot design a risk score that's both well-calibrated within each racial group and equal in its error rates across groups, so long as the two groups have different underlying reoffense rates. Ok, that part I could follow fine.

I pushed back on instinct but couldn't really pin down where her reasoning went wrong, if it even did. Is she right that this kind of impossibility result means algorithmic risk scores are basically a dead end compared to human judgment, or is she overreaching from what the math actually shows?
