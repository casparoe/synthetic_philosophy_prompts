I've been reporting a piece on this recent-ish wave of physicists — Carlo Rovelli is the one I keep circling back to, in that book of his, "The Order of Time" — who argue that time just isn't fundamental: no universal "now," no built-in flow, no arrow at the deepest level, just a fog of events that our brains stitch into something timeline-shaped because we're macroscopic creatures stuck with a blurry, averaged-out view of the world (Rovelli, who helped found loop quantum gravity, ties this explicitly to entropy, to memory, and to the limits of what a physical observer can actually track). Somewhere around my third coffee reading this stuff it hit me that Jorge Luis Borges basically beat these guys to the punch, minus the physics, in his 1946 essay "A New Refutation of Time" — except his argument runs through Berkeley and Hume instead of thermodynamics and quantum gravity, and I can't stop poking at how two totally different roads seem to arrive at basically the same "time isn't what it seems" destination.

Here's the bit I keep getting stuck on: Borges's essay leans hard on Hume's claim that there's no persisting self behind our perceptions, just a "bundle" of them with nothing underneath stitching "me now" to "me a minute ago" — no continuous subject, no one for time to happen to, therefore (he argues) no time. But the Rovelli-style move seems to need the opposite: it explicitly requires an observer — something that blurs, averages, and remembers — in order for time to emerge out of a fundamentally timeless universe. So it looks like todays "time is an illusion" crowd is quietly leaning on exactly the kind of persisting, remembering self that Borges's argument was built to dissolve.

So, assuming Borges somehow got read into all this contemporary physics (maybe somebody hands him Rovelli's book in the afterlife), what's your best guess at his reaction? Would he treat it as vindication — physics finally catching up with a joke he was already in on — or would he spot the sleight of hand and push back, on the grounds that it's smuggling back in the very thing his Humean argument denied? And does his own famous hedge at the end of the essay, where after all that work he admits, "the world, unfortunately, is real; I, unfortunately, am Borges," give us any clue which way he'd land?
