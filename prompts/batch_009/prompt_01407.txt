So I'm about a third of the way into a novel where the central bargain is a demonic one, but with a twist: the devil-figure doesn't grant wishes, he alters what people *want*. Instead of "you'll get riches," its "you'll come to crave this one specific, worthless thing, and once you do you'll finally be at peace." My editor keeps pushing back that the metaphysics of it feels arbitrary, so I went digging for somethig to hang it on and landed in a corner of metaethics I didn't even know existed: apparently there's a whole literature on "fitting attitude" or "buck-passing" theories of value, where to say something is good just means it's fitting to desire or admire it, and these theories run into something called the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. The stock example (I think it's originally Roger Crisp's, picked up by a bunch of people after) is almost exactly my premise: a demon threatens to torture you unless you desire a saucer of mud. That threat obviously gives you a reason to want to desire the mud, but the mud sure doesn't seem to actually become good. Which threatens to blow up the whole "good = fitting object of desire" idea, unless you can somehow carve out which reasons are the "right kind" without secretly just assuming what value is in the first place.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers hashing this out - one defending the fitting-attitude/buck-passing account, the other pressing on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem as hard as they can? I don't want either of them to be a pushover or a strawman; if the defender reaches for something like the "object-given vs. state-given reasons" move, I want the critic to actually engage with whether that distinction can be drawn without quietly smuggling value back in, and vice versa if the critic has a comeback the defender should get a real shot at answering it. Real back-and-forth, not two monologues stapled together. It doesn't need to land on a tidy resolution either - honestly a stalemate where they at least pin down exactly where the disagreement lives would be perfect, since that's closer to what I actually need for the scene anyway.
