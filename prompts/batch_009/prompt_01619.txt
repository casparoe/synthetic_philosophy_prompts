I'm a senior taking an elective on philosophy of logic and language, and for our final project we get to pick our own topic as long as we clear it with the teacher first. In the unit on Frege, we spent a while on how his whole logicist project rested on rejecting "psychologism" — the view that the laws of logic are just contingent facts about how human brains happen to work. Frege wanted logical and mathematical truth to be completely objective, independent of any mind, culture, or historical moment; in "The Thought" (1918) he even named this realm of mind-independent thoughts a "third realm," his actual German phrase being "drittes Reich." That detail stuck with me, so afterward I went looking into Frege as a person, and found out about the diary he kept for about a month in 1924, the year before he died: virulently antisemitic and anti-parliamentary, including entries reasoning toward the conclusion that Jews should be stripped of political rights, and apparently so damning that the editors of his official collected papers quietly left it out. Michael Dummett, who came across it in the archives in 1954 (it wasn't formally published until the 1990s), has written about how shaken he was to learn this about a man he'd revered as the very model of a rational human being — a shock sharpened, for me, by the fact that one of the students who most valued Frege's teaching was Gershom Scholem, who went on to become one of the twentieth century's great scholars of Judaism.

Here's the core paragraph of the essay I'm drafting, and I'd like an honestly critical read before I write the rest: "Frege's political and philosophical selves are usually treated as two unrelated facts that happen to belong to the same man: a brilliant, dispassionate logician who also, unaccountably, held vicious and irrational political prejudices. I want to resist that separation. Frege did not think politics should even aspire to the kind of objectivity he demanded of logic: he said outright that political judgment is a matter of 'feeling,' not reason. But the feeling he describes is not humble or provisional — it is absolute, in exactly the way his logic is absolute. What connects the two, I want to argue, is not their content but their temperament: an inability to tolerate ambiguity, compromise, or plurality, whether the plurality in question is rival foundations for arithmetic or rival peoples inside one republic. His logic and his nationalism read like the same demand for a single, uncontested authority, aimed at two very different targets."

Does that argument actually hold up, or is it a "just-so story" — a neat-sounding narrative standing in for evidence, maybe even a version of the genetic fallacy, explaining Frege's views away instead of engaging them? I'd genuinely like pushback, plus a flag on anything I've gotten factually wrong so far. As a smaller aside, since it's been bugging me: is it worth a footnote, or is it pure coincidence, that Frege's own term "drittes Reich" for the realm of objective truth predates Arthur Moeller van den Bruck's nationalist book "Das Dritte Reich" by five years — a book published, as it happens, the year before Frege sat down to write his diary?
