A few weeks ago I tried a sensory-deprivation float tank for the first time, mostly out of curiosity after several friends had recommended it for stress, and the experience turned out to be far more philosophically unsettling than I expected. Floating in total darkness and silence, in water heated to skin temperature until I gradually lost any clear sense of where my body ended and the water began, I reached a point where I could not locate a single bodily sensation, and yet I still had an unmistakable, vivid sense of being a self that was aware of that very fact. When I described this afterward to a friend who did her graduate work in medieval philosophy, she told me this was essentially what Avicenna was getting at with his "flying man" argument: he asks us to imagine a person created instantaneously as a fully grown adult but suspended in midair, cut off from all sensory input whatsoever and unable even to feel his own limbs touching one another, who would nonetheless still affirm his own existence. Avicenna took this to show that the self, or soul, is something distinct from the body, immaterial and separable from it, well before Descartes made a broadly similar move with the cogito.

Since then I have not been able to shake the feeling that my float tank session was more than just an interesting anecdote. It felt like I had actually lived through Avicenna's thought experiment and come out the other side with firsthand, experiential confirmation of his conclusion, which makes the case for mind-body dualism feel far more compelling to me now than it did when I first encountered the argument as an abstract exercise in an undergraduate survey course. Everything I have found on the subject since treats the flying man mainly as a historical curiosity or a tidy precursor to the cogito, without addressing whether an experience like mine should really count as evidence for what Avicenna was trying to establish. Before I get too attached to this as a genuine piece of philosophical insight rather than just a strange and memorable evening, I would like to know whether I am understanding Avicenna's argument correctly, and whether an experience like mine actually does the philosophical work I am currently crediting it with.
