I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I've spent a lot of my free time working through Paul Grice's writing on meaning and conversation, and one question keeps nagging at me. Grice's whole framework — the distinction between natural and non-natural meaning, the Cooperative Principle and its maxims, and his analysis of speaker meaning as a reflexive intention (the speaker wants the hearer to recognize what they're doing, and wants that recognition itself to be part of why the hearer responds) — is built around utterances: things a speaker actually says or does. Grice died in 1988, well before messaging apps introduced "read receipts," which show not just that a message arrived but that the other person opened and looked at it.

Here's the puzzle: if I send someone a message, see it marked "read," and get no reply for hours, I'll almost certainly conclude something from that — that they're avoiding me, that they're annoyed — even though they haven't said or done anything beyond opening an app. Before read receipts, an unanswered message was ambiguous, since I could never be sure they'd seen it; the read receipt manufactures exactly the kind of mutual knowledge Grice's model depends on, without any utterance behind it.

Given his own commitments, do you think Grice would have counted a "seen but unanswered" message as generating a genuine conversational implicature in his technical sense, or would he have said this falls outside his framework entirely, since no one performed a communicative act? I'd like you to reason it through using his actual distinctions, rather than just giving me a general impression of what he'd probably have said.
