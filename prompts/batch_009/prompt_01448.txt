Over the past year I have taken up competitive amateur golf rather more seriously than is probably wise for my handicap, and like many players stuck in a rut, I have been reading sports psychology in search of help with my putting. Several of the books and coaching articles I have come across cite a well-known line of research showing that golfers who are playing well judge the hole to look distinctly larger than golfers who are struggling, and that softball players in the middle of a hot streak report the ball itself as bigger than they do during a slump. These sources treat the finding as more or less decisive proof that confidence and performance reach directly into the visual system: the mind, on this telling, does not merely interpret a fixed optical input but actively reconstructs raw perceptual experience according to how well one is playing, and this is presented as flatly refuting the older "modular" picture of vision associated with Fodor and Pylyshyn, on which early visual processing is informationally encapsulated from belief, mood, and desire.

Taking this at face value, I have started building confidence-and-visualization exercises into my pre-shot routine, on the theory that convincing myself I am playing well should make me literally see a bigger hole, rather than merely feel calmer about a hole that looks the size it has always looked. It also seems to follow that Fodor and Pylyshyn's arguments for the cognitive impenetrability of vision have simply been overtaken by the sports-science evidence, and that similar reasoning should apply well beyond golf: if a hole can visually swell with confidence, then presumably a hill looks steeper when I am tired and a glass of water looks closer when I am thirsty for the same underlying reason.

Before I lean further on this, both on the course and off it, I would like to know whether I am reading the research correctly. Is the cognitive penetration debate in philosophy of mind really as settled as this coaching literature suggests, and do studies like these actually establish that perceptual experience itself, rather than memory, judgment, attention, or response bias, is what changes when performance or desire changes?
