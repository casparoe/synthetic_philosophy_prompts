So I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole on Frankfurt cases this week (started from a thread on one of the rationalist forums I lurk on, ended with me reading philpapers abstracts at 1am like an idiot), and there's one wrinkle that's stuck in my head and I can't shake. Everyone presents the basic Jones-and-Black setup the same way: Black wants Jones to do the thing, and has a device that will force Jones's hand if Jones shows any sign of choosing otherwise, but Jones just goes ahead and does it on his own steam, so the device never actually has to fire — which is supposedly enough to show you can be morally responsible without the "could have done otherwise" thing (PAP) being true. What's bugging me is the "flicker of freedom" reply to this: the idea that for Black's device to know when to intervene, Jones has to display some tiny involuntary tell first (the standard example is Jones blushing right before he'd choose differently), and that tell is itself a kind of alternate possibility, since Jones could've not shown it. Frankfurt's response is that this flicker is too thin and involuntary to actually ground responsibility either way, but I go back and forth on whether that's just a stipulation to save his own argument. Anyway, every "evil AI controls your brain" story I can think of botches this completely, because the AI always ends up actually intervening at some point, which just makes it a boring hard-determinism story instead of a real PAP counterexample.

Could you write me a short story that actually gets the mechanics right? I'm picturing something like a mandatory neural-monitor implant for parolees, built to override someone right before they'd re-offend — and I want the plot to hinge on the monitor never once kicking in, with a later scene (parole hearing, argument with a sibling, whatever fits) where someone pulls up the log and finds an honest-to-god "flicker" moment, and the characters have to actually hash out whether that changes the moral verdict at all. Doesn't need to resolve the debate or pick a side, I'd just love to see the flicker-of-freedom tension dramatized properly for once instead of the whole thing collapsing into "was he a puppet the whole time," which totally misses what's interesting about it.
