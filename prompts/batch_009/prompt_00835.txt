I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and since retiring three years ago I have been trying to fill in the gaps in my philosophical education, largely with a small reading group made up of former colleagues from my department. We have just finished working through Section X of Hume's first Enquiry, "Of Miracles," and it struck all of us as one of the tightest pieces of reasoning in the whole book. As we reconstructed it, Hume is essentially comparing two bodies of inductive evidence: on the one side, the "firm and unalterable experience" that has established a law of nature, which counts as the strongest kind of evidence experience can ever furnish; on the other, a single report resting on fallible human testimony. Because the first body of evidence is by its very nature uniform and exceptionless, while the second is merely one more fallible report layered on top of everything we already know about the frailty of witnesses, weighing the two against one another should always come out the same way, however impressive the witnesses. On this reading, Hume has not merely shown that miracle reports are usually false; he has shown that no conceivable testimony could ever be strong enough to outweigh our experience of nature's regularity, so that the probability of any reported miracle is driven to zero as a matter of principle, rather like a conservation law ruling out certain physical processes altogether.

What I cannot square with this is the sheer amount of philosophical literature still devoted to picking the argument apart, much of it by serious scholars who treat the question as genuinely unsettled, or even conclude that Hume's reasoning fails on its own terms. If a wise man really does proportion his belief to the evidence, and the evidence is as one-sided as Hume presents it, the matter would seem to be closed in advance for any conceivable case, in the same way we don't keep the question of a perpetual motion machine open just because someone produces a fresh set of blueprints. Could you help me understand what my reconstruction of Hume's argument is missing, and why philosophers apparently continue to treat "Of Miracles" as one of the more contested arguments in the canon rather than as a settled result?
