I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a long time thinking through the free-will debate on my own. Recently I worked through Galen Strawson's essay 'The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility' and its 'Basic Argument' against ultimate moral responsibility. I can follow the core regress: to be truly responsible for an action you'd have to be responsible for the mental character that produced it, which would require having chosen that character, which would in turn presuppose some earlier character you used to do the choosing, and so on without end. What I can't get straight in my own mind is Strawson's insistence that this conclusion holds up just as well under indeterminism as it does under determinism. My instinct is that if some of the mental events feeding into a decision are genuinely undetermined, rather than fixed by earlier states of me, then the regress should be broken at that point, and that this ought to count for something in favor of responsibility. Could you explain in detail why Strawson thinks an undetermined element in the causal story does nothing to help the case for responsibility, and is, if anything, worse for it than a fully determined one?
