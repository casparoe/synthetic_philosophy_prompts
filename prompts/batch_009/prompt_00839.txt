For a novel I'm drafting, one chapter is a letter written by Samuel Butler—the Erewhon novelist, not the Hudibras poet—after an imagined tour of a modern AI lab; the idea occurred to me after learning that Dune's "Butlerian Jihad" is usually read as a nod to his Book of the Machines chapters. I want the letter to reflect what Butler actually argued in Erewhon and in his 1863 essay "Darwin among the Machines," rather than a generic "don't play God" anxiety: that mechanical evolution could outrun biological evolution by orders of magnitude, and that the Erewhonians, after years of wrangling over exactly where to draw the line, resolved this by destroying every machine invented within the preceding 271 years. Given that argument, and given that the later Butler broke with orthodox Darwinism to argue that heredity is itself a kind of inherited memory rather than blind selection, would a system that improves through training on accumulated data trouble him more or less than one shaped by trial and error alone? Using his own logic, would he treat things like RLHF, interpretability research, or corrigibility guarantees as genuine answers to the danger he described, or would he side with the Erewhonians that nothing short of prohibition will do? Commit to a definite verdict and defend it.
