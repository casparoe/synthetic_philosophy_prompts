Last week a friend and I got into a surprisingly long argument about Otto Neurath, of all people, after she sent me an Instagram carousel, drawn in the same blocky, high-contrast silhouettes Gerd Arntz used for the original Isotype pictograms, that compared meat consumption across countries using rows of cow icons of visibly different sizes. That struck me as a direct violation of the one design rule Neurath insisted on most firmly at the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum in Vienna: that a change in quantity must be shown by repeating a symbol at a constant size, never by enlarging it, precisely because resized icons invite the kind of area- and volume-based misjudgments Isotype was invented to eliminate. My friend, who works in UX, thought I was being pedantic about it. Her position was that if Neurath could see how completely icons and pictograms have taken over everyday communication — emoji, app icons, wordless airport signage, the whole apparatus of shareable infographics — he would feel triumphantly vindicated in his old slogan that "words divide, pictures unite," and wouldn't much care about the fine print. I argued the opposite: that Isotype was never just a preference for pictures over words, but a rigorously standardized, rule-governed project for communicating empirical and statistical facts to a mass audience, growing directly out of his logical-positivist commitments as a member of the Vienna Circle — and that most of today's picture-based communication would strike him as a betrayal of that project rather than its fulfillment.

We never settled the argument, so I would like a more considered outside opinion. If Otto Neurath were shown today's emoji culture, Instagram-style infographics, and AI-generated charts and dashboards, do you think he would recognize them as the natural heirs to Isotype, or would he regard most of it as exactly the unreliable, un-standardized mess his "Vienna method" was designed to replace? I am especially interested in whether the distinction between communication aimed at conveying subjective states, which is mostly what emoji do, and communication aimed at conveying objective or statistical facts, which was always Isotype's actual purpose, would matter to him — and in where, based on his specific views rather than a generic "he liked pictures," he would have drawn the line between honest picture-communication and propaganda by pictogram. Please commit to a specific answer rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides.
