I spent about thirty-five years as a condensed-matter physicist before I retired (mostly disordered magnets, plus some early high-Tc stuff), and only in the last year or two have I actually started reading philosophy of science properly instead of just muttering about it over beer with old colleagues. This week I went back and reread Phil Anderson's "More Is Different" from 1972, which I half-remembered from grad school, and then for contrast picked up Weinberg's "Dreams of a Final Theory," specifically the "Two Cheers for Reductionism" chapter. It's funny for me personally because I actually lived through the Superconducting Super Collider fight in the early 90s, and I remember Anderson and other solid-state people (Krumhansl too, I think) publicly campaigning against it, arguing particle physics wasn't obviously more "fundamental" than the rest of physics — and at the time, being a solid-state guy myself, I was firmly on their team, no question.

Reading the actual texts now instead of just the folklore, I notice Anderson is careful to say he's NOT denying reductionism in the plain sense — he explicitly seperates the "reductionist hypothesis" (everything's made of the same stuff obeying the same laws) from what he calls the "constructionist hypothesis" (that you could run those laws forward and rebuild chemistry, biology, whatever, from scratch), and it's only the second one he's attacking, using broken symmetry as his main example. Then Weinberg, in his chapter, seems to concede a lot of that same territory — new concepts needed at each level, no realistic hope of ever deriving biology from the Standard Model — but he still insists on calling himself a reductionist, just a "grand" one rather than a "petty" one. So on paper it almost looks like the two of them agree about the actual science and are just fighting over who gets to keep the word "reductionist," maybe with some left-over bad blood from the funding wars mixed in.

Is that too cynical a read on my part? Could you properly compare Anderson's emergentist argument with Weinberg's grand-reductionism response and tell me where, if anywhere, they have a genuine substantive disagreement rather than a merely verbal one — and if there is a real disagreement, what it's actually about (ontology, epistemology, the nature of explanation, whatever the right category is)? I've also started running into the term "supervenience" in my philosophy reading and can't yet tell whether that's the concept that actually settles, or maybe dissolves, this whole dispute, so feel free to bring it in if it's relevant.
