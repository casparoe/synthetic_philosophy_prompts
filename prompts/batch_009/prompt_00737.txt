Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending G.E.M. Anscombe's verdict that Harry Truman was a murderer for ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and a consequentialist who disputes it. Anscombe pressed this charge in 1956, opposing Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary degree in a protest she later published as the pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree": Truman, she argued, had intentionally killed innocents as a means to securing Japan's surrender, and no good consequences could make that anything other than murder. The defender should draw on the doctrine of double effect and Anscombe's broader case against consequentialism. The consequentialist should first press some version of the "closeness problem" against the intention/foresight distinction itself, arguing that the bombing can be redescribed so that the civilian deaths come out as a foreseen side effect of destroying legitimate targets rather than as an intended means, and should then argue that even a well-drawn version of the distinction ought to yield to the sheer number of lives the bombings plausibly saved by ending the war quickly. Have the Anscombean answer both moves directly rather than simply reasserting the verdict, and have both sides engage with each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another.
