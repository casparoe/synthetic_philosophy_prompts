so for my philosophy of religion class this week we actually had to read the real Pascal text instead of just the SEP summary of "Pascal's Wager" that everyone already knows from like every intro ethics class ever. I'm fine with the famous bit (if you win you win everything, if you lose you lose nothing, so bet that God exists) but there's a paragraph right after the "three lives" / "infinity of chances" part where Pascal seems to be fending off some objection about an "infinite distance" between what you're staking and what you might win, and I've read it over like five times and I honestly can't tell what point he's making anymore. Here's the bit (Trotter translation, from Pensée 233, my prof gave us a scan of it):

"For it is no use to say it is uncertain if we will gain, and it is certain that we risk, and that the infinite distance between the certainty of what is staked and the uncertainty of what will be gained, equals the finite good which is certainly staked against the uncertain infinite. It is not so, as every player stakes a certainty to gain an uncertainty, and yet he stakes a finite certainty to gain a finite uncertainty, without transgressing against reason. There is not an infinite distance between the certainty staked and the uncertainty of the gain; that is untrue. In truth, there is an infinity between the certainty of gain and the certainty of loss. But the uncertainty of the gain is proportioned to the certainty of the stake according to the proportion of the chances of gain and loss. Hence it comes that, if there are as many risks on one side as on the other, the course is to play even; and then the certainty of the stake is equal to the uncertainty of the gain, so far is it from fact that there is an infinite distance between them."

Can you help me figure out exactly what objection Pascal thinks he's responding to there, and what his actual counter-move is? Like is "proportion of the chances" basically just a clunky way of saying expected value, or is he arguing against some deeper worry that "certainty" and "uncertainty" are incommensurable categories you can't weigh against each other at all, period? My TA mentioned in office hours that theres a real ambiguity running through this whole section about whether Pascal's argument needs the odds of God existing to be an actual 50/50 (like the coin-flip "heads or tails" language earlier suggests) or whether it still goes through even if the odds are tiny but nonzero, and I've got a feeling that ambiguity is hiding somewhere in this specific "proportion" paragraph, but I can't spot exactly where or how.
