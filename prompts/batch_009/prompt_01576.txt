I made a mid-career switch a few months ago, moving from general backend engineering into a mechanistic interpretability team, and a disagreement with a colleague after last week's reading group has been nagging at me since. We'd just gone through the "linear representation hypothesis" and the older Othello-GPT result, where a transformer trained only to predict legal moves in the board game Othello turns out to encode the board state in its activations. The board state was originally recoverable only by a non-linear probe, though it was later shown that a linear probe recovers it too once each square is framed as "mine" versus "theirs" rather than "black" versus "white" — and that the discovered direction supports causal intervention: directly editing a model's activations along that direction changes its subsequent moves in just the way you'd predict if the direction really encoded the board. My colleague takes this, and findings like it, as showing that the network has built a genuine internal representation of the board. I said I wasn't sure that "there's a direction correlated with some feature of interest, and manipulating it has the predicted downstream effect" gets you all the way to "representation" in any philosophically interesting sense, but I couldn't back up the objection with anything more precise than a hunch.

That sent me back to William Ramsey's Representation Reconsidered, half-remembered from an undergraduate philosophy of mind course. Ramsey argues that a theory of representation only earns its keep if it explains why some internal state deserves to be called a representation rather than merely a reliable causal intermediary — his "job description challenge." On his view, mere "receptor" representations — states that simply covary with, or get reliably triggered by, some feature, the way a thermostat's coil responds to temperature — don't meet this challenge, since being a good detector doesn't by itself require the system to be doing anything representational with the information. What can meet the challenge, he argues, are "structural representations": states whose internal relations mirror the relations among the things represented (his standard illustration is something like a cognitive map), where the system actually exploits that structural correspondence in guiding further processing. Crucially, he's careful to note that structural correspondence alone is cheap — some isomorphism can be found between almost any two sufficiently complex systems if you look hard enough — so the real weight in his account falls on the exploitation condition, not on the mere existence of a matching structure.

I'd like help thinking this through properly. Could you compare Ramsey's structural-representation criterion with the realism about representation that seems to be doing the work in claims like the linear representation hypothesis and the Othello-GPT findings? I'm especially interested in whether interpretability evidence of the sort my colleague was citing — a probe direction whose manipulation causally shifts behaviour as predicted — actually satisfies something like Ramsey's exploitation condition, or whether it's really just strong evidence of correlation (and of the direction being read by downstream computation) dressed up as something stronger. Where does the analogy between the two literatures genuinely illuminate what's going on inside these models, and where might it mislead?
