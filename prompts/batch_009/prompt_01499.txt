For the past year or so I have been teaching myself quantum mechanics through popular books, recorded lectures, and physics blogs, largely because a friend of mine kept insisting that entanglement proves that "everything in the universe is secretly connected" — a claim that struck me as too vague to be either confirmed or refuted. That side project eventually led me to Bell's theorem, and to a worry about it that I have not been able to shake. Nearly every popular account ends the same way: Bell proved that any theory reproducing the predictions of quantum mechanics has to abandon locality, so Einstein's hope for a purely local universe was misplaced. But once I read past the popular treatments, I found that a number of serious physicists refuse to accept that ending. Gerard 't Hooft and, more recently, Sabine Hossenfelder defend what they call superdeterminism, on which locality survives because the experimenter's choice of what to measure was never really independent of the hidden variables being measured. Huw Price and Ken Wharton instead appeal to retrocausality, letting influences travel backward in time within the light cone rather than sideways outside it. And I have even come across physicists who argue that the many-worlds interpretation was local all along, since Bell's proof quietly assumes that every measurement has a single, definite outcome, which Everettians deny — though I also found others arguing that many-worlds turns out not to be local either, once you move past simple two-particle pairs to three-particle, GHZ-type correlations.

I do not have the background to referee this dispute myself, and what I am hoping for is not another summary that just ends with "therefore physics is nonlocal." Could you write me an essay that first lays out, precisely, what Bell's theorem does and does not assume — locality itself, as distinct from the looser talk of "realism" one usually finds in popular treatments, and the easily overlooked assumption that measurement settings are statistically independent of the hidden variables — and what it strictly rules out? Then please weigh, as honestly as you can, whether superdeterminism, retrocausality, and the many-worlds interpretation genuinely manage to buy back locality, or whether each of them simply relocates the strangeness elsewhere (into a cosmic-scale conspiracy among initial conditions, into influences running backward in time, or into an endlessly branching multiverse) at a cost that is arguably just as high. Please end by actually taking a side on whether nature is fundamentally nonlocal, rather than retreating into the claim that the interpretations are all empirically equivalent and it is simply a matter of taste; a conditional verdict is fine (for instance, "nonlocal, unless one is prepared to accept superdeterminism"), but a genuine verdict of some kind is what I am after.
