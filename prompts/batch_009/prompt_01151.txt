Over the past year, my job has shifted from general backend engineering to building evaluation pipelines for the large language models my company ships in customer-facing products, which has unexpectedly pulled me back into a philosophy of mind argument I hadn't touched since a single undergraduate seminar. The occasion was a design-review meeting where I asked how confidently we could claim, in our marketing copy, that our assistant "understands" what customers are asking. One of our senior researchers settled the question for the room by invoking Searle's Chinese Room argument, or rather by dismissing its relevance to us.

His point was that Searle's thought experiment specifically targets a serial, symbolic kind of system, in which a single clerk consults an explicit rulebook of syntactic instructions for manipulating Chinese characters, whereas a transformer contains no lookup table or hand-coded symbolic rules anywhere in it: its "knowledge" lives entirely in billions of continuously adjusted weights produced by gradient descent, distributed across the network the way processing is distributed across neurons in a brain. Since there is no rulebook-following homunculus anywhere in a neural network, he argued, the syntax-without-semantics worry that powers the Chinese Room simply gets no grip on connectionist architectures, and citing Searle against claims of LLM understanding is a category error inherited from an earlier era of symbolic AI. Everyone in the room, myself included, found this quite persuasive, and it has since become something of a house view that Searle's argument is simply moot for systems built like ours.

I've now been asked to turn this reasoning into a short internal note on what we can and cannot responsibly claim about our model's "understanding," and before I do, I want to represent the strongest remaining skeptical position fairly rather than dismiss it out of hand. Given that the classic objection to machine understanding doesn't even apply to how transformers work, what serious philosophical grounds, as opposed to mere lingering intuition, are actually left for someone to deny that our models understand the language they process?
