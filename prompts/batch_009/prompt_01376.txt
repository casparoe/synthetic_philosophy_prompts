I'm a software engineer with no formal background in mathematical logic or philosophy, but I've been reading primary sources on famous old problems for fun, and I've gotten stuck on part of David Hilbert's 1900 address "Mathematical Problems" (quoting from Mary Winston Newson's 1902 English translation, Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society, vol. 8). Problem 1, "Cantor's problem of the cardinal number of the continuum," is essentially the continuum hypothesis. Right after stating it, Hilbert writes:

"Let me mention another very remarkable statement of Cantor's which stands in the closest connection with the theorem mentioned and which, perhaps, offers the key to its proof. […] Now Cantor considers a particular kind of ordered assemblage which he designates as a well ordered assemblage and which is characterized in this way, that not only in the assemblage itself but also in every partial assemblage there exists a first number. The system of integers 1, 2, 3, ... in their natural order is evidently a well ordered assemblage. On the other hand the system of all real numbers, i.e., the continuum in its natural order, is evidently not well ordered. For, if we think of the points of a segment of a straight line, with its initial point excluded, as our partial assemblage, it will have no first element. The question now arises whether the totality of all numbers may not be arranged in another manner so that every partial assemblage may have a first element, i.e., whether the continuum cannot be considered as a well ordered assemblage — a question which Cantor thinks must be answered in the affirmative. It appears to me most desirable to obtain a direct proof of this remarkable statement of Cantor's, perhaps by actually giving an arrangement of numbers such that in every partial system a first number can be pointed out."

I can follow each definition here in isolation, but I can't reconstruct the inference connecting them. Cardinality (how many elements a set has, up to a bijection) and well-ordering (whether some arrangement gives every subset a least element) seem to me like logically independent properties of a set. Why did Hilbert think that exhibiting a well-ordering of the continuum would supply "the key" to determining whether its cardinality is the very next one after that of the integers? Separately, I've learned that Zermelo later proved that every set, including the continuum, can in fact be well-ordered — yet the continuum hypothesis remained open regardless. Where exactly does the link Hilbert draws here break down?
