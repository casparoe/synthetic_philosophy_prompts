For the past year I have kept paying for a gym membership I barely use, even though I tell anyone who will listen that getting back into shape is one of my top priorities. When I complained about this recently to my sister, who studied economics, she offered an argument that has stayed with me ever since. She said that if you really want to know what someone values, you should ignore anything they say about their own priorities and look only at what they consistently do when a real choice is in front of them, since unlike words, choices always cost you something. On her view, the fact that I keep choosing the couch over the treadmill, week after week, shows that I do not actually value fitness the way I claim to, and that the membership fee is really just money spent on a flattering story about myself. She pointed out that the same pattern shows up with my finances: I say I want to save, yet my bank statement tells a different story every month.

I have no formal background in economics or philosophy, but I have been turning this over for weeks and cannot quite settle it. Part of what unsettles me is that my sister is not merely accusing me of being weak-willed; she seems to be making a claim about what "wanting" something actually means, namely, that there is nothing more to a preference than the pattern of choices behind it. That seems to prove too much, though, since it would mean a person could never sincerely want something they routinely fail to do, which does not match how it feels from the inside. So which is it: is consistent behavior really the final, honest measure of what a person wants, with everything else amounting to self-serving talk, or is there some coherent sense in which I genuinely do prefer fitness and saving money, even though, when the moment comes, I almost always choose otherwise?
