My friend and I have been going back and forth about Kavka's toxin puzzle for way too long on a road trip -- you know, the eccentric billionaire who'll wire you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a nasty but non-lethal toxin the next afternoon, and the twist is you don't even have to actually drink the stuff to keep the money. After a couple hours of arguing, my friend basically convinced me that this is just Newcomb's problem wearing a trenchcoat: the billionaire is doing the same "predict-your-future-behavior" move that Newcomb's demon does, so whatever you already think about one-boxing vs two-boxing should carry over directly. On this view a committed one-boxer should have zero trouble with the toxin case -- just resolve to be the type of agent who actually follows through, get predicted correctly, take the million, and then go ahead and drink the toxin the next day too, same as taking only the one box.

Assuming thats right, I've pretty much decided I must be a one-boxer on this one too, since a day of stomach pain for a guaranteed million seems like an obvious trade. But then I was flipping back through some old notes from a philosophy of action class and noticed the toxin puzzle keeps getting cited as its own distinct, still-debated problem -- Gauthier and McClennen apparently write about it on totally separate terms from Newcomb's problem -- rather than something that's automatically settled once you've picked a side on one-boxing. So is my "just be a resolute one-boxer about it" solution actually missing something important, or is this really just a case of decision theorists being slow to notice two puzzles are secretly the same?
