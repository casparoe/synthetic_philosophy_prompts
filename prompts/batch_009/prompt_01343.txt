A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about laws of nature and got nowhere. It started when I posed this case: imagine a universe with two fundamental kinds of particle, X and Y, each of which interacts constantly and predictably with particles of its own kind and with several other kinds — enough to fully reveal their dispositional profiles — yet, simply because of how sparsely they happen to be distributed through that universe's history, no X particle ever once comes near a Y particle. My friend, a committed Humean, insisted that without a single actual X-meets-Y event, there is no further fact about what would happen between them, nor about whether some regularity counts as a genuine law governing that encounter. I wanted to say that the dispositional essences of the X and Y properties, already evident from those other interactions, settle the matter regardless of whether the meeting ever happens — but I couldn't quite pin down why that settles it rather than being just our best guess. Write a dialogue that continues this argument between a Humean about laws (in the spirit of David Lewis's best-system account) and an anti-Humean who grounds laws in the dispositional essences of properties (in the spirit of Alexander Bird's view). Make them equally sharp: they should engage each other's actual arguments point by point rather than talk past one another — including how each side accounts for the seeming truth of claims like 'if an X had met a Y, they would have interacted a certain way,' and the charge that Humean properties are mere freely swappable 'quiddities' — and neither should be allowed to win too easily.
