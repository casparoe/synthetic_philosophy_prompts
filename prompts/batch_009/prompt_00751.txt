Over the past year I've become fairly involved with my city's Effective Altruism chapter: I've taken the Giving What We Can pledge, and I spend a couple of evenings a month with a group that debates things like cause prioritization and how to weigh a modest, well-evidenced local intervention against a more speculative one that might do vastly more good on paper. Purely by coincidence, a Victorian literature reading group I also belong to just finished Middlemarch, and the timing has left me a little unsettled. I already knew that George Eliot lost her Christian faith as a young woman and spent the following decade translating German critics of religion like Strauss and Feuerbach before she ever wrote a novel, so I went in expecting a writer who would be sympathetic to a rigorously secular, this-worldly ethics. What I hadn't bargained for was how insistently the book argues that the effects of our actions can never really be calculated, and how it closes by praising a life spent on small, unheralded, local acts of care over anything more legible or scalable - which felt uncomfortably like a rebuke aimed at my Tuesday evenings.

It's bothered me enough that I'd rather think it through properly than just feel vaguely scolded by a 150-year-old novel. Eliot never encountered anything resembling the effective altruism movement, of course, but she did spend a lot of time in the orbit of an earlier, highly systematic attempt to place ethics on a secular, post-Christian footing - the Comtean "Religion of Humanity," which several of her closest friends were devoted to - and from what I understand she kept a real distance from it even while sharing much of its underlying humanism. Given what she actually believed about sympathy, calculation, and duty, do you think she would have recognized effective altruism as a continuation of her own post-religious ethics, or would she have raised serious objections to it? And if she would have objected, do you think that would come down mainly to Victorian temperament and squeamishness about strangers, or would it point to something she thought was a genuine mistake in how the movement reasons about doing good?
