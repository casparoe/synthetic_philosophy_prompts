I teach a philosophy of economics seminar, and the last two sessions were on interpersonal utility comparisons — starting with Robbins basically declaring them meaningless value judgments that have no place in scientific economics, then Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, which supposedly derives a weighted-utilitarian social welfare function without needing interpersonal comparisons at all, and then Sen (and Broome, and Weymark) pushing back that the weights in Harsanyi's result are arbitrary and can't actually be pinned down unless you sneak comparability back in through the side door. We also got into Arrow's later "extended sympathy" move from the second edition of Social Choice and Individual Values, where instead of comparing utility levels directly you rank things like "being me in my situation" against "being you in yours." It's great fun to teach in the abstract, but I honestly never thought I'd need any of it for actual departmental buisness, until this week.

Here's the situation: our department has exactly one semester of paid research leave to hand out this year, and rather than let the chair just pick, the personnel commitee sent round a form asking each of the five eligible faculty to self-rate, on a scale of 0 to 100, "how much would this leave benefit your research." Whoever comes out on top gets the leave. About half the department thinks this is a perfectly sensible Benthamite solution, and the other half (me included) has a nagging sense that we're maxing over numbers that were never comparable in the first place, though I'm struggling to turn that hunch into an actual argument for the next meeting. Could you spell out the most important implications of the interpersonal utility comparisons debate for whether a procedure like ours can be defended at all? I'd like you to actually engage with the specific technical positions here (Robbins's skepticism, the Harsanyi/Sen/Weymark dispute, extended sympathy, maybe even how applied welfare economists try to sidestep the whole mess with money-metric or willingness-to-pay measures) rather than just telling me that comparing utility across people is hard, because that much I already knew going in.
