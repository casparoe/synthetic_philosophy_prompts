Over a long car ride, a friend and I got into a running argument about how much weight to put on a line of Pascal's that I had introduced a bit glibly: "Justice without force is impotent; force without justice is tyrannical," from the Pensées, along with his related remark that, being unable to make what is just strong, people have instead made what is strong pass for just. I was using the fragment to argue that the entire modern edifice of international human-rights law—the Universal Declaration, the UN treaty bodies, the International Criminal Court—rests on a kind of category error, since it dresses up justice claims in the language of universal reason while possessing essentially none of the force needed to bind the states that most need binding. My friend, who knows the Pensées better than I do, thought I was flattening Pascal into a crude Hobbesian ("might makes right") when his actual argument was closer to the opposite: precisely because he held that unaided human reason could never settle what justice really is (hence his remarks elsewhere about how a few degrees of latitude can reverse an entire body of law, or how what counts as a crime on one side of the Pyrenees is treated as innocent on the other), he wanted people to submit to established custom and authority not because force thereby becomes just, but as a humble, second-best substitute for a justice that only faith and grace could ultimately disclose.

We never really settled it, and I would like a more careful verdict than either of us managed after a few hours in the car. Working from what you take to be Pascal's actual argument in the Pensées, rather than the popularized, free-floating version of the "justice and force" fragment, how do you think he would have judged the modern project of international human-rights law and institutions like the ICC? I am especially curious whether his skepticism about reason's capacity to ground justice, combined with his views on custom and the necessity of force, would make him more contemptuous of an enterprise that is unusually explicit about deriving justice from reason while being unusually short on enforceable force behind it—or whether the distinction my friend drew, between a merely temporal, custom-bound justice and a higher justice glimpsed only through faith, actually leaves him room to regard such institutions more charitably, as at least gesturing, however impotently, at something real.
