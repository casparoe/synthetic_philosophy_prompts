hey, I'm drafting the historical background section of my diss chapter on the combination problem and got stuck on something in James. This is from *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), ch. 6 "The Mind-Stuff Theory," p. 160 — basically the locus classicus for the whole problem:

"Where the elemental units are supposed to be feelings, the case is in no wise altered. Take a hundred of them, shuffle them and pack them as close together as you can (whatever that may mean); still each remains the same feeling it always was, shut in its own skin, windowless, ignorant of what the other feelings are and mean. There would be a hundred-and-first feeling there, if, when a group or series of such feelings were set up, a consciousness belonging to the group as such should emerge. And this 101st feeling would be a totally new fact; the 100 original feelings might, by a curious physical law, be a signal for its creation, when they came together; but they would have no substantial identity with it, nor it with them, and one could never deduce the one from the others, or (in any intelligible sense) say that they evolved it.

Take a sentence of a dozen words, and take twelve men and tell to each one word. Then stand the men in a row or jam them in a bunch, and let each think of his word as intently as he will; nowhere will there be a consciousness of the whole sentence. We talk of the 'spirit of the age,' and the 'sentiment of the people,' and in various ways we hypostatize 'public opinion.' But we know this to be symbolic speech, and never dream that the spirit, opinion, sentiment, etc., constitute a consciousness other than, and additional to, that of the several individuals whom the words 'age,' 'people,' or 'public' denote. The private minds do not agglomerate into a higher compound mind."

So this is the bit everyone cites as the OG statement of the combination problem. But I keep getting stuck on wether the two examples are actually doing the same argumentative work, or if James is quietly running two different arguments together. The "hundred feelings" case is about whether a bunch of feelings belonging to some single mind-stuff substrate could add up to a further, emergent feeling. The "twelve men" case is about twelve already-separate, already-conscious people failing to fuse into a group mind — which seems almost trivially true in a way the feelings case isn't (of course 12 guys in a row don't become a hive mind, but that feels like a different question from whether basic "atoms" of experience could combine into a bigger one). Is James just letting the obviousness of the second case smuggle in intuitive support for the first, or is there a good reading where they're genuinely the same kind of combination and I'm not seeing it? Would love a close read of what's actually doing the logical work here.
