As a PhD student putting together a lit-review chapter on inner alignment, I've been working through Nate Soares's "sharp left turn" argument and Quintin Pope's reply to it. Soares argues that AI training will recapitulate what happened in human evolution: optimizing hominids for inclusive genetic fitness produced humans whose capabilities generalized far beyond the ancestral environment while their actual motivations came apart from that objective almost entirely, and he expects gradient descent to eventually produce an analogous collapse between an AI's trained objective and its actual goals. Pope's reply is more specific than "evolution is just a bad analogy": he locates the mechanism in the fact that within-lifetime learning was normally discarded at the end of each generation, so capability gains could compound across generations only once culture let each generation pass some of its learning on to the next — a discard-and-reset dynamic that, he argues, ordinary gradient-descent training doesn't exhibit. Please grant Pope's diagnosis of the human case, but argue that it actually identifies a specific future trigger for an AI sharp left turn rather than ruling one out: namely, that once AI systems' within-deployment learning starts being distilled efficiently back into later training runs (say, via recursive self-improvement or large-scale training on model-generated trajectories), Pope's own mechanism should predict exactly the capability–alignment decoupling that he thinks current training avoids. Be precise about exactly what would have to change for that flip to occur.
