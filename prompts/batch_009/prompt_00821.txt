I'm taking a graduate seminar on Frege this term, and for the term paper I want to write on a question a few of us keep arguing about outside class: does Frege's 1924 political diary have any real bearing on how we should assess him as a logician and philosopher of language? The basic facts aren't in dispute. The diary survives only as a fragment that was omitted from Hans Hermes's edition of Frege's Nachlass, and it records enthusiastic anti-parliamentary nationalism, explicit sympathy for Hitler and Ludendorff, and calls for depriving Jews of political rights and expelling them from Germany. It left Michael Dummett -- who first came across it in 1954 but did not discuss it in print until the introduction to Frege: Philosophy of Language almost twenty years later -- confessing that he could no longer regard Frege as, in his own words, an "absolutely rational man." What complicates the easy "keep the logic, discard the man" response is more recent work on Frege's other political writings, including a 1918 proposal for electoral reform, which suggests that he tried to derive conclusions favoring race-based legislation from his own technical apparatus: from his treatment of the ancestral of a relation, and from worries about how vague predicates would affect the enforceability of antisemitic statutes. Write an essay that weighs the view that a philosopher's technical achievements stand independently of what he personally believed or tried to derive from them against the view that Frege's case reveals a deeper continuity worth taking seriously, and defend a specific conclusion rather than leaving the question open.
