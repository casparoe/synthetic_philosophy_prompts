I've been going down a bit of a Frank Ramsey rabbit hole for a history-of-economic-thought seminar (yeah, the guy the Ramsey model and Ramsey pricing are named after - died at 26, back in 1930). Everyone talks about the technical stuff, but I stumbled on this much more personal essay of his called "Epilogue," apparently a talk he gave to that secretive Cambridge society, the Apostles, in 1925, only published after he died. In it he's oddly insistent that cosmic scale just doesn't move him at all - his line is that "the stars may be large, but they cannot think or love," and that his picture of the world is drawn in perspective rather than to scale, with humans filling the whole foreground and the stars shrunk down to nothing. He even extends this to time, not just space: he says the eventual heat death of the universe is so far off that, discounted back to the present, it's worth is basically nothing, and that this doesn't make the present any less worth living in.

Here's what's been bugging me though: in his actual economics, in the famous savings paper, he makes what looks like the opposite move. There he argues that discounting the welfare of future generations just because they come later in time is "ethically indefensible," basically just a failure of imagination on our part. So he seems to want it both ways - don't discount the far future at all when the question is how much weight future people's welfare should get in social policy, but it's totally fine, personally and philosophically, to discount that same far future down to almost nothing when you're deciding how much it should matter to you right now.

So, given both sides of him, what do you reckon Ramsey would say if someone sat him down and explained modern longtermism to him - the effective-altruism-adjacent view (Will MacAskill, Toby Ord, Nick Bostrom, that whole cluster) that humanity could have an astronomically long future containing vastly more people than have ever lived, so reducing existential risks, like from misaligned AI, should dominate how we spend our moral effort today. Would he have signed up as a longtermist, or would he have seen it as exactly the kind of not-to-scale thinking he thought he'd already caught himself out of? Genuinely curious for a worked-through answer, not just a one-line vibe.
