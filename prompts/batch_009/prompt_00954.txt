I've spent the last couple of months reporting a long-form profile of a prominent AI lab founder, the kind of piece that depends on unusually deep access: weeks embedded with his team, interviews with estranged family members, a seat on his jet during a fundraising swing through Asia. Midway through the reporting, his head of communications floated what she framed as a modest condition for keeping that access open: somewhere in the finished piece, I would need to include a line reflecting that I had "come to genuinely admire" him. My editor and I are treating this, correctly I think, as a straightforward conflict-of-interest problem, and we've firewalled the actual reporting from it. But discussing it with a colleague over drinks last week, I realized the arrangement bothers me for a second, odder reason that has nothing to do with journalistic ethics.

Once the condition was on the table, I noticed that I had actually acquired some motive to feel more admiring toward him — not merely to write the sentence, but to hold the attitude sincerely, since that would make the access less uncomfortable and the eventual copy less strained. Yet that motive plainly has nothing to do with the sort of thing that would normally make someone admirable: his judgment, his conduct, what he has actually built. It is as though two entirely different kinds of consideration are in play here — considerations that bear on whether admiration would be a true or fitting response to who he actually is, and considerations that bear on whether it would be convenient or advantageous for me, personally, to feel that way regardless of who he is. My colleague thinks I am drawing a distinction without a difference: in her view, a reason to feel something is a reason to feel it, full stop, wherever it comes from. I cannot shake the sense that only the first kind of consideration should count toward whether the admiration is actually warranted. Is there a genuine distinction here, or is she right that I am overcomplicating a simple case of self-interest dressed up as feeling? I would like to know whether philosophers have found a rigorous way of separating these two families of reasons, and, if so, what they say about situations like mine, where a reason of the second kind is doing real psychological work.
