I've been teaching myself about the history of infographics as a personal hobby, and that's what eventually led me to Otto Neurath and his Isotype system from the 1920s and 30s — the picture-statistics method where, say, a bigger workforce is shown by adding more identical little worker icons in a row rather than by drawing one bigger worker. At first I was only interested in the design side of it, but then I tracked down a scanned copy of his 1936 book International Picture Language online, and I realized that the "repeat the icon, don't resize it" rule wasn't just a stylistic preference for him — it was tied to a much bigger philosophical program connected to the Vienna Circle.

What struck me is that Neurath seems to have genuinely believed that restricting a language to pictures of concrete, tangible things (a worker, a ship, a house) protected people from the kind of "metaphysical" nonsense he thought ordinary abstract language invites. He even singles out Heidegger's talk of "the nothing that nothings" as exactly the sort of statement a picture-script couldn't produce, since, on his view, pictures can show you a sword or a table but never something like "being" itself. Oddly, though, in that same discussion he also describes the historical move toward abstract, flexible language as a genuine triumph that made modern science possible — so he doesn't seem to think abstraction is simply bad, just dangerous once it's cut loose from anything concrete.

What I haven't been able to work out on my own is how any of this squares with what Isotype was actually used for. The Vienna museum's charts weren't pictures of swords and tables — they showed things like unemployment, population growth, trade, and whole national "economic systems," which strike me as exactly the sort of abstract, relational facts that a language restricted to concrete objects shouldn't be able to touch. So I'd like help thinking through whether Neurath's own framework has a real answer for how a row of little repeated figures manages to represent something like "unemployment" without quietly depending on the very abstraction he says pictures can't handle — or whether the "pictures are safe because they only show the concrete" idea is doing much less work than it sounds like, with the actual conceptual labor of defining a category like "unemployed worker" happening beforehand, in ordinary abstract language, and Isotype just repackaging the result for a general audience. I'm also curious whether this counts as a genuine tension in his position or whether there's some distinction he (or later commentators) drew that resolves it, and how the whole thing connects to what I gather is called his "physicalism," and his broader case against metaphysics.
