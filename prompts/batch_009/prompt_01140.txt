I spent thirty-five years running a molecular genetics lab before retiring, and in all that time I don't believe I ever paused to ask whether "the genetic code" deserved its name. It was simply how everyone talked: DNA "encodes" proteins, the ribosome "reads" and "translates" messenger RNA, and mutations are "copying errors." It was only in retirement, reading my way into philosophy of biology, that I discovered how contested this vocabulary actually is. I was struck to learn that Schrödinger, writing years before anyone knew DNA's structure, had already guessed that the chromosomes must carry some sort of hereditary "code-script" — though he immediately doubted his own term, since the thing he had in mind seemed to be "law-code and executive power" simultaneously, both the plan and the machinery that executes it. When the codon table was finally worked out in the 1960s, many biologists treated this as a vindication of his hunch, but I gather that historians of science are not all convinced that what Schrödinger had in mind was the same sort of code that molecular biology eventually found.

My interest in the question was revived recently when a former student of mine, now working in synthetic biology, described some of the field's stranger achievements to me: entire bacterial genomes with every stop codon freed up and repurposed to encode unnatural amino acids, and, separately, cells engineered to stably copy an entirely synthetic base pair alongside the usual four letters, in effect adding new "characters" to the genetic alphabet. Somehow, being able to rewrite the code this deliberately makes it tempting to conclude that it must have been a real code all along, in some sense stronger than metaphor — though I suspect a sufficiently determined skeptic could turn that very fact around against me.

I would like you to write a dialogue — perhaps between two old colleagues catching up after a philosophy of biology workshop — in which each defends the strongest version of one of these two positions, so that I can see the disagreement worked out properly rather than merely gestured at. One, a realist about genetic information, holds that the vocabulary of coding and translation in molecular biology is literal and explanatorily indispensable, not just a convenient manner of speaking. The other, a deflationist, holds that "code" and "information" are heuristics inherited from mid-century communications engineering, and that nothing in the biology itself singles out DNA — as opposed to the cytoplasmic machinery, chromatin state, or developmental environment — as the privileged bearer of "the" information. Please have the realist confront the standard objection that a genuine code requires something like a convention and an interpreter capable of misreading its symbols, perhaps by arguing that natural selection fixes a notion of "proper function" that supplies its own kind of normativity, and have the deflationist press on whether that move actually earns the normativity it needs or merely relocates the mystery one level down. The two should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another, and neither should be left holding a tidy, unanswerable last word. Close the dialogue with each of them stating, concretely, what kind of biological finding, if any, would change their mind.
