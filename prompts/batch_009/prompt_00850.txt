I'm a backend engineer, and a few months back I went down a rabbit hole that started with Ramsey numbers. I was brushing up on graph theory for interview prep, hit the classic "party problem" (R(3,3) = 6), and got curious about whoever this Ramsey person was. That's how I found out Frank Ramsey wasn't just a name attached to a combinatorics result: before he died in 1930 at age 26, he also did foundational work on subjective probability and expected utility, and wrote a short, still-cited paper on how much a country ought to save for the future.

Two things from that rabbit hole have been rattling around in my head since, because they seem to pull in different directions. In "A Mathematical Theory of Saving" (1928), Ramsey argued that discounting people's welfare just because it lies further in the future is "ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination" — a zero-discount-rate position that I gather still gets invoked in arguments about how much weight climate policy should give to future generations. But I also came across a short essay of his, fittingly titled "Epilogue," where he writes about refusing to feel humbled by the sheer size of the universe, since stars can't think or love and therefore don't outweigh human beings in his reckoning. He explicitly extends the same move from space to time: the world will eventually die out, he says, but that event is so remote that "its present value at compound discount is almost nothing," which shouldn't make the present, human-scale business of life feel any less worthwhile.

I mentioned this to some friends who are deep into the effective altruism and AI-safety world, and they immediately heard an echo of longtermism: the view, associated with people like Nick Bostrom and William MacAskill, that because the future could contain an astronomically large number of people, reducing existential risk should dominate our moral priorities today over more immediate concerns. That argument leans on exactly the "don't discount the future" logic Ramsey defended as an economist. But it also treats sheer scale — of future time and population, rather than physical size — as the thing that settles what matters most, which sounds uncomfortably close to the kind of reasoning he waved away in "Epilogue." I have no formal philosophy training beyond what I've picked up from blogs and podcasts, so I can't be sure I'm even setting up the tension correctly, but: do you think Ramsey, confronted with the modern longtermist case for prioritizing existential risk, would have recognized it as the natural extension of his own discounting argument and signed on, or would he have picked apart the analogy and resisted it, the same way he resisted the idea that he should feel small before the stars? I'd like to see the reasoning, not just a final verdict.
