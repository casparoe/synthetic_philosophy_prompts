For the past few years I've been quietly obsessed with the Fermi paradox as a total amateur — no training in physics, statistics or philosophy, just years of popular books, blogs and the occasional paper I've had to read slowly. It was Toby Ord's "The Precipice" that pulled me toward the existential-risk side of things, so it was a bit of a shock to later discover that Ord had, a couple of years earlier, co-written a paper with Anders Sandberg and Eric Drexler called "Dissolving the Fermi Paradox." Their argument, as I understand it, is that the "paradox" is really a statistical artifact: instead of multiplying single best-guess values for each term of the Drake equation (which makes an empty sky look wildly improbable), you should carry the full, honestly enormous uncertainty in each term through the calculation — and once you do, their own numbers say there's something like a 53-to-99.6 percent chance we're the only technological civilisation in the Milky Way, and still a 39-to-85 percent chance we're alone in the observable universe entirely. No dramatic "Great Filter" event required.

This has become a running argument between me and a friend who takes AI and pandemic risk much more seriously than I tend to. Her objection is that the "dissolution" quietly does its work through modelling choices that aren't forced on anyone: treating the Drake terms as independent, using priors that let something like the probability of abiogenesis range uniformly over dozens of orders of magnitude, and generally exploiting the fact that multiplying several fractions with no principled floor will spit out tiny numbers almost no matter what the world is actually like. Worse, she says, even granting the whole calculation, it's silent on the one question that actually matters for how worried we should be: whether whatever makes civilisations rare sits behind us (abiogenesis, the jump to multicellularity) or ahead of us (something that reliably destroys technological civilisations) — and that people who cite the paper to feel better about the future are quietly helping themselves to the comforting half of that disjunction. I go back and forth on who has the better of this, and I'm honestly no longer sure whether we're disagreeing about what the statistics show or about something else entirely.

Please make both characters equally sharp and equally fair-minded, with no strawmanning in either direction: each should respond to what the other has actually just said, including the specific statistical points above, rather than restating their own view past each other. Since neither of them is an academic, I'd rather they argue in plain language than in jargon. And I'd like the dialogue to actually go somewhere — even if "somewhere" just means a clearer sense of which part of this is a real dispute about the maths and which part is really a disagreement about how to weigh uncertainty when the stakes might be existential.
