A few of us in a long-running rationalist-adjacent reading group have been working through Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence*, and chapter seven ("The Superintelligent Will") has produced more disagreement than anything else in the book so far. The chapter introduces two theses in quick succession, and I'd like a genuinely careful treatment of how they relate, rather than the usual one-paragraph gloss. The first is the orthogonality thesis, which Bostrom states as the claim that "more or less any level of intelligence could in principle be combined with more or less any final goal"—on its face, a modal claim about bare logical or metaphysical compatibility between intelligence and goals, not a claim about what's likely or typical.

The second is the instrumental convergence thesis, which sounds similar but seems to me to be making a different kind of claim: that certain sub-goals—self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, and resource acquisition, among others—will tend to be pursued by more or less any sufficiently capable agent regardless of its final goal, simply because they are useful for almost any final goal whatsoever. That reads as a claim about statistical typicality among realistic agents, not mere possibility. Taken together, the two theses seem to be in some tension: orthogonality is invoked to establish that a superintelligence's goals could be anything at all, including things utterly indifferent or hostile to human welfare, while instrumental convergence is invoked to establish that, whatever those goals are, the agent's actual behavior will look reassuringly similar across cases—right up until it doesn't.

What I can't settle, and what I'd like you to adjudicate, is whether there's a subtler problem here than mere tension. Stuart Armstrong's more formal defense of orthogonality explicitly restricts itself to a narrower thesis about agents that could actually be built, rather than the full space of logically possible minds, and instrumental convergence is likewise framed as a claim about realistically "situated" agents rather than exotic edge cases; some more recent alignment-oriented critiques go further and argue that most items on Bostrom's specific list of convergent values don't actually survive scrutiny, with only a thinner, option-preserving or power-seeking core doing real work. If both theses are only defensible in these narrower, practically relevant forms, does instrumental convergence end up doing almost all the load-bearing work in Bostrom's argument that a randomly selected superintelligence should be expected to be dangerous by default, leaving orthogonality as a much weaker and less action-guiding claim than it is usually advertised as? Please state both theses as precisely as you can, say explicitly what kind of claim each one is (modal, statistical, or otherwise), explain how Bostrom relies on both to reach his conclusion, and then commit to an actual verdict on whether the two theses make separable, complementary contributions or whether one of them is really doing most of the work.
