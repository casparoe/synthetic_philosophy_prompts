I spent almost forty years as a physicist before I retired, and back then philosophy never really entered into it - it was all equations and lab work. Since retiring though I've gotten properly into it, and I joined a little discussion group that meets at my local library once a month (mostly retired teachers plus one very opinionated ex-lawyer, fun bunch). We just spent two sessions on Galileo and the Church, which I ended up finding way more interesting than I expected going in.

Here's the thing that's been bugging me since. Everyone in the group kept bringing up that line of his about the Bible teaching you how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go, and treating it like Galileo had basically invented what Stephen Jay Gould centuries later called "non-overlapping magisteria" - science and religion occupy seperate turf, so there's nothing for them to actually conflict over, and asking whether the Bible is "right" about the earth moving is a kind of category error. Everyone agreed Galileo was just centuries ahead of his time on this, and that its obviously the sensible way to defuse the whole science-vs-religion mess.

Except when I actually went and read chunks of the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina myself, that isn't really what he spends his time doing - he goes through specific verses (Joshua's sun standing still, that Psalm about the sun's "tabernacle," and so on) arguing for how they ought to be read. That seems like an odd thing to bother with if scripture is simply none of astronomy's business and vice versa. So which is it - did I get the group's takeaway wrong, or was Galileo not actually the clean separationist everyone made him out to be?
