This semester, in my seminar on decision theory, we've spent the last couple of classes on Newcomb-style prediction problems, and last week we arrived at Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" case: their 1978 decision-theoretic reworking of the old parable, not Maugham's Baghdad-and-Samarra version but one set in Damascus and Aleppo, where a man flees the city in which he met Death only to find Death waiting for him at his destination.

What has stuck with me isn't that folk-tale surface but the wrinkle that turns it into an actual decision problem: Death is stipulated to work from an appointment book that already states where he will find his target, and to be reliable enough that whichever city the man deliberately chooses thereby counts as strong evidence that the book names that very city. A few of us ended up debating this after class, because it means staying and fleeing look symmetrically doomed from the inside: evidential decision theory can't settle on a recommendation that doesn't undercut itself, and the closest thing to a fix — what our professor called a "ratifiable" strategy, where you flip a coin instead of reasoning your way to a city — feels less like a solution than an admission of defeat, since you only seem safe once you have stopped being predictable.

I'd like to see that bind dramatized rather than just stated, but without another reskin of the same parable — my TA joked that half the class would hand in "Death in Cleveland" for the response paper, and I'd rather not be one of them. Could you write me a short story, maybe two or three pages long, whose protagonist faces this exact structural trap: some person, institution, or process that predicts their deliberate choices accurately enough that reasoning openly toward any option becomes evidence against that option, so that only an act of genuine randomness offers any real cover? You don't need a literal Death or an explicit appointment book — a contemporary or invented setting is fine, as long as the underlying logic carries over intact. I'd especially like the story to dwell on the moment where the protagonist realizes that thinking clearly about their situation is itself the danger, and to leave it ambiguous, by the end, whether choosing to gamble instead is really a way of outwitting the prediction or just a subtler way of fulfilling it.
