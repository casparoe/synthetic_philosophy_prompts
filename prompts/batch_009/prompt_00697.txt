ok so i had a two-hour argument with my friend Mara last night about van Fraassen's Reflection Principle and neither of us gave any ground. quick context in case it's useful: the principle says your current credence in A, given that your future credence in A will be r, has to just equal r (Van Fraassen backs this with a diachronic dutch book argument in "Belief and the Will"). Mara thinks its basically dead on arrival because of stuff like Christensen's drunk-driving case -- you know tonight you'll be wasted and will assign like a one-in-a-million credence to crashing the car on the way home, but you obviously shouldn't defer to that credence right now -- or the Ulysses-and-the-Sirens case, which van Fraassen actually wrote a whole seperate paper responding to. I kept pointing out that his reply is basically: Reflection only ever tells you to defer to a future opinion you're already presupposing comes from staying rational and better-informed, so a credence produced by booze or magic singing was never covered by the principle to begin with, meaning these aren't real counterexamples. Mara says that "fix" just makes the principle empty, since now you need some independent way to know in advance which anticipated changes count as legit updating before you can even apply Reflection -- and once you have that, Reflection isn't doing any work of its own.

Neither of us could kill it off, so: write me an essay that actually adjudicates this. Does some real, non-circular version of the Reflection Principle survive Christensen-style counterexamples once you factor in van Fraassen's own replies, or does his fix hollow the principle out? Don't just present both sides -- pick a verdict and defend it (a qualified verdict like "reflection only holds for ___" totally counts, as long as you say plainly what's left standing and what work it still does).
