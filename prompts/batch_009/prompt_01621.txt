I'm working on a novel in which two old friends—who studied philosophy together as students and are now reunited after forty years—spend an evening arguing over Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life," a text that shaped their lives in opposite directions.

The first took Schlick's argument to heart: that a life organized as a chain of purposes—studying for the sake of a degree, working for the sake of a wage, striving for the sake of retirement—never actually arrives at meaning, since each link only defers the question to the next. He rebuilt his life around Schlick's alternative, activity pursued for its own sake in the spirit of play and "youth," and has drifted amiably between interests and modest jobs ever since, refusing to mortgage the present to any future.

The second accepts Schlick's diagnosis but not his cure. She thinks he is right that meaning cannot be located in a goal considered on its own, but wrong to infer that only play-like, outcome-indifferent activity escapes the problem. She spent thirty years in demanding, often joyless work raising a difficult child and building an institution that outlasted her marriage, and insists this was among the most meaningful things she has done—not because any of it was fun, but because of what, and whom, it was for.

Write their conversation as a real, sustained dialogue. Have each press the other on whether Schlick's own fallback moves—his claim that "creative work" can itself be play, and his distinction between joy and mere pleasure—can accommodate her kind of life, or whether stretching "play" that far empties the notion of any content. Neither should land a tidy knockdown; let the disagreement stay genuinely unresolved.
