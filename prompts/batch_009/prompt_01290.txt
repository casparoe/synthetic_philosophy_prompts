I've been reporting on the ethical fight over thoracoabdominal normothermic regional perfusion (TA-NRP), a fast-spreading transplant procedure in which, minutes after a patient is declared dead by circulatory criteria, surgeons clamp off the vessels feeding the brain, then use a pump to restart circulation to the rest of the body, warming it back toward normal temperature — sometimes including the heart itself, which briefly beats again in the donor's chest before being stopped, removed, and restarted once more in the recipient.

Defenders argue this is no different, ethically, from ordinary donation after circulatory death, where the statutory requirement of "irreversible cessation of circulatory function" has always really been read as permanent cessation (circulation will not be restored, because no one intends to attempt resuscitation) rather than as circulation that could never physically be restored; otherwise, they point out, no standard DCD donor was ever truly dead, since CPR can often restart a stopped heart within the standard five-minute stand-off period.

Please argue that this permanence defense, whatever its merits for ordinary DCD, cannot rescue TA-NRP from violating the dead donor rule (that organ recovery must not itself cause the donor's death): there is a principled difference between merely declining to resuscitate a donor and actively restoring circulation to nearly the entire body before its organs are removed.
