I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've been down a philosophy-of-science rabbit hole lately that started with Ladyman and Ross's "Every Thing Must Go," which argues for something called "ontic structural realism": roughly, the idea that what physics gives us — and maybe all there ultimately is — is relational structure (symmetries, invariants, the shape of the equations) rather than some further "intrinsic nature" that the structure belongs to. Coming from a software background, I couldn't help mapping this onto the distinction between structural and nominal typing: in a structurally-typed language, two values count as the same type simply because they expose the same interface, regardless of how they're implemented underneath or what they're nominally declared to be — if it quacks and walks like a duck, it's a duck, no further "duck-nature" required. That analogy gave me enough of a grip on the position that I drafted a short paragraph for a blog post arguing that scientific realism should be "structurally typed": we're entitled to believe in whatever relational structure a well-confirmed theory posits (the "interface" reality presents to measurement), but not in any further claim about the intrinsic, nominal nature of whatever implements it, since that nature never shows up in an observation and does no predictive work.

Before I post that, I'd like your comments on two things in particular. First, is the interface/implementation analogy actually doing philosophical work, or is it just structural realism re-described in programming-language vocabulary, quietly smuggling back in the very notion of "nature" it's supposed to dispense with? Second — and this is the part I really can't evaluate myself — I have a nagging half-memory from a Stanford Encyclopedia detour that there's a well-known objection, I think from a mathematician named Newman, originally aimed at Russell, to the effect that "we only know the structure of things" is nearly empty of content, since almost any sufficiently large collection of underlying items can be assigned relations that realize whichever structure you like, so the whole "structural" claim collapses into a fact about how many things there are. If I've got that roughly right, I'd like your honest read on whether my structural-typing analogy is vulnerable to the same move — couldn't almost any set of underlying "nominal" implementations be reinterpreted after the fact to satisfy whatever interface we care about? — and whether retreating from the epistemic version of structural realism to the more radical, no-nature-at-all ontic version actually blocks that objection, or merely relocates it.
