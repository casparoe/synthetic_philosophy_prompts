After forty years in evolutionary biology, where game-theoretic reasoning was a daily tool, I have spent my retirement reading more widely in philosophy, and two puzzles now sit side by side in my mind. In the centipede game, backward induction implies that a rational player should defect on the very first move, forsaking the far larger payoff that sustained cooperation would eventually yield. In the surprise examination paradox, students reason backward from the last possible day to "prove" that no surprise test can occur, and are then duly surprised when one is given anyway. Both arguments chain backward from a fixed, commonly known endpoint to a conclusion that seems to be falsified in practice, yet I have not come across a discussion that treats the two side by side. I would welcome a comparison of them: do they share a common logical flaw, does whatever resolves (or dissolves) one paradox also handle the other, and if not, precisely where do they come apart?
