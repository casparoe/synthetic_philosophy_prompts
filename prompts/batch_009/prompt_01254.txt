As a software engineer, I spend a good part of my working life thinking in Bayesian terms: priors, likelihoods, posteriors. So I was struck to learn that the philosopher Richard Swinburne runs the question of God's existence through exactly that machinery in "The Existence of God." He assigns theism a prior probability (largely on grounds of simplicity), updates it on evidence such as the existence of an orderly, life-permitting universe, the fact of human consciousness, and reports of religious experience, and concludes that the resulting posterior probability of theism exceeds 0.5.

I also recently came across Wittgenstein's 1938 "Lectures on Religious Belief," where he observes that hedging an ordinary empirical claim, such as the presence of an aircraft overhead, with "probably" leaves two speakers merely disagreeing by degree, whereas doing the same to someone's claim that they believe in a Last Judgement opens up something more like a deep rift between them, as if evidence-proportioned confidence were beside the point.

Wittgenstein died nearly three decades before Swinburne published this argument, so any answer here is necessarily reconstructive: drawing on his actual lectures and preoccupations, how would Wittgenstein most plausibly have responded to Swinburne's specific project of assigning theism a Bayesian probability? Please address whether his objection would simply collapse into the logical positivists' charge that theological claims are meaningless or unverifiable, or would be doing something importantly different, and whether Swinburne could reasonably reply that natural theology is a different sort of enterprise than the everyday avowals of faith Wittgenstein was describing.
