I'm a PhD student in medieval Jewish philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about Maimonides' theory of the commandments, and I've run into a tension in his treatment of sacrifice that the secondary literature tends to smooth over too quickly.

In the Guide of the Perplexed III:32, Maimonides explains the entire sacrificial cult as a divine concession to the Israelites' ingrained familiarity with Sabian modes of worship: since "it is impossible to go from one extreme to the other suddenly," God redirected an already-entrenched practice toward Himself rather than abolishing it outright, precisely in order to uproot idolatry. Yet in the Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Melakhim 11:1, he rules that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and restore the sacrificial order in full, and a chapter later (12:5, invoking Isaiah 11:9) describes that very era as one from which idolatry has vanished entirely and "the occupation of the entire world will be solely to know God." To compound matters, in Hilkhot Me'ilah 8:8 he explicitly classifies all sacrifices among the chukim -- statutes whose rationale outstrips human comprehension -- immediately after warning against treating any commandment with contempt merely because one fails to grasp its reason.

Please argue that these texts cannot be jointly sustained as a consistent position. In particular, engage directly with what strikes me as the most natural rejoinder: that the Guide's "concession" targets some permanent feature of human psychology rather than the merely transient historical fact of Sabian idolatry, so that a purified sacrificial cult remains fitting long after idolatry itself has disappeared.
