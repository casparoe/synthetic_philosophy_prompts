I am roughly two-thirds of the way through a novel that follows a philosophy graduate student finishing a dissertation on Frege's Begriffsschrift, and I have built the book's climax around a real historical episode rather than an invented one: the political diary Frege kept for a few weeks in the spring of 1924, the year before he died. It was first unearthed by Michael Dummett in the 1950s while he was going through Frege's papers, then kept out of the official edition of Frege's writings for decades before it was finally published in German in the 1990s and translated into English soon after. In it, Frege turns out to have been bitterly opposed to parliamentary democracy, hostile to Catholics and the French, and, above all, committed to the view that Jews should be stripped of political rights and, ideally, expelled from the country altogether.

The thematic engine of my novel is my protagonist's growing conviction that this discovery does not merely tarnish Frege's biography but retroactively unmasks his logicism itself: the famous claim that arithmetic reduces to pure logic was, on her reading, never really the neutral, dispassionate result it presented itself as, but an expression of the same appetite for a rigid, hierarchical, exclusionary order that appears undisguised in the diary. The diary, in other words, is the moment the mask finally slips, and I have written the scene to land as the intellectual and emotional climax of the book, the point toward which everything else has been building.

Before I commit another three hundred pages to that ending, I would like a sanity check from someone who knows this material better than my protagonist does. I am not asking whether Frege held repugnant political views; that much is settled and not in question. What I want to know is whether the further step my novel takes for granted — that his purely formal, symbolic work in logic can be shown to encode or depend on that same exclusionary impulse, so that the diary exposes something about the logic itself and not only about the man who produced it — is a piece of reasoning that a careful historian or philosopher of logic would actually stand behind, or whether it is the kind of connection that feels satisfying to a novelist but would not survive scrutiny from someone who actually works on Frege.
