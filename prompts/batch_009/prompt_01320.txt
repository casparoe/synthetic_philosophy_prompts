I've been trying to read stuff outside of tech lately and a friend told me Zhuangzi is basically the ancient Chinese version of "stop being scared of dying," so I figured I'd check it out. Didn't want to pay for a nice modern edition so I grabbed this free public-domain translation by a guy named James Legge from 1891 (its part of some "Sacred Books of the East" series) that's floating around online. The combo of Victorian-era English translating 2300-year-old Daoist philosophy is rough going, not gonna lie, but there's a passage in the chapter called "The Great and Most Honoured Master" that I've re-read like five times now and I can't tell if im just dumb or if the argument genuinely doesn't hold together. Quick setup: a guy named Dze-lai is dying, his friend Dze-li comes to check on him, and this is what Dze-lai says:

"Before long Dze-lai fell ill, and lay gasping at the point of death, while his wife and children stood around him wailing. Dze-li went to ask for him, and said to them, 'Hush! Get out of the way! Do not disturb him as he is passing through his change.' Then, leaning against the door, he said (to the dying man), 'Great indeed is the Creator! What will He now make you to become? Where will He take you to? Will He make you the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect?' Dze-lai replied, 'Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious. There is the great Mass (of nature);—I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it:—what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.

'Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, "I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-yeh," the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, "I must become a man; I must become a man," the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking.'"

What's bugging me is that these feel like two totally different arguments duct-taped together. The first part (parents telling kids where to go, obeying Yin and Yang or else you're "obstinate and rebellious") reads like a duty/obedience thing, basically "nature is your manager and you don't get to reject the sprint plan." But the metal-in-the-furnace bit isn't about obligation at all, it's more like "wanting to permanently stay a human is a confused thing to want in the first place," the same way it'd be weird for a blob of bronze to insist on which sword it gets turned into. Owing-obedience and your-preference-doesn't-even-make-sense seem like pretty different kinds of claims to me, so: am I missing how these two bits connect into one argument, or is Zhuangzi just stacking metaphors that don't need to add up? And is "the Creator" here supposed to be an actual intentional being (something closer to a personal god deciding your next form), or is that just Legge's somewhat loaded word choice for what's really more of an impersonal process — because that seems like it'd change what kind of argument this even is. Would love it if someone could untangle this for me.
