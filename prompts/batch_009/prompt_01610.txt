I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I have been working through Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding on my own, and a passage in Book II, Chapter XXI, "Of Power," has left me puzzled. Locke first sets out his definition of liberty:

"8. Liberty, what. All the actions that we have any idea of reducing themselves, as has been said, to these two, viz. thinking and motion; so far as a man has power to think or not to think, to move or not to move, according to the preference or direction of his own mind, so far is a man free. Wherever any performance or forbearance are not equally in a man's power; wherever doing or not doing will not equally follow upon the preference of his mind directing it, there he is not free, though perhaps the action may be voluntary. So that the idea of liberty is, the idea of a power in any agent to do or forbear any particular action, according to the determination or thought of the mind, whereby either of them is preferred to the other: where either of them is not in the power of the agent to be produced by him according to his volition, there he is not at liberty; that agent is under necessity. So that liberty cannot be where there is no thought, no volition, no will; but there may be thought, there may be will, there may be volition, where there is no liberty."

He then gives this example:

"10. Belongs not to volition. Again: suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in, i.e. prefers his stay to going away. I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone. So that liberty is not an idea belonging to volition, or preferring; but to the person having the power of doing, or forbearing to do, according as the mind shall choose or direct. Our idea of liberty reaches as far as that power, and no farther. For wherever restraint comes to check that power, or compulsion takes away that indifferency of ability to act, or to forbear acting, there liberty, and our notion of it, presently ceases."

I've read that this "locked room" case is sometimes described as an ancestor of the thought experiments Harry Frankfurt later used against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities -- the claim that a person is morally responsible for an act only if they could have done otherwise. But on my own reading, Locke isn't making any claim about responsibility, praise, or blame here. His point looks narrower: that "voluntary" (caused by one's own preference) and "at liberty" (having the power to have acted otherwise) are simply different ideas, and that the man in the room has the first without the second.

What I can't work out is whether Locke's example is really doing the same philosophical work as a Frankfurt case, or only resembles one on the surface. Does showing that voluntariness doesn't require alternatives tell us anything about whether responsibility doesn't require alternatives either? Or is there a real gap between those two claims -- one that Locke's passage doesn't touch, and that Frankfurt and those who followed him had to close with some separate argument of their own? I would like to understand exactly where the analogy holds and where it breaks down.
