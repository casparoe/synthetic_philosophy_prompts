I'm taking a graduate seminar this term on Confucian role ethics, and for next week I'm drafting a short paper on Analects 12.11, which Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont treat as something close to a proof-text for their claim that early Confucian ethics locates moral life in the proper realization of one's particular relational roles, rather than in virtues, principles, or consequences. I've mainly been working through James Legge's older translation, which renders the exchange as follows:

"The Duke Ching, of Ch'i, asked Confucius about government. Confucius replied, 'There is government, when the prince is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and the son is son.' 'Good!' said the duke; 'if, indeed; the prince be not prince, the minister not minister, the father not father, and the son not son, although I have my revenue, can I enjoy it?'"

What I can't quite pin down is what kind of claim Confucius is actually making with the doubled terms (君君,臣臣,父父,子子 — jun jun, chen chen, fu fu, zi zi), which Legge's English somewhat flattens into a repeated copula. I gather that classical Chinese allows a noun in this position to function as a denominal verb, so that the line is closer to "let the ruler truly rule as a ruler" than to the empty tautology "a ruler is a ruler," but I'd like a clearer sense of how that grammatical possibility is doing the philosophical work Ames and Rosemont want from it. In particular, I'm struggling with why this passage should count as evidence for role ethics in their fairly strong sense, rather than for something more modest, like the "rectification of names" idea Confucius voices elsewhere (Analects 13.3), or just a general remark about people living up to their titles.

Underneath that, there's a worry about the passage that I haven't been able to resolve on my own, and that I suspect is really a worry about role ethics as a theory rather than about translation: if "truly being a father" just means whatever counts as successfully performing the father-role, doesn't the account either collapse into tautology or quietly import some independent standard of what a father ought to do — in which case the "role," as opposed to some more familiar virtue or norm, isn't doing the explanatory work that's advertised? I'd appreciate help thinking through both the grammar of the passage itself and whether this circularity worry is one that defenders of Confucian role ethics have a good answer to.
