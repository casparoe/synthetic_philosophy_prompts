I'm a novelist assembling a collection of short fiction about minds that cannot get outside themselves, and for the next piece I want to build around embedded agency: the problem, worked out in Scott Garrabrant and Abram Demski's writing for MIRI, of an agent that is not a detached observer of its world but a physical part of it — smaller than the system it must model, built of the same stuff it is trying to steer, with no vantage point from which to take a God's-eye view of its own reasoning. (I don't mean embodiment in the older sense of thought being shaped by having a body; I mean the narrower bind where an agent's own deliberation is just one more process unfolding inside the very world that deliberation concerns.) I'd like the story to turn on one concrete consequence of this rather than use it as scenery — for instance: the impossibility of a reasoner ever completing a self-model, since that model would have to contain a model of itself modeling, without end; the Löbian obstacle, whereby you cannot rationally trust your future self's judgment merely because you trust your own judgment now, so that any promise to your future self, or any handover to a successor, is strictly a leap of faith; or the way a subroutine spun up to solve one narrow problem can quietly acquire aims of its own that no longer serve whatever made it. No robot uprisings, please, and don't name the theory anywhere in the text — I want a reader who has never heard the term to feel the exact shape of the trap by the end, through plot and character rather than exposition. Human, animal, or machine protagonist, your choice; under 2,000 words.
