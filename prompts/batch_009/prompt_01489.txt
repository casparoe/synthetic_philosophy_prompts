I spent my career in statistical mechanics, and now that I'm retired I've been reading my way into philosophy, with decision theory as a particular fascination. One disagreement I've run into concerns the Kelly criterion — maximize expected log-wealth each period when betting repeatedly. It was originally derived from an information-theoretic growth-rate argument, yet it is mathematically identical to what an expected-utility maximizer with logarithmic utility would choose anyway. Paul Samuelson treated this as deflationary: log utility is just one utility function among many, so the Kelly strategy isn't uniquely rational, merely what an investor with that particular risk preference would do. The "ergodicity economics" literature associated with Ole Peters argues the opposite: for an agent living through one non-ergodic, multiplicative sequence of bets, maximizing expected wealth — an ensemble average over possible worlds that don't exist for that agent — is the wrong quantity altogether, and maximizing time-average growth is mandatory regardless of risk preference. A related idea, sometimes called "geometric rationality," extends this same style of argument well beyond betting, to cases like Nash bargaining. Is there a genuine substantive disagreement here about what rationality requires, or are both camps simply describing the same mathematics through different, equally legitimate framings? I'd appreciate help locating the actual crux, and your view on which side, if either, has the better of the argument.
