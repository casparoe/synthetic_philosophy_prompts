Ok here's one that's been bugging me since my medieval Jewish philosophy seminar. Maimonides' account of animal sacrifice in Guide III:32 (plus the bit about Egyptian animal-worship in III:46) is basically that the whole sacrificial cult is a concession -- the Israelites were so steeped in Sabian/Egyptian sacrificial worship that God couldnt just yank it away cold turkey, so instead He redirected the practice toward Himself as a kind of way station en route to something purer, like prayer. Nahmanides goes after this pretty hard in his comment on Vayikra 1:9: his main objection is that Kayin, Hevel, and Noach were all already sacrificing back when there was no idolatry around for anyone to be weaned off of, so the "concession to primitive habit" story can't actually be why the Torah has sacrifice in the first place. I think Rambam has one possible out here -- individuals sacrificing spontaneously is arguably a different phenomenon from a commanded national cult, so maybe the objection doesn't really touch his theory -- but I'm not sure that fully rescues him.

Can you write me an essay that actually adjudicates this: does Nahmanides' objection sink Maimonides' account, or can it be patched up (along the lines I sketched, or some other way)? And seperately, whose overall theory of why God would command sacrifice at all is the more satisfying one, textually and philosophically? Don't just lay out both sides evenhandedly -- actually pick a side and defend it.
