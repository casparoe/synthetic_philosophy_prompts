About three weeks ago, a fifteen-second clip of a comment I made during the public-comment portion of our school board meeting was cut from the district's livestream, stripped of context, and passed around a couple of large regional Facebook groups and then X. Within two days I had several thousand replies, quote-posts, and messages from people who have never set foot in our town and will never have to see me at the grocery store. A friend, half-consoling and half-teasing, sent me Kierkegaard's "Two Ages" — the review he wrote in the wake of his own mauling by the satirical paper The Corsair, which spent months caricaturing his appearance and turning his name into a street-corner joke — and told me I'd feel better knowing someone had been through this before Facebook existed. I did read it, along with some of the surrounding journal material, and was genuinely unsettled by how well concepts like "leveling," the press as manufacturer of an otherwise nonexistent "public," and reflection without passion seemed to fit what had just happened to me. I've since discovered I'm hardly the first person to notice this: there is an entire minor genre of essays making the "Kierkegaard predicted the internet" point, and I even tracked down a 1999 paper by Hubert Dreyfus arguing, on similar grounds, that Kierkegaard would have hated the internet because of what anonymity does to commitment.

What I'd like from you is something more fine-grained than another pass at that same observation. Kierkegaard, on my reading, actually lived through two distinguishable phenomena that tend to get run together under "the public": the diffuse, low-passion "leveling" produced by anonymous newspaper gossip and public opinion in general, and the very specific, sustained, personally targeted mobbing he suffered at the hands of the Corsair, where cartoonists and jeering crowds knew exactly who he was. My own experience, and what I understand of pile-ons, review-bombing campaigns, and quote-tweet dogpiles more generally, seems much closer to the second case than the first, even though "leveling" and "the public" are the concepts everyone reaches for. There's also his separate claim, from "That Individual," that a crowd dilutes each member's sense of responsibility down to a fraction, which seems relevant specifically to the people doing the piling-on rather than to the ambient "public" that merely half-attends. Working only from what Kierkegaard himself says, rather than from the secondary literature, could you: (1) say whether his framework actually gives us the resources to treat targeted pile-ons and diffuse leveling as one phenomenon or whether they need different diagnoses, and how the responsibility-dilution point bears on that; (2) assess whether an interactive, algorithmically curated medium is different in kind from the one-directional press he was attacking, given how much weight his argument puts on the specific mechanics of the press; and (3) tell me whether he would count any present-day online spaces — a private group chat, a small forum with real membership and norms — as a genuine community of "single individuals" rather than an instance of "the public," and therefore outside the scope of his critique. Given how little faith he had in political or institutional remedies, I'd also value your sense of whether his diagnosis leaves room for anything an individual can actually do, or whether, on his own terms, the only available response is inward and religious.
