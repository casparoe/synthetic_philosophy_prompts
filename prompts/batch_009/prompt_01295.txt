Been down a rabbit hole on "Ulysses contracts" lately — the idea of binding your future self in advance so it can't screw things up, like Ulysses tying himself to the mast so he can hear the sirens without steering the ship onto the rocks. People use this to justify why constitutions should be hard to change. Germany's constitution literally has an "eternity clause" (Art. 79(3)) that makes stuff like human dignity permanently unamendable — not even a unanimous vote can touch it, the only way around it is scrapping the constitution entirely. Jefferson thought the opposite: no generation has a right to bind the next one, and he ran the actuarial numbers and decided every law and constitution should automatically expire after 19 years.

As a dev this feels like shipping an immutable prod config with no override flag, ever, for anyone — sometimes that's genuinely the right call, but it's a hell of a design decision to make on behalf of 80 million people who never agreed to the deploy. And that's really what bugs me: Ulysses tying himself to the mast is one guy constraining his own future self, who fully consented. A constitution written in 1949 (or 1787) binding people who weren't even born yet feels like a totally different move wearing the same name.

Write me a dialogue between two equally sharp people hashing this out — one defending hardcore entrenchment (eternity-clause style) as a genuine Ulysses contract, the other pushing the Jefferson-style objection that it's illegitimate precisely because the binder and the bound aren't the same person. Make them actually engage with each other's strongest moves rather then talking past each other, and don't force a neat resolution at the end.
