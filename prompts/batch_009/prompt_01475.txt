A while back, a post making the rounds on the rationalist blogs and forums I've been reading for years resurfaced the story of Christoph Benzmüller and Bruno Woltzenlogel Paleo's 2013 project formalizing Gödel's ontological argument in a higher-order automated theorem prover, complete with the inevitable "computer scientists prove God exists" framing that the tabloids ran with at the time. That framing always struck me as missing the point — nobody thinks a valid derivation from a set of axioms about "positive properties," "essences," and "necessary existence" settles anything about theology; the whole game is in whether the axioms are even coherent. Digging past the tabloid coverage, though, I found that the same automated-reasoning project didn't just check that the argument goes through — it also confirmed something I'd never seen mentioned in any pop-level treatment of Gödel's proof: Howard Sobel's 1987 result that Gödel's axioms entail full modal collapse, meaning every true proposition turns out to be necessarily true, with no contingency left anywhere in the system. The same authors apparently even suspected, and later confirmed, that Gödel's own original (pre-Scott) axioms are outright inconsistent, rather than merely collapse-inducing.

I tried sketching out the collapse derivation on paper with a friend who knows more modal logic than I do, and we kept losing the thread of exactly how "necessary existence is a positive property," combined with the theorem that God-likeness is an essence of anything that instantiates it, ends up entailing every arbitrary true proposition, rather than just the comparatively modest claim that a God-like being exists necessarily. I gather the trick has something to do with treating a predicate like "being an x such that p" (for some perfectly ordinary, contingently true p, with no free occurrence of x) as a legitimate property that the underlying logic's abstraction principle lets you form, and then feeding that property into the essence-and-necessary-existence machinery. Could you walk me through the gut-level intuition for why this produces collapse, at whatever level of formality is useful without requiring me to track every quantifier? And separately, I'd be curious how people like C. Anthony Anderson thought this could be patched by revising the axioms rather than treating it as a decisive strike against the whole approach, and whether that sort of fix is generally regarded as having worked.
