Here's the passage (translated — this is close to what's in our course reader, not word for word since I'm partly working off my own notes): "Wisdom must always keep her gambling chips in hand; for whoever says 'wise' says 'happy' — at least in my dictionary. One must have passions in order to be happy; but one must make them serve our happiness, and there are some to which all entry into our soul must be forbidden. I am not speaking of those passions that are vices, such as hatred, revenge, anger. Ambition, for example, is a passion against which I believe one must guard one's soul, if one wishes to be happy — not because it affords no pleasures, for I believe this passion can supply them; not because ambition always desires more, for that is assuredly a great good; but because, of all the passions, it is the one that makes our happiness most dependent on others: and the less our happiness depends on others, the easier it is for us to be happy. Let us not fear cutting back too much on this score — enough dependency will remain regardless. For this same reason of independence, the love of study is, of all the passions, the one that contributes most to our happiness. Enclosed within the love of study is a passion from which no elevated soul is ever entirely free: the passion for glory. Indeed, for half of humankind this is the only means of acquiring it — and it is precisely that half whom education strips of the means, and for whom it makes even the taste for it impossible. It is certain that the love of study is far less necessary to the happiness of men than to that of women. Men have an infinity of resources entirely lacking to women, and far better ways of attaining glory, whether through skill in war, or through talent for government or negotiation. But women are excluded, by their condition, from every kind of glory; and when, by chance, one of them is born with a sufficiently elevated soul, only study remains to console her for all the exclusions and dependencies to which her condition has condemned her."
