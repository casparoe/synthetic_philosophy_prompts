I'm working on a novel with a secondary character closely modeled on Gottlob Frege, and I want a late scene to turn on a genuine philosophical fault line rather than a cheap irony. The historical Frege built his career on the idea that logical 'thoughts' are objective and mind-independent — residents, as he put it, of a 'third realm' entirely untouched by the psychology of whoever happens to grasp them. Yet the diary he kept in the final year of his life reveals a committed antisemite who wanted Jews stripped of political rights and driven out of Germany. Before drafting the scene, I want to weigh Frege's own anti-psychologism — which seems to entail that a thinker's private convictions, however repellent, have no bearing on the objective standing of their logical work — against the more ordinary intuition that a thinker's character is at least some evidence about the soundness of their thinking. Does his doctrine actually settle the matter in his own favor, or does his case expose a limit to it that his own framework couldn't have let him see? Where do serious readers of Frege who've grappled with the diary tend to come down on this?
