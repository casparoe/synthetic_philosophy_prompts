So I fell down a Wikipedia hole last week that started at the Monty Hall problem and somehow ended at the Sleeping Beauty problem, which I guess is a whole thing in philosophy of probability (the halfer vs thirder debate). I'm a backend engineer, and about halfway through reading about it I realized it maps almost exactly onto a dedup issue we've had forever with one of our queues: our consumer occasionally gets redelivered a message when an upstream ack times out (happens some small percentage of the time, call it p), and because of how the handler was written years ago, it literally can't tell, from inside a single invocation, whether its looking at the original delivery or a redelivery of the same message — no sequence number, no flag, nothing, the payload is byte-for-byte identical either way. So every single invocation is basically Sleeping Beauty waking up with no memory of which day it is.

What I'm trying to figure out is what the actual implications of the halfer/thirder debate are for a situation like this. Two things specifically: first, if we tried to back out our true upstream error rate p just from counting invocations in the logs (instead of deduped events), does this debate tell us we'd be systematically biased, and if so, in which direction? Second, if you grabbed one on-call engineer mid-incident and asked them to just state their gut credence that "this particular invocation in front of me is a redelivery," is there actually a fact of the matter about what number is correct, or does the philosophical disagreement mean that question doesn't even have a well defined answer? I already know the "real" fix here is to just add a dedup key so this never comes up in practice — that's not really what I'm asking. I want to know what's actually at stake in the philosophical dispute and whether it should change how I think about probability more generally, not just get a patch for this one queue.
