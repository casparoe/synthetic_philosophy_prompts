I'm putting together a graduate seminar that reads early modern rationalist theodicy alongside contemporary population ethics, and a student raised a challenge this week that I haven't been able to fully answer myself. Leibniz's claim that God creates "the best of all possible worlds" is usually remembered only in Voltaire's caricatured form, but the actual selection criterion he lays out across the Discourse on Metaphysics, the correspondence with Arnauld, and "On the Ultimate Origination of Things" is considerably more precise: among the infinite set of compossible worlds, God actualizes the one exhibiting the greatest quantity of essence, or degree of perfection, compatible with the greatest order and simplicity of laws — the world he elsewhere describes as "simplest in hypotheses and richest in phenomena." That is, in effect, an aggregative axiology for ranking populations of monads, arrived at roughly two and a half centuries before Derek Parfit devised the mere addition paradox and the repugnant conclusion specifically to stress-test views of that shape: theories that maximize total value seem thereby committed to preferring a vastly more populous world of barely-worthwhile lives over a smaller, flourishing one.

What I'd like from you is a considered reconstruction, grounded in Leibniz's actual texts and concepts rather than the popular slogan, of how he would most plausibly respond if the repugnant conclusion were put to him directly. Does his criterion of maximizing quantity of essence and perfection commit him, on pain of inconsistency, to something in the neighborhood of the repugnant conclusion — preferring a world teeming with the lowest grade of monads, whose perceptions are so confused as to amount to little more than a permanent stupor, over one with far fewer but vastly more perfect rational spirits? Or does some other resource already built into his system block that result: his insistence that order and harmony, not bare quantity, are constitutive of perfection; his graded hierarchy of monads by distinctness of perception; or his jurisprudential distinction between strict right, equity, and the highest grade of justice as "the charity of the wise" (caritas sapientis), which might weight the good of rational spirits quite differently from that of bare monads or animal souls? I'm less interested in a survey of considerations on both sides than in your best-supported verdict on which horn Leibniz would actually have grasped, and why.
