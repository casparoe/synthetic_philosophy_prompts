I'm drafting a column about the history of technological risk that uses John von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" — written the same year he was diagnosed with the cancer that would kill him within two years — as a lens on today's AI-risk debates. Before I go further, I want a hard-nosed read of the central argumentative move, not just a reaction to the prose. Below are my working notes and the claim I'm building toward.

Von Neumann's diagnosis is that the industrial revolution's old safety valve — what he flatly calls "geographical and political Lebensraum," an ever-expanding frontier able to absorb the tensions new technology generates — has run out, because the earth is now too small relative to what technology can do to it. The technology he lingers on most vividly isn't the bomb; it's climate engineering. He works out how little energy it would take to shift the earth's temperature by several degrees in either direction (citing the 1883 Krakatoa eruption as a natural experiment), and predicts that deliberate climate control is coming — and that it will be at least as destabilizing as nuclear weapons, since the same science that could stabilize climate could just as easily be turned to "forms of climatic warfare," with effects that would ignore borders entirely.

The part I want tested is his treatment of solutions. He rejects outlawing dangerous technology altogether, not merely because enforcement would be hard, but for three distinct reasons: useful and harmful applications are too entangled to separate, since "it is never possible to separate the lions from the lambs"; any authority capable of enforcing a worldwide ban would have to be so powerful that the underlying political problem would already be solved by its very existence; and prohibition is simply "contrary to the whole ethos of the industrial age." He's separately skeptical that abolishing war outright could be a durable fix, given how fast antiwar feeling has faded after past wars. Taken together, these lead him to conclude that "for progress there is no cure" — that day-to-day, opportunistic judgment, guided by "patience, flexibility, intelligence," is the only realistic posture, rather than any once-and-for-all fix.

My claim is that this argument quietly slides from "a total, enforced, worldwide ban is unachievable" to "there is no point trying to shape which technologies advance faster than which others" — that his pessimism about prohibition gets treated as if it were pessimism about anything resembling what's now called differential technological development: deliberately racing ahead on protective or defensive applications while dragging your feet on dangerous ones. I want to use that gap to push back on the AI-world descendant of his fatalism, on which an unenforceable global pause is taken to imply that opportunistic muddling-through is the only serious posture left.

Tell me, first, whether that gap is real: does at least one of von Neumann's three reasons, read carefully, actually generalize to differential development too, such that his fatalism is more defensible than I'm giving it credit for? And second, setting fidelity to the text aside: is "you can't ban all of it, therefore you can still selectively steer it" a good inference on its own terms, or is there a failure mode in it I'm not seeing?
