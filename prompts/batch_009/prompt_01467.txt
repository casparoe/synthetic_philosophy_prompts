I'm a site reliability engineer, and a recent incident at work has left me stuck on a question in the metaphysics of causation that I never expected to become personally relevant to my job.

I ran the postmortem for a six-hour outage caused, proximately, by an expired TLS certificate. The automatic renewal job for that certificate had been disabled about eighteen months earlier, as a "temporary" workaround during an unrelated incident, and nobody ever turned it back on. Our postmortem process is explicitly "blameless": we're told to write up "contributing factors" rather than a single "root cause," and to avoid any language that assigns fault to a person or team. But when I actually tried to write the sentence "the disabled renewal job was a contributing cause of the outage," I realized I couldn't defend it without some theory of causation by omission on hand, and it turns out there isn't an easy one available.

Below are some notes I've drafted toward a short essay on the question, working through three positions I know from the literature. I'd be grateful for your comments before I try to develop this into something longer.

The naive counterfactual test — had the job been enabled, the outage wouldn't have happened — is satisfied, but it's satisfied just as well by an unbounded number of other omissions: our not running a second, independent monitor; our not having a stricter change-review policy; our not having hired a dedicated platform-security engineer years earlier; and so on indefinitely. Lewis, in "Void and Object," essentially accepts this consequence: he treats absences as facts capable of standing in relations of counterfactual dependence and so, on his view, as genuine causes and effects, explaining our reluctance to mention most of them as a pragmatic feature of conversation rather than a metaphysical constraint on what counts as a cause. That's uncomfortable for my purposes, since the postmortem template presupposes there's a fact of the matter about which handful of omissions are "contributing factors" and which are mere background — not just a fact about what's conversationally worth saying.

Sarah McGrath's response in "Causation By Omission: A Dilemma" looked more promising to me at first: an omission is a cause only relative to some norm specifying what should have happened — an engineering standard, a runbook, a documented on-call practice — which is why "we didn't re-enable the job" is a contributing cause while "we didn't hire a security engineer in 2019" is not, even though both satisfy the same counterfactual equally well. The cost is that whether some omission counts as a cause now turns on essentially normative facts about expectations, which sits awkwardly with causation being the kind of mind-independent relation it's usually taken to be.

Michael Moore's view in Causation and Responsibility is more deflationary still: omissions are never really causes, because genuine causation requires something like a causal process connecting cause and effect, and an absence cannot be part of one; what grounds our holding someone responsible for an omission is counterfactual dependence directly, without that dependence needing to be a species of causation at all.

My tentative conclusion is that if McGrath or Moore is right, a truly blameless postmortem cannot single out particular omissions as "contributing causes" without covertly relying on exactly the normative or responsibility-fixing judgments that "blameless" is supposed to bracket — you can't pick the disabled renewal job out from the indefinitely many other omissions without already asking what someone should have done. So either the practice is less blameless than it claims, or it should stop describing omissions as causes at all. I don't hold this conclusion very confidently.

I'd appreciate comments on the following: whether I've represented Lewis, McGrath, and Moore accurately and fairly; whether the tension I'm drawing between blameless postmortems and causation by omission is a genuine one, rather than my conflating a causal question with a normative one somewhere; and whether my tentative conclusion actually follows, or whether there's a more defensible way of reconciling the two that I've missed.
