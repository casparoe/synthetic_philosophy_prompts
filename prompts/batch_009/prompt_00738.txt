I'm drafting a novel that includes an imagined scene in which David Hilbert — who in fact died in 1943 — lives long enough to be told of Paul Cohen's 1963 proof, completing work Gödel had begun in 1940, that the continuum hypothesis can be neither proved nor refuted from the standard (ZFC) axioms of set theory. Hilbert had placed Cantor's continuum problem first on his celebrated 1900 list, and built much of his public philosophy of mathematics around the claim that it admits no "ignorabimus": that every definite problem yields either a genuine answer or a proof that none is attainable, and that either outcome counts as knowledge — a conviction later carved, in its most quoted form, on his own tombstone: "We must know — we will know." Rather than simply have him look stunned, I want to reason the scene through properly: on Hilbert's own terms, would Cohen's result count as exactly the sort of "impossibility proof" he had always treated as a satisfying resolution, or does it undercut something more basic in his optimism — the assumption that a question like the continuum hypothesis has a determinate answer for a proof to find in the first place?
