I'm on my school's Ethics Bowl team, and one of our cases this year is about climate policy and what (if anything) we owe to future generations. While we were prepping, our coach introduced us to Derek Parfit's "nonidentity problem," and it's been bugging me ever since. As I understand it, the argument goes roughly like this: if a country picks a resource-depleting policy over a conservation-minded one, the specific people living generations from now won't just be worse off than they could have been — they'll be entirely different people, because even small policy differences ripple out into who meets whom, when couples have children, and so on. That seems to mean we can't say we've "harmed" those future individuals by choosing the worse policy, since under the better policy those exact people would never have existed at all. Our coach mentioned that this kind of puzzle didn't really get formulated until the twentieth century, which got me wondering how someone writing long before Parfit would have handled it if the setup were explained to them.

Purely by coincidence, in my philosophy elective we'd just finished a unit on John Locke's theory of personal identity from An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, where he argues that being the same "person" over time is a matter of continuous consciousness (roughly, memory connecting you to earlier selves), and that this is a completely different question from being the same "man" (the same living organism) or the same underlying soul or "substance." When I brought the nonidentity problem up with a couple of teammates, one of them pointed out that the whole argument seems to assume a different sperm-and-egg pairing automatically produces a different "person" — and that this is exactly the kind of assumption Locke's distinction might complicate, since for him personhood tracks consciousness rather than biological origin, and it's not obvious that a not-yet-conceived, not-yet-conscious being even falls under his theory of personal identity at all.

So here's my actual question: reasoning strictly from Locke's own framework, how do you think he would have responded to the nonidentity problem? Does his person/man/substance distinction give him a principled way to resist the assumption that different timing of conception means a different person, or does his view end up committed to something close to Parfit's picture anyway, just for different reasons? I'd appreciate it if you could reconstruct his likely reasoning step by step rather than just asserting a verdict, and if you think his framework genuinely wasn't built to handle a question like this, I'd rather you say so honestly than paper over the gap.
