Ok, this has been rattling around in my head and I want an outside take. I write romantasy, three books in, and I'm trying to decide whether to put my next one into Kindle Unlimited, where instead of getting paid per copy sold you get a slice of a big monthly pot based on how many individual pages a reader actually gets through. I joined an indie-author forum to learn the ropes and it's been a bit of an eye opener — theres a whole folk science built around "pages read" that isn't really about writing a good book, it's about writing a book that generates a lot of countable pages. People swear by splitting one story into a trilogy of thinner volumes instead of a single novel (readers auto-borrow the next one, so you rack up totals across three transactions instead of one), front-loading your strongest material into the first few chapters even if the back half sags, and engineering chapter-ending beats specifically so nobody closes the app for the night.

One old-timer on the forum mentioned that years back, when Amazon just paid a flat fee the moment someone read past 10% of a book, that same forum was full of people writing suspiciously short novellas for the opposite reason, since that made the 10% mark trivial to hit. Amazon eventually rewrote the rule to kill that trick, and now here we are with a whole new set of workarounds for the replacement rule, which kind of makes me wonder if this is just how its always going to go.

My writing partner thinks I'm overthinking it, that every format has always bent writers toward whatever the medium rewards, radio serial writers hit their cliffhanger right before the ad break, Dickens padded things out because he got paid by the installment, so this is nothing new. Maybe she's right. But something in my gut says it matters when the number being tracked and the thing I actually care about (telling a story that rewards someone's time) can come apart like that, and I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just me being precious about "commercial" writing. Is there an actual principled way to think about when shaping your work around a stand-in number is basically fine versus when it quietly hollows out the very thing the number was meant to track? I'd like something more useful than "just write a good book and trust the process," because for plenty of the people on that forum, that's clearly not how it's playing out.
