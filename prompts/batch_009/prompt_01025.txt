My grandma died last month and the funeral was a full Catholic mass, which I hadn't sat through in something like fifteen years since I stopped believing any of it. Standing there mumbling along to the Creed and the hymns, I found myself actually moved — like genuinely moved, not just "sad because grandma's gone" moved — even though if you'd pulled me aside mid-service and asked "do you literally think there's a bodily resurrection happening" I'd have said no, obviously not. I mentioned this to my friend Priya (she's a few years into a philosophy PhD) and she said it sounded exactly like the thing Wittgenstein was on about in his lectures on religious belief, and sent me the text. I've spent the last few weeks reading it plus some stuff written around it, and I tried writing up my own take on the whole thing, though its really more like structured notes than an actual essay at this point. Before I inflict it on anyone else I'd love your eyes on it, especially on whether I'm getting Wittgenstein right or just retrofitting him to justify my own weird feelings at a funeral.

Here's the gist of what I've got so far: Wittgenstein has this example (from the 1938 lectures — so really just students' notes, he never wrote this stuff up himself) of a guy who believes in the Last Judgment, and the point is that the belief doesn't show up as some highly-confident empirical forecast, the way you'd treat a scientist's prediction. It shows up instead in how it regulates the guy's whole life, what he risks and gives up because of the picture, even while by ordinary standards of evidence its totally ungrounded. And Wittgenstein says that if I don't share the belief, that doesn't straightforwardly mean I believe the negation of what the believer believes — he says there's "an enormous gulf between us" rather than a normal factual disagreement where we're at least fighting over the same claim. That felt like it mapped onto my Creed experience: the words were operating as something more like a picture I was inhabiting for an hour than a set of propositions I was asserting or denying.

Where it gets complicated is that when I ran this by Priya she pushed back hard, saying this is basically the view Kai Nielsen went after in his 1967 "Wittgensteinian Fideism" paper — that carving out religious language as its own self-contained game insulates it from any outside rational or moral criticism, so you couldn't even say a religious practice was false, let alone call it harmful, since it was never making the kind of claim that could be false in the first place. Digging into this more, I found that D.Z. Phillips, who's the guy most associated with actually defending this kind of reading, explicitly refuses the "fideist" label and insists internal criticism of religious belief is still possible. I also stumbled on a newer move by Duncan Pritchard, "quasi-fideism," that ties all this to the "hinge" propositions from On Certainty — the idea that basic religious convictions are arational hinge commitments just like everyday ones (e.g. "the earth has existed for a long time"), so they're not epistemically worse off than ordinary belief. I like this a lot, which makes me suspicious of myself for liking it, since On Certainty is a totally different text, written over a decade later, about a totally different problem.

So concretely, could you tell me: (1) whether the Last Judgment stuff above is a fair rendering of what's actually in the lecture notes, or whether I've quietly swapped in Phillips's gloss and am calling it "Wittgenstein," (2) whether my one-line dismissal of the Nielsen worry (basically just citing that Phillips rejects the label) actually answers the substantive objection about immunity from moral criticism, or if that's a total dodge, (3) whether reaching for Pritchard and On Certainty is a legit extension or textually irresponsible, and (4) generally where this is boring, confused, or needs a better example than a funeral I'm clearly not over yet. Accuracy comments matter way more to me here than "nice writing" comments.
