I spent nearly forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and I have lately been filling my days with philosophy of physics, a subject I wish I had discovered decades ago. Enclosed is a short essay I have drafted on the substantivalism/relationism debate, starting from Newton's bucket but trying to follow the question into general relativity, where my instincts as a physicist and my still-shaky grasp of the philosophical literature seem to pull in different directions. I would value your comments, particularly on:

1. Whether I have represented the "hole argument" correctly, since I am relying on secondary summaries rather than the original sources;
2. Whether treating frame-dragging as evidence relevant to a metaphysical conclusion is a piece of naturalistic overreach; and
3. Whether the position I land on at the end is a genuine, previously articulated view, or an unstable hybrid of two views that don't actually fit together.

I would also welcome any other reactions, including to points I haven't thought to ask about.

Newton's Bucket and the Trouble with Rotation

Newton's bucket argument is simple enough to state from memory. A bucket of water is set spinning; eventually the water rotates with it, and its surface grows concave. The concavity tracks the water's rotation relative to something, but not relative to the bucket: the water is stillest relative to the bucket right at the start, when the surface is flat, and rotates with zero relative motion exactly when the concavity is greatest. Newton took this to show that the relevant rotation is relative to space itself, conceived as a substance, since no material body will do the job.

Leibniz's relationist reply, that space is nothing over and above the relations among bodies, handles ordinary translation nicely: a universe shifted three feet to the left is not a distinct possibility, merely a redescription of the same one. Rotation is a harder case, and I don't think Leibniz ever quite explained why the water climbs the walls of the bucket if there is no space for it to rotate relative to. Mach's proposal, that the relevant relation is to the bulk of matter in the universe rather than to an unobservable container, struck me on first encountering it as exactly the move a physicist would want to make.

What I hadn't appreciated is that general relativity pulls on this tidy Machian picture from two directions at once. It offers some vindication: a rotating shell of mass genuinely drags inertial frames around with it, and Brill and Cohen's old calculation shows that as the shell's radius approaches its Schwarzschild radius, a gyroscope inside is dragged around at the shell's own angular velocity — "perfect dragging." But it also offers a counterexample: a lone rotating black hole in an otherwise empty universe still drags frames around it in vacuum, with no other matter for the rotation to be relative to, which suggests that whatever does the dragging is a structure in its own right. And quite apart from either result, there is a purely formal difficulty — the "hole argument," originating with Einstein and revived by philosophers in the 1980s — to the effect that identifying spacetime with a manifold whose points carry their identities independently of the metric leads to a bizarre indeterminism, since a suitable diffeomorphism yields a distinct-looking solution that agrees with the original everywhere outside some region and disagrees inside it.

Weighing all three considerations, I have tentatively landed here: spacetime is a substance, but the substance in question is the dynamical metric field itself, not a bare manifold of self-identifying points, and that field's points do not carry their identity across different solutions independently of the field's own structure. This seems to preserve the moral of frame-dragging — there is something for rotation to be relative to, beyond the matter distribution — without inheriting the indeterminism that cruder substantivalisms face. But I suspect I may simply be re-deriving, clumsily, a position that already has a name and a long list of objections in a literature I haven't read closely enough yet.
