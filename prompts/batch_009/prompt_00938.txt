I'm writing a piece on the history of the "arrow of time" and have been rereading the chapter in which the astrophysicist Arthur Eddington first coined that phrase: "The Running-Down of the Universe," from his 1928 book The Nature of the Physical World. Eddington builds his case with the example of a falling stone, whose organized, parallel molecular motion is scattered into random heat on impact; from this he concludes that "organisation cannot be brought about by continued shuffling," which is why the stone's own heat energy can never lift it back to where it fell from. Immediately afterward, though, he adds the following passage, which I haven't been able to reconcile with what came before:

"Here a point arises which unfortunately has no analogy in the shuffling of a pack of cards. No one (except a conjurer) can throw two half-shuffled packs into a hat and draw out one pack in its original order and one pack fully shuffled. But we can and do put partly disorganised energy into a steam-engine, and draw it out again partly as fully organised energy of motion of massive bodies and partly as heat-energy in a state of still worse disorganisation. Organisation of energy is negotiable, and so is the disorganisation or random element; disorganisation does not for ever remain attached to the particular store of energy which first suffered it, but may be passed on elsewhere. We cannot here enter into the question why there should be a difference between the shuffling of energy and the shuffling of material objects; but it is necessary to use some caution in applying the analogy on account of this difference."

What I can't square is how the second half of that passage is consistent with the rule Eddington had just finished establishing -- that "organisation cannot be brought about by continued shuffling." If disorganisation can be siphoned off "elsewhere" and organised energy drawn back out again via a steam-engine, why doesn't the same maneuver let you un-shuffle a deck of cards, or restore the stone's original organized motion? Is the distinction Eddington draws between "the shuffling of energy" and "the shuffling of material objects" a genuine physical distinction -- and if so, what is it, stated in terms a modern reader would recognize as correct statistical mechanics -- or is he glossing over a real gap in the argument at exactly the point where it matters most? I'd like to get this right in the piece, so I could use help disentangling the physics from the rhetoric here.
