Write a dialogue between Maimonides and Nahmanides on the purpose of animal sacrifice in the Torah. Maimonides should defend the view he develops in the Guide of the Perplexed (III:32 and III:46): that sacrifice is a divine concession to the idolatrous habits the Israelites brought with them out of Egypt, a pedagogical half-measure meant to wean them toward the truer worship of prayer. Nahmanides should press the objections from his commentary on Leviticus 1:9 — that Noah's and Abel's sacrifices, which Scripture describes as pleasing to God, predate any idolatry there was to react against, and that placing on God's altar precisely those animals the pagans revered would seem to honor rather than discredit them — and should also ask why Maimonides' own legal code still expects sacrifice to resume under the messiah, if it is merely a concession to a primitive stage of religious life. Each should engage the other's actual arguments rather than restate his own position, and Nahmanides should gesture at, without fully spelling out, the deeper intrinsic significance he thinks the rite really carries.
