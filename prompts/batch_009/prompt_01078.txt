I've spent a lot of time on LessWrong and adjacent blogs over the years, and one thing that has always nagged at me is how casually people invoke notions like "Schelling point" and "common knowledge" as though the philosophical work behind them were fully settled. So I recently sat down and read David Lewis's Convention properly, instead of just absorbing the secondhand version, and it left me wanting to push on something more specific than the usual potted summary.

Lewis defines a convention as a coordination equilibrium sustained by mutual expectations, and part of the cleverness of the book is showing that these expectations needn't bottom out in an actually-completed infinite regress of "I believe that you believe that I believe…"; instead he grounds them in a shared "basis" — some state of affairs everyone has independent reason to believe holds, which "indicates" to everyone both the regularity in question and the fact that everyone else has reason to believe the basis holds too. That's a neat way to dissolve the regress for something like which side of the road to drive on. What I can't work out is how far this machinery travels once you move from mere coordination to norms with real normative bite — the kind whose violation earns you resentment or guilt, not just a coordination failure. Bicchieri's well-known move of layering "normative expectations" (believing others expect, and may sanction, your compliance) on top of Lewis's "empirical expectations" still seems to stay entirely inside a self-interested, expectation-tracking framework. But then there's the Fehr and Gächter finding that subjects will pay to punish free-riders in one-shot games against strangers they've been told they will never interact with again — something their own data suggests is driven by anger at the defector rather than any expectation-based calculation about future reputation. That looks like exactly the sort of case a Lewis-style account struggles with, and it's the kind of thing people working from an evolutionary angle — Trivers on moralistic aggression, Boyd and Richerson on the cultural evolution of costly punishment, Gibbard's norm-expressivism — treat as evidence that something Lewis's apparatus doesn't have is doing real work.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two people who have clearly both read all of this and disagree about exactly that question — say, the two stragglers left at the end of a reading group on the evolution of cooperation, still arguing after everyone else has gone home. One of them thinks the Lewis-plus-Bicchieri picture can be extended all the way to genuine normativity, sanctions and all, without ever leaving rational-choice territory; the other thinks no amount of iterating expectations gets you from "people expect X and prefer to conform" to the distinctive sense that a violator owed you better, and that this requires positing evolved moral sentiments that are not just further preferences over coordination outcomes. Please make both of them genuinely sharp, with neither serving as a strawman for the other, and have them actually engage each other's strongest moves rather than restate their own position — I'd specifically want the Lewisian to have a real answer to the Fehr-and-Gächter-style challenge, and the other side to engage with whatever the best rational-choice comeback actually is, rather than just asserting that sentiment must be doing the work. They needn't reach agreement, but I would like the exchange to end with both of them clear on exactly where the disagreement bottoms out.
