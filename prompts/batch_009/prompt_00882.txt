After a long, unresolved argument with a friend over dinner, I would like a more rigorous verdict than we managed to reach ourselves. The disagreement began when I invoked I. J. Good's 1967 theorem that a Bayesian decision-maker who will act on the results of an inquiry has no reason to refuse free, relevant evidence, since its expected value is provably non-negative. My friend countered with Jack Hirshleifer's 1971 argument that the public arrival of information can be socially harmful, since it can destroy insurance arrangements that were only viable while everyone remained ignorant of who would eventually win or lose. She treated this as a direct counterexample to Good's theorem, while I suspected the two claims are simply about different things and do not really conflict. Could you carefully compare what each result actually asserts, identify the precise assumption or shift in scope that keeps them from contradicting one another, and say which of us was closer to being right?
