I've spent a long time on LessWrong and its offshoots, where a recurring move is "tabooing" a contested word and restating your claim without it, just to see whether a dispute was ever about anything besides definitions. Revisiting Bertrand Russell's 1905 paper "On Denoting" recently, I was struck by how much of that same spirit is already present in his theory of descriptions. Rather than treating "the present King of France is bald" as a subject-predicate sentence that attributes baldness to some entity denoted by "the present King of France" (and then having to decide what happens to its truth or even its meaningfulness when no such King exists, in the manner of Meinong's non-existent objects), Russell paraphrases the whole sentence into a conjunction of existence, uniqueness, and predication claims: something is presently King of France, at most one thing is, and that thing is bald. Since nothing satisfies the description, the sentence comes out false rather than meaningless, and the description itself dissolves under analysis into what Russell calls an "incomplete symbol," with no meaning of its own apart from the whole sentence it occurs in.

What I'd like help with is placing this against Keith Donnellan's 1966 paper "Reference and Definite Descriptions," which I only read afterward and found genuinely unsettling for how tidy I'd assumed the Russellian picture was. Donnellan's referential/attributive distinction, illustrated by his well-known case of someone asking "Who is the man drinking a martini?" at a party about a man everyone can see is actually drinking water, suggests that descriptions are sometimes used merely to direct a listener's attention to a particular individual the speaker has in mind, with the descriptive content almost dispensable, rather than to say something true of whoever uniquely satisfies it. My understanding is that Kripke later tried to rescue the Russellian analysis in "Speaker's Reference and Semantic Reference" by arguing that this is a fact about how speakers use language on particular occasions (pragmatics) rather than about the semantic content of "the F" itself, which he thought could remain thoroughly Russellian.

Could you compare Russell's theory of descriptions with Donnellan's referential/attributive distinction in some depth, including how the Kripkean semantics/pragmatics rejoinder is supposed to defuse it? I'm especially interested in whether Donnellan's cases strike you as a genuine semantic counterexample to Russell, or whether they're fully absorbable into a pragmatic story that leaves Russell's underlying semantics untouched, and in which respects — handling negative existentials, identity statements, or scope interactions with modal and intensional operators, for instance — Russell's original quantificational analysis remains distinctively powerful even if it turns out not to be the whole story about how definite descriptions function in ordinary conversation.
