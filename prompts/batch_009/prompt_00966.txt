A few of us on my team — mostly backend and ML engineers with essentially no formal humanities background — run an informal lunchtime reading group we half-jokingly call "philosophy for engineers." This month's thread started from an argument about emergent behavior in multi-agent reinforcement learning (the way surprisingly coordinated group-level strategies can fall out of very simple per-agent reward functions) and ended, a dozen Wikipedia tabs later, at John Stuart Mill's 1843 "A System of Logic," which apparently contains one of the founding statements of what's now called methodological individualism in the social sciences, in contrast to the more holist views later associated with people like Durkheim. I'd appreciate some help working through the passage that got us stuck, which opens Book VI, Chapter VII of the book, "Of the Chemical, or Experimental, Method in the Social Science":

"The laws of the phenomena of society are, and can be, nothing but the laws of the actions and passions of human beings united together in the social state. Men, however, in a state of society are still men; their actions and passions are obedient to the laws of individual human nature. Men are not, when brought together, converted into another kind of substance, with different properties; as hydrogen and oxygen are different from water, or as hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, and azote, are different from nerves, muscles, and tendons. Human beings in society have no properties but those which are derived from, and may be resolved into, the laws of the nature of individual man. In social phenomena the Composition of Causes is the universal law."

Here's what confused us. A few hundred pages earlier, in Book III, Mill introduces "Composition of Causes" as a specific technical label for just one of two ways causes can combine: a "mechanical" pattern, where the joint effect is simply the sum of what each cause would have produced acting alone, versus a "chemical" pattern, where the combination yields a genuinely new effect that you couldn't have predicted from the ingredients' separate properties. The illustration he uses for that chemical, non-additive pattern is the very same hydrogen-oxygen-water example he recycles above to describe what society is supposedly not like. So when he then declares that social phenomena obey "the universal law" of Composition of Causes, is he really committing to the claim that group-level social phenomena never have anything like an emergent, "chemical" character — that they always reduce, without residue, to individual causes simply added together? That seems like a much stronger and stranger thesis than the "explanations should ultimately bottom out in individuals" idea I'd associated with methodological individualism before reading this. Am I misreading the passage, and either way, how would you characterize the relationship between what Mill seems to be asserting here and the more familiar, modern version of methodological individualism that gets traced back to him — and, if it's not too much of a stretch, whether the same tension shows up when people today argue about whether "emergent" behavior in multi-agent systems is really more than the sum of the individual agents' learned policies?
