I recently joined the trading team at a small prediction-market startup, where a good part of my day involves scanning our order books for what everyone here calls "Dutch books": combinations of prices across a market's contracts that would let anyone lock in a riskless profit no matter how the event resolves. The term rang a bell from an undergraduate formal epistemology course I took years ago, where the same basic idea was used to argue that a rational agent's credences must obey the probability axioms, on pain of being exploitable by a sufficiently cunning bookie. Digging back through my old notes to reconnect the two, I've realized that what I was taught as "the" Dutch book argument was really only the synchronic version, targeting an agent's credences at a single moment, and that there is a distinct diachronic version -- originally due, I believe, to David Lewis, and first reported in print by Paul Teller -- meant to justify updating one's credences over time specifically by conditionalization, rather than by some other rule.

I'd like to understand how seriously the diachronic argument should be taken relative to its synchronic sibling, since on the surface they look like the same maneuver -- find a combination of bets that guarantees a loss -- just run across time instead of at an instant. Is that appearance misleading? My hazy recollection is that the diachronic version needs some extra assumption the synchronic one doesn't, something about the agent being committed in advance to a specific updating plan, and that it has consequently met with substantially more skepticism in the literature. Could you compare the two arguments properly and tell me whether that impression is accurate, or whether I'm overstating the asymmetry between them? I'd like to get this right, partly out of curiosity and partly because I've taken to telling my coworkers that our arbitrage-hunting is basically applied Bayesian epistemology, and I should probably find out whether that line survives contact with the literature.
