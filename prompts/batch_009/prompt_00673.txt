For the past couple of months I've been reading through rationalist and AI-safety blogs in my spare time, and one idea that keeps resurfacing is "acausal trade": the claim that two agents with absolutely no way of causally affecting each other — because they occupy separate branches of a multiverse, say, or separate simulated timelines, or because one is simply a very accurate model that the other is running of it internally — can still end up cooperating, purely because each can predict how the other reasons and acts accordingly. As I understand it, nothing is communicated and no history is shared between them; the whole arrangement rests on each party's decision-making being predictable enough to the other that honoring an implicit bargain becomes the right move anyway, similar to how in Newcomb's problem it's supposedly rational to one-box even though the money was already placed in the boxes before you ever chose.

Purely by coincidence, and just because I enjoy old philosophy books, I've also been slowly working through Leibniz's Monadology, and one part of it has been nagging at me ever since I noticed the overlap. Leibniz says monads are completely sealed off from each other causally — "windowless," as he puts it, so that nothing can ever pass between them — yet the universe still unfolds in perfect coordination because God arranged a "pre-established harmony" among all of them from the moment of creation, so that every monad's internal states track everyone else's without any actual interaction ever taking place. I mentioned this to a friend who studied some philosophy in college, and she said I was probably just pattern-matching on a surface resemblance, but I haven't been able to talk myself out of the idea that both stories are trying to answer the same basic puzzle: how can separate things end up perfectly coordinated with no causal channel between them at all?

What I'd like from you is a real answer, not a hand-wave. If Leibniz were somehow shown the modern idea of acausal trade, exactly as decision theorists describe it, do you think he would recognize it as basically a version of his own system, just with the coordination achieved by mutual prediction between the parties themselves instead of by God arranging everything in advance? Or would he insist the two ideas are actually incompatible, or at least far less alike than they first appear, once you take seriously how his system actually works — things like the principle of sufficient reason, the idea that each monad's complete concept already contains everything that will ever happen to it, and his particular views on contingency, possible worlds, and compossibility? I'd rather you really engage with the mechanics of his metaphysics than just repeat the one-paragraph version of "pre-established harmony" that shows up in every summary.
