This term I have been taking a graduate seminar that moved from Nelson Goodman's "grue" puzzle directly into Saul Kripke's discussion of rule-following in his book on Wittgenstein, and by the end of that unit I had more or less convinced myself that the two are essentially the same argument dressed up in different examples. My professor resisted this comparison when I raised it with her after class, though she did not have time to explain her objection in full, and I would like to understand whether she was right to push back.

Goodman's puzzle begins from the observation that the predicate "grue" (true of an emerald just in case it is green and examined before some future date t, or blue and examined only after t) fits every observation of green emeralds we have ever made exactly as well as the predicate "green" does; since our past evidence is equally compatible with both predicates, nothing in the data alone tells us which one we should project onto emeralds we have not yet examined. Kripke's skeptic, reconstructing an argument he attributes to Wittgenstein, seems to run the very same trick on arithmetic: he invents a deviant function "quus," which is stipulated to agree with ordinary addition for any pair of numbers small enough that I have actually performed the calculation before, but to yield 5 for a pair like 68 and 57, which he assumes is larger than any sum I have previously computed by hand, and then asks what fact about my past use of "+" could rule out that I meant quus all along rather than plus. Both arguments seem to reduce to exactly the same structural point: a finite stretch of past data underdetermines which of infinitely many rules is the "correct" one to extend into new cases, and some deviant alternative always fits the evidence just as well as the intended rule did. Given this, I am struggling to see what is left in Kripke's version of the argument that is not already present in Goodman's, and I would appreciate a clear account of whether there is a genuine philosophical difference between the two puzzles, or whether my professor's hesitation was really just misplaced caution.
