hey, working on a piece about Victorian-era scientists who took the paranormal dead serious, and I keep circling back to Henry Sidgwick — the guy who wrote the Methods of Ethics, basically the driest, most hyper-careful utilitarian text of the 19th century, and who was also the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research. Found this bit from his second presidential address to the SPR (Dec 1882, Proceedings vol. 1) and it's bugging me:

"if doubters of telepathy will not yield to half-a-dozen decisive experiments by investigators of trained intelligence and hitherto unquestioned probity, let us try to give them half-a-dozen more recorded by other witnesses; if a dozen will not do, let us try to give them more; if a score will not do, let us make up the tale to fifty. The time and trouble will not be thrown away if only we can attain the end."

(earlier that same year, in his inaugural address, he'd already called it "a scandal" that "the educated world, as a body" remained in "the attitude of incredulity" despite the testimony of "so many competent witnesses.")

What I cant quite pin down is what's actually supposed to be doing the persuading in that "fifty" passage. Is Sidgwick just assuming the skeptics are being stubborn and will eventually cave to sheer accumulation of instances, or is there an actual epistemic argument buried in there — something like, each fresh replication by a new batch of "unquestioned probity" witnesses independently drives down the odds of fraud or error, so a rational doubter's confidence has to erode as the pile grows? And if that's the argument, does it actually survive the fact that these witnesses weren't neutral (a lot of them came out of a society founded specifically to find this stuff) and that the experiments may not have been independent of each other at all? Basically: is this a legit early instinct toward something like replication as an answer to skepticism, or is he dressing up a bad statistical intuition in confident Victorian prose? Curious what you make of it.
