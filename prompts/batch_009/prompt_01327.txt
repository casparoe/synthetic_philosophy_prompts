Prepping for my social ontology seminar this week and I've argued myself into a corner I can't get out of. Both Searle (in the "Collective Intentions and Actions" paper) and Gilbert (going back to On Social Facts) want we-intentions, or joint commitments, to be conceptually primitive -- you can't cash "we intend to X" out into some combination of individual I-intentions plus mutual beliefs about each other's plans, a la Bratman. Fine so far. But it seems to me that once you say the content just isn't decomposable into individual intentional states, even in principle, you've quietly given up on the intention being had by any individual -- there's no single person you could point to and say "there, thats the seat of the we-intention" -- so the real subject of the state has to be the group itself, over and above its members. Which sounds like exactly the kind of spooky Durkheimian holism that gives social ontology a bad name among the naturalistically-minded people down the hall in the science building.

So, am I right that Gilbert basically bites this bullet openly with her plural subject talk (all that "pool of wills" business), while Searle wants the same irreducibility about content but keeps insisting -- implausibly, on my reading -- that it's all still happening inside individual skulls? I've got a room of first-years walking into this on Thursday and would rather not just wave my hands at them. Any help sorting out how much metaphysics we're actually committed to here would be great.
