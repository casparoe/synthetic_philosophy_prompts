Random request, but you might be perfect for this. I'm slowly assembling a collection of short stories that each hang off one real philosopher's idea rather than a full biography — just a spark, not a lecture. This week I fell down a rabbit hole on Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge mathematician/philosopher who more or less invented modern subjective probability theory and did a bunch of foundational work in economics and logic before dying at 26, and who reportedly used to run intellectual circles around Wittgenstien. In 1925 he read a talk to a Cambridge discussion society, later published as "Epilogue," where he gives his own answer to the meaning-of-life question, and its not the answer I expected going in.

He doesn't do the defiant-Camus thing of making meaning in an indifferent universe. He basically argues the whole setup is confused — that size and duration were never relevant to value to begin with. He says his picture of the world is "drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale," so the stars end up smaller than the people in his foreground, and even the eventual heat-death of the universe has a present value "at compound discount" that's basically nothing. Then, oddly, he closes by telling a depressed friend that he (Ramsey) has reason on his side and the friend doesn't, while admitting in the same breath that neither of their feelings actually "correspond to the fact." It's a strange mix of ice-cold logic and weirdly cheerful temperament, and I can't decide if it's the most sensible or the most glib thing I've ever read about mortality.

Could you write me a short story built around that specific move, rather than the generic "vast cosmos, small defiant human" story everybody's already written a hundred times? I don't want it to be about Ramsey himself, biographical fiction feels like cheating for this project — give me some other character, any time period or setting you like, who genuinely runs into this argument (found in an old book, overheard at a hospital bedside, whatever works) and has to sit with how cold and how strangely comforting it is at the same time. If the ending doesn't just resolve into tidy reassurance, and keeps a little of that unsettling "I have reason and you don't" aftertaste instead, even better.
