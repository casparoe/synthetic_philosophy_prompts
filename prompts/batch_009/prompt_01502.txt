I was out way too late last night arguing with my friend Priya about this whole longevity/anti-aging scene — the Bryan Johnson "Blueprint" stuff, rapamycin, all these people trying to biohack their way out of aging entirely. She thinks its basically just responsible healthcare taken to its logical extreme, and I don't see whats wrong with wanting more healthy years either, but I kept arguing there's something almost off about refusing to let your body just age, like your fighting the whole premise of what a life is supposed to look like. We went back and forth for like two hours and never actually resolved anything.

At some point I brought up Cicero's De Senectute, which I had to read for a class ages ago — the dialogue where old Cato lays out the four standard complaints people have about old age (it pulls you away from active life, it weakens the body, it robs you of your pleasures, and it's basically a waiting room for death) and then spends the rest of the book knocking each one down. There's that bit near the end where he compares a full life to a play and says nature, like a decent playwright, wouldn't botch the last act, and another bit where he says old men dying is like ripe fruit dropping off a tree on its own, versus young men dying being like unripe fruit torn off by force. Priya and I couldn't agree on whether any of that even applies to a world with anti-aging medicine, since obviously Cicero never had to imagine someone seriously trying to stay biologically 35 forever.

So here's what I actually want to know: based on the specific arguments Cato/Cicero makes in that text — not just a vague "ancient Roman guy would probably distrust technology" vibe — what do you think he'd say about the modern longevity movement, i.e. people seriously trying to extend healthy lifespan indefinitely or reverse aging altogether? Would he see it as a natural continuation of the whole cultivation-of-mind-and-character project he's so big on, or would it undercut his own argument, since half his rebuttals depend on old age being a distinct and valuable stage of life with its own proper compensations, not just a defect to be engineered away? I'd like an answer that actually wrestles with his specific reasoning (the four complaints, the play, the fruit) rather than just gesturing at "stoic acceptance of nature."
