I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm currently working on a feature about how courts and economists try to put a dollar figure on a human life. My hook is a wrongful-death case I've been following in which, unusually, liability is not in dispute—only damages are. The plaintiffs' economic expert wants the jury to award substantial money for what specialists in this area call "hedonic damages": compensation for the value of the years of life and experience the deceased was deprived of, above and beyond lost wages or medical costs. The defense's expert calls this category of damages confused at a more basic level: once someone is dead, he argues, there is no one left who could have those years back, and no subject who is worse off for having lost them, so paying out on this theory doesn't compensate any harm actually done to anyone. A philosopher I interviewed for background context remarked, almost in passing, that the defense is essentially running Epicurus's ancient argument that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, since by the time death has occurred, its supposed victim no longer exists to suffer it.

That offhand comment has led me into more of the literature than I expected. It seems philosophers have never treated Epicurus's argument as obviously settling the question. There are apparently real puzzles about exactly when, and for whom, a premature death could be bad, if it can be bad at all. There is also a curious asymmetry in how easily most of us accept the years before we were born as no loss whatsoever, even though the same reasoning would seem to cover them just as well as the years death takes from us later in life. I would like my article to do justice to what is genuinely at stake here rather than simply narrating dueling expert witnesses. Could you lay out the most important implications of this debate for how a dispute like this one should be argued, and perhaps even decided? I am especially curious whether taking the philosophical wrinkles seriously ends up favoring one side over the other, or whether it instead suggests that both sides are quietly relying on a notion of "loss" that doesn't hold up once examined closely.
