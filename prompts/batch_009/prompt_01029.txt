I'm a novelist working on a book whose two central characters, Ilse and Daniel, trained together as philosophers of physics before their careers took them in different directions: she now holds a professorship in a philosophy department, he works in the foundations group of a physics department across town. Both are, by reputation, formidable. In the chapter I'm drafting, they're reunited at a retirement dinner for their old advisor and afterward wander back into the department's teaching lab, where a battered old bucket-on-a-turntable — used for two generations of undergraduates to demonstrate Newton's argument for absolute space — still sits in a corner. Half nostalgically, half competitively, they end up spinning it, and their old graduate-school disagreement comes back to life: Daniel has remained a committed spacetime substantivalist, convinced that the concave curve of the water is evidence of spacetime itself as a substantial, dynamical thing against which true rotation is defined; Ilse became, and has stayed, a relationist, convinced that rotation, like all motion, must ultimately be cashed out in terms of relations between material bodies.

I'd like you to write their dialogue, but I want it to go well past the version of this debate you'd get in an introductory course. In particular, I want Ilse to be pressed on how uncomfortably Mach's own proposal — that inertia is fixed by the distribution of matter in the universe — actually sits with general relativity, even granting that phenomena like frame-dragging have since been experimentally confirmed; and I want Daniel to have to answer for the hole argument, the charge that if spacetime points are the kind of primitively individuated objects his view requires, general relativity ends up saddled with a baffling and empirically idle indeterminism. Neither of them should have a tidy answer ready to go, and neither should be allowed to simply out-argue the other and close the matter down: I want genuine back-and-forth, with each of them advancing the conversation by responding to what the other has actually just said, so that a reader who knows the material comes away unsure who, if anyone, got the better of the exchange.
