I teach a graduate seminar on conditionals, and this week's session is devoted to the indicative/counterfactual distinction. I like to open with Adams's well-worn minimal pair — "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" against "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" — since it shows so cleanly that no single truth-functional or strict-conditional reading of 'if' can be doing the same job in both sentences: the first is obviously true given what we know, the second is not. This year's cohort is unusually well versed in formal semantics, and when a colleague from linguistics sat in on my planning session, she pushed back on the tidy moral I usually draw from the example. She wants to insist that 'if' names one semantic mechanism throughout, with the indicative/counterfactual contrast reducible to a difference in how context constrains that mechanism; I'm inclined to think the two constructions come apart at a deeper level, once you factor in how badly probability behaves for one of them and not the other.

Before I revise my slides, I would like your help thinking this through properly. Please compare indicative and counterfactual conditionals, addressing in particular: why the material and strict conditional analyses fail for both constructions; how Stalnaker's selection-function semantics purports to give a single formal treatment of both, differentiated only by a pragmatic constraint tying the selection function to the conversational context set; and how Adams's thesis about the probability of indicative conditionals, together with Lewis's triviality results, complicates that unified picture — including what Lewis himself actually concluded about the semantics of 'if' from his own triviality result, as distinct from the more deflationary morals that Adams, Gibbard, and Edgington drew from the same result. I would like you to close by taking a side: is the kinship between indicative and counterfactual 'if' best explained, à la Stalnaker, as one mechanism wearing two pragmatically distinct hats, or do the triviality results show that the two constructions are, at bottom, different kinds of device that only happen to share a word?
