Last night a friend and I argued for hours about moral luck, using the standard case of two equally drunk, equally reckless drivers: one's road happens to be empty and he gets home without incident, the other's has a child dart into it and he kills her. I don't think the second driver is more blameworthy than the first, only more unfortunate; she was adamant that he is, and argued that Kant had already settled the question in the Groundwork by locating all moral worth in the will alone, making outcomes irrelevant to judgment. Checking the text, I found the passage below (Abbott's translation, corresponding to Kant's Ak. 4:393–394), and I'm not convinced it does what she claims:

"A good will is good not because of what it performs or effects, not by its aptness for the attainment of some proposed end, but simply by virtue of the volition; that is, it is good in itself, and considered by itself is to be esteemed much higher than all that can be brought about by it in favour of any inclination, nay even of the sum total of all inclinations. Even if it should happen that, owing to special disfavour of fortune, or the niggardly provision of a step-motherly nature, this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose, if with its greatest efforts it should yet achieve nothing, and there should remain only the good will (not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power), then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself. Its usefulness or fruitfulness can neither add nor take away anything from this value."

Could you help me work out exactly what Kant is doing in this passage, rather than just what he's famous for concluding elsewhere? I'm stuck on three things: (1) whether "it would still shine by its own light" is an argument for why only volition can be unconditionally good, or merely a restatement of that premise in image form; (2) what "the summoning of all means in our power" actually commits an agent to, and whether it would, say, excuse a driver who never even touched the brakes, provided his prior willing was sound; and (3) even granting that the passage insulates moral worth from resultant luck (how an action turns out), whether it does anything at all to address circumstantial or constitutive luck—the driver's luck in which road he was on, or in whatever upbringing and temperament produced a will that would or wouldn't swerve in time.
