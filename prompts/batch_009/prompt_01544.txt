I'm a software engineer working adjacent to ML, and I've been reading about RLHF and reward modeling. On a whim, I went back and actually read Plato's Euthyphro instead of relying on the one-line pop-philosophy summary of the dilemma I already knew, and I think there's a closer analogy to reward modeling lurking in the text than the usual gloss suggests. Below are notes toward a short essay making that case. I'd like your honest comments: is the analogy doing real philosophical work, or am I forcing a superficial pun on "grounding"? Where does it break down, and is there a better existing framing I'm overlooking?

Notes:

1. Euthyphro's first move is to define the pious as "whatever is loved by the gods." Socrates doesn't go straight for the famous priority question here — he first objects that the gods, on the traditional stories, disagree with each other constantly, so the same act could end up both loved (by some gods) and hated (by others), i.e., both pious and impious at once. Only after Euthyphro patches this by restricting the definition to what all the gods love unanimously does Socrates ask the classic question: is a thing loved by the gods because it's pious, or pious because it's loved by the gods?

2. That two-step structure maps unexpectedly well onto reward modeling. Step one (gods disagreeing) looks like the familiar problem that individual human raters disagree with each other about which model outputs are better, so "rated highly by a rater" isn't yet a stable target. The standard fix is structurally the same as Euthyphro's patch: aggregate over many raters (majority vote, preference models fit to pairwise comparisons, etc.) to get something closer to "loved by all the gods."

3. But then the second step still bites. Is an output good because the aggregated reward model scores it highly, or does the reward model score it highly because the output is already good (helpful, honest, harmless, or whatever the target property is supposed to be)? If it's the latter, the reward model is only ever tracking a downstream symptom of goodness rather than defining it, which sounds a lot like Socrates's later point that "being loved" is an effect of being pious, not identical to it. That, I think, is basically a philosophical way of restating Goodhart's law: once you optimize hard against the score itself, you can drive a wedge between the score and the property it was only ever a proxy for.

4. Where I'm least confident: is there a real analogue, on the ML side, of "piety itself" as an independent standard that the reward model is trying (and potentially failing) to track? Or does the analogy quietly smuggle in a kind of moral realism about "actually good outputs" that most people building these systems wouldn't sign up for? I can't tell if that's a genuine disanalogy or just a restatement of the alignment problem in fancier language.

I'd rather find out now if I'm overreaching with this parallel than after writing the whole thing up, so please be blunt about any weak points.
