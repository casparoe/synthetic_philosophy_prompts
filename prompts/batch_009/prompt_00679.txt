I'm a software engineer who spends most of my time these days on ML infrastructure, including building calibration checks for the probabilistic classifiers we ship — Brier scores, log-loss, reliability diagrams, and so on. Partly out of curiosity about where these "scoring rules" actually come from, I started reading about something philosophers call "accuracy-first epistemology," which turns out to repurpose exactly this kind of machinery for a very different project: arguing that rational credences must obey the axioms of probability. The core move, as best I can tell from Jim Joyce's 1998 paper, is a dominance argument: if your credences are not probabilistically coherent, there is always some coherent credence function that is strictly more accurate than yours in every possible world, where accuracy is measured by something like the Brier score. Since holding a position that is strictly dominated in this way seems obviously irrational — you'd be guaranteed to do worse than some available alternative no matter how the world turns out — the conclusion is supposed to be that probabilistic coherence is a requirement of rationality, not just a pragmatic convenience.

The more I dug into this, though, the shakier the argument started to look. Patrick Maher argued in 2002 that Joyce's case for restricting attention to certain "legitimate" measures of inaccuracy was not well supported, and offered a concrete counterexample: an "absolute value" measure of inaccuracy (roughly, the distance |credence − truth-value|, summed over propositions), which looks just as natural as the Brier score at first glance, but under which some incoherent credence functions turn out not to be dominated by anything at all, coherent or otherwise. Joyce's 2009 reply was to argue that the absolute-value measure isn't a legitimate accuracy measure in the first place because it fails to be "strictly proper" — very roughly, a proper measure can't recommend anything other than your own true credence as optimal by your own lights — and to re-derive the dominance result for the whole class of strictly proper measures rather than just the Brier score. That seemed like a reasonable fix until I came across references to an unpublished manuscript by Aaron Bronfman, with what looks like a related point made in a chapter of Richard Pettigrew's book on this topic: even once you restrict attention to strictly proper measures, which coherent credence function, if any, dominates a given incoherent one can still differ from measure to measure, so two measures that both satisfy propriety can disagree about whether, or by whom, a specific incoherent agent is dominated.

I'd like you to lay out and defend, as rigorously as you can, the position that Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism fails to establish that incoherent credences are irrational, even in its 2009, propriety-restricted form, because of this residual rule-relativity. Please explain concretely why the move to strict propriety doesn't by itself settle which particular measure, or family of measures, ought to be used to judge any given incoherent agent, and then engage with the strongest reply that a defender of the accuracy-first program could give — whether that involves some further structural constraint on legitimate accuracy measures, an argument that disagreements among proper measures are too rare or minor to undermine the conclusion, or a direct case that one measure, such as the Brier score, really is uniquely privileged — before explaining whether you think that reply actually rescues the argument.
