I am drafting a multigenerational novel centered on a besieged town whose rationing board chose to let a quarter of its people starve so that the rest might live. Through sheer contingency, that decision — and the particular marriages and conceptions that followed in the town's chaotic aftermath — is also the reason my present-day characters exist: a fairer rationing scheme would have spared the dead, but it would just as surely have produced different descendants, not healthier versions of the ones who were actually born. Before I write the scene where my protagonist reckons with this, I would like the philosophical case argued properly rather than assumed. Please write a dialogue between a person-affecting theorist and an impersonal-value theorist debating whether the rationing board's decision was bad for anyone alive today. The person-affecting theorist should deny that any living descendant was harmed by it, while facing the worry that this seems to excuse almost any injustice provided it is severe enough to change who gets conceived afterward. The impersonal-value theorist should insist the choice was simply worse, leaning on something like Parfit's Same Number Quality Claim (that when the same number of people will ever live, it is worse if they are worse off), and should in turn be pressed on whether that reasoning commits them to the Repugnant Conclusion. Both should be formidable; neither should concede outright, but each should close by naming the one point in the other's case they cannot fully answer.
