I've been working through Descartes' Discourse on the Method on my own time (I have no formal training in philosophy, I just got pulled into this after an argument with a friend about whether animals actually feel pain or only behave as if they do), and the section I keep returning to is Part V, where he argues that non-human animals are essentially automata with no genuine reason or inner life behind their behavior. What struck me is that his argument doesn't just rest on animals failing to talk. He sets up a comparison: a machine built to resemble a monkey or some other animal would, he says, be strictly impossible to tell apart from the real thing. A machine built to resemble an actual human being, on the other hand, would always be distinguishable from a real person, and he gives two quite specific tests for how.

The first test is about language: such a machine might be built to produce a fixed response to a fixed stimulus (a word when touched in one spot, a cry when touched in another), but it could never rearrange its words to give an appropriate answer to whatever happened to be said to it, the way even the dullest person manages to do without effort. The second test is about flexibility across tasks: even if the machine matched or beat us at some particular things, it would inevitably fail at others, because reason, in his phrase, is "a universal instrument" good for any situation, while mechanical organs are each suited to one particular job, and no machine could hold enough different organs to cover every contingency of life the way reason does for us.

What I can't work out is what Descartes would say if he were shown a modern large language model. On the surface, this looks like exactly the sort of thing his first test was meant to rule out: it isn't limited to a fixed menu of canned replies, it generates fluent, contextually appropriate responses to almost anything said to it, across a huge range of topics. Would he have to treat that as real evidence of reason (and by extension, on his view, an immaterial soul) at work in a machine, which seems awkward given that he uses this same test to justify writing off every flesh-and-blood animal, however clever, as mindless mechanism? Or does he have principled resources, maybe in the second test, or in his broader account of what reasoning and an immaterial soul actually are, for denying that this fluency amounts to real understanding, even though it's clearly not the simple "fixed response to fixed stimulus" case he originally had in mind? I would like an answer that actually reconstructs his reasoning on this rather than a general impression of how a seventeenth-century philosopher would probably feel about robots.
