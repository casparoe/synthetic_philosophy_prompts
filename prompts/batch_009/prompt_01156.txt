In "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (1943), Simone Weil argues that any political party can be identified by three structural features, independent of whatever program it professes: it is "a machine to generate collective passions"; it exerts organized pressure on its members' private judgment, so that members come to preface a view with "I, as a Socialist..." rather than simply thinking it through; and its one constant, if unstated, aim is its own growth without limit. On these grounds she calls for abolishing parties outright rather than reforming them, since she locates the defect in their structure rather than in any particular party's abuses.

Weil died in 1943, decades before anyone could be sorted into a political tribe without ever joining one. Engagement-maximizing recommendation algorithms now sort users into ideologically homogeneous audiences, reward the most inflammatory framing of any dispute, and are commercially bound to grow their user base and attention share without limit, all without membership, doctrine, or leadership. Working strictly from Weil's three criteria rather than from her general hostility to factionalism, argue for how she would judge such algorithmic sorting: as a party in every sense that matters to her argument, as something worse than any party, or as a phenomenon her argument does not actually reach, because it depends on a conscious, nameable act of subordinating one's own judgment to a collective.
