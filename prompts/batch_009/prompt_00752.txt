I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the new billionaire-funded push to defeat aging — Altos Labs' work on reprogramming cells back to a more youthful state, the cryonics outfits quietly signing up clients on the bet that the science will eventually catch up with the marketing. For a sidebar, I want to bring in a philosophical counterweight from about twenty-three centuries before any of this was conceivable: Zhuangzi. The popular image of him is the man who drummed on a basin and sang after his wife died, treating her death as just another turn of the seasons, and the traveler who used a skull as a pillow and, in a dream, was told by the skull that it wouldn't trade the freedom of being dead for a king's throne back among the living. Taken on its own, that would seem to make him a fairly hostile witness for the longevity industry.

But the more of the text I dig into, the less tidy the picture becomes. The same book gives us the "true man" who doesn't drown in water or scorch in fire and breathes from his heels rather than his throat, and, in another chapter, the "spirit man" of far-off Mount Guye, who eats no grain, lives on wind and dew, and rides the clouds beyond the four seas — imagery that later, more literal-minded strands of Daoism took as a warrant for their own very physical pursuit of immortality. There is also the dying Master Lai, who compares himself to metal in a smith's furnace and says it would be presumptuous of him to insist on being melted down and recast as exactly the same sword — a passage that seems to bear rather directly on any scheme, cryonic or digital, whose whole premise is getting the same person back.

So here is what I would like help thinking through, on the text's own terms rather than a generic "ancient wisdom counsels acceptance" gloss: if Zhuangzi were handed a cryonics contract, or a pitch for whole-brain emulation, which strand would actually win out — the equanimity that treats insistence on any particular form as the root problem, or the evident fascination with beings who have simply gotten much better at not dying? And would he have principled grounds, in his own terms, for treating cryonics and mind-uploading as different problems, or would both come down to the same mistake?
