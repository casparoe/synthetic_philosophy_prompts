I'm taking a seminar this term on social ontology that spent its first two weeks on the opening chapters of David Lewis's *Convention*, where he builds up to his official definition: a convention is a regularity in behavior that constitutes a coordination equilibrium in a recurring coordination problem, sustained by common knowledge that everyone conforms to it and expects everyone else to as well. The stock illustrations from lecture all share a feature Lewis leans on heavily: which side of the road we drive on, where to meet again if separated in a crowd, who calls back after a dropped call — in each case everyone prefers to conform given that others do, but which regularity gets settled on is arbitrary, since some other regularity would have served everybody just as well.

Last week we moved on to Lewis's later attempt, in "Languages and Language," to extend this apparatus to a much more loaded case: honesty. His move, as I understand it, is to concede that bare truthfulness isn't itself a convention in his technical sense, because there's no alternative regularity (say, a standing policy of lying) that would serve communication just as well if everyone conformed to it — so it fails his own arbitrariness clause. Instead, he proposes that what's conventional, and therefore arbitrary, is the choice of which language a population is truthful-and-trusting in, with the substantive obligation to be truthful simply riding along with whichever language gets chosen. A friend in the seminar thinks this is a clean solution: locate the coordination problem one level up, in the choice of language, and let the normatively loaded part piggyback on it for free. I suspect it's closer to a sleight of hand that relabels, rather than explains, an obligation Lewis's machinery was supposed to generate from scratch — and I doubt the same trick is available for norms like promise-keeping or honesty in non-linguistic dealings, where there's no convenient extra "which-language"-style variable to relocate the arbitrariness onto.

Could you write an essay that adjudicates this? Walk through the actual mechanics of Lewis's definition and his truthfulness-and-trust proposal rather than an impressionistic gloss on "convention," and use at least one concrete example to test whether his maneuver generalizes to other non-coordination norms or is a special-case dodge available only for language. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but don't end on a fence-sitting note: tell me, and defend, exactly how far a broadly Lewisian, coordination-based analysis of convention can be pushed to cover norms that are not themselves solutions to pure coordination problems.
