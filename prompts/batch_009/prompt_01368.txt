Ok this is going to sound like a weird rabbit hole but hear me out. I was arguing with a friend on a discord that's basically SSC/LW-adjacent about whether it even makes sense to say a group — a company, a movement, an "egregore" if you want to be cute about it — can intend or want something over and above what the individual members intend and want. That argument sent me chasing footnotes into the actual academic literature on collective intentionality, and I landed on the Bratman vs. Gilbert fight, which I dont think I've seen discussed anywhere outside a couple of philosophy-of-action syllabi.

As I understand it: Bratman thinks shared intention is fully reducible — "we intend to J" just IS a structure of interlocking individual intentions (I intend that we J partly by way of your intending it too, you likewise, our sub-plans mesh), plus the relevant mutual beliefs/common knowledge, plus ordinary norms of individual rationality like stability and means-end coherence. No extra ontological glue required. Gilbert thinks this can't be the whole story, because it can't explain the distinctively obligatory character of joint commitments — her classic case is two people out on a walk together (Jack and Sue in one paper, I've seen a Ned-and-Olive version elsewhere too), where if one of them just wanders off without warning, the other isn't merely disappointed the way you'd be if a friend flaked on individual plans, they're entitled to actually rebuke the other, because the two of them jointly committed to a single shared goal and thereby became, in her phrase, a "plural subject." Bratman's side has replied that you can earn those same demands/requirements out of the individualist picture too, without positing anything as metaphysically heavy as a primitive joint commitment or plural subject.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Bratmanian and a committed Gilbertian working through this? I'd love it if you literally staged it as the two of them out on a walk together (a little on-the-nose, I know), hashing out the rebuke case and the bigger question of whether Gilbert's holism is doing real explanatory work or whether Bratman's individualist machinery can get you all the same normative conclusions more cheaply. Please make both sides genuinely sharp and actually responsive to each other's specific moves rather than just re-asserting their own view back and forth — I want to feel the real force of both positions, since as far as I can tell this is a case where two very smart people stared at the same examples and just didn't converge.
