So at work we build software for warehouse robots (nothing crazy, just automated forklifts and picking arms), and last month one of them clipped a guy on the floor because of some edge case in the path-planning model that nobody had tested for. Nobody was badly hurt, but it kicked off this whole HR/legal fire drill about whose fault it actually was, and it sent me down a rabbit hole reading about self-driving car incidents (the 2018 Uber/Tempe one especially) and this idea philosophers apparently call the "responsibility gap" - basically the worry that once a system is adaptive/complex enough, you can't pin the blame on the programmer (they didn't specifically cause this exact outcome, it emerged from training and interacting with the world), you can't blame the company in any satisfying moral sense beyond "pay up," and obviously you can't blame the robot since it's not a moral agent. So supposedly nobody is really responsible, which bugged me, because SOMEONE has to be, right?

Then I found some stuff pushing back pretty hard on this, arguing it's not actually some brand new problem, and that we already have ways of dealing with exactly this kind of diffuse-cause harm (strict product liability, the way a company is on the hook for a self-driving car even without a human "at fault" in the moment, or how a commanding officer can be held responsible for a subordinate's actions they didn't directly order). I also saw someone bring up a seperate thing called the "retribution gap" - like, even if you DO manage to legally pin liability on somebody (say, the company has to pay out), that doesnt actually satisfy the human urge for there to be someone who did wrong and deserves blame or punishment, and that gap might be the harder one to actually close. Can you write me an essay that digs into this and comes down on one side: is the responsibility gap (and/or the retribution gap) a real, distinct problem that our current moral and legal categories genuinely can't handle, or is it basically old wine in a new bottle that strict liability / corporate responsibility / chain-of-command style thinking already solves? A conditional answer is fine (like "it's a real gap for autonomous weapons specifically but not for consumer self-driving cars") as long as you actually commit to something instead of just going "there are good points on both sides." And if you think we genuinely need some new category or policy to close the gap, give me a rough sketch of what that would actually look like.
