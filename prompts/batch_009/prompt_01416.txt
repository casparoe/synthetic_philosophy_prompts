My day job involves building and checking Kripke structures for model verification, which is how I first got curious about Saul Kripke the philosopher rather than just the formalism named after him. I've since read enough of Naming and Necessity to get the basic shape of Kripke's argument: proper names are rigid designators, picking out the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists, and Kripke extends this treatment to natural-kind terms like "water" and "gold" to explain how a statement such as "water is H2O" can be necessary even though it's knowable only a posteriori.

I'd like you to argue against that extension rather than explain it back to me. Specifically, construct the strongest case you can that treating natural-kind terms as rigid designators in the same sense as proper names is not well motivated, because "sameness of kind" across possible worlds isn't fixed independently of essentialist assumptions in the way "sameness of individual" is usually taken to be. The history of "jade" — which turned out, on closer chemical analysis, to cover two unrelated minerals, jadeite and nephrite — seems like a good test case for this worry, so feel free to use it. If the argument works, tell me what it does to the confidence with which claims like "water is necessarily H2O" typically get asserted. Please engage with the best Kripkean replies available, not a strawman.
