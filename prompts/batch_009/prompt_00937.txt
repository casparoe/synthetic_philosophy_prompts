I am preparing a graduate seminar on theories of constitutional interpretation and would like help thinking through a historical case I plan to use as a teaching example. William Lloyd Garrison judged the Constitution to be, in his words, "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," because clauses such as the fugitive-slave clause—covering any "person held to service or labor"—entrenched slavery, and Frederick Douglass agreed with him for over a decade. By 1849, however, Douglass had already granted that the constitutional text, strictly read, never uses the words "slave" or "slavery" — yet he still insisted for two more years that the document's public meaning was obviously pro-slavery, since everyone understood such euphemisms to refer to slaves regardless of the framers' careful, evasive wording. Only in 1851, under the influence of Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner, did Douglass reverse course and argue that only the literal absence of the words should count, concluding that the Constitution, properly construed, was in truth an anti-slavery document betrayed by pro-slavery practice.

What are the most important implications of this reversal for the modern jurisprudential distinction between "original intent" and "original public meaning" originalism? In particular, should Douglass's mature position count as an early instance of public-meaning originalism, or is it better understood as a natural-law canon of construction merely expressed in textualist terms?
