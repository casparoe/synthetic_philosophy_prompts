In software estimation, "reference class forecasting" is often recommended as a fix for our chronic over-optimism: instead of building an estimate bottom-up, you find a set of comparable past projects and use their actual outcomes to predict the new one. Whenever I've tried this, the hard part is deciding what counts as "comparable" — projects at the same company, projects on the same stack, and projects of similar scope are all reasonable reference classes, and they routinely disagree. I've since gathered that this is a concrete instance of the reference class problem in the philosophy of probability, usually discussed as a difficulty for frequentist accounts. What I'd like is an intuitive explanation of why this is a genuinely unresolved, in-principle problem rather than just a shortage of data — and, in particular, whether a Bayesian forecaster who skips the reference class altogether and just states a subjective probability for the project's completion date is actually avoiding the problem, or just hiding it a layer deeper.
