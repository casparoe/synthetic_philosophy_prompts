I'm writing a piece about philosophers who stepped outside their technical specialisms to intervene in public debates, and I keep circling back to Michael Dummett's late-career book *On Immigration and Refugees*. Dummett is remembered in Britain as one of the most dogged anti-racist campaigners of his generation — he devoted enormous energy outside his Oxford teaching duties to fighting racially motivated border enforcement, and he and his wife helped found an immigrants'-rights organisation back in the 1960s. He is, of course, equally celebrated among philosophers for his case against classical bivalence: his insistence, developed through decades of work on Frege and intuitionistic logic, that we ought to reject the idea that every meaningful statement is determinately true or false, in favour of a notion of truth tied to what can actually be recognised or verified. Putting those two facts together, I had assumed that his immigration book amounts to a philosophical argument for open borders — that someone who spent his career dissolving artificially sharp, verification-transcendent boundaries would end up treating the citizen/alien distinction itself as exactly the kind of bivalent fiction his logic was built to undermine. A colleague who covers refugee policy was doubtful when I ran this by her, but I couldn't immediately see where the inference breaks down.

For a section of the piece, I would like to present Dummett's actual argument rather than the streamlined version I have been assuming. Could you reconstruct how, if at all, his technical work on meaning and logic bears on the position he stakes out in *On Immigration and Refugees*, and tell me how close that position really comes to an outright open-borders view?
