I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, where the Yudkowsky line about politics being "the mind-killer" comes up constantly as shorthand for how partisan identity corrodes reasoning. A friend from my local meetup recently passed me the NYRB Classics edition (Simon Leys's translation) of Simone Weil's 1943 essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," written in London during her final months with the Free French — she reportedly resigned that post in protest at exactly the sort of party infighting the essay condemns — and I was struck by how much further she pushes this worry than any contemporary rationalist does. Weil isn't just flagging tribalism as a correctable bias; she argues that any political party, regardless of its platform, and even within an otherwise functioning multi-party democracy, necessarily has three features — it manufactures collective passion, it organizes pressure on the private judgment of its own members, and it treats its own unlimited growth as an end in itself that displaces truth and justice — and that these three features make every party "totalitarian" in tendency. Her proposed remedy is not reform but outright legal abolition: no party organizations at all, only individual candidates and loose, non-binding circles of intellectual affinity that form and dissolve issue by issue.

I raised this with my reading group, and we argued about it for an hour without getting anywhere, because the standard defenses of party democracy — that parties make electoral accountability legible to voters, that they solve the coordination problem among lawmakers who would otherwise face a new ad hoc majority on every single bill, that they let structurally weak interests achieve through organization what they could never win as scattered individuals, and that political systems with weak or fragmented parties have historically tended toward instability or strongman rule rather than Weil's imagined regime of individual conscience — all seemed basically right to us, and yet none of them felt like they actually engaged with Weil's specific argument rather than talking past it.

So I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters who both know this material well: one arguing, in the spirit of Weil's actual essay rather than a softened version of it, for the total abolition of political parties, and the other mounting the strongest available case for party-based democracy against that specific argument. You're welcome to invent a frame for the exchange — I keep picturing two members of a committee drafting electoral rules for a new constitution, arguing over whether to write Weil's proposal into law, though feel free to use your own setup instead. What matters more than the setting is that neither side is a pushover: each should take up the other's strongest point as it comes, concede what has to be conceded, and press on the real weak spots, so that it's genuinely unclear by the end who comes out ahead.
