Hey, I'm putting together notes for a work-in-progress talk in a few weeks on Frege's political diary and could use an outside read before I write it up properly. Roughly where I'm at:

Quick background in case its not fresh: the diary (kept March–April 1924) got left out of Frege's Nachlass when his posthumous writings were officially edited and published in the late 60s, and only surfaced in German in the mid-90s, with an English translation not long after. It's ugly stuff — virulently antisemitic (he wants Jews stripped of political rights, ideally gone from Germany entirely), anti-Catholic, anti-French, openly contemptuous of the whole Weimar parliamentary setup. Dummett, who'd basically built his picture of Frege around the guy's supposed icy rationality, read it and was shaken — his line (paraphrasing) is that the politics doesn't touch the validity of the logic, but it makes it a lot harder to approach the work with the same warmth.

That's the standard move: wall off the biography, judge the arguments on their own terms. What I want to push on is whether that wall really holds for Frege specifically, rather than just gesturing at "seperate the art from the artist." My hunch is that Frege's contempt for psychologism and his full-throated Platonism about a mind-independent "third realm" of thoughts aren't merely compatible with the politics — they're expressions of the same underlying temperament: a hunger for one uniquely correct, non-negotiable order, immune to individual will, majority opinion, or "leveling," full stop. Same drive, different domain.

Does that hold up, or is it too cute/reaching? Tell me if I've got any of the history wrong, and push back with counterexamples if you've got good ones (rigid formalists/logicians who weren't remotely authoritarian, or authoritarians who were philosophically sloppy) — and flag it if I'm strawmanning Dummett's actual position.
