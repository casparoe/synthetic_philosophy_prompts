Years of reading rationalist blogs and forums have left me primed to see public-choice arguments everywhere—concentrated benefits, diffuse costs, small organized interests capturing policy at the expense of a large disorganized public—so I was struck, on rereading the "Of Colonies" chapter of Smith's Wealth of Nations (Book IV, Chapter VII, Part III), to find what look like two distinct versions of that argument within a few pages of each other. I can't tell whether Smith means them as one mechanism or two.

First, on why Britain maintains the colonial trade monopoly even though he has just finished arguing that it is, on net, harmful to the country:

"To found a great empire for the sole purpose of raising up a people of customers may at first sight appear a project fit only for a nation of shopkeepers. It is, however, a project altogether unfit for a nation of shopkeepers; but extremely fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers. Such statesmen, and such statesmen only, are capable of fancying that they will find some advantage in employing the blood and treasure of their fellow-citizens to found and maintain such an empire. Say to a shopkeeper, Buy me a good estate, and I shall always buy my clothes at your shop, even though I should pay somewhat dearer than what I can have them for at other shops; and you will not find him very forward to embrace your proposal. But should any other person buy you such an estate, the shopkeeper would be much obliged to your benefactor if he would enjoin you to buy all your clothes at his shop."

A little later, arguing that Britain could never simply relinquish the colonies even though he thinks that, on balance, it should:

"No nation ever voluntarily gave up the dominion of any province, how troublesome soever it might be to govern it, and how small soever the revenue which it afforded might be in proportion to the expence which it occasioned. Such sacrifices, though they might frequently be agreeable to the interest, are always mortifying to the pride of every nation, and what is perhaps of still greater consequence, they are always contrary to the private interest of the governing part of it, who would thereby be deprived of the disposal of many places of trust and profit, of many opportunities of acquiring wealth and distinction, which the possession of the most turbulent, and, to the great body of the people, the most unprofitable province seldom fails to afford."

The first passage blames merchants who profit from the monopoly and lean on government to preserve it; the second blames officeholders who would lose patronage and prestige if the colonies were let go. Those are two different constituencies with two different motives, yet Smith writes as if "a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers" and "the governing part" of the nation were the same culprit. Is there something elsewhere in the chapter, or in Smith's treatment of chartered trading companies more generally, that would justify collapsing these into a single mechanism? Or is this a real equivocation on Smith's part between a commercial story and a bureaucratic story about who captures the state? I'd like a close reading of how the two passages fit together, not a general summary of his case against mercantilism.
