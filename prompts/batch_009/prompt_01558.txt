For the past year I have been an active participant in an online forecasting tournament, logging calibrated probability estimates on everything from central bank decisions to election outcomes. The platform recently circulated individual calibration reports, and mine contained an uncomfortable finding: on questions where I had reported confidence between 80 and 95 percent, the outcomes I favored actually occurred only about two-thirds of the time. I have no real reason to doubt the report; the sample size is respectable and the methodology sound. What strikes me as strange, though, is that this knowledge has done nothing to dampen my confidence at the moment of forecasting itself. Filling out a new question on a rate decision last week, I found myself sitting at 90 percent, fully aware in the abstract that my own track record counsels heavy discounting of numbers that high, and my felt confidence simply refused to budge.

Trying to make sense of this, I have been reading around the topic of higher-order evidence: evidence that bears not on the world directly, but on the reliability of one's own reasoning about it. The standard example in that literature involves a pilot with good reason to suspect she is hypoxic, even though her fuel calculations still feel perfectly sound to her; cases structurally like mine, it turns out, are said to generate what philosophers call epistemic akrasia — believing p while simultaneously believing that one's evidence does not support p. Several writers present this as the doxastic counterpart of the much older notion of practical akrasia, or weakness of will, which goes back to Aristotle's puzzling over how someone can act against her own better judgment, and they gesture in passing toward more modern treatments, such as Davidson's account of an agent who acts on weaker reasons despite forming an all-things-considered judgment that favors the stronger ones.

I would like to understand exactly how far that parallel extends, rather than just take it on faith. Could you compare epistemic akrasia, as it figures in the higher-order evidence literature, with the classical practical akrasia from which it borrows its name? I am especially interested in whether the moves typically used to defuse the practical puzzle — for instance, distinguishing an agent's considered judgment from whatever actually moves her to act — have any real analogue on the belief side, and in whether the fact that both the first-order and higher-order states in the epistemic case are beliefs, answerable to one and the same standard, makes epistemic akrasia a tighter and less escapable form of irrationality than its practical namesake ever was.
