I've been mulling over something for the past few months that sits at the intersection of a personal rumination and a conceptual puzzle, and I've increasingly come to suspect the two are more entangled than I first assumed. Eight years ago, in my mid-twenties, I turned down a two-year research fellowship abroad in order to stay with a partner; the relationship ended less than two years later regardless. A close friend accepted an almost identical fellowship that same year, and the career she has built since then closely resembles the one I used to picture for myself. These days, when I'm in a reflective mood, I notice that I don't simply regret the choice in the ordinary sense of wishing I had decided otherwise. Instead, I catch myself picturing a specific other person — "the me who got on the plane" — as someone who is, in some hard-to-articulate sense, really me, quietly living out the branch of my life I didn't take.

When I described this to my partner recently, he pushed back in a way I haven't been able to shake off. He argued that there is no meaningful sense in which some other person, however similar, "is" me, since the only me that exists is the one sitting in this room, and that treating the hypothetical fellowship-taker as a genuine continuation of myself is just a flattering way of dressing up an ordinary regret. I found this fairly persuasive until I noticed that it seems to prove too much: taken seriously, it looks like it should also rule out perfectly mundane claims such as "I could have taken that job," which none of us are actually prepared to treat as meaningless. I've spent enough time reading rationalist blogs and forums, the kind that linger on decision theory and personal-identity thought experiments, to suspect this isn't just a rhetorical dead end but a place where serious, technical disagreement exists among philosophers about what grounds claims of the form "I could have been otherwise." So: is my partner right that there is no fact of the matter here and I'm just dramatizing an ordinary regret, is my resistance to his argument tracking something real, or does the dispute perhaps not have a determinate answer at all? I would like to actually understand the crux of the disagreement, rather than be offered the usual advice about learning to let go of the past.
