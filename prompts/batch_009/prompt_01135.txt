I spent almost forty years as a research chemist (mostly industrial catalysis, with a stint in academia early on) before retiring about five years back, and since then I've somehow ended up in a little "great books" reading group with three or four other retired friends — none of us has any real philosophy training, we just like arguing about this stuff over coffee. This month we did Cicero's De Senectute (On Old Age), which felt fitting since we're all pushing 70 or passed it. I wrote up a short thing summarizing Cato's argument and pushing back on it a bit, and I'd like some outside comments before I bring it back to the group, since none of us read Latin or know any of the secondary literature.

Here's roughly what I wrote: Cicero puts his own views in the mouth of old Cato the Elder (he's 84 in the dialogue), who lectures the much younger Scipio and Laelius by knocking down four standard complaints about old age — that it pulls you out of active life, weakens the body, strips away your pleasures, and brings you near death. Some of the counterarguments genuinely got me — I liked the line that no old man ever forgets where he buried his treasure, i.e. memory doesn't fail on its own, you just stop excercising it, and the bit about Sophocles' own sons trying to get him declared incompetent and him shutting them up by pointing at the play he'd just finished. But my objection (and maybe this is just the scientist in me) is that basically every positive example Cato/Cicero reaches for — Cato himself, Appius Claudius giving fiery Senate speeches against Pyrrhus while blind and ancient, Solon bragging about learning new things into old age — is a wildly unrepresentative, high-status outlier. It reads less like an argument that old age is generally fine and more like survivorship bias with a toga on: you don't get testimony from the guys who just got frail, miserable, and died, because they're not the ones dictating philosophical dialogues about it.

So a few things I'd love comments on: am I even representing Cicero's actual argument fairly, or have I oversimplified/misremembered it (I'm going off an old translation and my own dodgy notes from group discussion)? Is my "selection effect" objection actually a serious problem for what Cato/Cicero are arguing, or is there a version of the argument that survives it and I'm just not seeing it — like maybe he's only claiming old age *can* be good, not that it usually is? And does this kind of objection have an actual name somewhere, either in the literature on ancient consolation writing or in modern gerontology's "successful aging" debates, which I gather run into a similar problem? Don't be shy about disagreeing with any of this, honestly that's the main thing I'm after — I'd like to sound at least a little informed next time we meet, not just smug.
