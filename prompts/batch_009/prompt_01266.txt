I work as a teaching assistant for an introductory metaphysics seminar, and this week we covered Max Black's argument that two qualitatively identical iron spheres, alone in an otherwise empty and symmetric universe, would be enough to refute the identity of indiscernibles, since no intrinsic property could tell them apart. One student proposed that the spheres remain discernible through the relation each bears to the other — being two miles from the other sphere — but a classmate objected that this already presupposes there are two things to relate, making the move circular. The exchange left the whole room, myself included, unsure whether that relational escape route is legitimate or merely question-begging. I'd like a rigorous answer ready before next week's section, so I want to press the puzzle somewhere with real stakes: quantum mechanics, where physicists must decide whether "identical" particles like electrons count as discernible individuals at all. Could you construct a concrete, physically accurate example along these lines, and explain whether the same relational defense that's contested for Black's spheres actually survives in that setting?
