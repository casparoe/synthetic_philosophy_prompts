I'm currently at work on a novel whose central figure is, appropriately enough, a novelist: a man who abandoned his wife and two young sons some twenty-five years ago to move to the coast and write full-time, on nothing more than a private conviction that he had one great book in him. The novel follows his estranged daughter, now a family lawyer, as she tries to decide how much weight to give, in her final reckoning with her father, to the fact that the conviction turned out to be justified — he did write the book, it is genuinely very good, and he is now celebrated for it. A friend from my writing group, who studied philosophy, told me this is essentially a version of the standard case philosophers discuss under the heading of "moral luck," and pointed me toward Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel. Reading around the topic a little, I gathered that much of this literature positions itself as a reply to Kant, so I went back to the opening pages of the Groundwork — in T. K. Abbott's translation, which is the one I happened to have on hand — and got stuck on the following passage:

"A good will is good not because of what it performs or effects, not by its aptness for the attainment of some proposed end, but simply by virtue of the volition; that is, it is good in itself, and considered by itself is to be esteemed much higher than all that can be brought about by it in favour of any inclination, nay even of the sum total of all inclinations. Even if it should happen that, owing to special disfavour of fortune, or the niggardly provision of a step-motherly nature, this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose, if with its greatest efforts it should yet achieve nothing, and there should remain only the good will (not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power), then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself. Its usefulness or fruitlessness can neither add nor take away anything from this value. It would be, as it were, only the setting to enable us to handle it the more conveniently in common commerce, or to attract to it the attention of those who are not yet connoisseurs, but not to recommend it to true connoisseurs, or to determine its value."

I follow the headline claim well enough: moral worth is meant to be entirely insulated from how things turn out. What I would like help with is more specific. Kant is careful to say that the good will he means is not a "mere wish" but "the summoning of all means in our power," so he isn't simply retreating into some private intention sealed off from the world of trying and doing — effort and agency are still very much in play; only results are being set aside. Given that, could you help me work out, using the more fine-grained vocabulary Nagel later developed, exactly which varieties of luck this passage manages to wall the good will off from, and which it doesn't? My own suspicion, which I can't quite substantiate on my own, is that the "step-motherly nature" clause deals reasonably well with what would now be called resultant luck (and perhaps circumstantial luck too), but has no obvious answer to constitutive luck, since the very capacity to summon "all means in our power" toward one's duty looks just as dependent on temperament and upbringing as the talents of mind and gifts of fortune Kant is busy devaluing one paragraph earlier. Is that a fair pressure point to put on this passage, or does it trade on a distinction Kant would insist on keeping separate? And is there anything elsewhere in his system — I'm thinking of the distinction between empirical and intelligible character — that might plausibly be recruited to blunt the objection, even though nothing like it appears here?
