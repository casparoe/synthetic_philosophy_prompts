I'm a novelist working on a book about the ethics of restoration: the central plot concerns a bomb-damaged medieval cathedral, and a conservator character who refuses to let the west front be rebuilt "as new," against the wishes of the diocese and a rival architect. I want her convictions to have real argumentative bite rather than just sounding like period sentiment, so I've been reading Ruskin, and I've gotten stuck on the logic of the following passage (John Ruskin, The Seven Lamps of Architecture, 1849, from the chapter "The Lamp of Memory"):

"Neither by the public, nor by those who have the care of public monuments, is the true meaning of the word restoration understood. It means the most total destruction which a building can suffer: a destruction out of which no remnants can be gathered; a destruction accompanied with false description of the thing destroyed. Do not let us deceive ourselves in this important matter; it is impossible, as impossible as to raise the dead, to restore anything that has ever been great or beautiful in architecture. That which I have above insisted upon as the life of the whole, that spirit which is given only by the hand and eye of the workman, never can be recalled. Another spirit may be given by another time, and it is then a new building; but the spirit of the dead workman cannot be summoned up, and commanded to direct other hands, and other thoughts. ... What copying can there be of surfaces that have been worn half an inch down? The whole finish of the work was in the half inch that is gone; if you attempt to restore that finish, you do it conjecturally; if you copy what is left, granting fidelity to be possible (and what care, or watchfulness, or cost can secure it?), how is the new work better than the old?

Do not let us talk then of restoration. The thing is a Lie from beginning to end. You may make a model of a building as you may of a corpse, and your model may have the shell of the old walls within it as your cast might have the skeleton, with what advantage I neither see nor care; but the old building is destroyed...

... Take proper care of your monuments, and you will not need to restore them. ... bind it together with iron where it loosens; stay it with timber where it declines; do not care about the unsightliness of the aid; better a crutch than a lost limb."

Two things confuse me. First, is Ruskin arguing that restoration is objectionable only because it is invariably executed with imperfect fidelity to the lost original — a practical shortfall that sufficient skill, money, and record-keeping could in principle close — or is he making the far stronger claim that even a materially perfect replica, indistinguishable stone for stone from what was lost, would still be "a Lie," because what's missing isn't a visible feature at all but something like the causal history of the object: the plain fact that it was cut by that particular hand, at that particular time? Second, if it's the stronger claim, why doesn't it equally condemn the iron ties and timber props he recommends only a few lines later, which surely also alter the fabric of the building? What principle keeps propping from counting as "restoration" in his sense, and so from being a Lie in its own right? I need to understand exactly what distinction is doing the work here, since it's the hinge on which my conservator's argument with her rival has to turn.
