So this got personal fast. I'm in a graduate seminar working through Hegel's Philosophy of Right, and we just spent a whole class on §321–329 (the sovereignty-and-war stretch) the same week my younger brother got his reserve call-up papers. Reading Hegel calmly explain that a permanent peace would let a state go stagnant, and that war is actually what keeps "the ethical health of peoples" from rotting the way a pond rots without wind (§324), hit different than it would've a month ago — a month ago this was just a paragraph number, not something happening to someone I know. My seminar-mate is a committed Hegelian and thinks the argument basically holds up: that a state's sovereignty and a people's ethical substance only get actualized through the willingness to risk life, and that §325's point about sacrifice being the real tie between citizens and the state is just honest about what political obligation actually costs. I'm way more sympathetic to the Kantian line that using citizens as raw material for the "ethical health" of the nation is exactly the treating-persons-as-a-mere-means move the categorical imperative is supposed to rule out.

We've had this same argument in the hallway three times now and keep just talking past eachother. Could you write it up properly as a dialogue between the two of us (H for the Hegelian, K for me), where each one actually has to answer the other's strongest point instead of caricaturing it? I don't want H flattened into "war is glorious," and I don't want K flattened into "war bad, categorical imperative, done" — so make H address the obvious fascism-adjacent worry about treating lives as instruments of the state's health, and make K reckon honestly with the fact that Kant himself thought conflict and "unsocial sociability" were part of what drove humanity toward a lawful international order, so the Kantian doesn't get to pretend discord never did anything but harm. If the two of them actually get somewhere by the end — even if it's just pinning down exactly where the real disagreement lies — that'd be great.
