Coherence arguments for expected-utility maximization have had an odd run on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum: near-consensus for years on the strength of the usual Dutch-book/money-pump case for the VNM axioms, then serious internal pushback lately, much of it aimed at the completeness axiom and partly motivated by proposals to keep AI systems safely shutdownable using incomplete preferences. The pushback runs roughly as follows: an agent with a genuine gap between options A and B (not mere indifference) can be walked through a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off for certain, per the textbook money pump — but only if it accepts each trade myopically, in isolation. An agent that instead looks ahead — and refuses to enter any trade that it can see is the first step of such a losing sequence — keeps its gap intact and is never actually pumped.

The obvious worry about this rejoinder is that the very foresight which lets the agent dodge the pump will, over a long enough life of varied sequential choices, quietly force it to choose in every case just as a complete-preference expected-utility-maximizer would — even though, asked point-blank, it still professes no preference between A and B. Please argue that this "trammelling" effect is real and general enough to rescue the substantive conclusion of the coherence argument (that sufficiently rational agents will act like expected-utility-maximizers), even though the narrow money-pump argument for completeness fails on its own against agents with enough foresight to avoid it. Don't just assert that trammelling occurs — show precisely what it is about the agent's own foresight-based decision rule that would force this convergence.
