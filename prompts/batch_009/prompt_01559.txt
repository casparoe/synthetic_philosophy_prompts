This semester I'm taking a seminar on decision theory that has spent the last couple of weeks on what happens to expected-value reasoning once we allow very small probabilities to be multiplied against very large, or infinite, payoffs. Before assigning Bostrom's short 2009 paper that coins the term "Pascal's mugging," our instructor had us go back to the original wager argument in Pascal's Pensées, on the theory that the mugging problem is really just Pascal's own move turned back against him. A few of us ended up arguing about this over coffee after seminar, because everyone came away with a different reading of one particular stretch of the argument, and I'd like a more careful reconstruction than we managed on our own.

Here is the passage, from the Trotter translation (Pensées, no. 233):

"That is very fine. Yes, I must wager; but I may perhaps wager too much." Let us see. Since there is an equal risk of gain and of loss, if you had only to gain two lives, instead of one, you might still wager. But if there were three lives to gain, you would have to play (since you are under the necessity of playing), and you would be imprudent, when you are forced to play, not to chance your life to gain three at a game where there is an equal risk of loss and gain. But there is an eternity of life and happiness. And this being so, if there were an infinity of chances, of which one only would be for you, you would still be right in wagering one to win two, and you would act stupidly, being obliged to play, by refusing to stake one life against three at a game in which out of an infinity of chances there is one for you, if there were an infinity of an infinitely happy life to gain. But there is here an infinity of an infinitely happy life to gain, a chance of gain against a finite number of chances of loss, and what you stake is finite. It is all divided; where-ever the infinite is and there is not an infinity of chances of loss against that of gain, there is no time to hesitate, you must give all.

Two things about this passage trip me up, and I would appreciate a close reading rather than the standard capsule summary of "the Wager" one gets in intro courses. First, I cannot quite reconstruct the logical progression from the two-lives case, to the three-lives case, to the final case of "an infinity of chances, of which one only would be for you." Are the two- and three-lives cases meant as pedagogically simpler versions of exactly the same principle at work in the infinite-chances case, or does something logically distinct happen once Pascal moves from even, finite odds to a single chance among infinitely many? Second, and this is really what I'm most interested in: given that this passage is supposedly the ancestor of Pascal's mugging, does Pascal's reasoning here already commit him to being "mugged" by anyone who claims, however implausibly, to offer an infinite (or merely very large) reward at a vanishingly small probability, or is there some feature of his specific setup, such as the wager being a fixed contest between exactly two hypotheses rather than an open-ended field of claimants, that would let him block a mugging-style argument while leaving the reasoning in this passage otherwise intact?
