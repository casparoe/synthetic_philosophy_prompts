ok so i think i noticed something at work that might secretly be a hundred year old philosophy problem, and i want a sanity check before i write it up as a blog post (i only know phil of science from wikipedia rabbitholes so pls be gentle).

context: theres this thing in ML people call "underspecification" (theres a well known google paper on it) or the older name for basically the same phenomenon, the "Rashomon effect" (from Breiman's "two cultures" paper). basic idea: if you train a bunch of models on the same data -- same architecture even, just different random seeds, or different-but-reasonable architectures/hyperparams -- you get a pile of models that all get virtually identical loss/accuracy on train and test, but rely on totally different internal "reasons" for their answers, and diverge like crazy once you push them off-distribution. classic example is a model that "identifies" cows by noticing grass in the background instead of learning cow-shaped features, which you'd definately never catch just by looking at accuracy.

this feels to me like an actual, measurable version of "underdetermination of theory by evidence" -- the idea (Duhem originally, then Quine's more radical version, i think in "two dogmas"?) that no finite body of evidence ever pins down one single theory, there's always other theories, sometimes wildly different ones, consistent with all the same data. our "theories" are the trained models, our "evidence" is the training set, and a Rashomon set is basically a pile of currently-empirically-indistinguishable theories just sitting there. felt like a nice little "aha" when i noticed it.

before i spend a whole saturday writing 1500 words about this, can you poke holes in it for me:

1. does the analogy actually hold up, or is there a reason it quietly falls apart that i'm not seeing
2. is this a comparison phil-of-science people already make constantly, i.e. am i about to loudly reinvent a wheel here
3. these models DO start disagreeing once you test them out-of-distribution, so they're only "equivalent" relative to the limited data i happened to test them on, not equivalent for all possible future evidence. does that distinction actually matter for the real philosophical version of underdetermination, or is that basically what the thesis is about anyway?
