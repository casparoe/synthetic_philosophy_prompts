I'm a retired organic chemist who got into philosophy about a year ago, and lately I've been enjoying "experimental philosophy," where you just run surveys on ordinary people instead of arguing from an armchair - feels a lot more like real science to me then most of what philosophers do. The example that really got me was Knobe's old chairman study: people are told a CEO says "I don't care about the environment, I just want profit" and starts a program that, as a side effect, harms it - most people say he harmed it intentionally. Flip it so the side effect is helping the environment instead, same indifferent attitude from the CEO, and hardly anyone says he helped it intentionally.

That sent me down a rabbit hole into older attempts to separate "intended" from "merely foreseen" effects, and I dug up the actual passage where Aquinas first lays out the doctrine of double effect, justifying killing in self-defense (Summa Theologica, II-II, Q.64, A.7):

"Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental... Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in 'being,' as far as possible. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end."

The scholastic phrasing loses me a bit - can you walk me through what exactly makes the aggressor's death "beside the intention" for Aquinas, rather than just a second thing the defender is also choosing to bring about? And, the bit I'm really curious about: does Knobe's result basically undercut this whole approach, if ordinary people's sense of what's "intended" already shifts depending on wether the side effect is good or bad? Seems like Aquinas needs "intended" to be a pretty stable, unmoralized category for his argument to do any work.
