Hey, so I'm in a political philosophy seminar this semester that's doing a slow, line-by-line read through Hegel's Philosophy of Right, and this week we're on the section about the state's sovereignty toward foreign states — basically the war part. Our assigned translation is Knox, but my library copy got checked out from under me, so I ended up reading paragraph 324 in this older 1896 translation (by S.W. Dyde) I found on an archive site instead, which is probably why the wording below looks a bit different from whatever everyone else in my discussion group is quoting from the secondary lit.

Here's the bit I'm stuck on (the Note to the paragraph, plus part of the later Addition to the same section):

"War is not to be regarded as an absolute evil. It is not a merely external accident, having its accidental ground in the passions of powerful individuals or nations, in acts of injustice, or in anything which ought not to be. Accident befalls that which is by nature accidental, and this fate is a necessity. ... Moreover, by it, as I have elsewhere expressed it, 'finite pursuits are rendered unstable, and the ethical health of peoples is preserved. Just as the movement of the ocean prevents the corruption which would be the result of perpetual calm, so by war people escape the corruption which would be occasioned by a continuous or eternal peace.'"

Addition: "Everlasting peace is frequently demanded as the ideal towards which mankind must move. Hence, Kant proposed an alliance of princes, which should settle the controversies of states... But the state is individual, and in individuality negation is essentially implied. Although a number of states may make themselves into a family, the union, because it is an individuality, must create an opposition, and so beget an enemy."

I get what each sentence means on its own, but I'm having trouble seeing the actual arguement connecting them. Like when he calls war a "necessity," does he mean that in some kind of logical/dialectical sense (necessary the way a step in a proof is necessary), or more of a natural-law/causal sense, or is it something else entirely? Is the winds-and-ocean image doing real argumentative work, or is it just a metaphor dressed up as a reason? And I'm especially lost on the jump from "the state is an individual" to "individuality implies negation" to "therefore any league of states just creates an enemy" — that seems to be doing a lot of heavy lifting against Kant's perpetual peace essay specifically, and I don't see why a Kantian couldn't just bite the bullet on part of it. If someone could walk me through the logic step by step, and maybe flag which claims are load-bearing for Hegel's bigger system (ethical life, sovereignty, individuality) versus which ones are more like illustrations, that'd be clutch. Kind of a bonus question: if this argument is sound, does it generalize to stuff like nuclear deterrence or total industrial war, which seem like they'd threaten a people's "ethical health" way more than preserve it, or does the whole picture just break down once war stops looking like the fairly contained affair Hegel seems to have in mind?
