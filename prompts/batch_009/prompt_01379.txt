ok so this is a weirdly specific thing to be tilted about but it's been living in my head for like two weeks now. my housemate is halfway through an econ phd, and i was complaining to her about how i keep telling myself i'll stop doomscrolling at night and read instead, and never actually do it, and she just shrugged and said "i mean, by revealed preference you obviously prefer scrolling, that's literally what you keep choosing." something about that answer has bugged me ever since, so i spent way too much of this week reading about revealed preference theory to figure out why, and wrote up my case. would love a sanity check before i bring it back to her.

here's basically the argument:

1. when samuelson set up revealed preference theory in the 30s, the whole point was to get "utility" out of the business of describing feelings or judgments about what's good for you, and just make it a label for whatever ranking is consistant with someone's choices (there's some consistency requirement on the choices, i think it's called WARP, and then a stronger version called SARP). so "preference" in that technical sense just means "the ranking that rationalizes your past choices" — it's not a claim about what you want, or what's good for you. those are two very different claims and only the first one is actually doing work in the math.

2. so when my housemate says "you revealed you prefer scrolling," she's using the authority of claim 1 (there's a consistent ranking here) to sneak in claim 2 (this is what you want / what's good for you). but i don't even think claim 1 is true in this case — i choose to scroll at 11pm and i choose to buy books about breaking phone habits at 2pm, which isn't one stable preference getting revealed, if anything it seems like exactly the kind of thing the consistency requirement is supposed to rule out.

3. the part i'm shakier on: apparently economists already have whole models built around exactly this problem — some kind of "planner vs. doer" split inside one person (thaler and shefrin? early 80s, i think), where which preference gets "revealed" just depends on who's holding the wheel at that moment. and then there's a different move (gul & pesendorfer, maybe?) where instead of giving up on revealed preference for people with self-control problems, you push it up a level — people reveal preferences over sets of options instead of individual options, so choosing to keep a smaller menu around (no junk food in the house) can itself count as rational, revealed-preference-style behavior. kind of elegant, but also feels like a quiet admission that the naive version breaks.

4. even actual welfare economists seem to have had to deal with this — i stumbled on a paper (bernheim & rangel?) that tries to rebuild something welfare-like on top of choices that are allowed to be inconsistent, by only counting "x is better than y for you" when y is never chosen over x in any context, which is a much more careful thing than "whatever you just picked is what's best for you."

so my actual conclusion is that "revealed preference," used the way my housemate used it, isn't doing any real philosophical work — it's just "you chose it" dressed up in fancier vocabulary, then quietly sliding into a welfare claim the theory was specifically built not to make. but i'm not fully confident i'm not strawmanning the economics here. is the core equivocation actually right, or is there a real sense in which economists do think choice just is welfare and i've got the motivation/history wrong? and did i basically get the planner-doer / menu-preferences stuff right, or am i garbling it? not fishing for reassurance, more interested if there's an actual hole here.
