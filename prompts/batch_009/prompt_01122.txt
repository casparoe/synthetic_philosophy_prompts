I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but the lottery paradox and the preface paradox recently struck me as having almost identical logical structure, despite provoking very different intuitive reactions. As I understand them: in the lottery case, it seems rational to believe of each individual ticket in a large, fair lottery that it will lose, yet irrational to believe that no ticket will win. In the preface case, an author seems rational in believing every individual sentence of her book, while also believing, in the preface, that the book almost certainly contains at least one error. Both cases involve rationally believing each member of a large set of claims while denying their conjunction, and yet the lottery believer is usually treated as caught in a genuine paradox, while the author is treated as merely being appropriately humble.

Please construct a single, concrete scenario drawn from everyday software engineering — something involving a CI test suite, a staged rollout, or a large fleet of services, for instance — in which both paradox structures arise side by side and pull our intuitions apart in the same way the original cases do. Then explain what is actually doing the work in distinguishing the two cases within your scenario, and state plainly which of the standard philosophical responses to these paradoxes you find most defensible once someone has to act on both kinds of belief in practice, rather than merely contemplate them.
