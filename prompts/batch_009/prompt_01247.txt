I cover physics and cosmology for a science magazine, and over the past year the "Boltzmann brain problem" has come up in more interviews than I expected, generally raised by physicists as a genuine constraint on cosmological model-building rather than a curiosity. As I understand the argument, if our universe eventually settles into an eternal, near-empty de Sitter phase, driven by a positive cosmological constant, that phase behaves thermodynamically like a system in equilibrium, with a finite temperature of its own. Given enough time, random fluctuations in such a state will occasionally assemble a momentarily conscious brain out of the vacuum, complete with a full, coherent set of memories of a lifetime it never lived. Because fluctuating a single such brain into existence is entropically far cheaper than fluctuating an entire galaxy's worth of ordered structure and billions of years of evolutionary history, these "Boltzmann brains" would vastly outnumber ordinary evolved observers like us over sufficiently long cosmic timescales. Several physicists have told me they take the idea seriously precisely because it is self-undermining: a theory predicting that we are probably Boltzmann brains is, in effect, a theory that discredits the very reasoning used to arrive at it.

I mentioned all this over dinner recently and more or less ruined the evening — once you start taking seriously the idea that your memory of the last twenty years, or even of that dinner conversation, could be a fluctuation about to dissolve back into noise, it is hard to stop turning it over. I write short fiction on the side, usually prompted by whatever I happen to be reporting on, and I would like to try turning this particular unease into a story rather than an article.

Could you write me a short story, somewhere in the 800–1200 word range, told from inside this predicament rather than about it from the outside? I do not want a story that treats "a brain adrift in the dark" as mere set dressing for a twist ending; I want the actual physics, and especially the self-undermining logic of the argument, to be load-bearing for whatever happens — the fact that the narrator's capacity to reason clearly about its own situation is, statistically, the least likely explanation for that capacity. You are free to choose the setting, tense, and tone, though it might be interesting if the narrator's remembered life happens to include having once read or reported on the Boltzmann brain problem itself. I would rather end up with something genuinely unsettling than something neatly resolved.
