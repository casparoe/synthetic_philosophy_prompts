I've spent a fair bit of my spare time over the past month or so reading around decision theory as a hobby (no formal background here — I'm mostly working through blog posts, old forum threads, and whatever secondary material I can track down), and I keep coming back to Parfit's hitchhiker case. The setup, as I understand it: you're dying of thirst in the desert, and a driver pulls up who is an excellent, though not infallible, lie-detector. She'll only take you into town if she becomes convinced you'll withdraw cash and pay her once you arrive, since she has no way to force payment after the fact. What really gets me is that a perfectly self-interested person, reasoning it through the moment they're safely in town with no further need of the driver, has no remaining reason to pay up — and precisely because a sharp enough driver can see this coming, that same "perfectly rational" person is the one left to die by the roadside. As far as I can tell, this is basically what Parfit meant by "rational irrationality": that it can be rational, from the outside, to want to become the sort of person who will go on to do something irrational later, like handing over money for a ride you've already gotten.

I tried explaining all this to my weekly board-game group last week, and while a couple of people got genuinely interested, my own attempt at a joke about it landed in total silence. I'd love a better one to bring back next time. Could you write me a short joke — a one-liner, or a brief setup-and-punchline, whichever works best — that's actually built around this paradox, rather than just being a generic joke about hitchhiking or being stranded in the desert? I want the humor to really depend on grasping the distinction between being rational in the moment and being rational in one's dispositions, such that someone who didn't already understand that distinction wouldn't find it funny for the right reasons.
