In a graduate seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory, we've been puzzling over the backward-induction paradox as it plays out in Rosenthal's centipede game. The apparent core of the difficulty is this: Aumann's 1995 result shows that if rationality is common knowledge among the players in a game of perfect information, the backward-induction outcome results, meaning that, in the centipede game, both players should stop at the very first opportunity. But Pettit and Sugden, and independently Bicchieri, have argued that the reasoning behind this result is self-undermining once play actually gets past the first move: reaching a later node is, on the standard story, proof that the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption that was supposed to justify predicting further defection has already broken down, so it's unclear why that very assumption should still be trusted to predict what happens next. Binmore has pressed a related worry about whether Aumann's notion of "rationality" is doing any real explanatory work, once we ask what a player should believe on reaching a node that, by hypothesis, a player who is rational and commonly known to be so would never reach.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two players who find themselves genuinely in this situation: a six-move centipede game with a pot that starts small and grows with every pass, where each player has already passed once, bringing play to the third decision node, contrary to what backward induction from the outset would have predicted. One player insists that this history changes nothing: the correct continuation is still given by backward induction from here on, and the earlier passes should be treated as anomalies rather than as evidence bearing on how the rest of the game will go. The other insists that having reached this node at all is itself decisive evidence that common knowledge of rationality has broken down between them, so that defecting now would be drawing exactly the wrong lesson from what just happened. Let them argue the point out directly: what it even means to evaluate a player's rationality "at" a node that, by the theory in question, should never be reached; whether encountering such a node calls for a genuine belief update or only for the assessment of a counterfactual; and whether defining rationality so that it must hold even at nodes whose reaching refutes its own common knowledge is covertly assuming the very thing that is in dispute.

Please make both characters equally sharp: neither should be a strawman for the other, and each should respond to the specific point the other has just made rather than simply restate their own position. The dialogue doesn't need to end in agreement, but it should end with the two of them having pinned down precisely where the disagreement lies.
