A friend of mine, who is finishing an MPhil thesis on causal powers, and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about the conditional analysis of dispositions — specifically, about whether "finkish" and "masked" dispositions really pose two distinct problems for that analysis, or whether they are just two descriptions of the same underlying phenomenon. I found myself defending the view that C. B. Martin's electro-fink (the dead wire that a hidden device makes live at the very instant a conductor touches it) and Mark Johnston's masked glass (fragile, yet cushioned in packing material so that it never actually breaks) are, at bottom, doing the same philosophical work: in both cases, the counterfactual "if the stimulus occurred, the manifestation would occur" comes apart from the truth about whether the object really has the disposition. My friend pushed back hard, insisting there is a principled causal distinction between the two, not merely a difference of degree, and that this distinction is exactly why David Lewis's Reformed Conditional Analysis can be repaired to survive finks (by requiring that the relevant intrinsic causal basis persist through the stimulus) while remaining defenseless against masks and against Alexander Bird's antidotes — such as a poison taken together with something that neutralizes it — which leave that very basis fully intact.

We never resolved it, so I would like your help thinking it through more rigorously than we managed over wine. Could you compare finkish and masked dispositions in some depth: what precisely is the structural difference between "the causal basis is destroyed or installed at the moment of the stimulus" and "the basis survives untouched while the causal process toward manifestation is blocked further downstream," and is that distinction really principled, or does it break down under scrutiny? I would also like your direct assessment of whether Lewis's reformed analysis genuinely handles finks but not masks and antidotes, and why the extra machinery he adds — persistence of the causal basis — fails against the latter but not the former. Finally, since this was the crux of our disagreement: do you think a single case could be constructed that counts as both a fink and a mask on one and the same disposition at one and the same time, and if such a case is possible, what would it show about whether finks and masks are truly different kinds of counterexample, or simply two ends of a single continuum?
