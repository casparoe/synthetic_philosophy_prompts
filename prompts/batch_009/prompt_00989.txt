I've been rereading the early pages of David Lewis's Convention, where one of his central illustrations isn't driving on the right but a homelier case drawn from his own hometown of Oberlin, Ohio: when a telephone call was cut off, the convention was that the original caller redialed. This is what's sometimes called an "impure" coordination problem, since each party privately prefers that the other call back and bear the cost, even though both prefer resuming the call to silence. Crucially, the whole solution depends on there being a well-defined caller and callee to break the symmetry and serve as a focal point. Lewis died in 2001, long before group video calls became a routine part of daily life. These typically run through a shared meeting link that everyone joins independently, so when a call drops, there's no well-defined "original caller" to do the redialing; in practice, no consistent convention has taken the old telephone norm's place, and people just reconnect in whatever order, sometimes causing a small pileup when several try to restart the meeting at once. Reasoning strictly from the conditions Lewis himself lays out for a regularity to count as a genuine convention, what do you think he would identify as the missing ingredient that has kept a comparable convention from crystallizing here?
