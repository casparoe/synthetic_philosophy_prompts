So I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole for a piece on the early history of AI, and I finally reread Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" properly instead of just remembering the imitation game part like everyone does. Turns out that right before he pivots into his own positive vision of learning machines, the very last objection he tackles head-on is extrasensory perception — and not as a throwaway joke either. He states flat out that the statistical evidence for telepathy available in his day was basically overwhelming, and he seriously proposes testing contestants in a "telepathy-proof room" so a human couldn't get an unfair edge by reading the interrogator's mind. Kind of a wild thing to stumble across in a founding document of computer science.

Anyway it's been nagging at me since: Turing died in '54, decades before parapsychology had it's big meltdown moment. I'm thinking specifically of Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" paper, which got nine precognition experiments published in a mainstream APA journal, eight of them coming back statistically significant, before the whole thing fell apart under failed replications and a big Bayesian-statistics fight (Wagenmakers and co basically argued Bem's p-values evaporated once you redid the math properly). Historians of psychology usually point to that saga as one of the sparks for the replication crisis and the push toward preregistration that followed.

Given how seriously Turing took the parapsychology statistics of his own era, do you reckon he'd have updated hard toward skepticism once the Bem story played out, or is there something in his actual reasoning in the paper that suggests he'd have stayed more open-minded about this stuff than most working scientists were? Less interested in a flat yes-or-no, more in how you'd reconstruct his thinking process from what he actually wrote and the intellectual habits he showed elsewhere in the paper.
