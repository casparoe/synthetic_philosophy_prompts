Write a dialogue between two philosophers of computation who disagree about the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis. One holds that, once "effective calculability" is properly analyzed, the thesis is closer to a conceptual or mathematical truth than an empirical conjecture, appealing to Turing's own analysis of human computation, Gandy's physically-motivated axioms for mechanical devices, and Kripke's argument that the thesis follows from Gödel's completeness theorem once each computational step is required to be statable in first-order logic. The other holds that the thesis, especially in its "physical" form concerning what any device compatible with the laws of physics could compute, is a substantive and falsifiable empirical claim, pointing to proposed hypercomputers exploiting Malament-Hogarth spacetimes -- exotic relativistic spacetimes in which, on some proposals, an infinite computation could be observed to complete in a finite time -- and arguing that even Turing's and Gandy's analyses quietly presuppose contingent physical facts, such as a discrete state space and a bound on how much can happen in one step. Have the two engage each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another, with at least part of the exchange turning on whether the mutual equivalence of independently motivated formalisms -- recursive functions, lambda calculus, Turing machines -- is evidence for an empirical hypothesis or the product of a successful conceptual explication.
