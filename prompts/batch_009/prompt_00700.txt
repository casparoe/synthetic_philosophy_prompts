Since this past September I have been clerking for a federal appellate judge who is an unapologetic textualist, and a case on our docket this term has left me newly preoccupied with a puzzle about the internal consistency of that methodology. The statute we are construing lists several specific exempted items followed by a catch-all phrase ("or any other similar device"), and my judge's draft opinion fixes the catch-all's scope by invoking ejusdem generis and reading the provision against the structure of the surrounding subsections, all while treating the House committee report that speaks directly to the drafters' aims for the clause as categorically inadmissible. When I raised the apparent tension with my co-clerk, who spent a year in the Senate Legislative Counsel's office before this job, she told me the canons simply encode background facts about how competent drafters and readers use language, rather than covert inquiries into anyone's intent, so there is no inconsistency at all. That answer has stayed with me, but the longer I sit with it, the less sure I am that it holds up.

I recently came across John Manning's argument, from a 2003 Harvard Law Review piece on the absurdity doctrine, that the traditional doctrine sits uneasily with modern textualism: once you accept the public-choice-informed picture of a statute as a contingent, path-dependent bargain rather than the expression of some coherent collective purpose, you no longer have firm ground for saying what result "the legislature" could not have intended, which is exactly what the absurdity doctrine requires you to say. Manning uses this to argue for sharply narrowing, though not abandoning, the doctrine. What I cannot shake is the sense that the very same argument should equally threaten the whole-act rule, the presumption of consistent usage, and canons like ejusdem generis and noscitur a sociis, since using any of them to fix the scope of a catch-all likewise requires attributing some coherent "evident scheme" to a body that, on the public-choice picture, need not have had one.

So here is what I would like your help with. Please argue for the position that Manning's own reasoning against the absurdity doctrine generalizes to these structural and semantic canons, such that a new textualist who accepts his critique of the absurdity doctrine but continues to rely on the whole-act rule, ejusdem generis, and noscitur a sociis is being inconsistent. Take the strongest version of the "mere linguistic convention" rejoinder seriously (essentially my co-clerk's view) before explaining concretely why it does or does not save these canons from the extended critique.
