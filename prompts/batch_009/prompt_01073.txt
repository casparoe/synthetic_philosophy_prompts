So I had the most nerdy possible argument with my friend Theo last night at the bar -- he's doing a PhD in early modern philosophy and had just been reading Jesseph's book on the Hobbes-Wallis feud, so he was full of it. Somehow we spent like two hours on Thomas Hobbes and geometry, of all things. Backstory in case it's useful: Hobbes spent basically the rest of his life, decades, right up until he died, insisting he'd squared the circle, and John Wallis, the actual Savilian professor of geometry at Oxford, kept publicly demolishing his proofs over and over, and Hobbes just never let it go. What got us arguing is that in the same pamphlet where he's slugging it out with Wallis -- Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematiques, I think it's called -- Hobbes also has this line about how geometry is certain because we draw the lines and figures ourselves, and civil philosophy (his whole political theory) is exactly as demonstrable, for the same reason: we make the commonwealth ourselves too.

My take was that this is actually pretty damning for Hobbes -- like, he's confidently claiming geometry-grade certainty for his politics in literally the same book where he's confidently, repeatedly wrong about an actual geometrical construction, for over twenty years, without ever budging. Feels like the same "I built it so I get to know it perfectly" move misfiring in both places. Theo thinks that's unfair to him: his point is that being bad at one specific quadrature proof doesn't touch the general principle that we have privileged knowledge of things we construct, and that the "we make the commonwealth ourselves" argument has to stand or fall on it's own terms, not get smeared by association with the circle-squaring embarrassment. Neither of us budged and the bar closed on us mid-argument, which is annoying, becasue I do think there's a real connection there somewhere, I just couldn't pin down exactly what it was.

Could you actually settle this for me? Write an essay that lays out Hobbes's argument for treating civil philosophy as a demonstrative science on the model of geometry, gives it a fair hearing in its strongest form, and then decides whether the Wallis debacle counts as genuine evidence against that argument or is just an unrelated personal failing of his. Don't just land on "there are considerations on both sides" -- I want an actual verdict, even a conditional one; something like "the two failures share flaw X but not flaw Y, so here's the verdict" is the kind of shape I'm after. I want something concrete enough that I can go back and use it on Theo.
