I'm designing a session for a graduate seminar on the history and foundations of imprecise probability, and I've been re-reading Keynes's 1921 A Treatise on Probability as background reading. Everyone remembers Keynes for the argument, in the early chapters, that probabilities are not always numerically or even ordinally comparable; what I actually want the seminar to dwell on is a less-discussed companion notion that only shows up much later in the book, namely the "weight" of an argument, which Keynes defines as a measure of the balance of relevant knowledge to relevant ignorance, as opposed to probability itself, which measures the balance of favourable to unfavourable evidence. In Part IV, Chapter XXVI, "The Application of Probability to Conduct," section 8, Keynes tries to weld this notion of weight together with a separate notion of "risk" into a single quantity that could, in principle, help guide action, and it's this attempt that has me stuck. In the public-domain edition I'm using, the passage runs as follows:

"We may doubt whether the moral value of speculative and cautious action respectively can be weighed against one another in a simple arithmetical way, just as we have already doubted whether a good whose probability can only be determined on a slight basis of evidence can be compared by means merely of the magnitude of this probability with another good whose likelihood is based on completer knowledge. There seems, at any rate, a good deal to be said for the conclusion that, other things being equal, that course of action is preferable which involves least risk, and about the results of which we have the most complete knowledge. In marginal cases, therefore, the coefficients of weight and risk as well as that of probability are relevant to our conclusion. It seems natural to suppose that they should exert some influence in other cases also, the only difficulty in this being the lack of any principle for the calculation of the degree of their influence. A high weight and the absence of risk increase pro tanto the desirability of the action to which they refer, but we cannot measure the amount of the increase. The 'risk' may be defined in some such way as follows. If A is the amount of good which may result, p its probability (p + q = 1), and E the value of the 'mathematical expectation,' so that E = pA, then the 'risk' is R, where R = p(A - E) = p(1-p)A = pqA = qE. We could, if we liked, define a conventional coefficient c of weight and risk, such as c = 2pw / [(1+q)(1+w)], where w measures the 'weight,' which is equal to unity when p = 1 and w = 1, and to zero when p = 0 or w = 0, and has an intermediate value in other cases." A footnote attached to that last sentence adds: "If pA = p′A′, w > w′, and q = q′, then cA > c′A′; if pA = p′A′, w = w′, and q < q′, then cA > c′A′; if pA = p′A′, w > w′, and q < q′, then cA > c′A′; but if pA = p′A′, w = w′, and q > q′, we cannot in general compare cA and c′A′."

I have three requests. First, in plain terms, what is this "conventional coefficient of weight and risk" supposed to be doing, and why does Keynes propose it so hesitantly ("if we liked") immediately after insisting that weight and risk plainly matter to our conclusions but cannot be measured? So far as I can tell he never refers to this coefficient again anywhere else in the book. Second, I tried to check the arithmetic behind the footnote by fixing E = pA = p′A′ and expressing c purely as a function of p and w (using q = 1 - p); on that reconstruction, the fourth clause (w = w′, q > q′) looks like it should simply be the mirror image of the second clause (w = w′, q < q′), and therefore ought to yield an equally determinate comparison, with the inequality reversed, rather than the stated incomparability. Have I set up the algebra wrongly somewhere, or does this look like a genuine slip — Keynes's own, or perhaps introduced downstream in transcription — and if the latter, what would you guess the intended fourth clause actually was (something like "w > w′ and q > q′," perhaps, where weight and risk pull in opposite directions)? Third, and more broadly, how should we situate this whole apparatus relative to later treatments of decision-making under imprecisely specified probabilities, such as Ellsberg's work on ambiguity aversion (it's his dissertation, rather than the more famous 1961 paper, that engages directly with Keynes on weight) or Walley's behavioural theory of lower and upper previsions? Is Keynesian "weight" ultimately doing the same job as the width of an imprecise probability interval, so that more weight just amounts to a narrower credal state, or is it tracking something that interval-based imprecise-probability frameworks still fail to capture?
