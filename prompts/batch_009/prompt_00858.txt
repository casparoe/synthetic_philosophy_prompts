I don't have any formal education in philosophy — I work as an electrician — but for the past several months I've been slowly reading my way through Descartes' "The Passions of the Soul" (1649), which I found secondhand after getting interested in the question of what emotions actually are: whether they're basically feelings, or perceptions of some kind, or judgments we're making about the world. I finally reached Part I, Article 27, where Descartes lays out his official definition of a "passion" (his term for what we'd call an emotion), and I've read the sentence over and over without feeling like I've really understood it. In the translation I'm using, it reads: "those perceptions, sensations or emotions of the soul which we refer particularly to it, and which are caused, maintained and strengthened by some movement of the spirits."

From the articles leading up to this one, I think I've followed the general set-up well enough: Descartes sorts everything happening in the mind into "actions" the soul brings about on its own (willing something) and various "perceptions" the soul merely receives — some caused by objects outside the body (seeing a color, hearing a bell), some caused by the body itself (hunger, thirst, pain), and then a final, narrower group that we don't refer to any object or body part but to the soul alone, like joy or anger, which is apparently what Article 27 is trying to define once and for all. What's tripping me up is the definition itself, not the build-up to it.

First, why does Descartes call these things "perceptions" at all? An ordinary perception seems to be a perception of something — a tree, a sound, a pain in my foot — but fear or joy don't seem to be "of" anything in that same way; they just seem to be how I feel. Second, the definition strings together "perceptions, sensations, and emotions" as though offering three names for one thing, which in the copy I'm reading looks almost circular, since he's defining "passion" partly by using the word "emotion." I looked at a different free translation online out of curiosity, and it uses "commotions" instead of "emotions" in that exact spot, which makes me suspect that word is doing something different, or something anachronistic, in the original French than it would in modern English — I vaguely recall reading somewhere that "emotion" as a general psychological category is actually a fairly recent, nineteenth-century invention, so maybe that's relevant here. Third, the closing clause about being "caused, maintained and strengthened by some movement of the spirits" is obviously meant to carry a lot of the weight, since elsewhere Descartes uses it to separate passions from acts of will — but hunger and pain are also supposed to be caused by the body, so what exactly stops those from also counting as "passions" by this definition? Could you walk me through what each piece of Article 27 is actually doing, and say where you think Descartes' answer lands on the modern menu of judgments, perceptions, or feelings?
