I spent my career as a solid-state physicist, and one thing that always stuck with me is how fiddly it is to compute the Madelung constant for an ionic crystal: the lattice sum is only conditionally convergent, so you literally get a different number depending on whether you sum outward in cubes or in spheres. We treated that as a technical nuisance to be tamed with Ewald summation and a physically sensible ordering, then moved on with our lives.

Now that I'm retired and puttering aruond in decision theory, I've stumbled on the Pasadena paradox, which seems to be built on exactly the same underlying fact - a conditionally convergent series whose sum depends on the order of rearrangement (Riemann's theorem) - except here its treated as a genuine paradox that supposedly breaks expected utility theory, rather than a mere computational headache to be tamed the way we tamed ours.

Can you compare the two cases for me? Is there something that actually makes Pasadena a deep philosophical problem, over and above just being another instance of the Madelung situation, which decision theorists could defuse the same way we did (pick a well-motivated summation order and move on)? Or does our physicists' move secretly smuggle in something just as illegitimate as whatever's going on in Pasadena, and it just never mattered enough for anyone to notice?
