A friend and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about Kierkegaard, of all people, after I compared a mutual acquaintance's recent online pile-on to the Corsair affair. For context: in the mid-1840s, irritated that the satirical Copenhagen weekly The Corsair had been praising him while savaging every other writer in town, Kierkegaard more or less dared the paper to attack him too. It obliged, running months of caricatures depicting him as a hunchback with two different-length trouser legs, until strangers and children were jeering at him on his daily walks. My friend's contention was that Kierkegaard basically engineered his own persecution and then dressed up the resulting grievance as social theory in Two Ages, so that his famous remarks on "the public" are really just sublimated vanity.

I disagreed, and this is the part we couldn't resolve. It seemed to me that whatever its personal origin, what Kierkegaard lands on is a genuinely distinct claim about a new kind of social entity: one that is answerable to no one because, on his account, it corresponds to no actual people, generation, congregation, or association of particular persons, but is instead a kind of "monstrous abstraction" that the press calls into being. What makes it dangerous, on this view, isn't any individual's malice but the way it lets a great many separate people participate in judging someone without any one of them having to own the judgment — and, more provocatively, that ordinary people find something restful in dissolving into this anonymous "public" precisely because it excuses them from being what Kierkegaard calls "the single individual," answerable in one's own name for one's own opinions. My friend thinks this is all still just wounded vanity wearing a philosopher's robes; I think the biographical pettiness of the origin is beside the point.

Since we couldn't settle it over dinner, I'd like to try settling it through fiction instead. Could you write a short story, somewhere around 1,000 to 2,000 words, that dramatizes Kierkegaard's specific picture rather than a generic "the internet mob is cruel" parable? Feel free to set it wherever you like—nineteenth-century Copenhagen, a present-day office or forum, some unspecified near future—but I'd like the "public" at the center of it to be recognizably his: a force that crushes someone in particular while being, in its origin, nobody's doing in particular. If you can also work in the wrinkle my friend and I couldn't settle, by having the central character be, elsewhere or earlier in the story, not simply a victim of this leveling but also one of its anonymous instruments, so much the better.
