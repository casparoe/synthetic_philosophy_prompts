I cover research misconduct for a science outlet, and I keep running into the same inconsistency in how these stories get edited, one I can't quite explain. On one story, a data forensics expert showed me that 92 percent of papers exhibiting a particular statistical anomaly in their reported results later turned out to involve fabrication; that alone was judged too weak to let me write that a flagged paper "contains fabricated data" rather than "shows a pattern associated with fabrication." On another story, a single co-author told me directly, on background, that the underlying data had been faked — testimony I'd trust to be accurate about 92 percent of the time — yet my editor was comfortable letting me state flatly that the data was fabricated, sourced only to "a person familiar with the research." My odds of being wrong seem identical in both cases, so why does only one of them feel like something I actually know, rather than an educated guess? Is there a genuine epistemic distinction between the two kinds of evidence, or is this just an arbitrary journalistic convention?
