Back before I retired I spent about thirty years doing Bayesian analysis of particle-detector data, so betting talk about credences feels pretty native to me. Since hanging up my lab coat I've been falling down philosophy rabbit holes, and a few of us from the old department started an informal reading group that's currently chewing on Dutch book arguments. Everyone's on board with the basic "synchronic" version: if your credences at a moment don't obey the probability axioms, a bookie can hand you a stack of bets that each look fair to you individually but that combine into a guaranteed loss no matter how things turn out. That's just arbitrage dressed up in probability talk, and it converted me to probabilism pretty quickly.

Where the group split was over the "diachronic" version people use to justify updating by strict conditionalization -- the claim that if you don't plan to move your credence in H to your old value for P(H|E) once you learn E, a bookie can string bets across two times (one before you learn E, one after) and guarantee you end up loosing money either way. Most of the group figures this is the exact same coherence argument with a clock bolted on, so it should carry just as much rational force as the synchronic one. Something about that feels too fast to me, though I can't quite nail down why -- maybe it's that the bookie needs to already know how you plan to update, which seems like a different kind of assumption than anything in the synchronic proof.

Could you build the strongest case you can that this generalization doesn't actually go through -- i.e. an argument that avoiding a diachronic Dutch book does NOT rationally require conditionalization in anything like the sense that avoiding a synchronic Dutch book requires having probabilistically coherent credences? I'd like to see exactly where you think the disanalogy bites, not just a rundown of the standard coherence story applied twice.
