I'm drafting a short paper for a philosophy-of-economics workshop on what, if anything, "utility" still means once it has been reconstructed along revealed-preference lines, and I would value a colleague's reaction to the central argument before I commit it to paper. The occasion was an argument with an economist friend at a conference dinner, who maintained that Samuelson's program had simply replaced an embarrassingly introspective notion of utility with something empirically respectable, and that residual philosophical worries about what "utility" refers to are accordingly a hangover from pre-Samuelsonian confusion.

Here is the argument, still in note form. Revealed-preference theory promises to define utility purely in terms of the consistency properties of observed choice, cashed out through a hierarchy of axioms — Samuelson's Weak Axiom, Houthakker's Strong Axiom, and Varian's Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP) — the last of which is equivalent, by Afriat's theorem, to the existence of a continuous, monotonic utility function rationalizing any finite set of price-quantity observations. My suspicion is that this apparatus trades on a silent equivocation between two senses of "preference." On the official, thin reading, "x is preferred to y" just means "x would be chosen over y," which makes GARP-satisfaction close to a tautology: naturally, a consistent chooser's choices can always be redescribed as maximizing something. But economists keep reaching for a thicker sense of "preference" — a standing state that explains choice and that licenses saying a policy leaves someone better or worse off — and it is this thicker sense that Sen's distinction between sympathy and commitment seems to threaten: a person who commits to acting against her own perceived interest can satisfy every consistency axiom while her "revealed preferences" tell us nothing at all about her welfare. If that's right, rationalizability results like Afriat's theorem show only that consistent choice can always be redescribed by a utility function, not that the function tracks anything beyond that redescription, and every further inference economists draw from revealed preference to welfare quietly reintroduces the very psychological notion the program was meant to do without.

I would like your comments on three things: whether the argument actually holds together as I have stated it, or trades on a distinction a careful revealed-preference theorist would refuse to grant; whether there is a standard rebuttal, in the spirit of Friedman's instrumentalism about the realism of assumptions, that blocks the inference from "GARP-satisfaction is tautologous in the thin sense" to "welfare claims grounded in revealed preference are illegitimate"; and whether the sympathy/commitment case is really my strongest illustration, or whether something like intransitive choice under framing effects would make the point more forcefully to an audience of economists rather than philosophers.
