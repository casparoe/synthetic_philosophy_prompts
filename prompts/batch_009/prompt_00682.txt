Ok, so this happened at Priya's 30th birthday dinner a couple weeks ago and it's still bugging me. Priya found a wallet on the subway that afternoon, stuffed with something like $600 cash, and instead of just pocketing it she tracked down the guy (there was a business card inside) and gave him back everything, cash included, no hesitation. Which would be a nice story except Priya is also the person who has spent literal years telling everyone, very sincerely, that returning a wallet full of cash isn't just dumb but actually the wrong move — her whole thing is that "owing" honesty to some random stranger you'll never see again is a fake obligation, and that people who hand the cash back are chumps who fell for a social script instead of thinking for themselves. She'll argue this with total conviction, stone sober, any day of the week. So naturally I asked her what happened on the subway, and she just shrugged and said something like "I dunno, I just felt like I had to give it back, it wasn't even really a decision."

This turned into a whole arguement with my friend Dan (he did a philosophy minor and never lets you forget it) about whether what Priya did actually reflects well on her. Dan's take is that it reflects less than we think, maybe nothing at all — since she wasn't acting on any principle she'd actually endorse if you asked her, he says it's basically just a reflex, closer to flinching than to a real moral choice, and if anything it's a little troubling that her actions and her convictions are that disconnected. I think it's the opposite: the fact that her decency overrode a view she genuinely holds seems like it should count in her favor, not against her, maybe even more than if she'd calmly reasoned her way to the same conclusion. We went back and forth for something like 45 minutes at the table and got nowhere. Who's actually got the better argument here, and is there some principled way to adjudicate it, or does it just come down to competing gut feelings about gut feelings?
