A few of us at work have been trading philosophy podcast recommendations, and one episode on free will got me stuck on Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility. As I understand it, the argument goes roughly like this: whatever you do follows from the way you are (your character, values, and desires at the moment of choice); to be truly, ultimately responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for being the way you are; but you can't be, because any attempt to have shaped your own character presupposes an earlier self who did the shaping according to some prior set of values, and that just pushes the question back a step, until you eventually hit bedrock in your genes, upbringing, and other things nobody ever chose. So ultimate moral responsibility turns out to be impossible.

What I can't get past is a point I saw mentioned in a couple of write-ups almost as an aside: apparently Strawson insists that this conclusion holds no matter whether determinism or indeterminism is true, so bringing quantum-level randomness into the picture doesn't rescue anyone. That surprised me, because in my day job I work with the distinction between deterministic pseudorandom generators and "true" hardware generators seeded by quantum noise, and those really do behave differently — the latter aren't just complicated functions of prior state, they introduce an actual break in the causal chain. So my instinct is that if some of the neural processes behind character formation or decision-making turned out to be genuinely indeterministic in that strong sense, rather than simply inherited from your genes and upbringing, the regress Strawson describes wouldn't have to terminate in unchosen necessity — it could terminate in a bit of genuine chance instead, which feels like it should count for something.

Is there a specific flaw in that reasoning, or a piece of the argument's structure I'm not appreciating? I don't have any formal philosophy training beyond podcasts and the occasional Wikipedia rabbit hole, so I'd genuinely like to understand why people who accept the Basic Argument treat the determinism/indeterminism distinction as beside the point — because on the face of it, "caused by factors you never chose" and "partly the product of genuine chance" seem like quite different situations to me.
