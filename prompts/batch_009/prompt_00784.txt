hey so I've been TAing a seminar on rational choice and every treatment of Parfit's hitchhiker I've read stops at the same move: "well obviously you pay once you're in town, because that's the disposition that got you saved" -- like that settles it. It doesn't, really. What actually bugs me is that this isn't a CDT-vs-EDT split the way Newcomb's problem is: once your standing in town, paying isn't evidence of anything you don't already know (the ride already happened), so EDT welches on the deal right along with CDT. So both "standard" act-based theories agree the hitchiker shouldn't pay. Write me an essay that takes that seriously -- actually weigh whether appeals to resolute choice, Gauthier's constrained maximization, or FDT's "treat your choice as the output of the predicted algorithm" move are doing legitimate philosophical work here, versus just quietly changing the question from "what should I do right now" to "what policy should I have," which smells question-begging. Then actually commit to an answer: does the hitchhiker pay, and does the case show practical rationality has to be assessed at the level of dispositions/policies rather than individual acts, or is "rational agents can predictably end up worse off" a bullet that CDT (and EDT) should just bite? Don't split the difference.
