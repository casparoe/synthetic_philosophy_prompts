So my Philosophy of Religion seminar has spent like two weeks on Molinism now, and I finally feel like I actually get the classic grounding objection – the problem of what makes it true that if S were in C, S would freely do A, given that there's no actual S in an actual C to ground it. But then after class I was talking it over with a friend from the seminar and went down a rabbit hole in the library, and it turns out there's a totally seperate objection - originally from Robert Adams, later picked up and defended by Hasker - that doesn't lean on grounding at all. It's this "explanatory priority" chain: the counterfactuals about how I'd choose are supposedly explanatorily prior to God's decision to create me, that decision is explanatorily prior to my existing at all, and my existing is explanatorily prior to anything I ever actually choose. Since explanatory priority is supposed to be transitive, it follows that the counterfactuals about my choices end up explanatorily prior to the choices themselves - totally backwards from how we'd normally think about it, which I guess is why there's a chapter in Flint's book with something like "vicious circle" in the title.

From what I can piece together, William Lane Craig's move against this argument is basically to say it equivocates - that "explanatorily prior" can't mean the exact same thing at each of the three links, so the premise that explanatory priority is transitive doesn't actually get you the conclusion.
