I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion in which we work through Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument, and a pointed question from one of my students last week left me realizing that I do not actually have a satisfying answer to a well-known objection to it. I would like your help thinking the objection through before I next face the class. Below, in the numbering that has become standard in the secondary literature (Sobel, Oppy, and Anderson, among others, all use essentially this scheme, which derives from the version of the proof that Gödel allowed Dana Scott to copy out in 1970), are the relevant definitions and axioms:

Definition 1: An entity x is God-like if and only if x possesses all positive properties.
Definition 2: A property φ is an essence of an entity x if and only if x has φ, and, necessarily, anything whatsoever that has φ also has every other property that x has.
Definition 3: An entity x exists necessarily if and only if, for every essence φ of x, it is necessary that something instantiates φ.

Axiom 1: For any property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive.
Axiom 2: Any property necessarily entailed by a positive property is itself positive.
Axiom 3: The property of being God-like is positive.
Axiom 4: If a property is positive, then it is necessarily positive, i.e., positive in every possible world.
Axiom 5: Necessary existence is a positive property.

From these five axioms and three definitions, Gödel derives, in turn, that every positive property is possibly exemplified, that a God-like being is therefore possible, that being God-like is an essence of anything that has it, and, finally, that a God-like being exists of necessity.

The objection that has always eluded me is the one first developed by Jordan Howard Sobel and discussed since by writers such as C. Anthony Anderson and Robert Koons: that this apparatus proves something far stronger than Gödel seems to have intended, since it generates a "modal collapse" in which the distinction between contingent and necessary truth disappears altogether and whatever merely happens to be the case turns out to be necessarily the case. I can follow each step of the derivation on its own without ever feeling that I grasp why, taken together, they force so drastic a result. Could you walk me through, in an intuitive rather than purely symbolic way, how the modal collapse argument actually runs, which axiom (or which feature of the definition of "essence") is doing the crucial work in generating it, and whether any of the emendations proposed in the literature strike you as genuinely successful at blocking the collapse without abandoning the spirit of Gödel's proof?
