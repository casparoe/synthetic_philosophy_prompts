A friend and I spent much of dinner last night arguing about Augustine's absolutism on lying, and neither of us came away persuaded by the other. I'd been reading De Mendacio ("On Lying") and the later Contra Mendacium ("Against Lying," his letter to Consentius) for a seminar on patristic ethics, and my friend's position was that once you get past the popular caricature of "never lie, no matter what," the actual doctrine holds together better than it's usually given credit for. I thought the opposite, and on the walk home I worked out a more specific version of my objection, which I typed up this morning. I'd like your honest comments on it.

Some background in case it helps: in De Mendacio, Augustine sorts lies into eight kinds and ranks them by gravity. Worst are lies told in defense of religious doctrine. Then, in descending order of badness, come lies that injure someone while benefiting no one, lies that benefit one party at another's expense, lies told from sheer delight in deceiving, lies told merely to flatter or please, lies that harm no one but protect someone's property, lies that harm no one but save a life, and finally lies that harm no one but protect someone's chastity - the mildest kind, yet still, on his view, a genuine sin that may never be committed. When he turns to the hardest cases scripture hands him - the Hebrew midwives who lied to Pharaoh, and Rahab, who lied to shelter the Israelite spies, both apparently rewarded by God - his resolution in Contra Mendacium is that they were rewarded "not because they lied, but because they were merciful to God's people," and he goes on to insist they would have earned a still greater reward by refusing to lie even at the cost of their lives, since no temporal loss can outweigh an eternal one.

Here, roughly, is the argument I wrote up. Augustine's absolute prohibition on lying seems to rest on two arguments he never clearly distinguishes from each other. The first is the "double heart" argument: lying is wrong because it splits what is in the liar's mind from what comes out of the liar's mouth, a corruption internal to the liar that has nothing to do with outcomes and so admits no exceptions. The second, which only surfaces once the hard cases force his hand, is a cost-benefit argument run over an eternal timescale: refusing to lie is always the better bet because whatever it costs in this life is negligible next to what is gained, or kept, in the next. These are not the same argument. The first makes the wrongness of lying independent of consequences altogether; the second is a frankly consequentialist calculation that simply relocates the relevant consequences into the afterlife. My suspicion is that the second argument is doing all the real work whenever the doctrine is actually tested, which would mean Augustine's absolutism is not grounded in the nature of lying itself, as the double-heart argument advertises, but in a separate and far more contestable theological premise about how temporal and eternal goods weigh against each other. I'd value your view on whether this is a genuine tension in the texts or one I could be talked out of, whether there's a natural way for a committed Augustinian to hold both arguments together without embarrassment, and, if you think the central claim survives scrutiny, how you'd sharpen or reformulate it.
