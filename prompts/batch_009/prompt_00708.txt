I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on the methodology for inferring how consciousness is distributed across species, and I've gotten stuck on the internal structure of Lloyd Morgan's canon. Here is the original 1894 statement, from An Introduction to Comparative Psychology:

"In no case may we interpret an action as the outcome of the exercise of a higher psychical faculty, if it can be interpreted as the outcome of the exercise of one which stands lower in the psychological scale."

When Morgan revised the book in 1903, he restated the canon and then immediately appended a qualification:

"In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes, if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development. To this, however, it should be added, lest the range of the principle be misunderstood, that the canon by no means excludes the interpretation of a particular activity in terms of the higher processes, if we already have independent evidence of the occurrence of these higher processes in the animal under observation."

What I can't work out is how much of the canon's original bite survives that qualification. If the verdict in a given case turns on whether we "already have independent evidence" of the higher process in that species, the substantive work seems to be done by whatever standard fixes when such evidence counts as already in hand, and "higher" and "lower" here track an antiquated scala naturae rather than any general comparison of simplicity. Could you reconstruct the argument connecting the two sentences, and tell me whether the canon, so qualified, still supplies a non-question-begging constraint on attributing consciousness to animals, or whether it merely relocates that dispute onto prior assumptions about which species already deserve the benefit of the doubt?
