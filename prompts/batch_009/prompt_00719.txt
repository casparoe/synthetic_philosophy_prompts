In my computability theory course this term, we recently wrapped up the technical core of the syllabus — Turing machines, the recursive/recursively-enumerable hierarchy, and the equivalence proofs linking Turing-computability, general recursiveness, and the lambda calculus — and my professor used the last lecture to step back from the formalism and raise a more philosophical question: what kind of claim is the Church-Turing thesis, and what exactly is its epistemic status? She laid out what I gather is the orthodox, Kleene-associated position: that the thesis can never be formally proved, since it identifies a precisely defined notion (Turing-computability) with an inherently pre-theoretic one (whatever a human being can compute by rote, following fixed instructions), so the best we can ever do is accumulate indirect evidence for it — Turing's own analysis of the atomic steps available to a human "computer," together with the fact that every independently motivated formalism anyone has proposed (general recursive functions, Post production systems, register machines, and so on) turns out to characterize exactly the same class of functions.

What's been nagging at me since then is something a TA mentioned in passing during office hours: apparently Saul Kripke has argued, in a relatively recent piece, that the thesis isn't actually unprovable at all — that it follows as a "special corollary" of Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, once one grants an assumption he attributes to Hilbert, namely that the individual steps of any computation, viewed as a form of mathematical deduction, can always be stated in a first-order language. I haven't been able to get my hands on more than secondhand summaries of the argument, and I'd like to actually understand it well enough to assess it rather than just cite it in a paper.

Could you reconstruct, as best you can, how Kripke's argument is supposed to work, and why it would make the Church-Turing thesis a corollary of an existing theorem rather than a free-standing, unprovable conjecture? I'm especially interested in the work being done by the "Hilbert's thesis" assumption, and in the most natural objection a defender of the traditional Kleene-style view might raise — namely, that this move doesn't so much prove the Church-Turing thesis as relocate the very same gap between an informal notion and its formal proxy one level down, into the equally unproven claim that computational steps are always expressible in first-order logic. If there's room, I'd also be curious how this whole debate bears on physical "hypercomputation" proposals — things like oracle machines or infinite-time Turing machines — which I've seen described as challenges to the Church-Turing thesis, though I'm never sure whether those are really targeting the same thesis Kripke and Kleene are arguing about, or a different, more physically loaded version of it.
