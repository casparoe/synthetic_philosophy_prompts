In my economics elective, we used the centipede game to illustrate backward induction, and a footnote about the "backward induction paradox" prompted me to read further on my own. From what I can tell, it traces to a 1989 paper by Pettit and Sugden, and the deeper question of whether common knowledge of rationality really implies backward induction was later disputed among Aumann, Binmore, and Stalnaker. What interests me isn't the familiar empirical point that real players rarely defect on the first move, but the logical puzzle underneath it: the standard argument for immediate defection assumes common knowledge of rationality at every node, including nodes that, by that same argument, should never be reached -- so once such a node is actually reached, it's unclear what a player can still infer or rationally do, since the assumption that justified predicting defection in the first place seems to have already been contradicted by the history of play. Write an essay that states this paradox precisely, weighs the strongest proposed resolutions, and closes with a definite verdict on whether backward induction remains the uniquely rational solution to the centipede game once the problem is taken seriously.
