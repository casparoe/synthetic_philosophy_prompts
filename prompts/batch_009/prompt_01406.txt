I've been following Anthropic's Transformer Circuits Thread and the discussion it generates on LessWrong, and the realist rhetoric that's grown up around it has started to bother me. Since "Toy Models of Superposition," the standard picture is that networks represent more features than they have neurons by encoding them as almost-orthogonal directions in activation space; sparse autoencoders are then used to partially undo this superposition and recover more "monosemantic" features, each supposedly corresponding to a single human-interpretable concept. Researchers routinely describe this as discovering what the network genuinely represents, not merely as a useful redescription of its weights.

Philosophers of cognitive science raised a structurally similar worry well before deep learning existed. William Ramsey's "job description challenge" asks whether positing a representation earns any explanatory purchase beyond redescribing a state as a reliable causal correlate of whatever it's said to represent; he argues that the "receptor" notion of representation — on which a unit represents whatever reliably causes it to fire — collapses into mere causal mediation and fails this test across most of neuroscience and connectionism, though he spends considerable effort contesting the natural rescue attempt on which a state earns representational status once some downstream "consumer" in the system treats it as a stand-in for what it detects.

Write an essay putting these two literatures in direct contact. Does the fact that clamping a single SAE feature (e.g., forcing the "Golden Gate Bridge" feature to fire, which was enough to make a model insist it was the bridge) reliably and predictably changes a model's downstream outputs give that feature a "consumer" robust enough to satisfy Ramsey's challenge, or is this still causal mediation dressed up in representational language? Weigh the strongest form of both positions, then close with a definite verdict on whether current interpretability practice has earned its realist talk of representations — don't just conclude that it depends on one's philosophy of science.
