So I'm taking a philosophy of religion seminar this semester and we just spent two weeks on Hume's essay on miracles, and I think I've basically got the argument down, but something isn't sitting right and my study group couldn't agree on it either. As I understand it: a miracle is defined as a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature just is whatever uniform, unvarying experience has established - Hume even calls this kind of evidence a "proof" at one point, which made me assume he means something like a logical or mathematical proof. So testimony for a miracle is automatically testimony against the single best evidence there could possibly be, which seems to mean Hume has basically handed us a knock-down proof that no miracle report could ever be true, kind of like proving a four-sided triangle can't exist - its ruled out by the definitions involved, before you even get to asking who the witnesses were or how many of them there were.

But if that's the argument, I don't get why Hume then spends this whole second half of the essay listing empirical reasons miracle testimony tends to be unreliable - witnesses being uneducated, people loving marvelous stories, miracles being weirdly concentrated among "ignorant and barbarous nations," rival religions' miracles cancelling each other out, etc. If part one already rules miracles out logically, none of that stuff about the actual quality of the witnesses should matter one way or the other, right? And to make it more confusing, my professor pointed out Hume's own thought experiment about the sun going dark for eight straight days in 1600: he says that if every historian in every country reported it, we SHOULD believe it happened and go looking for a natural cause - which sure sounds like sufficiently overwhelming testimony COULD establish something wildly outside the ordinary course of nature. But then barely a page later he says even if every English historian agreed that Queen Elizabeth died and came back to life a month afterward, he still wouldn't believe it, even granting the exact same quality of testimony. So which is it? Is Hume arguing that miracles are impossible in principle, or just that the specific testimony we happen to have for any real miracle claim never clears the bar? Those feel like pretty different claims and I can't tell which one his argument is actually supposed to be, or whether he's just being inconsistent.
