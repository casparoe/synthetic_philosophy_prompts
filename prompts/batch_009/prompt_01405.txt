Over the last year or so I've been teaching myself the basics of game theory and decision theory, mostly through blogs, a couple of popular books, and long detours through Wikipedia. I don't have any formal background in mathematics or economics, so all of this has been purely out of personal curiosity. A while back I came across Aumann's agreement theorem and the "common prior assumption" it rests on, and it has genuinely changed the way I think about the arguments I have with people. As I understand it, the theorem shows that if two honest, rational people start out with the same prior beliefs and then talk for long enough that each one's opinion becomes common knowledge between them, they cannot end up sticking to different conclusions — any disagreement that survives that process has to mean that somebody isn't reasoning correctly.

This has been weighing on me lately because I have a friend with a background in economics with whom I have long, good-faith arguments about things like the long-run effects of minimum-wage increases or the real causes of inflation. We each lay out our evidence and reasoning in detail, sometimes over several conversations, and yet we never actually converge. Given what I've read about Aumann's theorem, I've started to suspect that this failure to converge is itself proof that one of us is being irrational, self-deceived, or quietly declining to update on the other's arguments — since two fully rational people who share a prior and genuinely absorb each other's reasoning are supposed to end up in the same place.

So my question is this: is that really the right lesson to draw from the theorem when applied to a case like ours, and if so, is there some principled way to figure out which one of us is the irrational one, rather than each of us just assuming it's the other?
