So I'm in a phil of language seminar on conditionals this term, and we just spent three sessions on Lewis's 1976 triviality result and I honestly still can't decide what I think the upshot is supposed to be. Quick recap in case it's useful context: the "Adams/Stalnaker thesis" says the probability of "if A, B" just is P(B|A). Lewis shows that if you want that to hold as a fact about the actual probabilty of some proposition expressed by ">" -- i.e. you want it to come out true for every reasonable probability function, or even just for every function in a class closed under conditionalizing -- the function collapses into near-triviality (in the toy versions of the proof you basically end up forcing A and B to be probabilistically independent, which obviously isn't true of conditionals in general). Lewis's own moral was that the Equation is a fact about assertibility, not truth, so indicative conditionals dont express ordinary truth-evaluable propositions. My advisor is completely sold on that and treats it as basically proving some kind of non-cognitivism/suppositionalism about "if" (the Edgington line). My officemate, who does more formal semantics, keeps insisting there's real wiggle room: the "indexical" escape going back to van Fraassen and Stalnaker, where you only demand the Equation hold of a single actual credence function rather than a whole algebra of them, or the trivalent de Finetti-style semantics people like Rothschild have revived, and apparently some people now think the real culprit isn't the Equation at all but the Import-Export law doing the heavy lifting in the proofs.
