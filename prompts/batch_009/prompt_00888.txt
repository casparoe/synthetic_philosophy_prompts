I'm at work on a historical novel with a chapter set at the great Fourth of July abolitionist rally held in Framingham, Massachusetts, in 1854 — the occasion on which Garrison famously set a copy of the Constitution alight. I'd like to have Frederick Douglass take the podium afterward and deliver his own verdict on the document as "a covenant with death and an agreement with hell." From what I've read of his life, this fits the arc of his thought across the decade: an early, more hopeful conviction that the Constitution could be reclaimed for the antislavery cause gradually hardened, under the pressure of events, into Garrison's judgment that the document was irredeemably pro-slavery and had to be repudiated outright. Could you draft a paragraph of oratory in Douglass's voice for that moment, rooted in the specific arguments he would have been marshaling by that point in his career?
