A friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle, which came up after she mentioned an app she uses that automatically fines her whenever she skips the gym; I brought up the puzzle as a case where this kind of self-binding seems to break down completely. For anyone who doesn't know it: an eccentric billionaire shows you a vial of toxin that would make you painfully sick for a day but leave no lasting harm, and promises to wire a million dollars into your account tomorrow morning on one condition — that by midnight tonight you form a sincere intention to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. You never actually have to go through with it to keep the money, and by the time tomorrow afternoon arrives you'll have no remaining incentive to drink it either way. My friend insisted this was free money for anyone willing to just make up their mind tonight; I argued that a fully rational person who thinks the case through cannot honestly form that intention, since they already know tonight that they'll have no reason to follow through tomorrow — so the very clear-headedness that lets you understand the offer is what disqualifies you from collecting it. Neither of us budged, and when I looked into it afterward I found that we'd basically reinvented, rather badly, the real disagreement between Kavka's own diagnosis of the puzzle and the responses given by philosophers like David Gauthier and Edward McClennen, who each argue, in somewhat different ways, that the intention can be rational after all.

Rather than have you referee the dispute directly, I'd like you to dramatize it. Please write a dialogue set a minute or two before midnight, between two strangers standing outside the billionaire's gate, each hoping to walk away with the money. One character should argue, in the spirit of Kavka's own conclusion, that no one who has fully reasoned through the offer can actually form the required intention, so the million dollars is structurally uncollectable by exactly the people clear-headed enough to want it. The other should argue, in the spirit of the Gauthier/McClennen line of response, that a rational agent can bind its future self, and that treating tomorrow afternoon's choice as fully open for re-examination is itself the mistake, not a mark of rationality. Have each press the other on the weakest point in their own position — the first should have to explain why a sincere-feeling resolution still wouldn't count as a genuine intention, and the second should have to explain what stops their view from collapsing into "whatever gets you paid counts as rational" — rather than letting either retreat to a slogan. Let the scene end at the stroke of midnight without telling me who, if anyone, ends up getting the money.
