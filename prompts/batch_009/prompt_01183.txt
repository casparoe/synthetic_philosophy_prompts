I've been spending a good deal of time reading through LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, where Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem keeps surfacing as the standard formal justification for a specific, consequential claim: that a sufficiently rational AI system trying to act well on behalf of several humans should end up maximizing some fixed weighted sum of their individual utility functions, rather than aggregating preferences in some other, less principled way. What I only appreciated after chasing the footnotes further than most posts bother to go is how much work the common-prior assumption is quietly doing. Harsanyi's original 1955 result is stated over a shared set of objective lotteries; once you instead let the humans (and the aggregating system) hold genuinely different subjective probabilities over some uncertain state of the world, the whole program apparently runs into real trouble. I gather this is the substance of the Hylland–Zeckhauser and later Mongin impossibility results, though I've only ever seen them glossed at a fairly high level of abstraction — something like "Pareto plus Bayesian rationality plus disagreement yields dictatorship or incoherence" — and I'd like to actually see the mechanics for once.

To be clear, I'm not after the more familiar Diamond-style objection, in which two people share identical beliefs but a fair coin flip is wanted on grounds of equity, creating tension between ex ante and ex post Pareto; I already have a decent handle on that one, and it turns on fairness rather than disagreement. What I'd like instead is a fully worked, concrete example of your own construction, not a case borrowed from some textbook treatment, involving two people with explicit and differing subjective probabilities over a single binary uncertain event, together with explicit von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions. The example should show each person, reasoning solely from their own beliefs and utilities, unanimously preferring some available act (or a small menu of acts or trades) over some alternative, and the AI system honoring that unanimity trade by trade, exactly as asked. Yet no aggregator that combines the two individuals' utilities into a single fixed weighted sum and evaluates acts under one shared probability distribution should be able to reproduce that whole pattern of choices — without either misdescribing what actually happened, or producing an outcome that at least one of the two people, by their own lights, comes to regard as a mistake once the uncertain event resolves. Please supply actual numbers, walk through the calculation rather than just asserting the conclusion, and finish by saying plainly which piece of the Harsanyi setup — Pareto indifference, the requirement of a single aggregate probability, or something else — is doing the real work of generating the tension.
