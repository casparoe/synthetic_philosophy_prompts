I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student working primarily on the nonconceptual content debate (Evans, Peacocke, McDowell, and the running argument over whether perceptual experience has content that outstrips the concepts a perceiver possesses). A few weeks ago a visiting scholar gave a reading-group talk arguing that a strikingly similar fault line runs through classical Indian epistemology, more than a thousand years earlier, and that it turns on how to read a single line of Sanskrit in the Nyāya school's canonical definition of perception. I've been trying to track this down on my own ever since, but between the terseness of the sutra style and my complete lack of Sanskrit training, I keep getting stuck exactly where I most need help.

The passage is Nyāya-sūtra 1.1.4, Akṣapāda Gautama's definition of pratyakṣa (perception), usually transliterated as "indriyārthasannikarṣotpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam," and rendered along the lines of: "Perception is that cognition which arises from the contact of a sense-organ with its object, and which is non-verbal (avyapadeśya), unerring (avyabhicāri), and determinate or well-ascertained (vyavasāyātmaka)." What stops me cold is the combination of the second and fourth conditions: how can a single cognition be both "non-verbal" — unassociated with any name or word — and yet "determinate," i.e., a definite judgment like "this is a cow" rather than some inchoate awareness of a patch of color? The commentators seem to have felt the same pull in two directions. Vātsyāyana, writing in perhaps the fifth century, reads both adjectives as jointly describing one and the same kind of veridical perception, with "non-verbal" only ruling out words as a cause of the cognition rather than describing its content. Vācaspati Miśra, writing something like half a millennium later and, from what I gather, responding to Buddhist critics, instead reads the two adjectives as picking out two different stages or kinds of perception: a wordless, indeterminate awareness (what the later tradition calls nirvikalpaka) and a subsequent conceptually structured, determinate one (savikalpaka).

Could you help me get a firmer grip on this? I'd like to understand, first, what work each of the sutra's four conditions is doing on its own terms, before any of the later disputes; second, how exactly Vātsyāyana's and Vācaspati's readings of "avyapadeśya" and "vyavasāyātmaka" diverge, and what in the text (or in the wider dialectical situation) might have pushed Vācaspati toward positing a nonconceptual stage that the earlier reading didn't seem to require; and third, which reading strikes you as sitting more comfortably with the sutra itself and with how the Nyāya-sūtra goes on to define the other pramāṇas. If you can see a genuine philosophical parallel with the Sellars/McDowell worries about the "Myth of the Given" that got me started on this, I'd be glad to hear it, but I'd rather you tell me if the parallel is overstated than have you force it to fit.
