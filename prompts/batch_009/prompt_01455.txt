A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about David Lewis's short paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", which came up after a dinner-table conversation about substitutionary atonement grew more heated than either of us expected. Lewis's puzzle starts from an asymmetry in our intuitions: almost none of us thinks a murderer's devoted friend could permissibly serve the murderer's prison term, or his death sentence, in his place -- if such a thing ever happened, we would treat it as a scandal, not a solution. Yet almost all of us think it unobjectionable for a friend to pay someone else's fine, provided the offender consents. Lewis forestalls the easy dismissal that fines are not really punishments at all: he concedes that trivial fines (his example is the surcharge for parking two hours instead of one) are basically disguised user fees, but insists that some fines are "serious" in exactly the sense that matters -- they carry real opprobrium and impose burdens comparable to a prison sentence -- and substitution is tolerated for these too. Near the end of the paper, he floats an explanation of the asymmetry that I found genuinely persuasive: we tolerate substitution for fines only because it is impractical to police (nothing stops a friend from simply handing over the cash), and because fines just are debts in the literal sense, this practical tolerance lets the misleading metaphor of a "debt of punishment" take hold of our thinking about punishment generally -- which is how otherwise sensible people, including Christians who treat the Atonement as a case of penal substitution, end up committed to a principle they would reject out of hand in any ordinary criminal case. But Lewis then largely retracts this explanation: he argues that if it were correct, we should expect to hear people complain about fines specifically on the grounds that they invite this kind of abuse, and since that complaint is conspicuously absent from ordinary moral and legal discourse, he concludes the explanation is probably mistaken.

My friend thought Lewis gave up on his own diagnosis too quickly, and that what I will call the "sloppy thinking" hypothesis is in fact correct; I found myself defending Lewis's second thoughts instead. Rather than adjudicate the matter yourself, I would like you to build the strongest possible case for my friend's side. Specifically, please argue that Lewis is wrong to treat the absence of an explicit, free-standing complaint about fines' vulnerability to substitution as evidence against the sloppy-thinking hypothesis -- that is, explain why this particular kind of dissatisfaction might fail to surface in ordinary discourse even if the hypothesis is exactly right, such that its absence carries much less evidential weight than Lewis assumes.
