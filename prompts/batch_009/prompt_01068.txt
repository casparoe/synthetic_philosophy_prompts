I teach a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and this term we've been reading the classical epistemic argument for democracy together with its two most influential contemporary heirs: Estlund's attempt to defend a qualified epistemic proceduralism without sliding into epistocracy, and Brennan's use of much of the same apparatus to argue for epistocracy outright. One of my strongest students, who came to the program after a first degree in statistics, handed in a response paper this week whose central argument I haven't managed to fault, though something about it nags at me, and I'd like a second opinion before I write my comments on her draft.

Condensed, her argument runs as follows. Condorcet's jury theorem tells us that if voters decide independently and each is individually more likely than not to be correct, the probability that a majority verdict is correct converges to certainty as the electorate grows without bound. Epistocratic proposals restrict the franchise to the demonstrably competent, but doing so necessarily shrinks the electorate, and shrinking the electorate is exactly what forfeits the asymptotic guarantee that gives the theorem its force. So, on her reading, the very theorem that epistemic democrats and epistocrats both like to invoke actually tells against the epistocrat: any trade of quantity of voters for quality of voters must, by the theorem's own logic, move the resulting body further from certainty of a correct verdict, not closer to it. She takes this to be a freestanding mathematical objection to epistocracy, prior to and independent of the more familiar worries about who gets to decide who counts as competent.

I'd like to know whether her objection succeeds as stated and, if it doesn't, precisely where the inference breaks down. My own hunch is that she is smuggling in an assumption about how competence is distributed across enfranchised and disenfranchised voters alike that the theorem itself does nothing to license, but I would rather have the diagnosis worked out rigorously than rely on my hunch. I would also welcome a sense of whether this exact move has already been addressed in the jury-theorem literature (Grofman and Feld's work comes to mind, as does the exchange surrounding Estlund's own discussion of the theorem), or whether she has stumbled onto something the standard treatments pass over.
