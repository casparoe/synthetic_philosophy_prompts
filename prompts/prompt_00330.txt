I've been working through the literature on dynamic choice and diachronic consistency for a dissertation chapter, and I keep running into a conceptual knot I'd like help untangling. Edward McClennen's resolute choice theory and the updateless decision theory (UDT) developed in the AI safety/rationalist literature (Yudkowsky, Hintze, and others) both seem motivated by a similar worry: that agents who revise their plans in light of new information can be exploited across time (money-pumped, or made to defect against their own past selves) in ways that a suitably "committed" agent would avoid. Both frameworks recommend sticking to a plan formed at an earlier point even when, from a later vantage point, deviating looks locally rational.

That said, I suspect the two views rest on quite different background commitments. McClennen's resolute choice is pitched within a broadly Kantian, agent-centered picture of practical rationality, and doesn't obviously require any revisionary metaphysics of decision-making -- it just asks the agent to treat her earlier self's plan as binding. UDT, by contrast, is typically formulated in terms of an agent selecting a global policy (a function from observations/inputs to actions) prior to any updating at all, which seems to presuppose a much more radical picture on which later "selves" or epistemic states have no independent standing to deviate from the policy chosen "at the beginning of time." I'd like to get clearer on whether this difference is substantive or merely a difference in framing. Could you compare resolute choice theory and updateless decision theory in some depth -- particularly how each would handle something like the toxin puzzle or Newcomb-like cases such as counterfactual mugging -- and say whether UDT should be understood as a formalization (or descendant) of resolute choice, or whether it's making a genuinely different theoretical claim about the nature of rational agency and the unit of rational evaluation (acts vs. policies vs. dispositions)?
