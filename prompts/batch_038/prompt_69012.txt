I am in my final year of secondary school and have been working on a long essay about how states borrow their history, largely because I kept encountering the claim that reasoning by historical analogy is the most dangerous habit in politics. What has unsettled me is how little the two camps in that argument appear to disagree about once you write down what each of them actually asserts.

One camp treats analogy as inescapable and, with discipline, as a genuine instrument. I have in mind the tradition running through Neustadt and May's Thinking in Time, and Khong's Analogies at War, which separates the several jobs a precedent performs at once — characterizing the present situation, marking its moral stakes, recommending a course of action, warning about the consequences of that course — and then grades the Munich analogy behind the 1965 Vietnam decisions against each job in turn. The other camp is skeptical: analogies are drawn from whatever is locally salient, they are recruited after an inclination has already formed, and a single episode reliably yields rival lessons pointing in opposite directions, which is what happened when the appeasement lesson and the quagmire lesson were both invoked in support of the same Gulf intervention thirty years apart. The skeptics say the disciplined procedures are a fig leaf that makes motivated reasoning look like method. The defenders say the documented failures are failures of practice, not of the practice, and that the discipline catches errors the unaided mind misses.

Here is my difficulty. Both camps seem to accept that decision-makers reach for precedents spontaneously and cannot be switched off; that the available stock is small and nationally idiosyncratic; that precedents are usually selected to support a leaning already formed; and that explicit procedures — enumerating likenesses and differences, reconstructing what the earlier actors knew and when they knew it — do surface some mistakes. If all of that is common ground, what is actually in dispute?

Three hypotheses, which I would like tested rather than flattered. First, that these are two different questions sharing a heading: a normative one about whether history could be used well and a descriptive one about how it is in fact used, with each side hearing the other as answering its own. Second, that a hidden empirical question is doing the work and nobody poses it — whether disciplined analogical analysis moves expert judgment at all relative to unaided intuition, and by how much — a question that looks answerable in principle yet has never been posed in a way either side accepts as answerable. Third, that the impasse signals an ill-posed question, since reliability in this sense is not a property any ampliative reasoning has, making the whole exchange a displaced restatement of an older quarrel about induction.

One piece of evidence that made me suspicious of myself: I ran the disciplined procedure on the 1938 and 1965 cases and arrived at the conclusion I had held beforehand. I cannot tell whether that means the procedure worked, that I performed it badly, or that its output is simply fixed by which differences I judged to be load-bearing. Is that a defect peculiar to analogy, or is that what all case-based reasoning looks like from the inside?

I would also like the diagnosis to survive a check I have not been able to run myself. Consider the Thucydides trap argument: defenders hold that the record of twelve wars out of sixteen historical pairings of a rising power with a ruling power identifies a structural regularity, while critics hold that the figure is an artifact of how the pairings were selected. On my reading that sub-debate is a different species again — a quarrel about the denominator rather than about the mechanism. A good diagnosis of the analogy debate should predict whether that is so, or explain what structure the two disputes share that I am missing.

What I want back is the diagnosis, argued for, not a survey of the positions. Tell me which of my three hypotheses best fits the evidence in what each side says, or propose a better one, and be specific about what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a study design, a formal result, a change of question — or make the reasoned case that no progress should be expected and why. I would like you to commit to a diagnosis rather than keeping all three alive as options, and to mark clearly where you are confident and where you are extrapolating beyond what you know. Finally: if you think my framing is itself confused — if the two camps I describe do not exist as camps, if what I have called common ground is exactly what is contested, or if this is simply the wrong way to put the question — please say so plainly and argue it, instead of answering the question you think I meant to ask.
