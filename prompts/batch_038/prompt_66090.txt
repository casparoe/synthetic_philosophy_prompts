Write a dialogue of roughly 1,100 words between two researchers who disagree about whether the presumption of independence — defaulting to an estimate of E[XY] as the product E[X]E[Y] whenever nothing is known about the correlation between X and Y — can be formalized into an account of heuristic mathematical evidence.

One is designing a heuristic estimator, a procedure that assigns numerical estimates to mathematical claims, and wants it to satisfy three conditions: it never estimates a conjunction above either of its conjuncts; it defaults to the product whenever no correlation is known; and it recovers the true asymptotic density whenever that density is actually known. The other holds that no procedure meeting all three can also refuse to license spurious arguments, and brings two test cases. The first is the naive product estimate for the claim that n, n+2, and n+4 are simultaneously prime, which predicts infinitely many such triples although the only one is 3, 5, 7. The second is Maier's theorem, which shows that Cramér's random model of the primes gets the count of primes in very short intervals wrong.

Both should argue at full strength, with genuine back-and-forth rather than alternating speeches. In particular they must engage, not dodge, with whether the local correction for divisibility by three is a principled fix available before the fact or an ad hoc rescue visible only in hindsight, and with whether the presumption of independence is a claim about the integers that could turn out false or merely a rule about who bears the burden of proof. End with neither side conceding, but with a precise statement of what the designer would have to establish for the project to survive.
