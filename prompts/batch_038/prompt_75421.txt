I'm sitting in on a perception seminar this semester (not my department, I just like arguing) and the prof basically opened by announcing that the cognitive penetration debate is over and the answer is no — everything that looks like beliefs changing percepts is either response bias, perceptual learning, or people being clever about what the experimenter wants. A bunch of us don't buy it and last week's section turned into a shouting match, so I started building a little scoring sheet of my own to force people to actually commit to numbers instead of vibes. I'm calling it the demand-adjusted penetrability index, which is a pretentious name, but the idea is that each paradigm gets scored by how much of its effect would survive a redesign where the participant has no motive to report anything particular and no cue about the expected answer, and reports through forced-choice discrimination or a continuous matching dial instead of a verbal label.

Here are the eight studies/paradigms that keep getting thrown around in the room, described as neutrally as I can manage:

A) Tachistoscopic playing cards where a few cards have been doctored so the suit and colour don't go together, e.g. a red six of spades. At brief exposures people overwhelmingly report the normal card, and correct-recognition thresholds are much higher for the doctored ones; some people report weird compromises like a purple six.

B) Cut-out shapes all dyed exactly the same shade of red — a heart, lips, a flag, plus abstract blobs — and people adjust a comparison patch until it matches the shape. The heart gets matched to a more saturated red than the blob does.

C) A picture of a banana whose colours have been shifted so the banana is physically achromatic. When people adjust the tint until it "looks grey," they overshoot toward blue, which suggests the genuinely grey banana is seen as yellowish.

D) Speakers of a language with two basic terms for blue (a light and a dark one) vs. speakers of a language with one term, doing a speeded two-alternative discrimination between swatch pairs. The two-term group is faster across the lexical boundary than within it, by the same physical distance, and the advantage goes away when they're doing a verbal side-task but not a spatial one.

E) Computer-morphed faces all held to exactly the same luminance. The versions that read as more Black get rated as darker-skinned than the versions that read as more White, and the effect also shows up in matching tasks rather than just ratings.

F) Athletes in the middle of a good performance report the ball as bigger and the target as closer than athletes who are slumping; same family includes hills reported as steeper when carrying a heavy pack.

G) A genuinely bistable figure — the thing that reads as either a B or a 13, or a horse or a seal — where which reading dominates shifts toward whichever one is attached to a reward or to something the participant wants.

H) Highly suggestible people given a post-hypnotic suggestion that the words on the screen aren't meaningful, showing a big drop in Stroop interference compared to the same people unsuggested.

For each of the eight, give me a number 0–10 on the index above, plus a second number 0–10 for how much of the effect can be explained by plain perceptual learning or attentional modulation without ever invoking beliefs, desires or concepts. Then give the overall ranking, most to least convincing as evidence that perceptual experience itself — what it's like to see the thing, not just what you'd say about it — is being changed by what you know. On top of that: which single one would you sacrifice first if you had to defend the "no penetration" line, which one do you think the skeptics are being actively unfair to, and which one, if it survived every control they could invent, would force the early-vision-is-encapsulated view to genuinely give ground rather than just bolt on another epicycle. And please flag explicitly any case where you think the paradigm is ambiguous between "changes the percept" and "changes the report of the percept," because I suspect I'm guilty of lumping a few of those together.
