I'm TA-ing a second-year game theory section for philosophy students — 22 of them, one 75-minute class — and we've just finished two weeks of iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies, all done with pure strategies only. They've completely internalized the procedure as "compare each strategy against each other single strategy, cross out the losers, repeat." I want an in-class game where that habit visibly fails them: a matrix where the deletion stalls, everything looks tidy and settled, and the strategy that still has to go is strictly dominated *only* by a randomization over two of the others — so the thing that kills it isn't any strategy anyone would think to name.

Design the whole thing end to end.

What I need:

- They hit the stall on their own. No pre-brief that names anything, and no hint that mixing is on the table until they've been stuck long enough to be annoyed by it.
- Give me the actual matrix. Three by three is fine, four by three if you need the room. Small integer payoffs so it fits on an index card and on one slide. Then show the checking work explicitly: demonstrate cell by cell that the doomed strategy is *not* strictly dominated by any single other strategy, and exhibit the mixing weights that do dominate it with the arithmetic for every column. If you can pick weights that aren't 50/50 so nobody stumbles into the answer by guessing, do that.
- The game gets actually played, not just solved on paper. Give me a mechanism where pairs physically commit to a randomization — cards, dice, folded slips in a cup — so a mixed strategy is something they hold rather than something they say. And build in at least one round where the realized outcome flatly contradicts the expected payoff, because that confusion is coming and I'd rather stage it than have it ambush me.
- Payoff scheme with no real money, but something they actually want. Not participation credit, not candy if you can help it.
- A minute-by-minute timeline, including roughly where the room should be at each transition and how long you let them thrash before intervening.
- The debrief, mapping particular moments from the activity onto the concepts: why the order of deletion doesn't matter here but would if we'd been using weak dominance instead; why "survives deletion" and "is a best response to some belief" are not automatically the same slogan; and the gap between a mixture that strictly dominates and one that merely ties, since at least three of them will conflate those.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My guesses: someone declares domination on a tie, someone randomizes with a thumbtack or a die they assume is fair, and one pair plays the doomed row, wins the round, and is now immovably convinced it was correct. Tell me the failure modes I haven't thought of and how to handle each one live.

One thing I'm genuinely unsure about, and I'd rather you check it than paper over it: I believe "never a best response to any belief" and "strictly dominated by some mixed strategy" line up exactly in two-player games, but that the equivalence has to do with whether the beliefs are allowed to be correlated, and that with three or more players it can come apart. Tell me whether the matrix you give me is safe to describe both ways at the board, and if it isn't, what I should say instead. I do not want to teach something false in the last ten minutes when I'm improvising.

Also: we already ran a guess-two-thirds-of-the-average game earlier in the term, so please don't hand me another version of that.

And be frank about what the activity *can't* reproduce. Physically rolling a die in front of a partner is not the same thing as a mixed strategy being part of a plan, and I don't want to oversell the exercise to myself. Tell me which feature of the idea has to come from me talking rather than from them playing, and give me the two or three sentences that carry it.
