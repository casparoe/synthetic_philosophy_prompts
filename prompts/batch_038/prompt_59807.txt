I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and for years my attitude to models was basically the Sequences one: the map isn't the territory, distrust the pretty number. Now I have a job where that suspicion has to become operational, and I'm finding that free-floating scepticism is worth nothing in a meeting.

Setup: I review cost-effectiveness models for a small grantmaker. Four to six spreadsheets a month, each gating somewhere between £30k and £400k, and my verdict goes to a five-person committee that's mostly non-quantitative. I get about three hours per model. I can open the file, change an assumption, watch the output move — I can't generate any new evidence, and I have no formal economics training. The committee wants one of "use it", "use it with these caveats", "don't use it", plus a paragraph readable in two minutes.

The reason I'm asking here rather than on an econ forum is that its the philosophy-of-modelling literature that seems to have the distinction I need, and nobody in my office has read any of it. Concretely: Friedman's F-twist — the claim that wildly false assumptions are no objection to a theory and may even mark it as significant, because only predictive accuracy matters. Model builders wave this at me constantly, and the rebuttals I can produce on my own are either too weak ("but the assumptions are obviously false!") or too strong (they'd condemn every useful model ever built). Two half-formed thoughts I want you to sharpen or kill:

- Nearly every model I review makes a claim about a counterfactual that will never be tested. No holdout, nobody will ever run the experiment that checks the key output. So "judge it by its predictions" seems to lose all content exactly where I need it. Is that right, or is there a way the F-twist still bites?
- The billiard-player / surviving-firms move depends on some selection process out there making agents behave *as if* the model were true. Charity programmes don't have one. So maybe the usable question isn't "is this assumption realistic?" but "is there anything in this setting that would make the as-if come out true?"

There's also a failure mode I keep hitting and don't have a name for, so I've started calling it assumption laundering: the model itself contains no dodgy assumption, but a key parameter is imported from another study estimated under a completely different assumption set, so the dodginess arrives through the citation rather than through the model.

What I want is a procedure my team can actually run in three hours: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where it terminates in each of the three verdicts. It has to (1) separate assumptions that are false but irrelevant to the output in the range that matters, from ones that are load-bearing, from ones that are fine inside the model's intended domain but are being applied outside it — because I suspect only the first is what the F-twist can defend, if anything; (2) contain a step for replying to a builder who invokes Friedman, and say when that reply genuinely succeeds, so we don't flag everything unrealistic by reflex; (3) say what to do when you can't run the numbers at all, e.g. the model arrives as a PDF; and (4) mark every point that is really a judgement call in a procedure costume — including where the question turns out to be about values (which outcome metric, what discount rate, how much weight on the tail) and so has to go to the committee rather than to me.

Then run it on three invented cases. (A) A cash transfer model assuming 100% of the grant is spent on the target good and that an effect measured at 12 months continues linearly in perpetuity; a sensitivity run shows the perpetuity assumption moves the headline figure by a factor of three, the fungibility one by 4%. (B) A job training model where people enrol iff expected earnings gain exceeds cost — i.e. as-if expected utility maximisers — and observed take-up is 92%, which the builder says validates the assumption. (C) A model from an organisation I like and have donated to personally, assuming zero administrative leakage and constant input prices, assumptions I think are flatly false, but where a sensitivity run shows they move the result by 2%. I want at least one verdict I'm going to hate, and I want you to say plainly what the procedure can't do.

British English, please. No reading list and no citations — I can't look anything up and it'd be decoration. If a distinction you use has a standard label, give me the label plus one sentence on what it means; that's plenty.
