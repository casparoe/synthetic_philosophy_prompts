I'm chairing an author-meets-critics session at our department's workshop next month. The guest defends a view he calls tractabilism: an appeal to what could be done "in principle" earns its keep only if the relevant task is feasible in polynomial time. From that he draws three conclusions I want the session to actually test — (i) normative theories that quantify over fully idealized agents aren't so much false as unintelligible, (ii) some possibility claims doing quiet work in thought experiments (a molecule-for-molecule duplicate assembled by chance, a machine stumbling onto a satisfying assignment to a hard combinatorial constraint) are not possibilities at all, and (iii) you don't understand a proof you can check but couldn't feasibly find, so there's a real class of checkable-but-unexplained truths.

Draft eight to ten questions for me. At least one should hit worst-case versus average-case hardness, since plenty of NP-hard problems get solved routinely on the instances that actually matter, and one should hit the awkward fact that the whole framework is hostage to an open problem. I also want one on "feasible for whom" — the agent, her species, a well funded lab in 2050 — and somewhere in there the hardware-constants worry: if feasibility tracks asymptotics rather than concrete resource bounds, why is asymptotics the philosophically intresting level? For each question give me the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. His favorite move is retreating from a claim about the metaphysics of explanation to the claim that he's only talking about discovery, so build the sequence so that retreat has to cost him either (iii) or the normative upshot, not nothing.

Dont give me softball openers about how he got interested in complexity. And at the end, tell me where you think you've probably got him wrong or built a strawman, and what would change your mind about which two of these questions deserve the most airtime.
