I'm TA-ing a grad reading group on the metaphysics of modality and we've blown three sessions on the possible-worlds debate, mostly because nobody can agree what the disagreement even is. Half the room thinks everything hinges on what worlds are made of. The other half thinks that's a red herring, and the real issue is whether "actually" picks out a metaphysically special property or just marks where the speaker happens to be standing.

So I want to build a sorting instrument to give out in week one next term: a decision tree where a student follows the path and lands on a named view without ever having to know the name beforehand. Audience is first-years and strong seniors — they're comfortable with first-order logic and with talk of sets and sentences, but they haven't read the literature and I don't want them to need to.

What I need:

- Six to eight branch nodes, and roughly ten leaves. Every node has to be answerable from a concrete case, never from a definition. Which means no node should read like "do you think possible worlds are concrete entities?" — instead something along the lines of: there could have been blue swans; does that require there to be, somewhere, blue swans, or is it only a fact about what the ingredients of this world could have been rearranged into?
- It has to sort on at least these four dimensions, in whatever order you think is cheapest: what worlds are made of (concrete sums of things / sentences or propositions or states of affairs / nothing at all, just a useful way of talking); whether "actual" works like "here"; whether one and the same thing can be in more than one world or only has counterparts in the others; and whether the space of worlds is guaranteed to be as full as it could be (some recombinaton-style plenitude principle) or only as full as our means of describing it.
- One node I especially want, because it's what wrecked our last meeting: the complaint that a world built out of sentences cant represent possibilities involving properties that nothing actual ever instantiates, and can't tell apart possibilities that differ only in how big an infinity they contain. Where does that objection bite in the tree, which leaves are meant to be immune to it, and does being immune cost them something else?
- Leaves: Lewis's own view, Adams-style linguistic ersatzism, Plantinga's states-of-affairs version, Armstrong's combinatorialism, and Rosen's fictionalism all need to show up as named destinations, plus whatever else you think earns a leaf. Where a combination of answers has no defender at all, keep it as an open cell and diagnose it — incoherent, unappealing, or just unclaimed.
- Be honest about the tree's limits: point out any two leaves it genuinely can't separate, and either give the extra question that would separate them or say there isn't one.
- Each leaf also gets a line on its most uncomfortable upshot, and a line saying which single node it answers differently from its nearest neighbor.

Please write the whole thing in American English, and keep each node to two sentences or less so it fits on a handout.
