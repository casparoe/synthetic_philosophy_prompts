In three weeks I'm giving a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on personal identity, and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of my view.

My thesis: the Buddhist reductionist argument against the self — decompose the person into the five aggregates, note that nothing over and above them answers to the name "self," conclude that the person is real only in the way a chariot is — is not merely a colorful ancestor of the contemporary reductionist view but a strictly stronger one. It is stronger because it disposes of the two objections that sink the standard reductionism: the charge that if identity is nothing but psychological continuity, I have no more reason to care about my own future suffering than about a stranger's; and the charge that reductionism leaves no subject to whom experience belongs. My reply in both cases is that these objections are demands for a referent at the ultimate level of description, and there isn't one to give. Persons and prudential concern are perfectly real — conventionally real, real enough to bear weight, to be harmed, to be liberated — and the questions are malformed, not unanswered. No self needs to be missing for anything to go wrong, because nothing was ever supposed to be there at that level in the first place.

The room is about twenty people. Roughly half are analytic metaphysicians working on identity, most sympathetic to reductionism but allergic to anything that smells of quietism or of a two-level escape hatch; one is an animalist who holds that I am this human organism and will not be impressed by talk of levels of description. Two are Buddhist studies scholars — one on Pali canonical and commentarial material, one on later Indian Buddhist philosophy of mind — and neither approaches the material as analytic philosophers do. One is a cognitive neuroscientist who studies self-representation, bodily ownership, and the sense of mineness.

Give me the eight or nine objections this room will actually produce, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper, each phrased in the voice of the person most likely to raise it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft and what the follow-up will be. Then tell me: what to concede in the opening five minutes so the metaphysicians don't spend the whole period on the two-level move; which single reply has to land for the argument to survive the hour; and the question I'm hoping nobody asks, with what I'd say if it comes anyway.
