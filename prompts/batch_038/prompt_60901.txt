Context: I run a Sunday rationality meetup, 12–16 people, mixed bag — a couple of philosophy grad students, several programmers who read way too many old sequences, some total newcomers who came because a friend dragged them. 90 mins. I want an in-class game about why promises actually bind, and I have a specific thesis I want the game to *test in play* rather than have people nod along to.

Here's the weeds part. I think there are three candidate engines that could be doing the work when a promise obligates you:

1. **Reliance** — the promisee has already spent something on the strength of your word (time, money, a foregone option), so breaking it hurts a specific person who counted on you.
2. **Reputation** — breaking it makes you the kind of person whose word is worthless, and everyone's future dealings with you get worse.
3. **Transfer** — when you promise, you hand over a claim on one of your own future actions, and that claim is now *theirs*: only they can release you. Breaking it isn't so much causing harm as taking back a thing you gave away.

My bet is that 3 is doing nearly all the real work and 1 and 2 are just the felt phenomenology that makes 3 visible. I know that's a weirdly specific thesis to smuggle into a Sunday meetup, but I've been chewing on it for months and I'd rather get it stress-tested than get agreement.

What makes me most confident about 3: promises where 1 and 2 are both switched off still feel binding. Promise a dying person you'll scatter their ashes somewhere specific; they die; nobody will ever find out whether you did it, and nobody is left who's made worse off by the outcome. Intuitively you're still on the hook — and it feels like the *estate*, somebody else, now holds the right to let you off. I'd love the game to have a round where something structurally like that lands on a player without warning.
