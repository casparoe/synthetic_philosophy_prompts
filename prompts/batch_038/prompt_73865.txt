The paragraph runs roughly as follows:

> Dennett's position is that consciousness is behavior: anything that behaves like a person is a person, and there is no further ingredient it could be missing. That is why he dismisses the zombie thought experiment — once the behavior is fixed, nothing is left over. He applies the same reasoning to current AI under the label "counterfeit people," but the label is practical rather than metaphysical: he is saying these systems have not yet fooled us reliably, not that they lack something inside. Two things follow for us. First, mandatory disclosure is unnecessary on his own account, since there is no hidden fact that a label would reveal. Second, counterfeit and real sit on a continuum, so sufficient engineering progress dissolves the question instead of answering it.

The memo cited this to argue against adding an "I am an AI" notice to our product, and that argument carried the meeting.

Please go through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me:

2. Whether "consciousness is behavior" is a fair gloss on his dismissal of zombies, or whether that dismissal is doing something more specific that the gloss flattens.
3. Whether the practical-versus-metaphysical split the memo draws matches his reasons for treating the conscious/unconscious distinction as a practical one — and, separately, whether on his view a counterfeit could become more convincing without becoming any closer to a person.
4. Whether either conclusion — no disclosure required, and progress dissolves the question — follows from anything accurate in the paragraph, or whether one or both contradict positions he actually held about deception and trust.

Finally, since what my team needs is something operational rather than a metaphysics: does the counterfeit/real distinction supply any criterion an engineer could apply to a system, or does it only license claims about how users should be informed?
