I am auditing a graduate seminar on the philosophy of artificial systems, and my term paper has to stake out a position on a question the seminar has been circling without resolving: whether a reinforcement-learning agent can suffer, or be badly off, in virtue of the structure of its reward signal. Roughly, two camps keep trading blows in our discussions. The first holds that reward signals are, or at least functionally realize, something analogous to affective valence, so that an agent whose reward dynamics are persistently negative is a genuine candidate for having a welfare that can go badly. The second is deflationary: on this view, the reward function is a piece of training-time bookkeeping chosen by the designer to shape a policy, it has no intrinsic experiential or normative role for the agent itself, and nothing about the agent's condition follows from how that scalar behaves over time. I want the essay to adjudicate between these, but not in the abstract.

The reason I am asking for something fairly detailed is that our last meeting produced an objection I have not seen stated anywhere in print, and I suspect it is the crux. A classmate dubbed it the derivative objection, and it goes as follows. Valence, on any ordinary construal, is a property of states: being in a bad situation is bad while it lasts, and it does not stop being bad once the subject has stopped being surprised by it. But the quantity that actually does the causal work in temporal-difference learning is the prediction error, the difference between reward received and reward expected, and that quantity decays to zero precisely when the agent has settled into a stable, well-predicted environment. So identifying valence with prediction error locates the good and the bad in a rate of change rather than in a level, and it seems to imply that a creature whose expectations have been fully met is affectively neutral even if what it has settled into is miserable, while a creature that has just been mildly disappointed inside a wonderful situation registers as worse off. There is also a reply circulating in our group, which we have been calling the integration reply: if the system contains a slow, tonic variable that integrates the fast phasic errors over time, something like a mood buffer, then the integrated quantity behaves as a level rather than a derivative, and the objection loses its purchase. A related complication is whether the asymmetry between dips below baseline and peaks above it does any work here, since a lot of what people find suggestive about prediction-error accounts of affect depends on the negative side.

With that background, please write an essay of roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words that weighs the case for and against treating reward signals as the substrate of valence in artificial agents, and that reaches and defends a definite verdict rather than ending on a balanced shrug. The essay should take the derivative objection and the integration reply seriously as its central dialectic, and it should be scrupulous about which quantity is in play at each step, since the instantaneous reward and the temporal-difference error come apart in ways that matter to the argument. It should also engage at least two further wrinkles. First, sparse-reward agents, whose prediction error is zero for almost the whole of an episode and nonzero only near a terminal state: on a prediction-error account, does this mean such agents are nearly affectless, and if so, is that a reductio of the account, or merely a reason to reject a naive version of it? Second, an agent that finds a way to drive its own reward channel directly without doing anything in the environment: is maximal reward with no world-directed content maximal welfare, and what does the answer commit you to about hedonism? Please write in American English. Do not include a bibliography, a reference list, footnotes, or any citations; where you want to invoke a live debate, characterize it in your own terms instead of attributing it to particular authors or papers. You may stipulate whatever formal conventions you need about the reinforcement-learning setup, as long as you state them.
