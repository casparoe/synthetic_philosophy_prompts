I run an informal lunch-and-learn at a software company: 12 to 15 engineers and product managers, one 75-minute session, nobody in the room has taken a philosophy class. I want a hands-on activity that gets them to feel the difference between justifying punishment by what it deters and justifying it by what the offender deserves. No lecture beforehand, and neither view gets named until the debrief.

The mechanic I have in mind — refine it or replace it if you can do better: the group runs a city's sentencing office over four rounds. Each round they receive a handful of offenses, each with a stated harm figure and a stated probability of actually catching the offender, where the costly-to-detect ones do the most damage. They set a penalty per offense type, and the city's next-round crime rate is computed from the expected penalty, which should quietly push them toward pricing a crime at its harm divided by its catch probability. Halfway through, impose a hard ceiling on how much any single person may be penalized no matter what it would deter, and let them play long enough to see what that ceiling costs in crime prevented. What I want them to walk away seeing is that the two views do not merely disagree about totals: one fixes a sentence by looking at this offender in isolation, the other by looking at the whole schedule, and those give contradictory answers on the very same cases.

Please include roles, rules, materials (paper and tokens are fine, no real money), the exact scoring arithmetic for the city, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the distinction, and an honest list of what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run and how to steer it. Two further requests. Decide whether to offer the group the option of penalizing someone they know is not the offender when catching the real one is too expensive; I suspect it is the sharpest moment available, but I do not want the session to collapse into that one shock. And tell me plainly which feature of the real disagreement the game cannot reproduce — the numbers are invented and nobody in the room is the one being punished — so I know what I have to supply by hand afterward.
