A reading group I belong to closed its term on distributive justice with an argument none of us can accept and none of us has been able to dismantle. It starts from the familiar complaint against telic egalitarianism and ends somewhere considerably further out.

(1) One outcome cannot be better than another in any respect unless, in that respect, it is better for someone who exists in both outcomes. A theory that denies this is committed to a kind of improvement that could never amount to anyone's life going better, and that is not a kind of improvement at all.

(2) The leveling-down objection to egalitarianism is just (1) applied to equality. When the well-off are brought down to the level of the badly off and nothing else changes, equality has increased while no one's life has improved. So the egalitarian who insists that the leveled-down outcome is better in one respect is asserting precisely what (1) forbids.

(3) The prioritarian cannot treat (2) as an objection that merely happens to work against equality. What the prioritarian holds is that the only distributively relevant feature of an outcome is how much well-being people have, with the well-being of the worse off counting for more, and their complaint about equality is that a relation between levels is the wrong sort of thing to matter. That complaint is (1) functioning as a general constraint on value, not as a local point about one rival view.

(4) If (1) is a general constraint, it costs considerably more than the prioritarian expects. Bringing an additional person into existence with a life well worth living is better for no one who exists in the alternative, so a world containing that person is in no respect better than a world without them. The extinction of a species whose members would have flourished, or the burning of the only remaining copy of a manuscript that no one will now ever read, is in no respect a loss. And since it is not better for anyone to be brought into a happy life than never to have existed, the asymmetry we want between creating happy people and creating miserable ones can no longer be grounded impersonally at all.

(5) Nor is (1) only an external threat. Suppose the weighting is applied to how well off a person is at a time, as it has to be if the priority view is to say anything about when in a life a burden should fall — a view indifferent between two lives with the same total but different shapes cannot advise us about relieving suffering now rather than later. Then, since the weighting is concave, a fixed lifetime total spread evenly across time is in one respect better than the same total with a deep trough early on, because a benefit matters more the worse off its recipient is, and the recipient is worse off during the trough. But that respect is not better for the person, whose life goes exactly as well overall in the two cases, taking lifetime well-being to be nothing more than the sum of how well its moments go. The weighting function therefore reproduces the objection at home, in a form the prioritarian cannot dismiss by pointing to how well people's lives go.

(6) So the prioritarian's escape from leveling down is not free. Either (1) is given up, in which case (2) loses its force and the egalitarian's claim that the leveled-down outcome is better in one respect is reinstated, or (1) is kept, in which case prioritarianism inherits everything in (4) and cannot block the self-application in (5). Either way, an objection that was supposed to be decisive against egalitarianism proves too much to leave the priority view standing as the safe alternative.

For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (2), since I still think something goes right about the leveling-down case, and I would like to keep some version of the thought that value has to be anchored in people's lives rather than in patterns. I am least attached to (3) and to the intrapersonal move in (5). My preferences are not reasons, though, and I do not want an answer that simply ratifies them.

Tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it goes wrong somewhere, and where it is sound but expensive. I want to know which single step you would reject, why that step looked innocent when we wrote it down, and what we should say at each of the other points where the chain could be stopped instead — in particular what the prioritarian owes if (3) is dropped, and what becomes of the leveling-down objection if (1) is dropped. If you think the argument is better read as sound throughout, say which of its consequences a prioritarian should simply swallow and why the swallowing is survivable.
