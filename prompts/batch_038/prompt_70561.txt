So, quick background before the ask: I'm not an academic and I've never taken a poli sci class. I own a small screen-printing shop and for maybe six years now I've been reading everything I can get my hands on about sortition — lotteries for political bodies, citizens assemblies, all of it — mostly late at night, mostly on my own. I've landed a 20 minute slot with Q&A at the monthly general meeting of a citywide progressive coalition here next Thursday. Q&A historically runs long and gets loud.

Here's the proposal I'm defending, roughly:

My city (~210k people, council-manager form of government) sets up what I've been calling a Citizens' Veto Panel. Once a year, 60 residents get drawn at random from a merged list of voter registration, utility accounts and the jury pool, stratified so the panel roughly matches the city on age, zip, household income, and whether you rent or own. They get a stipend, childcare, transit passes, and one weekend a month for a year. They are briefed by a nonpartisan staff office that writes a plain-language summary of every ordinance the council passes, plus the fiscal note and the arguments for and against from the public record.

The key part, and the part I need defending: they cannot propose anything. They have exactly one power, which is to strike down an ordinance the council already passed, by simple majority, within 90 days of it passing. A strike kills the ordinance for two years, and the council can only bring it back by putting it on the ballot for a citywide vote. That's it. No agenda-setting, no amendments, no budget authority.

My argument for why a veto instead of a legislature: (1) you don't need a random body to be smart, you only need it to be un-bought, and it's way harder to buy a blocking vote that produces no favors than to buy a vote that hands out a specific benefit; (2) judging whether something is a bad idea is a much easier cognitive task than designing a good one, so you're asking less of laypeople; (3) our council has a huge incumbent advantage — fundraising, staff, name recognition — so any real check has to come from outside the electoral system rather than inside it; (4) it can't deadlock the city the way a second legislative chamber could, because the default is always "the council's decision stands unless someone bothers to kill it."

Now the audience, and this is the part I most need you to get right. About 40 people in the room. Majority are neighborhood association leaders, organizers from two tenant unions, and people from a racial justice coalition that spent three brutal years getting two of their own elected to council. There are also two council staffers who will be there mostly to take notes, and one retired legal aid lawyer who cares obsessively about whether things would survive a challenge. Importantly, these people are NOT sortition skeptics in general — several of them think citizens assemblies are great and have said so. They're going to object to THIS design specifically. Their commitments: representation is something that was fought for and shouldn't be handed to strangers; the panel's legitimacy has to come from somewhere they can point at; they are terrified of anything that gives well-off homeowners a cleaner tool to block housing; they will want to know who writes the briefing book and who funds the staff office; and a lot of them have personally been on the losing end of a "neutral process" that wasn't.
