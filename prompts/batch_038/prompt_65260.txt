The case has to satisfy all of these at once:

Not because they found out they'd been lied to, not because the process turned out to be captured, not out of survivorship bias or regret about how the decision was made — but because getting the thing changed what they care about.

I should be explicit about what the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating. The point is to show that endorsement-conditional aggregation isn't self-validating: the system's own choice is what generates the preferences it will later be judged by, so there's no fixed point to converge on. The informational gap has to be experiential, not propositional — you can't satisfy the "fully informed" clause by hiding facts, because then it's just a manipulation case in a disguise. Likewise, stipulating the repudiation rather than generating it from the character of the outcome is a fail, and so is a case where the endorsement was really just adaptive preference formation from the start (that's the Elster case, not mine).
