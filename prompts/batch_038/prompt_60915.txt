A couple weeks ago two people in my reading group got into a long arguement about self-deception and neither would give an inch, so I'm hoping you can referee.

Mara's side: self-deception in the strict sense is impossible. To deceive yourself you'd have to deliberately get yourself to believe something you know is false, but any such project requires you to keep the unwelcome truth in view precisely in order to supress it, and a belief you're actively monitoring can't be extinguished. The plan defeats itself, like trying to surprise yourself on purpose.

Theo's reply: fine, grant that, but who said it had to be deliberate? Real cases are just motivated belief — skewed evidence-gathering, memory that keeps the flattering version — and none of that needs an intention. Mara: then "self-deception" is a fancy label for confirmation bias, and you've dropped the only interesting part, which is one person filling both the deceiver's and the deceived's role.

Theo's counter-case is the bit I couldn't evaluate. A woman writes in her journal, every January for six years, that she's leaving her husband in the spring. Shown the entries, she's genuinely shocked, says she never meant a word of it, and insists she has never once considered leaving. Theo says the artifact does the work: the deceived self encounters the other self's belief as external evidence, like testimony from a stranger, so you never need two contradictory beliefs in one head at the same moment and the paradox evaporates. Mara says that's forgetting plus confabulation, and that calling it self-deception assumes exactly what's at issue — if the January self sincerely intended to leave, there was no deceiver to begin with.

Mara then pressed a dilemma: any account either has the subject knowing the truth at the moment of deceiving, in which case the belief can't be acquired, or it drops the knowledge requirement, in which case it can't distinguish self-deception from plain getting-it-wrong. So the concept is stuck between impossible and empty. Theo said there's a third horn: the subject knows the truth dispositionally but not occurrently, and there's nothing paradoxical about a disposition being masked for a while. Mara said dispositions aren't beliefs, and if only the disposition survives then she doesn't actually believe the negation, so there's still no contradictory pair.

Go through it move by move and say who's right at each one. Especially: does the journal case really break the requirement that both beliefs be held at once, and does Theo's third horn escape the dilemma or walk straight into it? Then tell me what both of them are missing, who comes out ahead overall, and give me a straight answer on whether strict self-deception is possible. Please keep it in continuous prose like an email — no headings, bullets, bold or tables — and put your overall verdict in the first couple of sentences.
