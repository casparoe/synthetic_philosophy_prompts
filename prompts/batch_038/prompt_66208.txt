Argue for the following thesis about heritability. The standard philosophical criticism of the statistic — that it is defined only relative to a particular population within a particular range of environments, and therefore cannot support claims about other populations, about individuals, or about whether a trait is fixed or malleable — is correct but largely toothless, because it is aimed at inferences that working quantitative geneticists seldom make. The genuinely damaging criticism is modal rather than relativistic: the variance decomposition from which heritability is computed fails to license the counterfactuals that ordinary causal talk about how much of a difference genes bring about appears to require.

In developing the case, please do all of the following:

1. Set out what is and is not invariant about the decomposition of phenotypic variance into additive genetic, dominance and epistatic, environmental, interaction, and covariance terms when the distribution of environments, or of genotypes, is altered; use this to specify exactly which counterfactuals fail and which merely change their values.

2. Explain why gene–environment correlation and gene–environment interaction pose a deeper problem than population-relativity does. Population-relativity indexes the components to a setting; correlation and interaction instead undermine the reading of the additive component as a separable causal contribution, since the assignment of organisms to environments is itself partly a consequence of their genotypes, and the effect of a genotype is not constant across the environments it encounters.

3. Confront the strongest counterexample available to your own thesis: narrow-sense heritability appears to license precisely one modal inference, namely the prediction of response to selection from the strength of selection. Show why that prediction is a real one, and why its existence does not rehabilitate the broader causal idiom.

4. Close by stating what legitimate use of the statistic survives the abandonment of its modal ambitions, and assess the familiar illustration in which a genetically heterogeneous seed lot is sown in two trays, one well supplied with nutrients and light and one impoverished: determine whether it demonstrates the relativistic point, the modal point, or neither, and say what would have to be added to the illustration to settle the question.

Write this as a continuous argumentative essay rather than as a survey of positions, and do not supply references or a bibliography. Where you believe the thesis overstates, engage it on its own terms rather than retreating to a weaker and safer claim.
