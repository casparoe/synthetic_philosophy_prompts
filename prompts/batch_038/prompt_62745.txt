**Prize question.** Lorenz's 1963 model of atmospheric convection is a deterministic system of differential equations whose solutions never settle into a repeating cycle and which separate exponentially from neighbouring solutions. Does chaos of this kind damage the Laplacean picture of a world that is both fully determined and in principle knowable? Your answer should distinguish what determinism asserts about the world, what instability asserts about a subclass of deterministic systems, and what either implies about prediction by finite agents. It should also say whether any of this bears on the free will problem.

**Entry.** Deterministic chaos is usually reported as a refutation of Laplace, and I want to argue that the report is half right and half confusion. Laplace's picture has two components that are almost always run together: that the state of the world at a time fixes its state at every later time, and that an intellect of sufficient power could read the later states off the earlier one. Chaos leaves the first untouched and destroys the second. That is the whole of the result, and it is more interesting than the slogan.

Lorenz's model is a system of three ordinary differential equations with no stochastic term anywhere in it. Given an initial point the solution is unique, and it is unique forever; nothing in the mathematics licenses a branch. What the model also has is a positive Lyapunov exponent: two trajectories started a distance apart separate, on average, exponentially in time. Poincaré had already found something of the kind in celestial mechanics at the end of the nineteenth century, and Lorenz's contribution was to exhibit it in a system small enough to be studied on the computers of his day. Instability is not universal — integrable systems do not diverge exponentially at all, and much of the world's dynamics is neither unstable nor interesting — but where a system is dissipative the trajectories contract onto an attractor, and once a trajectory is on the attractor the exponential separation has been damped out. That is why the ceiling on predictability bites hardest during the transient, before the system has settled. Where the exponent is positive, though, the practical ceiling is severe, and there is a feature of it that popular writing almost never mentions: because the growth is exponential, buying ten times the precision in the initial data buys only a fixed additional interval of forecast lead time, proportional to the logarithm of ten divided by the exponent. Improving your instruments by an order of magnitude does not extend your horizon by an order of magnitude; it extends it by a constant. That is why decades of better satellites have moved the medium-range forecast out by days rather than by weeks-of-days.

Now for the part that is usually gotten wrong. Laplace's intellect was to be given the initial state exactly. In a chaotic system, specifying an initial state exactly requires infinitely many digits: any finite description, however long, is compatible with a continuum of states whose futures part company from the described one within a bounded time. So the demon is not merely hard to build; it is not a coherent object, because the information it would have to hold does not exist in a form any intellect could hold. And if no complete specification of the present can be had, the present does not fix the future in any sense that could matter. The weather next month is not merely unknown to us; it is unsettled. Determinism, as Laplace understood it, was an empirical hypothesis about the world's intelligibility, and chaos falsifies it.

One further consequence is usually noted and then dropped too quickly. Chaotic systems produce sequences that are, for any observer, indistinguishable from random ones, and the probabilities we attach to their outcomes are therefore chances in the same sense as the probabilities of quantum mechanics — features of the world rather than measures of our ignorance. A forecaster who reports a percentage is reporting something real.

Finally, free will. Compatibilists have long argued that what matters is not whether the future is fixed but whether it is fixed in the right way. But if the brain is a nonlinear dynamical system, and everything we know about neural tissue suggests that it is, then its trajectories are unstable too, and no outside observer can predict them. This is not a solution to the problem of free will, but it dissolves the part of the problem that was ever worrying: the sense of being readable from the outside. An unpredictable agent is one whose conduct cannot be derived from a description of the world, and that is all the independence anyone wanted. The remaining question — whether unpredictability is enough — is a question about control, and control is precisely what chaos does not threaten.
