I'm sitting in on a monetary theory seminar where one guy keeps insisting money is basically a language — a system of signs whose meaning is fixed by use, not by anything they stand for — and he drags in Berkeley's Querist as the ancestor, pointing at the early query asking whether the true idea of money is "altogether that of a ticket or counter." He uses it to argue that backing questions (gold, reserves, tax claims) are catgory errors: you don't ask what backs a word.

Can you audit the analogy for me? First the mapping — what plays the role of syntax, semantics, speakers, translation, prescriptivists, dead languages — then where it snaps, and in particular what it smuggles in about meaning being settled by collective use rather than by whoever is in a position to enforce it. Second, is Berkeley actually on his side? He's clear that money is only so far useful as it stirs up industry, that price is a proportion of demand to plenty, and that the denominations could carry on even if the bullion were gone — does the ticket-and-counter picture license "no backing needed," or does it just relocate the backing into whoever keeps the ledger? And would any of the alternatives he rattles off in the same breath (commodity, standard, measure, pledge) do the job better? American English please.
