I run a small grad seminar on the philosophy of computation this term and last week we got tangled up in the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis: is it a piece of conceptual explication, an empirical hypothesis about physics, a substantive a priori claim about the notion of an effective method, or all three at once depending on which paragraph you happen to be reading? One of my students, a physics double major who is very sharp and very impatient with philosophers, floated a thought experiment that I've been mulling ever since, and I want to see whether it can be made to bite. Here's the setup. A lab produces a sealed black box, call it B. You feed it a Diophantine equation in arbitrarily many variables with integer coefficients, and within about forty minutes it lights up either YES or NO according to whether the equation has an integer solution. It cant be opened without destroying the mechanism, nobody has a working model of what's inside, and the manufacturer's only story is that it exploits some exotic but perfectly lawlike physical process. It passes everything we throw at it: a billion random instances cross-checked against results established independently by hand, all the famous textbook cases, and a year of replication at three seperate sites with zero disagreements. Assume, per impossibile if you like, that it never fails and that we have overwhelming empirical grounds for expecting it never will.

My student's conclusion was that B refutes the Church–Turing thesis full stop, and that anyone who says otherwise is just defending a slogan. I want to argue the opposite and I'd like your help making the case as tight as I can get it: B refutes the physical or mechanical reading — the Deutsch-style claim, roughly, that any finitely realizable physical system can be simulated to arbitrary accuracy by a universal machine working by finite means — but it leaves the original 1936 thesis about effective methods entirely untouched, and the fact that both of those things can be true at once shows that the 1936 thesis was never the sort of claim that could have had empirical support in the first place, so all the usual appeals to "no counterexample has ever been found" are beside the point.

The hard part, and the bit I want you to actually wrestle with rather than wave at, is the objection that B doesn't merely sit outside the concept of an effective method but invades it. Because once B is on the lab bench and reliably working, a human clerk can decide Hilbert's tenth problem by a procedure that is set out in finitely many exact instructions, terminates after finitely many steps, and demands no insight, intuition or ingenuity whatsoever — write the equation down, hand it to the box, read the light, record the answer. That looks for all the world like an effective method in exactly the classic sense, which would make a provably non-computable function computable after all and collapse the distinction I'm trying to hold onto. So defend the position above and meet that objection head on: either show that the clerk-plus-box routine fails one of the conditions on effectiveness (and say which, and why that condition is doing real work rather than being gerrymandered to save my conclusion), or concede that it is an effective method and explain why the concession still doesn't hand my student the refutation he thinks he has. No citations and no bibliography please, I know this literature and it isn't the point — just argue. When you're done, give me a short JSON object laying out the structure of what you said, with exactly these fields: `claim_defended` (one sentence); `thesis_variants` (array of at least three objects, each with `label`, `what_it_says`, `epistemic_type` drawn from stipulative | substantive a priori | empirical | indeterminate, `verdict_on_box` drawn from refuted | untouched | indirectly undermined, and `reason` in two or three sentences); `crux` (the single distinction the whole argument turns on, one sentence); `clerk_plus_box` (an object with `objection`, `your_verdict` drawn from not an effective method | effective but irrelevant to the refutation | something else, and `response` in three to five sentences); `concession` (one thing in your argument you'd have to admit is shaky); and `bottom_line` (two sentences max, what I should tell the seminar on Thursday).
