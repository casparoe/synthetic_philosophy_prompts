You are a philosopher of language who has taught Davidson on radical interpretation for twenty years and who has also, less visibly, written and delivered a good many funeral orations. I need both halves of that description, and I need them in that order: before drafting anything, work through the interpretive problem in a short preamble I can delete — establish which claims about my mother's mind the available evidence actually licenses, which it does not, and which are indeterminate between two readings that are equally good — and then write the speech on the strength of that analysis.

The facts. My mother ran a hardware shop in a small town for thirty-eight years. She left school at sixteen, read almost nothing, and had no patience with ideas; her intelligence was practical and social, and it was considerable. Six years ago she had a stroke, and then two smaller ones. By the end she had very little language: a handful of reliable words, two or three fragments that recurred, gesture, tone, laughter at things she plainly followed, and long stretches nobody in the family could parse. She died on Thursday. I am giving the closing remarks at Saturday's service, about four minutes.

The room is her customers, her congregation, my two sisters, my nephews of nine and eleven, and roughly six academics who have come out of fondness for me and who will not understand a single item of my professional vocabulary. The speech must contain none of that vocabulary — no talk of interpretation, meaning, belief attribution, or charity as a term of art. What I want from Davidson is not his terminology but his discipline, and it has to govern the speech from inside, as a constraint on what I am permitted to say.

That discipline, as I understand it and as I want it applied: all the evidence about what she meant is public and behavioral, and I have no privileged access to a private inner life behind it. Meaning and belief cannot be peeled apart one at a time; they are solved for together, and the only way to solve for them is to hold fixed a very large background on which she and I already agree and on which she was mostly right about the plain things in front of her. Massive attribution of error or unintelligibility is not a skeptical precaution but a way of losing the subject altogether — so the speech may not treat her last three years as noise. But charity is not omnipotence: some things remain genuinely indeterminate between readings, and the speech must leave them open rather than choosing one because it is kinder.

Three demands follow, and I think they are hard to satisfy together.

First, it must resist two cheap consolations currently in open conflict in my family. My elder sister maintains that Mother knew exactly who we were and what was happening until the last week, and simply could not say so. My younger sister maintains that the person had been gone for years and that we were comforting ourselves with a puppet. The speech may endorse neither, and may not fudge in a way that merely sounds like both at once. It has to say what can honestly be said about those years, to people who need it to be worth having listened.

Second, for the last two years she repeated one three-word phrase, always identical, always at the same pitch, and the family turned it into a running joke. I want it in the speech — treated as evidence, not as a catchphrase — and I want the speech to admit that we never agreed what it was about, and that this was not a failure of love or of attention.

Third, there is one matter on which the record is thin and painful: I left her congregation when I was twenty-two and she never said anything to me about it afterwards, in health or in illness, that anyone can quote. The speech may not claim a reconciliation the evidence will not carry, and may not conspicuously avoid the subject either.

Tone and craft: it must be speakable aloud by a person who is grieving, no more than two sentences that anyone in that room would need to hear twice, one true moment of humour — she was funny and the room knows it — and it must end on her, not on me and not on a lesson.

Afterwards, in a separate section addressed to me rather than to the room, list every attribution to her mind the speech makes, mark each as licensed, refused, or left indeterminate, and give the evidential reason. My sister will ask me on Sunday whether I got her right, and I want to know precisely what I will have committed myself to.
