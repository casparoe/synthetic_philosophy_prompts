I write long-form features and a monthly column on probability, reasoning, and uncertainty for a general-interest science magazine, and over the past two years I have drifted, probably further than I realised, into the debate about whether an indicative conditional has a probability at all. Because my beat keeps returning to the topic — automated reasoning systems, the reliability of expert judgment, how people update on "if" claims — a philosopher I sometimes interview has invited me to sit in on an interdisciplinary reading group this term and, unusually, to open one session by laying out my own working position so the group can attack it rather than a text. The group includes two philosophers of language, a statistician, and a pair of machine-learning researchers who work on probabilistic semantics. I would like a rehearsal of that attack before I walk into the room. I am not looking for a tutorial on the literature and I explicitly do not want a reading list or citations; I want an audit of the package below, on the assumption that everything I say here is something you already know well enough to evaluate on its own terms.

Here is what I currently hold. (1) Indicative conditionals of the ordinary English kind — "if the reactor tripped, the alarm sounded" — are truth-apt: relative to a speaker and a context, such a sentence expresses a determinate proposition, the sort of thing a rational credence can attach to, and negation, conjunction, and disjunction behave classically over these propositions. (2) Adams's equation is not a technical stipulation about an invented connective but a substantive constraint on rational belief about ordinary "if" claims: whenever the antecedent has non-zero probability, the probability of the conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability of the consequent. (3) The material-conditional analysis is descriptively false for indicatives; I am not a Gricean here, and I do not think the trouble with "if the reactor did not trip, the alarm did not sound" when the reactor plainly tripped is merely a matter of assertability or misleadingness. Notably, I take (3) and (2) to be partners: the equation in (2) is what explains why the truth-functional reading mispredicts our confidence. (4) I accept Lewis's triviality results and I do not think they can be argued around: if a credence function assigns probabilities to conditionals in the classical way and the equation in (2) holds across the board, the assignment collapses — antecedent probability zero, or consequent probability one, or the like. (5) However, and this is the move I most want tested, I think Lewis's proofs are constrained by their formal setting in a way that limits their reach. They assume that probabilities are distributed over sets of possible worlds in a classical measure space and that updating proceeds by total conditionalization. Those are assumptions about the representation, not discoveries about the meaning of "if," so I take the results to leave open a non-classical semantics — something along the de Finetti, Jeffrey, and van Fraassen lines, on which a conditional is treated as a three-valued or random-variable-like object whose expectation recovers the conditional probability — in which (2) holds without triviality. That is how I reconcile (1), (2), and (4). (6) I also think the Ramsey test and (2) are not two independent theses but one thesis viewed from two directions: to evaluate an indicative you add the antecedent hypothetically to your stock of information and assess the consequent there, and the equation is simply the quantitative statement of that procedure. (7) Finally, I reject the suggestion that negated conditionals are second-class — unassertable, or lacking truth conditions, or outside the reach of the probability calculus. I hold that the equation applies across the board, and in particular that the probability of the negation of a conditional equals the conditional probability of the negated consequent, so that denying "if the reactor tripped, the alarm sounded" is a matter of credence one minus the conditional probability, in the same straightforward sense in which accepting it is a matter of credence equal to that conditional probability.

One piece of personal stake, so you can calibrate how much of this I am prepared to lose: a sentence in my second column on this topic reads, in effect, that logicians have established that the probability of an if-statement just is a conditional probability. I would like to know whether that sentence survives any revision you recommend, or whether it has to be withdrawn outright. With that in mind, please do the following. Go through (1) to (7) and tell me which pairs or triples are in genuine conflict and which merely look conflicted; do not flag everything, and do not treat a tension as real just because two theses sound like they belong to different camps. For each genuine tension, state what has to be given up or weakened, what the resulting position costs me, and how severe the problem is — and rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list. I suspect at least one of my commitments looks dangerous but is actually innocent, and at least one looks like a harmless elaboration of another but is where the real damage is done; tell me which is which and why. Then identify the single revision that would restore coherence most cheaply across the whole package, and be explicit about what I forfeit by making it — including whether I can still claim any version of (2) afterwards, and if so, in what weakened form. Separately, tell me where my package is silent rather than inconsistent, and mark clearly which of your conclusions are matters on which specialists still disagree as opposed to points that are simply settled by the proofs. Finally, run two tests on the resulting position. First: a fair coin is tossed and concealed, a card is drawn from a shuffled deck and placed face down, and I assert that if the coin landed heads the card is a heart — what does my package say my credence in that sentence should be, and can my principles actually deliver a number, or do they only generate a collapse? Second: it seems compelling that if the maid did it whenever the butler did it, and the maid also did it whenever the butler did not, then the maid did it — yet the two conditionals can each be highly probable on my equation while the bare consequent is improbable. Does my package explain the pull of that inference, or does it merely register the discrepancy?
