So I help run a little foundations-of-physics reading group in Bristol — mostly ex-physics people who've gone off into data work and meet in a pub back room — and we've just spent three weeks slogging through the whole is-the-wavefunction-real arguement. You know the terrain: the classification that sorts views by whether two different quantum states can be compatible with one and the same underlying physical state; the 2012 no-go theorem saying you can't keep treating the state as mere information unless you're willing to give up the assumption that independently prepared systems have independent underlying states; plus everyone's favourite move of declaring the entire debate ill-posed. Half the group thinks it's the most important question in physics, the other half thinks it's a fight about bookkeeping, and I want something to hand round at the end of term that both sides will laugh at without feeling patronised.

Could you write me a satire in the form of a product listing on a marketplace that sells fundamental ontology — call it something like Eigenstate & Sons — for an item titled The Wavefunction. Around 750–850 words, and please do it properly in the format: the seller's blurb, a specification box, four or five star-rated customer reviews with invented reviewer handles, at least one seller response to a bad review, and a footnote about the returns policy. British English throughout, obviously, and keep the register completely deadpan e-commerce — no winking at the reader, no final line that spells out the joke.

The bit I care about most: the humour has to come from getting the content right, not from generic physics-nerd gags about uncertainty or cats in boxes. Specifically, I'd like the marketplace to run a verified-badge scheme in which listings are audited for whether the item's underlying states overlap with those of a rival SKU, so the badge itself is the classification criterion — and one reviewer who is furious because they bought two units from two different suppliers, opened both boxes, and found the same thing inside, which is precisely what the badge was supposed to rule out. There should also be a warranty called the Preparation Independence Guarantee, which a customer discovers is void because it turns out both their units came off the same production line. Other reviewers I'd like: someone delighted that the item is unambiguously a real physical thing but furious about storage, since the twelve-particle version needs shelving in more dimensions than their flat has; a customer who recieved a parcel that was both delivered and not delivered and only resolved when the neighbour glanced at it, with the seller's response that this is a feature and not covered by the returns policy; a buyer asking whether the pilot-wave accessory comes in the box or is sold separately; and one reviewer who refuses to engage with the listing at all on the grounds that there is no such product independent of their own expectations about opening the box — with a seller reply that makes clear this reviewer has misunderstood what a marketplace is, which is itself sort of the point. Please don't quote real papers or textbooks word-for-word, don't give citations or a reading list, and invent all the brand names. If it works, the thing being satirised should be the argument itself and the way both camps talk past each other, not any single interpretation being stupid — every reviewer should be internally coherent, so a specialist reads it and thinks yes, that's exactly their position, only worse.
