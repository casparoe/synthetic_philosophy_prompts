I am at work on a sequence of stories about people who discover that the world contains fewer objects than they have been counting, and I want to attempt one of them out of the philosophy of physics rather than out of moral philosophy. My subject is the hole argument.

I know its shape well enough to state it plainly, and I will, because I want the piece built on the argument itself rather than on its reputation. A generally covariant theory — general relativity being the paradigm, and the one Einstein hesitated over in 1913 before the modern revival — represents spacetime as a manifold of points bearing fields. Take a region of that manifold containing no matter, so that nothing in it can be registered by anything outside it, and stipulate the fields everywhere else. Because the equations are covariant, one may apply a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside that region and drags the fields around within it. The transformed result is equally a solution; it agrees with the original on all initial data and on everything outside the region; and yet, taken point by point, it assigns the metric to different manifold points inside it. If manifold points have identities over and above the fields occupying them — if spacetime is a substance, a container that would still be there with nothing in it — then the theory carries a real indeterminism which no experiment could ever detect, together with facts about the world that are in principle unknowable. The familiar exit is to deny the antecedent: diffeomorphic models are not two possible worlds but one world written twice.

That exit is where I want the story to live, and I do not want it to be cheap.

Please write a story of roughly three to four thousand words, in whatever voice and register you judge best, observing the following.

First, no physics department, no blackboard, no character who explains the argument. The argument must be enacted by the plot and the imagery, not narrated. Nothing in the story should be footnoted or attributed.

Second, the hole must be a hole in the world of the story: a ward of a city, a span of a marriage, an unindexed stretch of an archive, a season in which no one was watching — somewhere nothing can be witnessed and which is nevertheless populated with something the characters cannot manage without.

Third, there must be two versions of what happened that coincide everywhere outside it and in every respect the characters can check, and differ as to what occupied what inside it. Someone must fail to tell them apart, and that failure must register as vertigo or bereavement rather than as a puzzle tidily solved.

Fourth, every fact available to your characters should be a fact about one thing meeting another: a pencil mark coinciding with a mark on a scale, a name in a register coinciding with a face at a door, a tide coinciding with a date. In one scene the reader should be allowed to notice that this has been all there ever was, without anyone announcing it.

Fifth, the substantivalist intuition deserves its strongest rendering as lived experience — the conviction that there is a there there, that this ground rather than that one holds the body, that the container is not exhausted by its contents. The relationalist escape should then cost something, and the story should be willing to show the payment rather than wave at it.

Sixth, do not settle the matter with a wise speech from anyone.

After the story, please add a note of some three hundred words, outside the fiction and without citations. It should say where you bent the argument for narrative effect — a diffeomorphism is a map of the manifold onto itself, and any story-world analogue will strain that, so tell me how and where you cheated; which side of the dispute your telling ends up favoring, and what an advocate of the other side would claim you begged; and, since a story cannot deliver a verdict, the nearest thing to one: what argument or consideration would change your mind about which side it ought to have taken, and at which point in your rendering you believe you are most likely to have gone wrong.
