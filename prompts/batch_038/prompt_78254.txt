You are a theorist of history who has spent many years teaching research-methods seminars to first-year doctoral students in history — students who are already fluent in their own archives and almost entirely unreflective about the assumptions of their practice. Your task is to design a self-diagnosis instrument, in the form of a short quiz with a scoring key, that sorts such a student according to what they would actually do and say about concrete historiographical choices, rather than according to which label they would claim for themselves. The topic is presentism, and the instrument will be used in the first week of a methods course and again in the last, so every question must be answerable by someone with no training in the philosophy of history.

**Step one: map the space before you write any questions.** Think carefully and, in a labelled passage of no more than 250 words, set out the axes along which a historian's practice can be more or less present-centred. I want at least three, and your instrument must read each of them separately:

1. *Selection* — whether the questions we put to the past are properly our questions, or whether the past's own concerns should govern what gets asked.
2. *Description* — whether we may characterise past situations in vocabularies unavailable to the agents: calling a pre-modern polity a "state", pre-modern attachments "homosexuality", eighteenth-century attitudes "racism".
3. *Evaluation* — whether we may praise and blame past agents at all, and if so by which standards.

State explicitly whether these axes come apart in practice, that is, whether a historian can be past-centred on one and present-centred on another, and identify a fourth axis only if you think one is genuinely missing and worth testing.

**Step two: the questions.** Write ten. Each must be a concrete case of the kind a working historian actually faces — what to call a thing, where to stop the narrative, whether to explain an outcome using knowledge the agents lacked, whether to record a moral verdict on a slaveholder or a colonial administrator, whether the choice of subject is itself open to criticism, whether a history that turns out to have been heading somewhere is thereby distorted. Each question offers two or three options, written in the first person as things a thoughtful historian might sincerely say. Two rules govern the wording. First, the vocabulary of the debate is banned from the questions and options entirely: no "presentism", "anachronism", "Whig", "contextualism", "historicism", "relativism". Second, no question may ask for the respondent's general attitude; nothing along the lines of whether one should judge the past by its own lights, or whether the past is a foreign country. At least two questions must bear on each of the three axes above.

**Step three: the scoring key.** Give every option a coordinate on each axis it engages, so that a completed quiz yields a profile of three or four positions rather than a single label. Then:

- List the profiles that have familiar names. At a minimum the key must locate: the view that the past is to be reconstructed only in its own terms and evaluated not at all; the view that a history which selects and judges by present standards is not thereby corrupted but is doing the only kind of history worth doing; the two-standards view, which describes in the agents' vocabulary and evaluates in ours; the view that moral judgement must be tempered by what the agent could reasonably have known; and the dissolutionist view on which the whole dispute concerns the meaning of words rather than any difference in practice. Where a profile has no current name, say so and coin one.
- Identify at least three profiles the questions permit but that no one occupies, and diagnose each: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?
- Flag any pair of profiles your ten questions cannot in fact separate, and say which eleventh question would separate them.

**Step four: a short coda.** State which single question does the most discriminating work in the instrument, and which is most vulnerable to being answered for the wrong reason — where a respondent's choice tracks something other than the commitment you intended to test.

Formal requirements: roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words; British English; headings and a table wherever a table is clearer than prose. Include no references, no footnotes and no bibliography, and do not attribute views to named authors unless you are entirely confident of the attribution, since the students using this have no library to hand.
