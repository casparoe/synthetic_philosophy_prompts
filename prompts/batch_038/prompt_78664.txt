So I'm the volunteer registrar on the collections committee at a small city museum. There's five of us, none of us art historians — I fix computers for a living and I've done the paperwork here on Saturdays for about nine years. We've got a mess and I want you to write the opinion I'll hand round before the meeting, because I don't trust myself to be even-handed about it.

In 1978 a sculptor who worked locally made a life-size kneeling figure out of unglazed stoneware, fired in one piece. We bought it in 1994, it's been on the same plinth in the same corner ever since, and its honestly the only object in the building anybody outside town has ever cared about. She died in 2011. Last January some scaffolding came down in the gallery during a lighting job and the figure got crushed — around three hundred pieces, most of them fist-sized or smaller. Our insurer paid out a total loss without an argument.

Here's where it got weird. The artist had taken a plaster mold off the figure back in 1979, when she was making a run of smaller maquettes, and the mold survived; it sits with her estate, which is basically her nephew. Within a month of the collapse he had her old studio assistant press a full-size version from it, using clay dug from the same pit she used, fired in the same kiln, and stamped on the underside with her chop under the estate's authority. The estate's position is that this is the work, that they'll place it with us at no charge, and that it should go back on the plinth under our original accesion number.

Meanwhile our conservator spent eight months reassembling the shards with a reversible adhesive. She got maybe 85% of the original surface back; the losses are filled with tinted plaster, and if you stand close you can see it's been hurt. The estate calls that object a relic and not a work, and they've offered to buy it for a retrospective they're organizing — sight unseen, and fairly generous money for us.

So we have three decisions, and a policy that's no use at all: our collections manual says the work is whatever the accession record says it is, which obviously begs the question here. First, which object, if either, holds the original accession number and gets shown as the work — and does the refire get its own number as a new acquisition, or do we not acquire it at all? Second, do we sell the reassembled fragments to the estate or keep them, and if we keep them, what are they: the figure, evidence, an archive of the figure? Third, whatever we decide, what does the wall label say? The insurer also needs to know which single object to schedule now and for how much, since they consider the claim closed.

The committee is split two-two and I'm the tiebreaker, which I hate. Two of us think the estate is right — that the work is a form the artist authorized and the matter is just the medium it happens to be in, so an authorized refire off her own mold is the work in a new body. Two think the figure is simply gone and neither candidate is it: the refire is a posthumous replica, honest but a different object with a different history, and the glued-together thing is a damaged remnant. I keep landing somewhere I'm not sure is defensible, which is that the clay arranged the right way is the figure, but the arrangement only counts if the right history runs through it continuously. On that way of putting it you can't get the same arrangement back by starting over from a mold, because then nothing has persisted and been re-arranged — there's just a new thing that matches. So the conservator's reassembly is the surviving figure, badly hurt, and the refire is a match rather than a survivor. Tell me straight whether that view survives these facts or whether it's a dodge that would embarrass me if someone pushed on it, including the obvious push: 15% of the reassembly isn't original material and I'm perfectly happy to call it the figure, so why does a mold-made copy get excluded when it has 100% of the form? If there's a principled line about how much intervention is too much, I want to know where it goes and what work it's doing there.

I want the memo to actually take a position on all three decisions, not survey the options and hope, and I want the strongest case for the losing side stated as well as its own defenders would state it — especially the estate's, because they aren't stupid and the nephew will be sitting in the room. If your conclusion is that we decline the refire, say what we owe the estate by way of an explanation that isn't just an appeal to policy. And flag anything in the setup you think I've mis-described or that would flip the answer if it were different: does it matter that the mold was hers, that the hands were her assistant's, that the clay came from the same pit, that she made the mold for a different purpose entirely? If the answer moves depending on which of those details you tweak, tell me which ones are load-bearing and which ones are decoration.

One more thing — I've tried reading philosophy about this and I bounce off it, so don't send me to books or tell me who said what, just argue it out in plain terms. And please write it the way a person would write a letter: continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered list of recommendations, even though it's a memo. Put your recommendation in the first couple of sentences so I know where you're going, then make the case for it.
