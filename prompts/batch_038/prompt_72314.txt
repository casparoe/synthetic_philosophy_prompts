I'm partway through a trade book about honesty at work — the author is a consultant who does cross-cultural training for multinationals — and there's a chapter I keep going back to because I can't decide whether it's sharp or sloppy. Here's the stretch, trimmed down but with his wording intact:

"Grice's insight was that conversation runs on a handful of standing assumptions — be truthful, be as informative as the exchange requires, be relevant, be clear — and that indirectness is a deliberate breach of one of them. The hearer notices the breach, assumes the speaker is still cooperating anyway, and works out what was meant instead. That is already most of the way to a conclusion people resist: a lie and a polite evasion are built out of the same parts. Both require the listener to supply a meaning the speaker never put into words.

The Dutch famously skip the step. Their low power-distance score means directness isn't experienced as a face threat at all — there's no positive face to protect, because a culture that treats you as an equal doesn't need to keep reassuring you that you're liked.

The Japanese do the opposite, and the high-context label captures why: in a high-context culture most of the message sits in the situation rather than in the words, so the speaker has, in a real sense, not asserted anything. In the meetings I sat in on in Osaka nobody ever said no. What they said was that it would be difficult, and everyone in the room knew what that meant. The refusal landed perfectly. And that's the point — the meaning got delivered without one false sentence being uttered, so there was nothing for anyone to be dishonest about.

Which is why I think the comfortable line — different cultures, different styles of honesty — doesn't survive Kant's essay on lying to the murderer at the door. Kant's objection was never about consequences. It was that a lie uses the listener as a mere means, because you've bypassed his capacity to consent to what you're doing to him. A hearer who is being fed an implicature has been bypassed in exactly the same way. So the Kantian has to say that the polite refusal and the white lie are wrong for one and the same reason, and that a culture which has turned the first into a virtue has simply made the second respectable."

I manage a team split between Amsterdam and Osaka, so the description of the meetings rings true in a way I can't quite dismiss, and the last paragraph feels like it's earning something. But I also have the feeling he's welding together three or four different toolboxes and that at least one of the joins is holding up more weight than it can take.
