I'm coordinating a two-day workshop where two of our faculty have finally agreed to co-write something instead of sniping at each other in meetings. One is a historical sociologist who thinks regimes get produced by class coalitions, state capacity, the timing of economic collapse, and how mass organizations mobilize — individuals are downstream of all that. The other is a political historian who thinks leadership has irreducible causal weight and that counterfactuals about specific persons are perfectly respectable history. Their chosen case is the old chestnut: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?

The deliverable is the document the two of them could actually put their names to. Not a transcript of them arguing, and not your verdict on who wins — the joint statement itself, cold enough that neither can say "that isn't me."

Here's what I need in it.

**A framing section first, where they agree on what is being asked.** The counterfactual needs an intervention point: do we take Hitler out on the Western Front in 1918, out of that Reichswehr assignment to sit in on the tiny workers' party meeting in autumn 1919, out of the wreckage of the 1923 putsch, or out of the picture in early 1933 when he's already the leader of the largest party in the country? And it needs a target, because "Nazi Germany" is doing four different jobs at once — the NSDAP in government, a single-party dictatorship of the radical right, a war of annihilation in the East, industrialized genocide of the Jews. My strong suspicion is that these two want different pairings of intervention point and target, and that alot of the heat in their argument has been that mismatch rather than anything substantive. Make them say which pairing each is answering, explicitly, instead of letting it slide.

**Then a numbered section of claims both can sign.** Some of these should be plain historical: the party peaking near 37 percent in the July 1932 election and slipping in November; how very few people in the room decided anything in January 1933; the fact that for most of the regime subordinates were guessing at what the top wanted and improvising toward it. Some should be methodological: what counterfactual reasoning is licensed to do in history at all, what a "probability" attached to a claim like this would even be a probability of, and whether either party is smuggling in assumptions about determinism or free will they'd disclaim if asked (I bet both would). Anything they can both sign that isn't trivially true goes here; don't pad it with tautologies just to make the list look long.

**Then a disagreements table.** Each row: the dispute stated in wording the relevant side would accept as a fair statement of its own position — so their side gets phrased the way they'd phrase it, not as a strawman — plus a column marking whether that row is a substantive disagreement or just two vocabularies grinding against each other. Be honest about the verbal ones. My guess is that at least one row is really about what "would have" means: how much probability is enough to license it, and whether we're talking about the nearest world where he's absent or about his actual causal contribution in this one.

**Then the cruxes.** The sub-questions where settling the issue would genuinely move at least one of the two — which is a higher bar than "an interesting question." For each crux, name the specific case, comparison, or body of evidence that both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance, so we can check later who budged and who didn't. If you conclude that there is no evidence either one would accept as counting against their own view, write that down as a finding. Do not manufacture a fake crux to make the exercise look successful.

No reading list, no bibliography, no "see also." I want the reasoning and the history in the document itself.
