Something about the apparatus that grows up around elections has always bugged me and I can't quite name it, so maybe you can.

Turnout is treated as the headline health metric of a democracy. Election commissions, news desks, civic groups — everyone leads with the percentage, and a low number gets described as a legitimacy problem, a "democratic deficit," a sign that something is wrong with the polity rather than with the menu on offer. Whole reforms (same-day registration, election-day holidays, mail ballots) get justified by the number they're expected to move. And in quite a few countries the election authority publishes turnout at intervals *while the polls are still open*, apparently on the assumption that hearing the number will pull more people in.

Here's the part I can't square. The political science these same institutions draw on says the chance any one voter is decisive is so small that it can't rationally outweigh the cost of getting to a polling place, and most people in the field now explain turnout by something other than wanting to affect the outcome — duty, expression, identity, the fact that the neighbours will know. Fine. But then the interim-announcement practice looks backwards on its own terms: if you were an instrumental voter, learning that everybody else has already voted should make your own ballot *less* likely to matter, not more. The authorities publish it expecting the opposite. So the practice seems to assume a vote is a kind of joining-in, and that turnout functions as a coordination signal rather than as a pile of tiny bets on an outcome.

So what theory of what voting is *for* does the whole turnout apparatus presuppose? Reconstruct it as carefully as you can. My hunch: if a vote were purely a statement of preference, low turnout wouldn't be a pathology at all — it'd be information, meaning most people were broadly content or didn't care much, which arguably is is a good result. The panic only makes sense if voting is something citizens owe, to the state or to each other or to the dead. Is that right, or is there a reading of the metric that's compatible with a purely preferential view — something epistemic, maybe, where more voters means better aggregation?

Then I want you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. There seem to be several candidate theories floating around — a duty view, a legitimacy view where authority comes from the sheer number participating, a participatory view where the act changes the person, a Condorcet-ish epistemic one — and I bet they come apart in what they'd predict and recommend. Which can license the practice, which can't? And is the practice internally consistent about them, given that the same discourse wants high turnout while also fretting that low-information voters will swamp the result, and given that the denominator itself (registered voters vs. voting-age population) quietly decides which countries look sick?

Last thing: if you swapped the presupposed view for an openly expressive or instrumental one, what would actually have to change — would we stop reporting turnout, report it differently, or put something else in its place? And what happens to the interim announcements and the get-out-the-vote industry under that swap?
