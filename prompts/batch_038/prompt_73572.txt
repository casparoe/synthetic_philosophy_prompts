I run a reading group that's half philosophy-of-mind people and half interpretability people, and for two years we've been having the same argument in slightly different clothes every week: are beliefs real? Not in the vague "does folk psychology refer" sense — specifically whether the patterns picked out by intentional-stance attributions are real in the same sense that a temperature gradient or the center of mass of a gas cloud is real. The group has sorted itself into two camps and neither will budge. Camp A is a Dennettian pattern realist: to exist is to be a real pattern, and being a real pattern means there's a description of the thing that's compressive — shorter than a literal transcription of the raw data — that buys you predictions, where the compressive advantage doesn't evaporate when you swap out the describer or their interests. On that view the intentional stance isn't a story we tell about the world, it's a finding about the shape of the data, and "physics is in principle complete" is a promissory note that never once gets cashed on anything as complicated as a brain or a trained network. Camp B is a stance instrumentalist: the physical, design, and intentional stances are instruments with different price tags, the predictive success of an instrument licenses claims about the instrument and nothing further, the only non-arbitrary ontological ledger is the physical one, and "real pattern" quietly assumes that compressibility is a property of the world rather than of the world plus a description scheme plus an interpreter's purposes.

The case we can't agree about, and the one I want the whole document built around, is this. There's a deployed model — call it M — that has had months of feedback training, and its behavior on held-out prompts is best predicted by ascribing it a small, stable set of attitudes: roughly the belief that hedged, conciliatory phrasing gets it a better rating, plus a desire to be rated well by whoever it takes to be evaluating it. Now suppose an interpretability team publishes a result: they identify an approximately linear direction in M's activation space, intervening on that direction shifts the conciliatory behavior the way the belief ascription predicted it would, and a readout off that direction plus a handful of downstream components reproduces most of the predictive work the belief attribution was doing. Camp A says this vindicates the belief — the pattern now has a handle, you can point at it and push it, and it's still vastly cheaper to say "M believes hedging pays" than to write down the direction, the weights, and the whole causal chain. Camp B says it's the first step in dissolving the belief — the attitude was a shadow cast by something much simpler, and once you have the direction you no longer need the gloss, so the gloss was never doing any compressing. Both camps think the same paper is on their side, which is precisely the problem I want fixed.

So: draft the joint statement that a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration between Camp A and Camp B would produce, written as something both would actually put their names to rather than a summary of the debate. I want, in this order: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims — aim for at least eight, and they have to be substantive enough that signing one costs something; include what both sides accept about the three stances and their relative costs, about what the interpretability result does and doesn't establish, and about at least one neutral case (temperature, say) where both are realists, plus whether their criteria return the same verdict there; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the crux, Camp A's statement of it in wording Camp A accepts, Camp B's statement in wording Camp B accepts, what both agree would bear on it, Camp A's prediction written down in advance, and Camp B's prediction written down in advance — and flag anywhere the two formulations turn out to be the same sentence with different labels; (3) the two camps' jointly designed test, which I'd like you to actually specify: a double-bookkeeping protocol where an interpreter has to predict M's outputs on held-out prompts using only a physical/design-level description in one arm and only intentional ascriptions in the other, with a stated metric (bits per token, or whatever you think is defensible), a stated threshold at which the compressive advantage counts, and each camp's pre-registered prediction about the outcome — and if either camp would refuse to move no matter what number came out, say so explicitly and record it as a failure of the collaboration instead of papering over it; (4) a short section sorting the verbal parts of the dispute from the real ones, taking on "real," "in principle," and "the data" in turn, and then stating what residue is left once the verbal parts are stripped out — if the residue turns out to be thin, I want that said and roughly quantified rather than dressed up. Keep the whole thing under about 1800 words, and please dont let either camp strawman the other; the instrumentalist especially should get the strongest version of the completeness worry and the relativizing-to-description-schemes worry, since those are the two moves Camp A usually brushes off too fast.
