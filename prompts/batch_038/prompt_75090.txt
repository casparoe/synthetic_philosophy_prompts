You are a philosopher of science who works on the demarcation problem as it arises in historiography and archaeology. Think carefully, and begin by sketching the criteria you would draw on — falsifiability, ad hoc rescue, method versus conclusion, deference to expert consensus — before settling on anything. Here are my verdicts on a set of fringe and semi-fringe historical claims, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Fomenko's New Chronology, on which most of recorded ancient and medieval history is a Renaissance fabrication, is pseudohistory, and the statistical machinery behind it does nothing to help. Plato's Atlantis: read as a moral fable inside a dialogue, not pseudohistory at all; read as a drowned civilization, plainly pseudohistory — so one and the same text gets opposite verdicts depending on the reader, which I find disturbing. Illig's claim that roughly three centuries of the early Middle Ages were inserted into the calendar strikes me as worse than the lost-continent claims, even though it is testable, was tested, and has been refuted, while Atlantis is testable by nobody. Heyerdahl crossing the Pacific on a balsa raft counts as good science for me, although his conclusion about the peopling of Polynesia is now rejected. Velikovsky is pseudoscience for me even though the prediction usually credited to him, that Venus would turn out to be extremely hot, came true — and even though the credit is disputed. For the Voynich manuscript I accept the radiocarbon date on the vellum placing it in the early fifteenth century, and reject the identification of its plants as New World species, though both verdicts rest on expert readings of the same object. And the proposal that sweet potatoes reached Polynesia before Europeans, dismissed for decades as diffusionist fancy, now looks well supported by the genetics of old herbarium specimens.

Give me a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough to be applied; say which of my cases motivates each clause; give its verdicts on two or three cases I have not mentioned; and state honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, so I know which one to abandon. Then tell me what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence would push you toward a different principle, and where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
