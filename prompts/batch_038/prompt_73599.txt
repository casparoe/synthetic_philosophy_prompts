I am trying to work out what my intuitions about the validity of consent actually amount to, and I would like help extracting a principle from them. Here are my verdicts.

1. A singing master tells a pupil that a procedure is a vocal treatment that will improve her voice; it is in fact sexual contact. Her consent is invalid.
2. A dentist suspended from the register extracts teeth from patients who believe she is still licensed. I judge that consent valid, even though every one of them would have refused had they known.
3. A woman agrees to sex with a client for £25, and he never pays. Valid.
4. A man agrees to sex with someone who has told him she is a well-known footballer, and she is nobody of the sort. Invalid.

Cases 3 and 4 are the pair I cannot reconcile: both are lies about something the other party cared about, and in both the truth would have changed the answer.

5. A mugger's "your money or your life" invalidates the handing over of the wallet.
6. An employer's "sign this non-compete or you are dismissed" does not, in my view, invalidate the signature, though the employee would far rather not sign.

I cannot say what separates these. "The threat makes some option much worse" fits both.

7. A fourteen-year-old's agreement is invalid whatever her maturity; a bright seventeen-year-old who understands the situation completely is still, I think, unable to consent to the same act — yet I would let her consent to a tattoo and to giving blood.
8. A woman who is moderately drunk but able to remember the encounter and to make up her mind the next morning consents to sex: valid. The same woman, equally drunk, sells her car for £500: voidable.
9. A man in the early stages of dementia agrees to move into a care home and has forgotten the conversation ten minutes later. Valid at the moment of agreement, I think, but I would not let it settle an irreversible decision.

Please state the principle — or the small set of candidate principles — that best captures these verdicts. Give its clauses precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I ought to surrender them rather than complicate the principle to save them. Then tell me what it delivers on cases I have not listed: a man who conceals before sex that he has had a vasectomy; a researcher who omits a one-in-a-million risk that duly materialises; a student who signs a plagiarism declaration to avoid expulsion; a worker who agrees to a ninety-hour week because there is no other job to be had; a patient who agrees to a risky operation only because her surgeon has told her it is the sole option.

Please write the answer in British English.
