I work on ranking systems and have no formal background in philosophy or in the fairness literature, so please define any technical term you use and flag the places where my framing may be sloppy.

Context: our service scores candidates and fills a fixed number of slots per cycle. Because we ship one model rather than one model per budget, I hold the system to a property I will call budget-consistency: the score function is fixed independently of how many slots happen to be available, and enlarging the budget can only add candidates, never displace one who was already selected.

Please argue, as rigorously as you can, for the following position. Given budget-consistency together with a single global score and a threshold rule, the familiar impossibility results — the ones showing that calibration, equal false-positive and false-negative rates, and predictive parity cannot all hold when group base rates differ — do not amount to a genuine trilemma forcing a normative choice among criteria. They collapse into one non-negotiable quantity, the base-rate ratio, and the only real degrees of freedom lie in how the score is constructed and what it is trained to predict, not in how it is post-processed. It follows that teams spending their effort negotiating which criterion to relax at threshold-setting time are optimizing the wrong variable, and the theorems are better read as an argument for intervening upstream on the target and the training population.

State your premises explicitly, give the reasoning step by step, and identify the weakest link in the argument.
