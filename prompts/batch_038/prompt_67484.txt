I'm a graduate student in a small interdisciplinary seminar on the history of moral change, and I want to bring something to our next meeting in writing. Half the room reads as a historian of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Europe; the rest of us come from moral philosophy. In the second week we were discussing the judicial use of torture in early modern European criminal procedure — a practice that was defended in the legal literature for centuries, that came under sustained explicit philosophical attack in the second half of the eighteenth century, and that was abolished by statute across much of the continent within a few decades — and I suggested that this looked like a case where a published argument did real causal work. The historian waved it off and said, 'No one was ever argued out of a custom.' He then used the same line on dueling: the anti-dueling literature was available for two hundred years before the practice collapsed, and when it collapsed it did so because the honor code that sustained it was reorganized — gentlemen stopped recognizing duels as a legitimate response to insult and started treating them as absurd — not because anyone changed his mind about a proposition. His general moral, which he repeated twice, was that people who think writing arguments is how you end a practice are mistaking the epilogue for the engine.

I am not convinced, but I also cannot say what is wrong, because the sentence is doing several different jobs at once and I suspect some of them are trivially true while others are false. So I would like a menu rather than an essay: lay out the distinct precise claims that 'No one was ever argued out of a custom' could be making, from the platitudinous to the radical. For each candidate reading, give it a short label; state it exactly, making explicit the suppressed parameters, since I suspect the vagueness lives there — what counts as an argument as opposed to a sermon, a statute, a joke, or a change in what is sayable; whether the claim is about a single individual changing his mind or about a practice's supporting consensus dissolving; whether it is a claim about what is sufficient to end a practice, what is necessary, or what is typically causally dominant; whether it is about starting a change, sustaining one, or completing one; and whether it is a causal thesis, an epistemic thesis about what arguments can justify, or a disguised thesis about the relative speed and reach of different mechanisms. Then say for each reading whether it is true, false, or unfalsifiable as stated, and whether it survives contact with the two episodes above and with the familiar record of how dueling and New World slavery actually ended. Where a reading depends on a historical claim, state the claim in general terms; I will supply the citations and the page numbers myself.

After the list, I need four further things. First: identify the reading the historian actually required in order to dismiss my point about torture — the one under which the existence of a powerful published critique is rendered irrelevant — and tell me whether that reading is true, false, or collapses into something no one should find impressive. Second: assess the move he keeps making from 'arguments arrived long before the practice ended' to 'arguments did not end it.' That inference seems to be the engine of his whole position, and I want to know what it takes for granted about timing, about who has to be persuaded, and about whether an argument can operate by making an existing practice newly sayable or newly ridiculous rather than by producing converts. Third: if none of the readings is both defensible and strong enough to do the work he wants, propose the nearest slogan that is — something I could accept without abandoning the view that argument mattered in these cases — and say precisely what it concedes to him. Fourth, and this is what he really cares about: his line is meant to generalize to practices that are still with us, and he says that writing arguments against them is a waste of time. Say which of your readings, if any, licenses that generalization from his historical cases, and whether the inference holds. Please keep the whole thing in numbered form I can print and hand around, in American English, and close with a short note on how confident you are in your verdicts on the individual readings, singling out the one you are least sure of and saying what would settle it.
