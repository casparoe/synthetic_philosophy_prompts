I've been sitting in a reading group on the legality of postwar trials for about two months now and we are hopelessly stuck on the retroactivity charge against Nuremberg and Tokyo. Everyone knows the two camps. Call them the notice camp and the authority camp. The notice camp says the defendants can't honestly claim they had no warning: aggressive war had been renounced in treaty form between the wars, the land-warfare rules were already codified and German officers had been trained on them, Germany itself had acceded to the 1936 naval protocol, German jurists in the twenties and thirties had argued for criminalizing war of aggression, and in any case the realistic alternatives in 1945 were summary execution, a Soviet-style show trial, or nothing at all — so whatever you call the proceeding, it wasn't a trap sprung on people who had no reason to expect a reckoning. The authority camp says all of that misses the point: a tribunal constituted by the victors in August 1945 applied a category, crimes against peace, that was not criminal law binding on the defendants when they acted, and neither Radbruch-style appeals to a morality above statute nor the Hart-style concession that it was retroactive but justified touch the legal question — if something counts as law just because it was morally right to impose it, the word has stopped doing any work. Eight weeks, nobody has moved a millimetre.

Here's the pattern that made me think the disagreement might not be what it looks like. Every time one camp produces a piece of history, the other says it's beside the point, and they read the same document as evidence for opposite conclusions. The Tribunal held that the pre-war persecution of German Jews fell outside its jurisdiction as crimes against humanity unless the acts were connected to a war crime or a crime against peace: the notice camp says that shows scrupulousness about its own limits, the authority camp says it shows a charter designed to exclude precisely the worst of it. Doenitz was convicted but the sentence was expressly not assessed on the submarine-warfare count once Nimitz's answers to interrogatories confirmed the US had waged unrestricted submarine warfare in the Pacific from its first day in the war: evenhandedness according to one side, a tu quoque admission according to the other. Pal's dissent at Tokyo works from the same record as the majority and reaches the opposite verdict. My hypothesis, which I can't get anyone to take seriously, is that nobody is actually disagreeing about Nuremberg. One camp is answering a question about the moral permissibility of what the victors did given their options, the other a question about whether "law" names a threshold property of an institutional act rather than a grade, and the argument stalls because each side assumes the other's answer is an answer to its own question. Or maybe there's a hidden empirical question doing all the work — what a defendant in 1939 could reasonably have expected to be held to — that both sides treat as settled in their favor and neither has actually tried to establish. Or maybe it's just verbal about 'law' and 'retroactive' and dissolves once you say so.

So: diagnose it, don't adjudicate it and don't give me a survey of the positions. Tell me which kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, a difference about standards of evidence, a difference about which historical facts count as data, bedrock intuitions about political authority that neither side can argue for, or two questions hiding under one heading — and argue for the diagnosis using what each side actually says rather than what I've summarized. Then tell me what would count as progress on that diagnosis: a distinction someone hasn't drawn, a change of question, an empirical finding about notice, a new argument, or nothing at all — and if nothing, whether the debate is still worth having and what it's really for. Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind about your own diagnosis and where you think you're most likely to be wrong; if one of the two camps has resources I've completely failed to notice, say so even though it cuts against what I want to hear.
