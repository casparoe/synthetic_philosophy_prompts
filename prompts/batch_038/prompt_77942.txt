I'm the animal welfare lead at a small marine research station, and starting this year our main funder wants a documented "sentience review" signed off before anybody runs a procedure on a cephalopod or a decapod crustacean — partly because the UK extended its animal welfare law to cover exactly those groups a few years back after an evidence review, and the journals our PIs publish in have started pointing at it. So I've been handed a 75-minute required session for about 25 people: the animal-care techs, two undergrad volunteers, a visiting vet, and three senior PIs who think the whole exercise is compliance theater and will say so in the first ten minutes. What I need from you is a plan for how to teach the one idea, not the talk itself — please don't write me a script and don't hand me a reading list, I can find my own papers. The idea is this: how you weigh evidence that an animal feels something when you can't ask it and its nervous system is nothing like a mammal's. The thing I want to land is that both of the obvious tests fail, and they fail in opposite directions. The brain-part test (does it have the right structure?) rules out fish, birds, and octopuses for reasons that have nothing to do with what those animals can actually do — I have a tech whose whole position is the slogan no cortex no pain. And the behavior test (does it act like it hurts?) is worthless on its own, because withdrawal from a noxious stimulus is a reflex that you get in spinal animals and even in people under general anesthetic. So what's left isn't a threshold test but a convergence structure, where certain kinds of evidence are strong because of what they rule out, not because they look dramatic.

That's where I need your help with the ordering, because the pieces clearly depend on each other and I can't tell which has to come first. Tell me the sequence and, for each step, what breaks if you put it later — e.g. can I do the reflex point before I've even raised the question of other minds, or does that just sound like I'm dunking on them? And tell me what the one carrying case should be. My instinct was the octopus, but I'm now worried it's the wrong animal for the job: it's charismatic enough that half the room will arrive having seen videos of it opening jars, which sets up exactly the smart-equals-feels confusion I'm trying to break, and its nervous system is so distributed — most of its neurons are out in the arms — that the neat integration-style tests don't obviously apply to it, so it might teach them "we can never know about anything" instead of the structure I actually want. The crab or lobster might be better precisely because the room finds it repulsive and unconvincing. Argue it out and tell me what the case has to have and what it has to lack. Related: I half-remember a crustacean experiment where they shocked an animal inside a shell it clearly preferred and it gave the shell up, and I want to know whether that's the right sort of thing to build the middle of my session around, or whether it's actually the same sort of thing as the reflex cases and I'd be teaching the wrong lesson — i.e. what category of evidence it belongs to and why.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I'm least sure about. I can predict a few: no cortex no pain; it's just a reflex; clever means conscious and dumb means it doesn't hurt; and the ethics slide — the moment I grant that a crab might feel something, someone will say so you're telling us we can't do any research on them, and I need to know where in the sequence to fence that off so it doesn't derail everything after it. But I also expect the fatalist move from the PIs: you can never prove another mind, so this whole review is vibes dressed up as science — and I suspect I'll be tempted to concede too much there and lose the room, so tell me how to defuse it without overclaiming and without turning the session into a philosophy seminar. For each one, give me the exact point in the plan where it should be headed off, not at the end. Also: what should I openly refuse to cover at this level and say out loud that I'm skipping — I'm guessing the named theories of consciousness and the moral-status question, but tell me what else and how to phrase the refusal so it reads as discipline rather than evasion. Finally, one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes whose answers would separate the people who got the evidence structure from the people who just left thinking octopuses are cool — ideally a case none of them have seen before, so it can't be answered from memory. And give me the timing budget across the 75 minutes with a note on what to cut first if the PI argument runs long, because it will.
