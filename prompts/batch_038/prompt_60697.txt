So I've been circling the same three intuitions about tiny harms and huge numbers for like two years now, and I want to stop and write down what a theory in this area actually has to deliver before I'd trust any of them. Not another take on whether you pick the specks over the torture — I want the spec, the checklist you'd hand somebody before you let them publish a view about aggregating trivial harms across vast populations. The reason I care is that this keeps coming up in decisions that aren't sci-fi at all: a chemical that gives a few hundred million people a mildly scratchy throat once a year, a notification policy that costs every user four seconds of irritation a day, microplastics, a slight permanent reduction in sleep quality for a whole metro area. In every one of those the per-person harm is genuinely trivial and the population is genuinely enormous, and I want a principle that returns a usable answer there instead of going lexical and shrugging. So for my purposes, adequate means: determinate, computable with real numbers, and not one that dissolves the question by refusing to compare.

Give me the requirements with the case or consideration behind each one, and be explicit about which are non-negotiable versus which are just the house style of a particular camp. Things I'd expect to see on the list, in no order, plus whatever I'm missing: it has to survive the Sorites pressure from both sides at once — no razor-sharp cutoff where one extra second of pain flips the verdict, but also no green light for a googol of specks to beat fifty years of agony; it has to say whether harms spread across seperate people aggregate the same way as harms stacked inside one life, and if they don't, what exactly is doing the work; it has to handle the probabilistic versions (a one-in-a-billion shot at a year of torture for somebody versus a certain speck for everyone alive) without the verdict depending on whether you write the problem up as risk or as diluted harm; it has to be invariant under redescription, meaning I shouldn't be able to change the answer by chopping the torture into 3,600 one-second pains or by regrouping the specks into bundles; it has to have something to say about infinitely many specks, or about harms smeared over several centuries of future people; and it has to not let me get money-pumped — I don't want a view where a cycle of individually-preferred trades leaves everybody worse off than they started. Also, and this one I think gets skipped a lot: it should explain why we aggregate tiny contributions without any angst in some domains (a billion small donations really do add up to a cure, votes really do add up, milliwatts of exposure really do add up) and resist it in others. A theory whose whole answer is "pain is just different" hasn't explained anything, it's just marked the spot.

Finish with a short scorecard, three or four contenders max, a line or two each against the list: plain cardinal summing, some threshold or lexical view, a view that denies transitivity, and whatever you think is the best current option.
