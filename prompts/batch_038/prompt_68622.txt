I'm on the exhibitions committee at a regional natural history museum and I need to get something circulated before Thursday's meeting. We're redoing the time strip that runs the length of the entrance hall — colored bands for each geological period, in order, ending with the Holocene, which currently runs right up to a mirror at the present-day end so kids see themselves standing inside it. A donor has paid for us to add an Anthropocene band and the committe has split three ways.

Our staff geologist says we can't do it at all. Her argument: the international stratigraphy bodies voted the formal epoch proposal down in 2024 — the one that would have started it at 1950, with the reference site in a Canadian lake — and every other band on our wall is lifted straight from the official timescale, so adding one they specifically rejected makes the whole strip something we can't defend to the school groups who use it. She does admit those same bodies said the word will keep being used to describe human impact, just not as a formal unit.

Two others want the band but can't agree where it starts. Options on the table: agriculture, roughly 10,000 years ago; 1610, the dip in atmospheric CO2 that some tie to the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas and farmland going back to forest; around 1800 with coal and steam; 1950 with the bomb-test layer; the 60s. Our Indigenous advisory circle has said a 1950 seam implies nothing was happening before European industry, and separately that 1610 defines an epoch by their ancestors' deaths. Education worries that visitors will read any single printed date as the moment climate change started.

Its a wall, though — that's the part I keep coming back to. The band has to change color at one specific point, and there's no room at a seam for a shrug.

Draft the memo I'll put in front of them, a page and a half max. It should recommend: whether the word goes on the wall at all and in what status (a full band like the others, something visually subordinate, or no label), where the seam goes if there is one, and roughly 60 words of label copy a twelve-year-old can follow. Give the reasons, and state the geologist's objection at full strength — she'll be in the room and should recognize her own argument — then say why the recommendation survives it.
