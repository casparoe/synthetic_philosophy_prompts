So — background first: I spent 34 years in observational astronomy, mostly cataloguing faint things and worrying about selection effects, and I retired two springs ago and fell down the philosophy hole. Ethics, mostly, which I did not expect. I run a little reading group of ex-scientists at the library and next month we're on what happens to moral theory if the universe is spatially infinite or just goes on forever. I found Bostrom's long essay Infinite Ethics and got maybe 60% of it, and the part that stuck with me is the argument that if there's already infinitely much good and infinitely much bad out there, then anything you do only adds or subtracts a finite amount, so you change nothing — he's got a word for this, something like infinitarian paralysis. I also half remember him saying the fixes have side effects he calls fanaticism and distortion, but I couldnt reconstruct why those are supposed to be worse than the original problem.

Could you act as a professional philosopher who works on population ethics and infinite-value decision theory, and build me a decision tree handout that takes one of my retired engineers from "fine, suppose the world really does contain infinitely many value-bearing lives" to a named position? Please start by brainstorming out loud: list the candidate branch questions you could use, and say which ones you're throwing out because they don't actually separate any two views. Then commit to the tree — call it 7 to 9 nodes and 6 to 8 leaves, plain indented text is fine.

Things I'd like honoured:

- Every node should be a question a smart layperson can answer from a concrete case or claim, without already knowing the label it leads to. No "are you a total utilitarian?"
- One node has to be about where the infinity lives. My instinct from cosmology is that an infinite elsewhere beyond the horizon and an infinite future are not the same kind of thing, and I want to know whether any ethical view turns on that, or whether that's a physicist's prejudice.
- One node about who gets to pick the aggregation rule — ethics or mathematics. If it's mathematics, split on the options I can think of: cap utilities so nothing ever diverges; discount the far away/far future; compare worlds by some rule over their finite parts rather than by a total (I think there's a name like an overtaking criterion, and something involving Banach limits?); or just accept that some worlds are incomparable. Use the technical names if you know them and gloss them if you don't, and tell me if you're unsure of a name rather than inventing one.
- One node on whether paralysis is a reductio or a bullet worth biting, and one on whether fanaticism (staking everything on a tiny probability of an infinite payoff) and the distortion of ordinary finite tradeoffs are dealbreakers or just the price of the view.
- One node on where value sits: in people's experiences, or in impersonal states of the world.

Each leaf gets a name if it has one — mark it unnamed if nobody actually defends it — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really tell apart, and note any combination of answers that has nobody sitting in it, with a guess at why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Here's my own corner, put it somewhere on the tree and be blunt about it. It seems to me that if the aggregation rule can't be settled by moral argument, maybe it's settled by physics instead: a rule that requires comparing spacelike separated infinities is a rule no observer could ever check or apply from inside their own lightcone, so the only legitimate rules are the ones an embedded observer can actually gather evidence about. Is that a real position, a muddled physicist's position, or a node that's empty for a good reason?
