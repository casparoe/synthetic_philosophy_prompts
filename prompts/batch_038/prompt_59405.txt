Eleven years ago someone in our department started a tradition: on the night of the annual public lecture, a single folded broadside is printed and left on the seats, and it carries one poem that bears on the lecture without summarizing it. This year's lecture is on mental causation, and the audience is the usual mix for a free public event — two or three philosophers of mind, a neuroscientist who attends every year in order to object to something, a scattering of undergraduates, and a good many people from town who like the wine afterwards. I have been asked to write the broadside, and I want the poem to stage the question by way of Douglas Hofstadter's careenium, the thought experiment in I Am a Strange Loop: a sealed chamber holding a very great number of small balls that careen about, collide, cling together for a while, and come apart. In the version I want to use, each ball carries a simple symbol that lights when the ball is struck hard enough, so a clump of balls is also a clump of lit symbols; the walls are not inert either, since their give varies with conditions outside and the world therefore leaks in through the cushion, and in the extended version of the picture a settled clump can move things on the far side of the wall. With three balls in the chamber, nothing happens but noise. With thousands, the clumping stops being accidental: certain clumps recur, recruit new balls, and resist coming apart, until at last a clump of clumps behaves as though keeping itself intact were its business. Hofstadter then presses the question I want the broadside to leave open — which of the two levels is pushing which. From one vantage the answer is obvious, because balls are all there is to bump; from the other it is equally obvious, because the balls come and go while the clumps persist, recruit, and steer. One colleague will say the second answer contributes no causation, only vocabulary, every collision being already fully spoken for by earlier collisions; another will say that this is exactly the wrong thing to say about a pattern that maintains itself. The poem must not adjudicate between them. It should make both answers feel available and leave the reader unable to settle on either.

The form is where I want the philosophy to live, so please treat what follows as load-bearing rather than decorative. Two stanzas of ten lines each, in loose iambic pentameter with an audible medial pause in every line, sharing a single rhyme scheme, followed by a coda of three lines. The first stanza is the outside view: its subjects are balls, cushions, clumps, and light, its verbs are physical verbs, and no mental term — want, mean, know, decide, remember, fear, self, I — may enter it. The second stanza is the inside view of the identical run of events, and it must run parallel to the first line by line: the same clause shape, the same number of constituents, the pause in the same place, the same rhyme scheme, with the mental vocabulary now performing every job the physical vocabulary performed. Laid side by side the two stanzas should look like one another with the lexicon exchanged, so that a reader can check, line by line, whether anything whatever has been added. No end-word may serve in both stanzas: the pattern repeats, the filler never does. Then the coda, which is the crux. The mirroring must fail there exactly once. In one of its three lines a symbol-level noun must be the grammatical subject of a transitive verb whose object is a ball, and that line must carry one stress more or one fewer than the lines around it, so the failure is heard as well as seen. Whether the break is in the world or only in the meter — whether a pattern has just moved a ball for which the first stanza had no line, or whether the poem has simply run out of parallels — must remain genuinely undecidable. The coda may not explain itself, and may not announce the ambiguity with a hedge; it should stop while the reader is still trying to place the extra beat.

A few prohibitions, since the room punishes both vagueness and showing off. Do not use emergence, supervenience, downward causation, epiphenomenon, qualia, or reductionism, and do not reach for the ghost in the machine. Do not name Hofstadter or his book inside the poem; the word careenium may appear once at most, and only in the first stanza, where it will read as a made-up name for a made-up room. Do not stage the higher level as a hand above the table, a cue, a puppeteer, or any other outside operator, which is the cheap way of faking the thing in question — whatever authority the clumps have must be visible from inside the chamber. And do not mark the two levels by size, tiny balls against vast patterns, since the claim at issue is that the levels differ in description rather than in scale; a clump should be no grander than the balls that compose it. Someone who has read the literature should be able to put a finger on the line where the exclusion worry is voiced and the line where it is defied; someone who has read none of it should come away with a poem about a roomful of bright colliding things that begins, at some point, to want something. All of it has to fit on one side of the sheet and read aloud in under three minutes.
