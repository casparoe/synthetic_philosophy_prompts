I'm a moral philosopher and, as penance for something I said at a faculty meeting, I now chair our university's research computing committee. Eight of us: two engineers, one administrator, one person who is quite sure philosophy is a hobby. Twice a term we split a fixed pool of accelerator hours across ~twenty proposals, and the VP for research signs off on whatever we send up, so our reasons have to survive an appeal from the people we cut.

The thing I keep running into is the utility monster, except it shows up as a claim rather than a person. Nozick's monster was meant to show that utilitarianism goes badly wrong if someone genuinely gets more out of a unit of resource than everyone else combined; what actually lands on my docket is a confident PI asserting they're that person, with a strong incentive to inflate, no obligation to be right, and benefits that are mostly diffuse strangers and future decades. Taurek's refusal to let the numbers count cuts the other way and makes it worse, since the biggest claims are always about enormous numbers of beneficiaries nobody in the room will ever meet.

Write me a decision procedure the committee can actually run: 90 minuites for a full docket, no economists, nobody who can audit a life-years model, everyone's read the proposals once. I want the questions to ask of a monster-claim in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which ones are settled by facts (marginal product per hour rather than total product, whether returns diminish at the scale they're requesting, how the claim survives being given half, whether the payoff is verifiable inside our time horizon at all) and which are really value calls that we should vote on and admit we're voting on; where the procedure outputs a flat no or a "not this round"; and — this is the part I care about most — how it stays inflation-proof, i.e. what it does once every proposal on the docket claims to be the monster, which is roughly what happens two cycles after you start rewarding big claims.

Then run it on three cases. (a) A drug-discovery foundation model asking for most of the pool, promising a large expected number of life-years, zero published results, but unusually candid about its own uncertainty. (b) A regional climate downscaling project, small well-documented benefits, long queue of dependent users, nobody's life on the line. (c) A digital humanities corpus proposal from my own department arguing the material is irreplaceable and will still be worth reading in four hundred years. Give me the verdict you'd defend, including on (c), which is mine, so be blunt about it rather than diplomatic.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: which real monster-claims it will wrongly kill, which distinctions it flattens when it gets turned into a form, and where it's just my priors about human nature wearing a flowchart as a costume. And be explicit about what would change your mind — which argument (Taurek's, or the impossibility of interpersonal comparison, or something I haven't thought of) would make you build this differently, and which single step you think is most likely to break when eight tired people actually use it.
