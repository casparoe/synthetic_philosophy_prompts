I am taking an advanced undergraduate course in social choice and voting theory, and for a seminar presentation I constructed an argument that generalizes Arrow's theorem from collective choice to individual deliberation. I built it to be provocative, but I have since become uneasy: the conclusion is not something I can accept, and when I put the argument to my classmates all I got back was that the conclusion is absurd, which is not a diagnosis. I would like an actual one.

Here is the argument as I wrote it out. Fix a finite menu of at least three options — say, three job offers — and one agent who must rank them.

1. Arrow's theorem. Given finitely many individuals and at least three alternatives, any function that assigns to every profile of the individuals' complete and transitive rankings a complete and transitive social ranking, and that satisfies unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, and independence of irrelevant alternatives, must be dictatorial. So no such function satisfies those three conditions together with non-dictatorship.

2. Nothing in the theorem or its proof refers to the inputs belonging to different people. It is a result about functions from profiles of orderings to an ordering, and it therefore governs any aggregation of orderings, whatever supplies them.

3. Deliberation is such an aggregation. Each consideration the agent takes seriously — salary, proximity to her family, intellectual interest in the work — generates its own complete and transitive ranking of the three offers, and deliberating consists in combining that profile into a single ranking she can act on.

4. The agent is not herself one of the inputs. If she were, non-dictatorship would fail as a matter of arithmetic and nothing interesting would follow. She is the function, not an argument of it; the arguments are the rankings contributed by her considerations.

5. Her aggregation must satisfy unrestricted domain. Any profile her considerations might generate has to be admissible, because a rule that is silent on some profile is incomplete, and incompleteness is a defect in a theory of how to decide.

6. It must satisfy weak Pareto. If every consideration ranks one offer above another, a ranking that puts the second above the first cannot be her considered view at all.

7. It must satisfy independence of irrelevant alternatives. How she ranks the first offer against the second may depend only on how each consideration ranks those two against each other, and not on the rest of the profile — not on where the third offer sits, and not on how strongly any consideration prefers one to the other.

8. It must satisfy non-dictatorship. If one consideration settles the ranking in every profile, whatever the others say, she is not weighing anything; she is executing a standing rule. That is dogmatism rather than deliberation.

9. So by the theorem no such function exists. There is no way for a single agent to combine her own considerations rationally, for exactly the reason there is no way for a committee to combine its members' preferences democratically.

10. The obvious escape is to drop independence. But then the ranking of two offers becomes sensitive to how the considerations rank the others, so a fourth offer that she would never accept could reverse her verdict between the first two. A ranking that can be flipped by an option nobody wants, and that ignores how much each consideration cares, is manipulable and arbitrary in precisely the way we call irrational.

11. Therefore either individual deliberation is impossible or it is irrational in the sense just described. In either case there is no rational way for one person to aggregate her own considerations.

12. Yet some people plainly deliberate well, and I take that to be a datum rather than an illusion.

I would be grateful for the following. First, tell me whether the conclusion genuinely follows from the premises, or whether some step is licensed only under a reading that the earlier steps have not established — and if the latter, say exactly where the reading changes. Second, name the step you think should be rejected and explain what made it look unobjectionable to me; I want to understand why the argument persuaded me, not merely be told that it should not have. Third, price the alternatives. For each step a defender might give up instead, say what is lost and whether the loss is fatal to the argument or merely uncomfortable. I should tell you that I am most attached to (3) and to the intuition behind (10) — that deliberation really is a matter of combining rankings, and that a verdict overturned by the presence of an option no one would choose is a genuine defect — so if your diagnosis requires surrendering either, please say plainly what I am buying with the surrender. Finally, if some weakened version of the argument survives your objections, state what it proves and why that conclusion should worry me less than (11).
