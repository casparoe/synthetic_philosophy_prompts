Roughly, as I read it: Hume defines a miracle as a violation of the laws of nature, and says that because a firm and unalterable experience has established those laws, the proof against a miracle is as complete as any argument from experience can possibly be. Then he turns around and says our confidence in human testimony is *also* built on experience — we've observed that people usually report things accurately, that memory is mostly reliable, that there's a general love of truth, and that shame attaches to being caught in a falsehood. He lists the things that weaken testimony: witnesses contradicting each other, few witnesses, witnesses of bad character, witnesses with something to gain, witnesses hesitating, witnesses asserting too violently. Then the rule that everyone on the forum was fighting about: testimony is not sufficient to establish a miracle unless the testimony is of such a kind that its being false would be more miraculous than the fact it's trying to establish. And somewhere near the end of that part he talks about there being a proof on each side, and the mind subtracting the weaker from the stronger and holding belief in proportion to what's left over.

I've written both as scoring functions and they disagree on the same inputs, so which one is the passage actually doing?
