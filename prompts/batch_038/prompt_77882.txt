I am a fourth-year doctoral student in historical sociology, and the first chapter of my dissertation leans on Braudel's *longue durée* to frame an argument about how durable institutional arrangements constrain state response to fiscal crisis. A reader on my committee, evidently impatient with the frame, sent me a chapter from a recent general-audience synthesis of twentieth-century historiography and suggested I either correct it or stop invoking Braudel. The passage runs as follows:

"Braudel's *Mediterranean* rests on a simple but radical idea: history runs on three clocks. The slowest is geography — mountains and plains, seas and passes, climate and crops — and over the *longue durée* this is effectively destiny: the Mediterranean world behaves as it does because its environment permits no other pattern of life, and the constraints that bound the sixteenth century bound antiquity and bind the present still. The middle clock is the conjuncture: prices, populations, trade circuits, states and civilizations, rising and falling in cycles of decades. The fastest is the event — battles, treaties, the decisions of ministers — which Braudel dismisses as foam flickering on the surface of a wave, an artifact of our own vantage point, caused entirely by what lies beneath it and causing nothing in return. Hence the book's own shape: its longest movement is devoted to the sea and the mountains, and by the time Philip II appears he has all but ceased to be an agent, a prisoner of his own basin rather than its author. That is what the Annales school meant by 'total history,' and it is why academic history abandoned narrative for a generation. The lesson for us is methodological: if you want to explain why some places are rich and others poor, put down the biography and pick up the map, because Braudel showed that leaders are decoration."

My own inclination — which may be a pious reconstruction of a thinker I have only read in extracts — is that the determinist reading is unfair, that Braudel's milieu was explicitly hostile to crude environmental determinism, and that the *longue durée* names a tempo of change rather than a length of period. But I cannot tell how much of the passage above is accurate compression, how much is a real distortion, and how much is merely loose.

Please audit it claim by claim, marking each as accurate, defensible simplification, or misrepresentation, and telling me what a specialist would object to and on what grounds. In particular: is the event for Braudel causally inert, or merely of low explanatory yield, and does the book's third part bear out the claim that the individual disappears? Are the three time scales meant to be studied in compartments or in interaction? Does "total history" mean what the passage says it means? Is the retreat from narrative fairly charged to Braudel personally, given that the school spans several generations with quite different programs? And does the final methodological lesson follow from anything true in the summary, or does it require the errors? Rank the problems by how much they would cost me if I repeated them, and identify the two or three sentences I can safely carry into my chapter. I do not want a bibliography or suggestions for further reading — only the diagnosis.
