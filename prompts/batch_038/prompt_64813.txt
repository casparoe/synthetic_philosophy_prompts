A summary of the handicap principle that I encounter constantly runs as follows: a signal can be reliable only if it is costly to produce, because cost is what makes dishonesty unaffordable for low-quality individuals. I want to pressure-test that summary against a case deliberately constructed to strain it, and to understand what work the notion of cost is doing in the underlying theory.

The case. Males of a species vary in a hidden condition: robust or frail. Females prefer robust mates and cannot observe condition directly. A male either grows a conspicuous crest or does not. The crest is metabolically trivial to grow, and it additionally functions as a heat-dissipating surface that improves the bearer's foraging efficiency in warm weather. It is also conspicuous to predators, and evading them demands sustained vigilance. Robust males, who forage efficiently, can spare that vigilance; frail males cannot, and for them the predation penalty exceeds the thermal and foraging gains. The population settles into a separating equilibrium: robust males crest, frail males abstain, and females read the crest correctly.

Please address the following, in order.

1. Explain, at the level of the incentive structure rather than the biology, why this population separates. Identify the conditions on the two types' payoffs that are doing the work, and state explicitly whether those conditions amount to the claim that the signal is costly.

2. In my case the robust males would grow the crest even if no female ever attended to it. Say whether that fact matters: whether the crest is properly a signal at all, whether the female's preference is tracking something strategically produced or something merely correlated with condition, and whether the handicap principle is even in scope here.

3. Give a verdict on whether the case is a counterexample to the handicap principle. Distinguish at least three readings of cost that the case pulls apart — the cost of producing the signal, the net effect of the signal on the signaler's fitness, and whatever differential property the equilibrium actually requires — and say which reading the principle must adopt if it is to be both non-trivial and true.

4. Now modify the case: suppose the crest is net-beneficial for frail males as well, though less so than for robust males, so that cresting makes no type worse off. Explain why separation would collapse if the female's response were held fixed, and then explain under what further conditions it could be restored — in particular, whether the female's own inferential response can supply the differential that the trait no longer does.

Begin your reply with a single sentence stating your verdict on question 3; develop the four questions in order after that, with no preamble and no restatement of the case or of the questions.

Finally, close by saying what would change your mind about that verdict: which feature of my description, if altered or removed, would flip it, and at which step in your answer to question 4 your reasoning is most likely to fail.
