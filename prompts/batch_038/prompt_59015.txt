I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy, and I have been asked to chair the question period following a departmental colloquium on divine hiddenness. Our speaker is a philosopher of religion who does not contest the empirical premise of Schellenberg's argument: she is prepared to grant, for the sake of discussion, that there are sincere nonresistant nonbelievers — people open to a relationship with God, who have looked, and who find the evidence insufficient — and that God is hidden in something like the sense the argument requires. Where she parts company with the argument is over its conception of God. Her position, roughly, is that the argument takes as its target a being who is a person among persons, a divine individual whose perfection is expressed in seeking intimate, mutually recognized interpersonal contact with each creature, and that this is not the God of classical theism. On her telling, a being who is not one item within the order of things but the ground of that order is not the sort of thing that could be an object of belief in the way that a person or a planet can; the appropriate creaturely stance toward such a being is not belief-that but something closer to trust, participation, or a reorientation of the will; and the hiddenness the argument diagnoses is therefore built into the difference between creator and creature rather than being a datum calling for explanation, let alone a reductio of theism. She is very good at closing off further pressure with a formulation to this effect: the argument succeeds only in showing that a certain description of deity is uninstantiated, and theists who never accepted that description have lost nothing.

For this task, adopt the role of an expert in analytic philosophy of religion with a detailed command of the hiddenness literature and of classical-theist theology. Please think carefully and work through the problem before drafting: begin with a short paragraph of brainstorming that identifies the four or five places where a position of this shape is most vulnerable and where the literature genuinely presses it, and only then give me the questions.

I need questions that make that closing move progressively harder to sustain, and that engage the view on its own terms rather than dragging her back to a version of the argument she has already declined to accept. Specifically:

- Nine or ten questions, in the order I would ask them, each followed by (a) the answer you most expect from her, stated in a sentence or two, and (b) a follow-up keyed to that particular answer rather than a generic probe.
- At least one question on each of the following: whether belief that God exists can really be pried apart from the trust or relationship she says is the appropriate stance, given that one can hardly orient oneself toward, or entrust oneself to, something whose existence one doubts; the coercion line descended from Hick's notion of an epistemic distance — that overwhelming evidence of God would compromise the freedom of creatures — and whether a God who is not a person can deploy that reply at all, since it appears to assume precisely the interpersonal picture she has disowned; what she thinks is going on in prayer, when someone addresses a being of whom it is supposed to be a category mistake to treat as a person; whether the position preserves anything recognizable as the God of Abraham and of the psalms, or slides toward a deism or an impersonalism that most theists would not accept as their own; the wrinkle from the cognitive science of religion, namely that belief in supernatural agents appears cognitively easy and near-universal, and whether that supports her claim that belief-that is the wrong category for the stance in question or instead undercuts it; and whether her view can absorb a nonbelief that persists for a lifetime and ends in death, or whether it quietly depends on an eschatology in which hiddenness is temporary and the relationship eventually consummated — in which case she has relocated the argument's temporal premise rather than answered it.
- The set should build from the most concessive question to the most demanding, so that the last three do the heaviest work, and it should end with a question asking what would count as evidence against her own view.
- Nothing of the "what first drew you to philosophy of religion" variety: every question must turn on the content of the position.
- The whole set has to fit a twenty-five-minute question period, so the questions themselves should be short enough to be delivered aloud in a breath or two, and the follow-ups equally terse.

Finally, a request about the standing of the questions. For each one, indicate briefly whether it presses a point that is genuinely live and contested among people who work on this material, or whether it depends on a premise most participants would regard as settled — and flag any place where the framing of a question quietly assumes a contested reading of the hiddenness argument (whether its target must be a personal being, for instance, or whether belief-that is really doing the work the argument needs it to do) rather than treating that reading as agreed upon. I would much rather have the contested status marked than have the questions look sharper than the literature actually is; please do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus among philosophers of religion.
