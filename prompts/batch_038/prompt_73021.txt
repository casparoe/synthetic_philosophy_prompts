so I'm six months into a novel and its turned out that the whole book is about people who never say anything false. Set roughly now, mid-sized city, most of the plot runs through a marriage and a workplace investigation, and my beta reader keeps writing "he's lying" in the margins of scenes where I have been extremely careful that every sentence out of the character's mouth is literally true. Which is the point of the scene! But it's got me worried I don't have a handle on the distinction I'm building the book on, so I typed out the cases I've actually been going by and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

All from the drafts:

1. Asked what he thought of his mother-in-law's cooking, a character says "the wine was excellent." Not a lie, and not a sin either. Nobody should be offended on his behalf.

2. Asked whether he was at the hotel Tuesday night, a character says "I was at the office until nine." True. He left at seven and was at the hotel at eight. My verdict: worse than if he'd just said "no." I want the reader to hate him here.

3. A character says "I've never been unfaithful to you" while holding a private definition of unfaithful that happens to exclude one particular afternoon. Verdict: this one IS a lie, even though the sentence comes out true by his own lights. I can hear that this is in tension with 2 and I can't tell you why.

4. In a sworn deposition, "I don't recall." He recalls fine. Lie, and the worst thing in the book.

5. A character tells his mother, three days before she dies, that his brother rang to ask after her. Nobody rang. Lie — and I want it to read as the most decent thing he does in 300 pages.

6. A department head opens a staff meeting with "the quarter went well" to a room of people who have all read the same numbers and know it's false. Verdict: a lie, sure, but nobody's been deceived and I don't feel it's as bad as 4. Almost the opposite. It reads like a move in a power game rather than a fraud.

7. A character writes "as you'll know, the deadline's passed" when he has no idea whether she knows, and later "glad you've finally decided to sort this out." Verdict: something rotten here, but I can't point at a single false sentence.

8. A character hangs his colleague's coat on his own hall stand so his wife will draw a conclusion he never states. Verdict: deception, definately, and if anything worse than the words. But is he lying? He didn't say anything. My gut says the word doesn't apply, and my gut also says that's a cop-out.

Three I can't rule on and want the principle to decide for me: an out-of-context quotation that is itself perfectly accurate; whether a first-person past-tense narrator who reports only true events but arranges them so the reader lands on something false is lying to the reader, or whether you just can't lie in narration at all; and the scene I'm stuck on, where a character answers a direct question with something true that he has chosen precisely so the listener will infer something false and act on it at their own cost.

What I want back: the principle, or the small handful of genuinely competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to my beta reader, with which clause is doing the work in which case; your verdicts on the three open ones; and straight up, which of 1 through 8 I have to give up if I want a coherent account instead of a pile of intuitions. If there are established names for these moves — I vaguely know there's some old casuistry about reserving the meaning in your head, and something about lies nobody believes — use the names, but don't hand me a reading list at the end. I won't chase it down and half of it would be wrong anyway. I'd rather have it tight than thorough.
