I want a disciplined case study, not a survey. The episode is the International Astronomical Union's August 2006 vote in Prague adopting a formal definition of *planet*, together with the run-up to it and the dispute that has continued since.

Let me stipulate the facts so you don't have to reconstruct them. The resolution defined a planet as a body that (i) is in orbit around the Sun, (ii) has sufficient mass for self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes hydrostatic equilibrium — a nearly round shape — and (iii) has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit; a non-satellite body meeting the first two but not the third became a *dwarf planet*. Pluto met the first two and failed the third. An alternative resolution that would have made dwarf planets a subcategory of planets, thereby admitting Ceres and Eris, was put and rejected. Only on the order of four hundred of the IAU's roughly ten thousand individual members voted, by show of hands, most of them those present for the closing session. The trigger was Eris, discovered in 2005 and initially measured as slightly larger than Pluto. Persistent objections: the definition applies only to bodies orbiting the Sun and so says nothing directly about exoplanets; criterion (iii) is location-relative, since a Mars-sized body parked in the inner Oort cloud would fail it; Mercury is not in fact in hydrostatic equilibrium but was retained as a planet; and a substantial group of planetary scientists, with Alan Stern the most prominent, rejects the dynamical criterion in favour of a geophysical one on which roundness alone suffices. There is also a precedent: after Ceres was found on 1 January 1801, and Pallas, Juno and Vesta followed, the planet list had grown to twenty-three by 1851 before astronomers began cataloguing the new bodies separately as asteroids — an extension change nobody voted on.

Now run that episode through two-dimensional semantics: primary intensions as functions from scenarios or worlds considered as actual, secondary intensions as extensions across counterfactual worlds once actual-world reference is fixed, plus the scrutability thesis and the method-of-elimination test for verbal disputes. I want five things.

First, give the primary and the secondary intension of *Pluto is a planet*, and show how each varies with the reference-fixing story you adopt for the term — fixed by dynamical dominance, by roundness and internal structure, or by something partly stipulative and anchored to our own system. Say which story best fits pre-2006 usage and defend the choice. Then address the Kripkean parallel: if the relevant property term is rigid, the secondary intension may be necessary while the primary one is contingent, in the pattern of *water is H₂O*; say whether that pattern holds here or breaks, and where.

Second, identify which intension the 2006 disputants were actually contesting. My suspicion is that neither camp was arguing about counterfactual worlds at all. Then apply the method of elimination: strip out the term and see whether the Stern camp and the dynamical camp still disagree, and say what the elimination bottoms out in — a real disagreement about which property in nature the term ought to track, something properly called bedrock, or nothing that survives.

Third, scrutability. Suppose a Laplacean description delivers every physical truth about the solar system, plus a that's-all clause and the indexical truths. Is *Pluto is a planet* scrutable from that by a priori reasoning? If not, name the further premise required and classify it as physical, semantic, or normative, and say what the answer costs or saves the thesis.

Fourth, the diachronic problem. The Ceres precedent shows the extension moving without stipulation; the 2006 vote shows it moving by stipulation. Does the framework have resources for both? In particular: is the primary intension of a term like this determinate at a time for a community, or does this case suggest systematic indeterminacy — many candidate primary intensions equally compatible with usage, differing exactly on the disputed bodies? If the latter, say whether that is a local defect in one semi-technical term or a general problem for a framework that relies on determinate primary intensions.

Fifth, say where the theory is silent. For each of three features — the restriction to bodies orbiting the Sun, the retention of Mercury by fiat despite failing criterion (ii), and the fact that a committee's definition rather than a discovery changed the extension of a natural-kind-looking term — say whether two-dimensional semantics has anything to say, and decline to force an answer where it has not.

Finally, commit. Tell me which of three things this episode is for the framework: a confirmation, a case of the sort the primary/secondary distinction was built to handle; an anomaly, something it gets wrong or cannot accommodate; or a case it never anticipated, because nothing in the machinery was designed for stipulative redefinition of a semi-technical kind term by majority vote. Pick one and argue for it. Commit as well on whether the dispute was verbal or substantive — if your answer is that it was partly both, say precisely which part is which and what carries the substantive weight — and on whether scrutability comes through the case intact.

I want a definite position with reasons rather than a menu of options. Where the analysis depends on a contested reading of the framework — whether primary intensions belong to individual speakers or to a linguistic community, whether bedrock is the right label for what the elimination test terminates in, whether scrutability is a claim about knowledge or about metaphysical determination — flag the reading at the point where you adopt it and say what turns on it, and mark the places where you are exercising judgment about the astronomy or about ordinary usage rather than following the theory. No citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I want your reasoning.
