Right — I'm seven months into a novel and there's a chapter I keep ducking. Before I write it I want to see the thing work as a standalone short story, mostly so I can find out whether the idea has any blood in it or whether I'm just attached to a footnote I read three years ago. So: could you write it for me? Two to two-and-a-half thousand words, first person, one continuous scene if you can manage it, two at most.

All the machinery below is invented. Don't go hunting for a real-world equivalent, there isn't one — just take it as the rules of the game and play inside them.

There's a visual prosthesis for people whose eyes are intact but whose visual cortex went quiet after a stroke. A camera clipped to the jacket collar drives electrodes at the back of the skull. The catch is the lag: driving cortex that way takes time to assemble into anything coherent, so the firmware holds each stretch of input in a buffer and only releases it once it's had a chance to compose. My narrator lives about four hundred milliseconds behind the world. She's been on it eleven months and she's good with it — she pours tea, she catches keys thrown at her, she crosses roads. Nobody who meets her would guess.

There are two builds of the firmware and the company insists they're functionally identical, and in the sense that matters they're right. In one, the buffer composes the whole stretch before any of it is released: her present is always a finished sentence, delivered complete, assembled from input that includes things which hadn't happened yet at the moment she experiences them as happening. In the other, the buffer releases the material raw and the composing happens downstream, in memory, where it can be quietly patched later — after she's been told something, after she's seen the aftermath, after a week of other peoples versions. No test distinguishes them. Her behaviour is identical either way. She can't tell which one she's running and neither can the clinic. That's not a plot twist, that's the load-bearing wall: she never finds out.

The seed of all this, if it helps you calibrate the tone: that old two-light experiment where a dot seems to change colour halfway across the screen and people will swear they saw the change before the second light came on. You know the one. Don't explain it to me and don't put it in the story.

The event. A car park, early evening, eleven months and four days into her new sight. A man goes down in front of a van and she is the only witness. She gives her statement inside the hour and she is calm and specific: the reverse lights came on, the man stepped back into the van's path, he went down. Three weeks later, at the deposition, she is shown the car park camera, whose clock is good to a tenth of a second. On the camera the man is already on the ground before the reverse lights come on.

Both builds account for the mismatch perfectly. Under one she really did see the right order at the time and something patched it afterwards, so she is an unreliable witness to her own past but there was once an accurate original. Under the other there was never an accurate original, not for a single instant: what she experienced was manufactured by machinery that had already been handed the whole sequence, and the account she gave the police is a faithful transcript of an experience that was never a recording. The defence will use whichever reading hurts more, and can't know which that is. She has to sit in the room and listen to someone argue about her and not be able to tell whether she is a person whose memories get edited or a person who never had any.

What I need, and how I'll know if it's working:

- The words never appear. No "Orwellian," no "Stalinesque," no "buffer," no "firmware," no "Cartesian." If a character says any of it out loud the chapter's dead, and so is the story.
- No deposition speech where someone explains the philosophy. The mismatch has to arrive as a fact on a screen and land in her body.
- The prose has to do what the buffer does. She should be describing things in an order that is very slightly wrong, in sentences that are honest and withhold nothing, and the reader should only be able to reconstruct the real order afterwards — without feeling cheated, and without a reveal. This is the hard part and it's the only part I actually care about. No lying narrator, no held-back cards. Just a woman reporting faithfully from a present that arrives late.
- Give me the hands. Early on, something small: she catches something, or ducks something, or answers a question about something she hasn't seen yet. Two sentences, no comment on it. Everything later should be readable back through those two sentences.
- One image near the end doing the work the explanation would have done. Not a metaphor somebody states — a thing she looks at.
- No dreams, no she-was-the-machine-all-along, no technician with a guilty secret, no scene where someone kindly explains to her what she is.

Then after the story, seperate from it, two sentences in your own voice: which of the two builds the story ends up endorsing, and which sentence commits me to it. If you think it endorses neither, say so, and say why that isn't a cop-out.
