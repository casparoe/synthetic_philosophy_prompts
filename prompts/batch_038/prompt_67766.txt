Third-year PhD student in political theory, writing a chapter on precommitment, and I want to pressure-test the Ulysses frame against the euro's no-bailout clause. Facts as I have them: Article 125 TFEU provides that neither the Union nor one member state is liable for or will assume the debt commitments of another, and it was put there at Maastricht precisely so that bond markets would keep disciplining national fiscal policy. Then 2010 happens, Greece can't refinance, and within two years you have the EFSF, a European Council decision adding a paragraph to Article 136 to make room for a permanent mechanism, the ESM treaty, and the ECB's OMT programme — all of it eventually upheld by the CJEU in Pringle and Gauweiler, with the German Federal Constitutional Court breathing down everyone's neck.

Here's what I cant get a grip on, and it's the bit I want you to actually work on rather than summarizing Elster at me. The self-binding literature gives you two ways a Ulysses contract fails: the ropes stretch, or Ulysses unties himself. But nothing here was ever breached. The crew re-fitted the mast mid-voyage, using the procedure the ship's own manual specifies, and the courts certified that is was still the same mast. So which is it — did the constraint hold, because it dictated conditionality, memoranda, last-resort status and no outright transfers, or did it fail, because the thing it forbade became legal? Does the framework even have a slot for a commitment whose unbinding is itself constitutionally authorised, and what does that do to the claim that binding the future is what makes a constitutional order legitimate rather than just a dead hand gripping the living? Map it carefully — who's Ulysses, who are the sirens, who holds the ropes, when does the voyage start — and tell me where the account goes quiet instead of filling the gap with something it can't support.
