I hold a rather specific view about the end of the western Roman empire, and I would like it attacked rather than explained back to me.

The view is not the familiar claim that Rome transformed instead of falling. It is the stronger, second-order claim that whether Rome fell is not settled by the evidence at all. On my account, catastrophists and transformationists largely share the same record: the frequency and distribution of dated Mediterranean shipwrecks, the spread and then narrowing of traded amphora types and fine wares, the replacement of standardised pottery by coarse locally made ware, the shrinkage of livestock body sizes in the post-Roman west, the disappearance of tile roofing and mortared stone from ordinary buildings, the contraction of literacy into ecclesiastical hands, barbarian rulers who claimed Roman offices and issued Roman-style law, an eastern half that never stopped describing itself as Roman, and a deposition of the last western emperor in 476 that no one living through it appears to have marked as an epoch. Where the two camps diverge is in deciding which markers carry weight — state capacity and taxation, long-distance exchange, material complexity, demographic scale, cultural and institutional continuity, the experience of contemporaries — and in deciding which losses count as losses. The dispute is therefore substantially normative and semantic rather than empirical: a disagreement about the weighting function, not about the values being weighted.

What would count against this position? I want a map of its exposure, and I want it to be honest about the places where the position is genuinely safe.

First, what kind of finding would be decisive — evidence that would settle the question under any defensible weighting? Say what such evidence would have to look like, and whether anything of that kind exists or is possible in principle. Two candidates occur to me and I want to know whether you agree: a break that is tightly dated and synchronised across provinces rather than regionally staggered, and a marker so central that its loss cannot be offset by gains elsewhere, such as the capacity to feed cities, collect taxes, or move goods and messages over distance.

Second, are there live factual disputes between the camps as opposed to disputes about weighting? Name the concrete ones and say how they cut. The demographic severity of the plague pandemic from the 540s onward, the scale of continental migration and population replacement in post-Roman Britain, and what the lead deposition record in polar and alpine ice implies about the pace of contraction in metal production all strike me as places where the camps disagree about the data itself rather than its significance. If that is right, my claim that they share the record is weakened, and I want to know by how much and in which regions.

Third, what does my position predict that a straightforward Rome-fell view does not, and where are those predictions risky? My own list includes the expectation that new data will be claimed by both camps, that period boundaries will prove arbitrary and regionally variable rather than synchronic, and that the sharpest declines will cluster in provinces most dependent on the imperial system rather than falling uniformly across the empire. Tell me which of these could fail, what failure would look like in the archaeological or documentary record, and whether any of them already has.

Fourth, the insulation problem. My position appears able to absorb any evidence of decline by redescribing it as transformation, and any evidence of continuity by redescribing it as compatible with collapse. Assess whether that is in fact so, and if it is, whether it is a defect. Please keep two sub-questions apart: whether a thesis about the underdetermination of a question by the evidence is permitted to be insulated in this way, and whether my thesis has a self-application problem — if the fall-versus-transformation dispute is merely a weighting choice, then so is my meta-thesis, which would make it a third party to the dispute rather than a dissolution of it.

Fifth, tell me what a committed defender of the meta-thesis would themselves concede as counting against it, as distinct from what its critics say. Two concessions strike me as available, and I want to know whether you think either is damaging. One is that contemporaries described ruin, famine, captivity and moral collapse in their own terms — Gildas lamenting the destruction of Britain and attributing it to the Britons' own sins, Salvian contending that the empire's vices invited its undoing and reporting that provincials sometimes preferred barbarian rule to Roman tax collection — which looks like first-person evidence of catastrophe rather than a weighting preference. The other is that there exist general theories of societal collapse, such as the account of collapse as diminishing marginal returns on investment in sociopolitical complexity, which claim to supply an objective and in principle measurable criterion; if a theory of that kind succeeds, there is no room left for my underdetermination claim.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, mark clearly which ones a defender should concede and which they should dismiss, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated and that this either is or is not a problem for a claim of this kind. For what it is worth, what would move me most is a single case in which one camp's factual claim is false by the other camp's own lights and by evidence both already accept — not a case in which they merely describe the same facts under different evaluative vocabulary.
