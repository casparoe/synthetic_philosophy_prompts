QUESTION (three parts, ~400 words expected):

"In Nicomachean Ethics V.5 Aristotle takes up exchange. (a) What problem of proportionality does exchange have to solve, and what is Aristotle's solution? (b) What role does money play, and on what grounds does Aristotle treat money as a matter of convention rather than nature? (c) What is Aristotle's attitude to the getting of wealth, and what does he single out as the worst form of it?"

ANSWER:

"(a) Aristotle is dealing with reciprocity here, which is a third kind of justice alongside distribution and rectification. The problem is that the parties exchanging are not equal and the things they exchange are not the same kind of thing at all — a house and a pair of shoes. If exchange were just a matter of handing back equal value one side would come out ahead and the association would break down, and for Aristotle it's exchange that holds the community together. His solution is proportionate reciprocity: the ratio has to run crosswise, diagonally, so the builder stands to the shoemaker as the shoemaker's product stands to the builder's product. Once the products are equalised in that proportion each party recieves the equivalent of what he gave.

(b) That equalisation only works if the two products have some common measure, and money is that measure. It functions as a kind of middle term and it lets us state how many shoes are worth a house. Underneath it the real standard is need: need is what holds everything together, and if need stops, exchange stops. Money only represents need conventionally, which is why it's called nomisma — it does not exist by nature but by convention, and can be altered or rendered useless whenever people decide to. That's really Aristotle's central point: money has no intrinsic value at all, its worth is entirely whatever the community agrees it is, so in principle it could be made of anything or of nothing. This is basically the modern account of fiat currency, and it's why Aristotle still gets cited in arguments about whether paper money needs backing.

(c) Aristotle distinguishes the getting of wealth that belongs to household management, which is natural because it provides the goods a household needs in order to live well, from money-making undertaken for its own sake, which has no limit and aims at mere accumulation. Trade and usury both fall into the second category. Usury is worst of all, because the usurer sells time and time belongs to god rather than to him, so he charges for something he does not own. Aristotle's condemnation of interest is therefore fundamentally religious, which is why the medieval church could take it over wholesale. Generally he is hostile to money-making as such and thinks that whoever pursues wealth without limit has confused the means of living with living well."
