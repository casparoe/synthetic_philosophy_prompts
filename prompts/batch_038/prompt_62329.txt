I have been invited to give the opening address at a two-day workshop on measurement and values in health priority-setting: twenty minutes to speak, thirty minutes for questions, an audience of perhaps thirty. The room will be mostly cost-effectiveness analysts and health economists who build QALY-based models feeding national and hospital-level allocation decisions; two or three Bayesian decision analysts who treat every allocation question as an expected-utility problem; a handful of public-health ethicists who are broadly prioritarian; and no one who works on contractualism or has read Scanlon.

My thesis is deliberately unfashionable, and I expect this audience to hear it as an attack on their working instruments rather than as a puzzle in moral theory. The thesis is this. The familiar objection to interpersonal aggregation — that summing across persons fails to take seriously the distinction between persons — is not the objection I want, and I think it is the weaker of the two. What I defend is a claim about measurement. Any sum of complaints presupposes a common unit, and the units in which distinct harms are measured — years of life, degrees of functional impairment, intensity and duration of pain, the loss borne by a dependent who survives the patient — are fixed by different and non-interchangeable conventions of zero point and interval. The QALY, on my account, is not a crude or noisy measure of a single underlying quantity that better instrumentation could improve; it is an instrument that silently converts one kind of claim into another. The consequence I press is that the aggregation problem is not a numbers problem at all: even two people with the identical complaint cannot be summed, because the meaning of the sum depends on which unit convention has been adopted, and no convention is non-arbitrary. My positive proposal, which I call serial justification, holds that a program is permissible if it survives pairwise comparison against each competing claim one at a time, under an ordering of relevance that never terminates in a sum. I concede openly, and mean to keep conceding, that within a single person's life I permit aggregation across time.

Prepare me a question-period sheet rather than a defense of the view. Give me the eight to ten objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by the product of how likely each is to be asked and how much damage it would do if I answered it badly. Phrase each in the vocabulary its asker would use — thresholds and threshold values, budget impact, opportunity cost, identifiable versus statistical patients, equity and severity weights, ex ante versus ex post evaluation, willingness-to-pay elicitation, sensitivity analysis — and not in the vocabulary of a moral-philosophy literature they will not have read. For each objection supply a reply I can deliver in roughly ninety seconds, an explicit note on where that reply is soft, and a judgement on whether I should concede the point, hold it, or reframe it. Indicate which objections are characteristically the decision analyst's rather than the health economist's, and identify the one that would be fatal, as opposed to merely irritating, if it were still standing when the session ends. Tell me what to concede in my first two minutes so that the room does not dismiss the talk as philosophy that has never met a budget, and what single reply has to land if the proposal is to survive the afternoon.

Finally, give me the question I will be hoping nobody asks. My guess is that it concerns the coherence of permitting aggregation inside one life while forbidding it across lives; if there is a worse one, tell me what it is and where it bites.

Please do not include a reading list or citations. Where a technical term of art matters to a reply, gloss it in a clause instead.
