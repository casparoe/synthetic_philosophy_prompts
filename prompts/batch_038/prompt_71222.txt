Two months ago I got made the person who owns our internal sanctions schedule — about 4,000 employees, a pile of policy violations everyones quietly tolerated for years, and now I have to write down what each one costs you. I keep bouncing between punishment theory and law-and-econ and they dont even agree on what the schedule is for, so before I read another chapter I want the spec.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of punishment has to deliver before I'd let it set actual announced sanctions. Which cases it has to get right (the attempt that fails, the offender who's already genuinely sorry, the one who pays up happily and keeps doing it, the violation almost nobody gets caught for). Which distinctions it can't collapse — justifying the practice versus justifying this particular infliction on this person, a penalty you're not supposed to treat as an available option versus a price you are, what this offender deserves versus what deters everyone else. And which empirical findings it can't be flatly inconsistent with, including the claim a colleague keeps making that publishing a fine for something people used to feel bad about can make them do it more, because it converts an obligation into a purchase. For each item, say why it's a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable versus which are just one camp's priors smuggled in as criteria.

Then the part I actually care about: which subset can't all be satisfied at once? My hunch is that announcing the schedule in advance, making each sanction track that individual's blameworthiness, and getting real general deterrence are in direct conflict — tell me if that's right or if I've picked the wrong three. And which single desideratum does the most work knocking out candidates. A paragraph each at the end on how retributivism, deterrence-based consequentialism, and some expressivist or communicative view do against the list is fine, but keep the weight on the list itself.
