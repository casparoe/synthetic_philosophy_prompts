For a jurisprudence seminar this term I am drafting a paper on the limits of codification, and I would like to pressure-test its central move before I commit another month to it. The thesis is that incompleteness is not a defect of badly drafted statutes but a structural feature of rule-following as such. Gödel established that any consistent, effectively axiomatized system capable of enough arithmetic contains a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, and that no such system can prove its own consistency. A fully codified legal order — call it the Ideal Code, a single frozen text with numbered sections and a fixed set of inference rules for deriving further norms from them — is exactly such a system, or so I want to argue. It follows, on my telling, that the Ideal Code must either leave some norms underivable from its own text or fail to certify its own validity, and hence that the ultimate rule identifying what counts as law cannot itself be one of the laws it identifies.

My reading group has raised two objections, and I believe I can answer both. The first is that real legal systems are not recursively axiomatized, because legislation and precedent accumulate without end. I am happy to grant this and retreat to the Ideal Code; indeed I take the retreat to strengthen my case, since a finite body of norms is much closer to the tidy systems the theorems concern than an open-ended corpus is, and finiteness should remove every worry about whether the axiom set is effectively given. The second objection is that law does not interpret arithmetic, so the theorems never get a grip. Here my reply is that arithmetization was only Gödel's implementation detail and that the diagonal lemma is what does the work: any system that can refer to its own sections can form a self-referential norm of the general shape "the norm bearing this number is not derivable from the Code," and self-reference is all the diagonalization I need. Numbering the sections is a coding of the Code by the Code, so the sentence comes for free. If that were right I would have expected the point to be too obvious to miss, and the fact that it does not feel that way to anyone I have described it to makes me suspect that something in it is wrong. I cannot locate what.

Two further things would help. First, I want to be clear that I am not running the old argument that Gödel's theorems show minds cannot be machines; nothing in my paper depends on that, and I would rather not inherit its problems. My claim is about codes and rules, not about consciousness. Second, I am unsure whether the sentence my diagonalization produces would actually be a substantive legal question — something like whether a given transaction is void, or whether a particular official acted within jurisdiction — or whether it would instead be a curiosity that matters only to a system already able to express its own derivability. My advisor's sole comment so far has been that a system's Gödel sentence is interesting chiefly to that system, which I do not find actionable. So: where exactly does the argument break, and is there any version of the thesis about codification that survives the break? If the Gödelian route is a dead end, I would like to understand what does the real explanatory work behind the intuition that a rule cannot fully determine its own application.
