Take the two Irish referendums on the Treaty of Lisbon. In June 2008 Irish voters rejected it by roughly 53 to 47; in October 2009 they approved it by roughly two to one, after the European Council issued a decision addressing Irish concerns on taxation, on security, defence and neutrality, and on the right to life, family and education, and agreed that every member state would retain one Commissioner. The treaty text itself was not amended — the concessions took the form of legally binding clarifications of a text that already stood. Ireland had already run this script once, with Nice.

I want the pair analyzed through three readings of what a referendum does, which I'll define so we're working from the same apparatus. On the *instrument* reading, a referendum measures a popular judgment that exists independently of the vote, so any given result is an estimate carrying error terms — information, salience, timing, framing; a second vote under better conditions is simply the better reading, and the first was noise. On the *act* reading, a referendum does not report a will, it constitutes one; the later vote is not more accurate, it is merely later, and supersedes. On the *authorization* reading, what matters is whether the electorate could knowingly hand over decisional authority on the question actually put — a test that can fail in both directions: voters who reject a text they admit not understanding have authorized nothing, but neither have voters who accept it on the strength of clarifications that restate what the text already said.

For each reading, say what it makes of the 2008 No and the 2009 Yes, and how it explains the fact that everyone — the No campaign included — treated the second vote as settling the matter. Then say where each reading breaks. Keep three features in view: post-poll surveys found the No reasons widely dispersed, the largest single category being unfamiliarity with the treaty rather than any substantive objection; the unanimity requirement means one electorate's ballot functions as a veto over twenty-six others; and by 2009 the implicit choice set had narrowed to Lisbon or something worse. Finish by saying which reading the episode actually favors, and whether it shows that a referendum of this kind can have a *result* at all in the sense each reading assumes.

Lead with your verdict in a single sentence, and keep the whole thing under 900 words.
