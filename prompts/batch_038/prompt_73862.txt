I have kept a third-of-an-acre kitchen garden on the edge of town for eleven years, first as something to do with my hands and now chiefly because my mother left it to me and I cannot bring myself to let it go. Our allotment society voted last month on whether to permit a contractor's seasonal spraying across the plots, and since I was the only member who spoke during the discussion, I have been asked to write the rationale for the notice board. That request has left me five weeks of cases to turn over, and I have arrived at the unwelcome conclusion that I do not understand my own position. I should say at the outset that I am not a philosopher: I know the words sentience and moral patient and roughly what they are meant to pick out, but I have no vocabulary for anything beyond that, so I will describe the cases plainly and leave the sorting to you.

Here is what I actually think, going through them one at a time. Treating a row of broad beans that has gone black with aphids using a horticultural soap: entirely unproblematic; I have done it every June for a decade and it does not register as a moral event at all. Paying for the termite treatment on the roof of the house we are buying: unproblematic, though I would pay three times as much for the bait-station system as for the liquid barrier even though the number of insects killed strikes me as comparable, and I do not think the barrier is wrong, only less good. Metaldehyde slug pellets around the lettuces: not acceptable, because of the dog next door that will eat a slug off the path and because of whatever eats the slugs after it; but hand-collecting the same slugs from the same beds after dark and drowning them in a bucket: acceptable, merely tedious; and surrendering the lettuces: permissible, though not something I would demand of my neighbours. Tipping out the water butt and the discarded tyres in April so that nothing breeds in them: not a moral question so far as I can feel. Treating the ornamental pond, which cannot be emptied, with a biological larvicide that I am assured is specific to mosquito larvae and leaves the newts and the damselfly nymphs alone, killing the very insects that would have come out of it: worse, distinctly worse, and I cannot say why, since it is narrower than the pellets, it prevents more bites, and those larvae were never going to be anything. A wasp trapped and thrashing between the panes of the double glazing in February: opening the window is what I do, but if I am already late for work, killing it with a folded newspaper so that it stops feels acceptable, while leaving it there for the day feels like the worst of the three. One honeybee working the plum blossom: I will not kill it, having been stung years ago; a whole branch of aphids: sprayed without a thought, though I am aware the arithmetic points the other way. My neighbour who keeps an electric racket by the back door and goes out in the evening looking for wasps: fewer insects die in his garden in a bad year than in mine, and yet I think he is the worse of the two of us, and I cannot tell whether I mean that morally or only aesthetically, which troubles me. Broad-spectrum treatment of an invasive leaf beetle through the county wood: not acceptable; the same beetle on my own beans: acceptable. And I buy my seed potatoes from the farm that lets a fifth of its crop go to pests rather than from the one that sprays as a matter of routine, without being able to say whether that is a principle or merely a taste for a more agreeable story about my food.

The nearest I have come to a rule of my own is this: it is all right to make a place impossible to live in, but not all right to kill the creatures already living in it; and, separately, it is all right to kill narrowly but not widely. Between them those cover most of the list, yet they are two different scales, they plainly do not reduce to one another, and neither says anything about the wasp behind the glass or the man with the racket. So: is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that genuinely generates these verdicts rather than restating them? I should like each clause stated precisely, with the case from my list that motivates it, so that I can see exactly which of my intuitions is doing the work at each step. Then tell me what it delivers on the thing I keep going back and forth on, which is the motion-activated floodlight I have just fitted to the side of the garage: it kills nothing directly, but the moths and midges come to it all summer and exhaust themselves against it, and I cannot decide whether that belongs with the water butt or with the slug pellets. Give me its verdicts on two or three cases I have not listed, preferably ones that would embarrass it. And be candid about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, since if the only way to save all of them is a rule bristling with half a dozen provisos, I would rather have a clean one that instructs me to give up the bee, or the pond, or my opinion of my neighbour. One last thing, and it is the one I most want answered. A friend who works in entomology insists that I am not tracking anything real at all: my verdicts, he says, line up almost exactly with how large the animal is, how directly it moves toward me, and whether I am made to watch it die, which is a description of my startle reflex rather than of the world. Is there anything to say to him besides hoping that he is wrong — and if the honest position is that a deflationary hypothesis of that kind fits my list at least as well as any moral principle does, I would much rather learn it from you now than from someone standing in front of the notice board.
