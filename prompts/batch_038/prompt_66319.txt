I run research ethics review at a mid-size health app company — about 250 people, mostly a symptom-tracking product plus a small arm that supports partner clinics running real interventional studies. I inherited the role a year ago; I studied philosophy but I have no legal or clinical training, and we push through somewhere between 25 and 40 protocols a month (surveys, paid usability sessions, passive sensor collection, a couple of actual trials). Right now the "is the consent okay?" part of the review is me reading a consent form and going with my gut, and I don't trust my gut, mostly because it gives different answers depending on how much I like the project team. So I want to replace it with a procedure. Our ops coordinator does the first pass and she has zero ethics background — 20 minutes max per protocol — then it comes to me, and only a subset goes to outside counsel, who bill by the hour, so the procedure has to be clear about what escalates and what I'm allowed to sign off myself.

What I need is a step-by-step decision procedure for "is the consent in this protocol valid," grounded in whatever the ethics of consent actually has to say about it rather than in what our current forms happen to assume. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, which steps are hard stops where the protocol goes back for redesign, which ones just get flagged, and which ones are honestly judgment calls that no checklist can settle — mark those explicitly, because the last thing I want is a form that lets me pretend a values question was a factual one. The thing I most need it to fix: our compliance people call every single thing they're uneasy about "coercive." A $400 stipend is coercive. A study where the person doing the recruiting is also the participant's own doctor is coercive. A consent screen nobody read is somehow also coercive. Those are not the same problem and the procedure should force us to say which failure mode we're actually in — too much disclosure, too little, not understood, understood but not free, or free but not competent to decide. Related, I was arguing with a friend who's an ICU nurse about whether paying people more makes consent worse or better and we got nowhere after an hour; whatever the procedure says about money needs to be able to survive that argument.

Then run it on three live ones. First: paid usability sessions, 90 minutes, $400, in a market where the people we recruit typically earn about $14 an hour, sourced from our own user base; last quarter two participants told the recruiter they "really needed this," and one asked if he could do a second session the same week. Second: a study of a tracking feature where we deliberately don't tell participants which of two hypotheses we're testing, because knowing would change their behaviour — we do tell them the topic, what's being recorded, the risks, that they can quit, and we debrief at the end. Third: passive collection under a single in-app consent screen, one tap, "Allow" on a 2,200-word scrollable policy, and the data feeds a feature we haven't built yet. (There's also a protcol with 16- and 17-year-olds where parents consent and the teen assents, and candidly some of the teens understand the study better than their parents do — say where that goes in the procedure, but keep the worked examples to the three above.) Two more things. I want at least one of the three verdicts to be one I'll be annoyed by — I'd much rather hear it from you now than from a regulator later, so don't soften it to be agreeable. And don't send me a bibliography or a reading list or case citations; if a distinction has a proper name, give me the name because our forms should use it consistently, but I'm not going to go read anything. Finish with where the procedure misfires: what kinds of protocol it will wave through that shouldn't be, and what it will kill that are actually fine.
