ok so I've been slowly working thru Hume's suppressed essay on suicide — the one he wouldn't allow to be printed in his lifetime and which only came out after his death, anonymously. I can follow most of it, but there's one stretch in the "duty to God" section that I keep reading without quite getting the mechanics of, and I can't tell where the load-bearing step is supposed to be. Here's the bit:

> The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time. All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty: They all proceed from those powers, with which he has endowed his creatures. A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation.

Questions, roughly in order of how much they're bugging me:

1. What is the actual inference? I can see two readings and I don't know which one Hume is committed to. Reading A, call it no-privileged-intervention: since God acts only through general laws, and the human faculties are as much his workmanship as gravitation, a human act is just one more lawful event, so redirecting a few ounces of blood is no more a usurpation of providence than redirecting a river. Reading B, licensed-agent: human acts really do stand apart from the mechanical order, but God endowed us with these powers and thereby delegated authority to use them, so it's authorized rather than impious. A seems to make the conclusion follow but at the cost of making the whole objection evaporate — if literally everything we do is providence, nothing can count as impiety, including murder. B keeps room for impiety but I can't find anything in these three sentences that establishes the delegation. Which one is doing the work, and does the passage support it?

2. The house example. Is "not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men" a claim about causal origin (both chains trace back to God) or about moral/religious salience (neither collapse is specially God's doing, so neither is a message from him)? For the suicide conclusion he seems to need the second, but the sentence looks like it only delivers the first. Or am I misreading the comparison entirely?

3. Here's where I think it gets interesting, and feel free to tell me I'm projecting. I spend a lot of time in the corners of the internet where people argue about asteroid deflection, solar geoengineering, radical life extension, eradication programs, etc., and the playing-God / don't-interfere-with-nature's-course objection shows up there constantly in secular drag. Hume's move looks like a general-purpose reply to all of it: there's no fact about which interventions count as violations of the natural order, because the natural order just is the totality of lawful processes, ours included. But does the argument cut both ways? If it's no impiety to divert the river, is it equally no impiety to decline to divert it — to not prop up the falling house, not deflect the rock, not take the treatment? He clearly wants permission to shorten a life; does anything in this passage give him grounds for a claim about omission as opposed to commission, or is the argument structurally silent on that? And if it's silent, does that mean it proves too much (a license for passivity about preventable deaths) or too little (it doesn't get him to the permissibility of suicide at all, since permissibility would have to come from somewhere else in the essay)?

4. Smaller thing. The paragraph just before this one says the material and animal powers "continually encroach upon each other," and that no discord or disorder results from the mixture. I've been reading a bunch of interventionist-causation stuff lately and it feels like Hume is pre-emptively denying that there's a privileged level of intervention — no level at which poking the system counts as breaking it. Is that a fair gloss or am I importing modern machinery into an 18th century theological arguement?

Please reason from the passage itself and from how the rest of the essay's argument goes, rather than pointing me at secondary literature or a reading list — I mostly just want to know whether I've got the structure of the inference right and which of my two readings survives contact with the text.
