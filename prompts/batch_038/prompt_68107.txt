I run a ~250-member mutual credit group — sort of a LETS/time-bank hybrid: members earn credits doing things for each other, spend them with other members, no cash conversion. Every couple weeks someone brings a proposal that sounds lovely and would quietly turn the thing into a money machine: arbitraging the credits, charging a fee to lend out a surplus you've accumulated, reselling credits to outsiders at a markup. Right now the committee votes on vibes, and I lose arguments I should win and win some I shouldn't.

Aristotle is the only old source I know that actually draws the line I need: household-management vs. wealth-getting as its own end, the thought that getting wealth has a natural limit becuase it's for the sake of something, money as a conventional stand-in for need rather than a thing that breeds, and proportionate reciprocity in exchange where builder and shoemaker get equalized by something that measures need. I want that turned into a screening procedure my committee can actually run in ~30 minutes on a written proposal, with no philosophy background required and no reference material in front of them.

So: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each — a real test somebody can fail, not "consider whether it serves the members"; where the procedure stops at a no; where it hands the decision up to the full membership because it's a values call and not a fact; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Please also flag where the analogy strains — our credits aren't coins, and the natural-limit argument is doing a lot of silent work when I apply it to a spreadsheet of balances.

Then run it on three proposals: (a) a member wants to buy credits from people running big surpluses for cash at a discount and spend them at face value; (b) a savings feature where you post your credit surplus and get a small fee from whoever takes it; (c) my own pet project — a fixed-supply founding-member instrument that pays early holders a premium out of future transaction levies. If (c) fails, say it fails plainly, I'd much rather know now than after I've socialized it.

No reading list, no citations, and please don't invent scholarship I can't check. But do tell me where your reasoning is weakest: which of those distinctions you're leaning on hardest and least safely, and what would change your mind about the whole exercise — like if the honest answer is that this is just a governance question and Aristotle adds no screening power that a plain conflict-of-interest check wouldn't.
