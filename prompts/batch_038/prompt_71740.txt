In three weeks I give a forty-minute paper, followed by thirty minutes of discussion, at a colloquium co-hosted by our philosophy-of-language group and the law school's private-law faculty. I need a question-period prep sheet: the objections this particular room will raise, in the order they will come, and what I say back. I am not asking for a defense of the paper, and I am not asking for the objections a general audience would give.

The thesis. Austin's tripartite scheme is almost always read as an anatomy: it tells you what kind of thing you are doing in speaking. I argue it should be read instead as an allocation of liability. Every speech act assigns to some party the cost of the act's failure; I call this the act's custody. Conventions, on this reading, are not recipes for producing acts but standing rules about who absorbs the loss when production goes wrong.

The argument I will sketch on the board runs in five moves.

1. Locutionary acts place custody with the speaker's competence. Illocutionary acts place it jointly, by convention. Perlocutionary effects place it nowhere in particular — which is why, on my view, they have no felicity conditions at all, and that absence is explanatory rather than an oversight in the taxonomy.
2. Infelicities are custody failures of two distinct kinds. What Austin calls a misfire is a case in which the act never entered anyone's custody, so no one can be held to it. What he calls an abuse is a case in which the act did enter custody and binds, though the custodian's own contribution was defective. This is meant to explain something the anatomy reading can only stipulate: why an insincere or unconscientious promise still binds. On my view it binds because the hearer's position has already changed, and that change is not a function of the speaker's inner state.
3. Uptake is therefore not a psychological state in the hearer — not a belief, and not the recognition of a reflexive intention — but an alteration in the hearer's liability position. To give uptake is to become someone who can be wronged by the act's failure. Uptake so construed is gradable, can be withheld, and can be withdrawn after the fact.
4. Four consequences I intend to press. (a) Infelicities the standard account treats as all-or-nothing come in degrees: the distance between a promise made in jest and a promise extracted under pressure is a difference in how much liability the hearer has actually taken on. (b) A corporate apology drafted by a communications firm and delivered by an officer can be wholly felicitous with nobody sincere, because custody sits with the corporate person. (c) A chatbot cannot promise — not because it lacks intentions, but because nothing can be held liable for it and nothing's liability position changes when it speaks; the failure is custodial, not mental. This is load-bearing for me, not a flourish, and I expect it to draw fire. (d) The objective theory of contract formation is a custody rule rather than an epistemic rule about what a reasonable person was entitled to believe. On this reading the Pepsi Points litigation over the fighter jet offered for collected loyalty points is not about the offeree's belief but about whether the advertiser ever took the jet into custody.
5. Finally, I claim the view relocates rather than resolves the standing dispute between the conventionalist accounts in Austin's and Searle's wake and the intention-based accounts in Grice's and Strawson's: intentions matter, but only as the mechanism by which a speaker takes on liability.

The room, as precisely as I can describe it. About twenty-five people, five of whom will do the talking. Two are committed intention-based theorists of illocutionary force who hold that conventions are at best a heuristic and that force is fixed by the speaker's reflexive intentions; one of them has written on insincerity and thinks my move 2 begs the question against him. One is a contracts scholar who works in formation doctrine and the objective theory and who is deeply allergic to any account that makes legal liability depend on psychological states — she will like the destination of move 4(d) and hate the route. One is a sociolinguist who does conversation analysis of naturally occurring interaction and considers philosophers' invented examples to be fantasy data. One graduate student works on slurs and on what happens when a hearer refuses uptake, and will push hard on whether withholding uptake can ever be legitimate. The rest are students and law faculty without a dog in the philosophy of language.

Give me the eight or so objections these five people will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do to the thesis rather than by how much fun they would be to answer. Each objection should be phrased the way that person would phrase it out loud, in first person, in their own idiom, reaching for the kind of example that person would reach for — the intention theorist will not object in a contracts scholar's vocabulary, and the conversation analyst will not object in either.

Deliver it in two parts.

Part one: eight numbered entries, each with exactly these labeled fields.
- Objector: which of the five, or which coalition of them.
- Said aloud: the objection in their own words, first person, one or two sentences.
- Targets: which of my five numbered moves it attacks.
- Damage: an integer from 1 to 5, plus one sentence saying what I lose if it lands unopposed.
- Reply: prose I could actually deliver in about ninety seconds, at most 130 words.
- Soft spot: where that reply is weakest, and whether I should concede it, hold it, or reframe it.

Part two: one summary table with the columns Rank | Objector | Objection in ten words or fewer | Move targeted | Damage | Hold, concede, or reframe.

After the table, answer three further questions in a short paragraph each. What single concession should I make in my opening five minutes to take the sharpest edge off the room? Which is the objection I am hoping nobody raises, and what is my fallback if it comes anyway? And which of my five moves is the one I should be prepared to abandon in real time without losing the paper?

Two constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations — I want objections and answers, not references to chase. If you reach for a named doctrine, case, or example, confine yourself to ones you are confident are real and correctly characterized; a described scenario is far more useful to me than a possibly invented citation.
