I spent eleven years as a clinical informaticist in a British hospital trust and have just begun a master's programme in health policy, where a seminar on performance measurement has left me wanting to pin down something I watched for years and never had a word for. It is not the familiar story about a measure going wrong. The version everyone knows — a hospital is judged on mortality, so it quietly stops admitting the sickest patients — is real, and I lived through a few rounds of it, but that is not what I am asking about. What I keep running into, and what I could never name for my colleagues, is the condition that persists *after* a broken measure has been formally retired: the distortion survives the measure it came from.

Three observations, which I suspect belong to one family. First: our emergency department was managed for years against a four-hour target. Leadership stopped using it in appraisal in 2019; it was on the record as retired; nobody's bonus referenced it. Six years later the triage algorithm still carried the branching logic that had been inserted to protect it, the staffing model still assumed a particular fraction of patients would be discharged inside the window, the induction deck for new charge nurses still taught the workflow built around it, and staff still visibly relaxed when a patient cleared four hours. Every one of those artifacts was constructed while the measure was live, none was removed when it died, and the behaviour continues with no incentive behind it. Second, and harder to dislodge: we could not simply substitute a better measure, because a decade of trend lines, capital cases, service-line decisions and published comparisons had all been expressed in its units. Adopting a cleaner definition would have torn a discontinuity through the middle of the institutional record, and the people who had to approve the change were the same people whose promotions rested on the numbers the old definition produced. The resistance did not feel like corruption, and I do not think it was; it felt closer to an unwillingness to render the past unintelligible. Third: newcomers absorb the old measure as simply how things are done here, because it now lives in artifacts rather than in anyone's stated aims. Ask a veteran why the workflow runs that way and they cannot tell you. The reason left the building years ago and the arrangement stayed.

So: does this configuration have an established name, in the performance-measurement literature or anywhere else? I have worked through the obvious neighbours and none of them reaches it. Goodhart's law and Campbell's law describe a measure losing its correspondence with the thing it was meant to track — but that is a live relationship between a measure and a target, whereas here the measure is no longer being used as a target at all, and the distortion has migrated into infrastructure, staffing models, training materials and the historical record. Surrogation, as I understand it, is a cognitive substitution: people lose sight of the construct and treat the measure as the construct. That happens inside someone who is currently looking at the measure; what I am describing persists in people who have never seen it reported, and would persist if everyone in the organisation forgot it existed. Metric fixation is an attitude toward quantification generally. Institutional hysteresis and path dependence cover the general fact that arrangements outlive their reasons, but they are broad enough that they do not separate my case from, say, a tram network still running a gauge nobody deliberately chose. And it is neither the ratchet effect, nor capture, nor sunk-cost attachment to systems we built.

What I want is the carving, not a label. Please tell me what the nearest established concepts actually are and precisely where each stops short of this case; whether my three instances are one phenomenon or two or three that I have mistakenly fused — in particular, whether the persistence-in-artifacts strand and the lock-in-of-the-historical-record strand need separate treatment, since the second seems to concern the measure's own accumulated data rather than the behaviour it induced; and if there is genuinely no name, coin one, with a definition precise enough that I could tell a colleague what is and is not an instance. That means boundary conditions: does it require formal retirement of the original measure, or does it also cover a measure still in use whose distortions have already been absorbed into the infrastructure? Must anyone still remember the measure for it to count? How long must the arrangement persist, and does the phenomenon require the original measure to have been *flawed*, or can a perfectly good measure leave the same residue? Please also identify at least one adjacent-looking case that ought to be excluded. Finally, tell me whether the machine-learning literature contains anything structurally analogous — I have a hunch about agents trained under one specification continuing to behave as though it were still in force after it has been replaced — but I would rather be told the analogy fails than have it handed to me unexamined.
