I'm partway through a trade-press book on end-of-life law — the kind written by a journalist who has clearly done the reading but wants to keep moving — and one chapter sets up the whole debate by summarizing the classic philosophical attack on the acts-and-omissions distinction. Here is the passage in full:

> Most of the confusion about euthanasia comes from a single distinction: the one between killing a patient and letting a patient die. The medical profession drew it officially in the early 1970s, and the law of most countries still embodies it — withdrawing treatment is permitted, injecting is murder. James Rachels attacked the distinction in a three-page article in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1975, and it has never really recovered. He asked readers to consider two men, Smith and Jones, each of whom stands to inherit a large sum if his six-year-old cousin dies. Smith creeps into the bathroom and holds the child under the water until he drowns. Jones intends to do exactly the same, but as he comes in the child slips, strikes his head, and slides under, and Jones stands by and watches him drown without lifting a hand. Nobody thinks Jones less guilty than Smith, yet the only difference between them is that one killed and the other let die — so that difference cannot be the thing that matters. Rachels added a second argument: because letting die is usually slower, the passive route often leaves patients in agony for days, which means the distinction does not merely fail to protect life, it sometimes destroys it more painfully. The doctrine of double effect is the same error in scholastic costume. It permits a doctor to give morphine to a dying patient even though the doctor foresees that the dose will hasten death, on the ground that he intends only the relief of pain; but intention is simply what you want, and every such doctor wants the patient to die, so double effect draws no line that active euthanasia does not cross just as freely. Once that is clear, the only remaining question is consent. If bringing about a death cannot be worse than allowing one, there is no principled basis for treating a patient who asks differently from a patient who can no longer ask: in both, the doctor causes a death that serves the patient's interest. The legal line is therefore arbitrary, and the honest reform is to delete it.
