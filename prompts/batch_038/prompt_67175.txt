Kind of a weird ask but I need help writing something real and I need it by Sunday.

I volunteer on the board of a small Holocaust education center in a mid-size city — we run school programs, we hold about 200 recorded survivor testimonies from this region, and next year we're redoing our one permanent exhibition, which is currently two rooms and hasn't been touched since 1998.

Here's the situation. A donor has offered us $1.8 million to build out a third room as a comparative gallery, tentatively titled "Never Again? Genocide Since 1945." The conditions are that the Holocaust be presented as one case among several (Cambodia, Rwanda, Bosnia and Darfur are the ones named in the letter), that the gallery be co-designed with two local diaspora organizations, and that it open within 30 months. Half the board loves it — the money transforms us, and three of the five districts we serve have asked for comparative genocide curriculum we dont currently offer. The other half thinks accepting means surrendering the claim that the Holocaust is in some sense without precedent, and that once it sits in a row of cases it just becomes an entry in a taxonomy.

I'm in the middle and it got personal in a way I didn't expect. My grandmother's family was from a small town in Poland and her recorded testimony is in our own archive; I listened to it again last month and she says something to the effect that what was done to them can't be put next to anything. I've repeated that line my whole life as a kind of inheritance. Now I may be voting on whether our institution affirms it. Also my kid asked me at dinner whether what's on the news is "the same thing" and I fumbled the answer badly, which is a big part of why I took this drafting task on.

I've been asked to write the memo that goes out with the board packet. What I want is the actual document, roughly 1000-1200 words, recommending a decision — accept, accept with conditions, or decline — with reasons a board member who disagrees can argue back against instead of just being told off. Bottom line up top, concrete timeline, and a named person or office responsible for each step.

But beyond the money and the politics I want the memo to actually grapple with the intellectual question, because that's where I'm stuck and where I think our board is faking it. Specifically:

1. What does "uniqueness" even claim here? I want the memo to seperate the candidate versions — uniqueness of scale, uniqueness of the industrial-bureaucratic method, uniqueness of intent (killing for its own sake rather than for a military or economic end), and uniqueness as a claim about the event's place in memory and identity rather than about its properties — and say which of these an exhibition can actually support, which are not historical claims at all, and whether the family of claims is falsifiable in principle or whether "unique" is functioning more like a proper name that can't be argued with.

2. The modernity angle. One of our historians leans hard on Bauman's line of argument — that the Holocaust was made possible by ordinary modern rationality, bureaucratic procedure, the division of labor into ever smaller tasks, and the social production of moral indifference across distance, so it's a warning about modernity generally rather than an aberration of it. I find that both compelling and suspicious. If it's a story about modernity, comparison to any modern state gets easy, and antisemitism seems to drop out as doing no explanatory work. Take that seriously: is the modernity framing the right spine for a comparative gallery, or does it flatten exactly the thing the uniqueness people are protecting?

3. There's a counter our historian doesn't like, and I want it handled rather than dodged: that the killing was in fact largely low-tech, local and face-to-face — shootings into pits, improvised logistics, neighbors and local auxiliaries doing much of the work — and that the picture of a uniquely industrial, assembly-line modernity was built out of Auschwitz partly because Auschwitz is what was left standing and photographed. If that's broadly right, what does it do to the modernity framing, and does it strengthen or weaken the case for comparison?

4. The West German historians' dispute of the 1980s over whether comparing the Holocaust to other mass crimes was a way of normalizing the Nazi past: I want the memo to extract whatever lesson from that episode actually transfers to a small education center in this country, not just name-check it. Does the worry that comparison relativizes have real force, or is the live worry about who is doing the comparing, from what position, and toward what end?

5. Somebody gave me a distinction I keep turning over and I want it pressure-tested before I put it in front of the board: split "uniqueness of kind" (a claim about the event's properties) from "uniqueness of standing" (a claim about who has the moral and epistemic authority to invoke it, and who gets to say what counts as a legitimate comparison). My hunch is that most of our fight is actually about standing and we're arguing about kind because that's the vocabulary we have. Is the hunch right? Is the distinction coherent, or does it collapse? And if it holds, does it change what the memo should recommend?

I don't want mush. I'd rather have a firm recommendation with a real argument than a both-sides shrug. But I do want the strongest case for the losing side stated by someone who actually believes it — including the version where the size of the gift is precisely the reason to say no — and not a strawman. And please tell me if you think there's a condition on the gift that resolves this better than a straight accept or decline, because I suspect that's the option the board hasn't thought of.
