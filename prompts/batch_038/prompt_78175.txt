ok so this is a weird ask but I need help writing a speech and I've been staring at a blank doc for three days.

My physics teacher Ms. Halloran is retiring at the end of the month and there's a dinner for her in the gym. I'm one of two students asked to speak and I've got maybe four or five minutes. Room will be around 120 people: her husband and her grown kids, the superintendent, a bunch of teachers and parents who've barely met her, and then like 40 of us who actually had her, some driving back from college for the night.

Here's what makes it hard. Every October instead of doing the curriculum she spent two weeks on George Berkeley, the Irish bishop who thinks things only exist insofar as they're being perceived. The department let her get away with it because her students kept winning the state physics competition. So half the room will know exactly what I'm referencing and half will be completely lost, and she would hate a speech that dumbed it down, but she'd also hate one that left her husband stranded at his own wife's party.

Specific true things I want it built around:

- She keeps a chunk of granite on the classroom windowsill that she just calls the rock. First day of every year she makes the whole class shut their eyes and try to picture the rock still sitting there while nobody is thinking about it. Somebody always claims they did it. She always says the same thing back, which is basically that no you didn't, you were thinking about it while you were thinking about it. We're giving her the rock as a gift at the end of the night and I want my speech to hand off to that moment.
- I do not believe that argument. I think the rock is obviously still there with it's eyes closed, I mean with ours. But she taught us the objection herself, she wasn't into winning cheap. Her version was that the argument only looks unanswerable because of a trick in how the sentence is assembled: the fact that you can't picture an unperceived rock tells you something about what picturing is, not anything about rocks. She said that's the actual question and she'd rather you got it right than agreed with her. So the speech can't be me pretending I got converted, but it also can't be me standing up there debunking a woman on the night of her own party. Figure out what the honest third thing is.
- The other one she drilled into us is that you never perceive a perceiver. Minds aren't the kind of thing you can have an image of, you can only have what she called a notion of one. Which means — and she pointed this out about herself, laughing — that any retirement speech made of little pictures and funny moments is structurally guaranteed to miss the person it's about, because the person isn't in the pictures. She asked me, half joking, to fail honestly rather than pretend the anecdotes add up to her. I want the speech to actually do that: use the stories, then admit what the stories can't do. Without turning into a philosophy paper about why its own raw material is inadequate. That balance is basically the whole job and I don't know how to get it.
- Her system needs God to keep the rock on the sill when the room empties out, the permanent perceiver. It's a public school, the superintendent is in the front row, and this cannot become a sermon or a declaration of anyone's faith. But it also can't just skip the load-bearing part of the thing she loved, because then it isn't her system, it's a greeting card. Thread that needle.
- She banned quoting philosophers at anything with her name attached. Her rule was that if you're going to say something a dead man said, you say it in your own mouth or you don't say it. So no quoted lines from Berkeley anywhere in this, not even the famous four-word one, and no titles, section numbers, or dates.

Tone: warm, a bit funny, not sentimental. I want one moment where the 40 of us laugh because we know precisely what's being pointed at, and the parents laugh too because the human part lands on its own — not a joke that needs a footnote. It has to sound like a seventeen year old reading aloud in a gym with bad acoustics while nervous, so mostly short sentences, nothing that needs a semicolon to survive being spoken. And the ending needs to work two ways at once: for the people in that room who actually accept what she believed, it should land as something true and a little overwhelming, and for me and everyone who doesn't, it should still be the right thing to say to her. If that's genuinely impossible, say why instead of faking it.
