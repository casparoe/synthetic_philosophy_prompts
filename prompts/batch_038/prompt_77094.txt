I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on individuation whose organizing question is whether the standard resistance to worlds of exactly similar objects is semantic (we cannot describe such a world without demonstratives) or metaphysical (no such world is possible at all). Max Black's 1952 article in *Mind* is the canonical stimulus — a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres — but I want my students working on a case that has been deliberately engineered against the two usual repairs, so please do not simply restate or lightly re-dress Black's world.

First, the vocabulary I want us to share, so the analysis is not talking past itself. Two objects are *absolutely* discernible if they differ in some monadic property, *relatively* discernible if they stand differently to some third thing, and *weakly* discernible if some irreflexive relation holds between them (Quine's grades). The Identity of Indiscernibles, as I want it defined here, is the claim that no two objects can fail to be at least weakly discernible. A bundle theorist — one who holds that an object is nothing over and above its bundle of properties — is committed to that principle, and must therefore say that a world correctly described as containing two exactly similar spheres contains only one sphere. A haecceitist, who allows primitive thisness, says two objects can differ *solo numero*. Note that the repair usually offered on Black's behalf is weak discernibility: the spheres stand some distance apart, and the relation of being exactly that distance from, for a distance greater than zero, is irreflexive and holds between them. Your construction must close that route.

**Specifications for the world W.**

1. W contains exactly two concrete objects that are exactly similar in every monadic qualitative respect, and they are not weakly discernible: no irreflexive relation holds between them other than one already defined in terms of their non-identity. Tell me whether you take that exclusion to be legitimate, since *is distinct from* is trivially irreflexive.
2. W is a single world with law-governed structure rich enough that its inhabitants could do physics in it. The two objects must be related to each other and to the rest of W. You may not buy indiscernibility by deleting structure: no relation-free world, no two merely conjoined universes, no arena in which nothing has a location.
3. Your description of W must be purely qualitative — no proper names, no demonstratives, no indexicals, no term whose reference is fixed by acquaintance or by a thisness. Numerical quantification (how many things of such-and-such a kind there are) is permitted, and is in fact where the crux lives.
4. The duality must be *manifestable*. Give an observation, measurement, inference, or law-governed counterfactual available to an inhabitant of W that could obtain if W contains two such objects and could not obtain if it contains one, and that does not break the symmetry between them. This is the hardest constraint; if you conclude that it cannot be met jointly with 1–3, say so explicitly, identify the minimal concession that would meet it, and explain what the failure shows.
5. The world description itself should run to no more than about 200 words.

Since the case is for students to evaluate, a description that asserts there are exactly two and moves on counts as a failure: the count of two has to be generated by the qualitative description together with W's laws.

**Then the checks, in this order.**

(a) Clause-by-clause verification against specifications 1–4.
(b) A list of every relation W actually supplies between the two objects, with one line per relation showing it is reflexive or presupposes non-identity.
(c) The bundle theorist's verdict and the haecceitist's verdict on how many objects W contains, and whether the purely qualitative description genuinely underdetermines that count. If you think it does not, say so and say which side wins.
(d) The single feature of your construction doing the work — then remove it and report what collapses.
(e) The strongest attack available to a defender of the Identity of Indiscernibles: for instance, that your world trades on a stipulation (a suspended impenetrability law, a doubled quantity of matter, an exotic mereology) no less question-begging than the thisnesses it is meant to replace, or that W is not a genuine possibility but a one-object world written in a misleading grammar. Answer it or concede it.
(f) Finally: does your case show that the principle is false, or only that it is not necessarily true — and does it settle anything about whether the anti-haecceitist's complaint is semantic or metaphysical?
