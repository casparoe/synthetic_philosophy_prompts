I am a second-year doctoral student in philosophy, and on Thursday our reading group on probability meets for the last time this term; I am presenting a case of my own, and I know precisely where it will be attacked. A hillwalker has gone missing on a moor. The search area is divided into a northern half and a southern half, and the northern half into three sectors, and the incident officer's prior assigns 0.6 to her having gone north. At ten at night a farmer who has been out with his dog — regarded by everyone as honest and reliable, but not as particularly observant — radios in and says: 'If she went north, I'd put it at three to one she's by the old quarry.' I want to argue that the correct response is to leave the 0.6 exactly where it is and to redistribute only within the northern half, so that the conditional credence in the quarry given north becomes 3/4: the move van Fraassen recommends in his Judy Benjamin case. What I additionally want to keep open is the thought that the farmer's having chosen to speak about the north at all may itself bear on whether she went north — perhaps his dog behaved oddly by the northern gate, perhaps he would not have volunteered a remark about the north unless something had already put the north in his mind. My worry concerns the selection of the utterance rather than its content.

Our convenor, a hard-nosed Bayesian who enjoys these moments, cut me off with a sentence he has now deployed three times this term: 'An "if" sentence is just a conditional probability in disguise — Adams proved that decades ago — so a conditional tells you nothing whatever about its antecedent, and anyone who thinks otherwise is doing armchair psychology instead of semantics.' On that basis he declared my worry not a live option but a category mistake. I am unconvinced, yet I could not say why at the time, and I suspect the sentence he reached for is being made to do several different jobs at once, some of which are platitudes and at least one of which is simply false. I have already noticed that it can be heard as a claim about what such a sentence means, as a claim about when a speaker is entitled to assert it, as a claim about what a hearer may infer from a sincere assertion of it, or as a claim about how a rational hearer's credences ought to move — and that these come apart in exactly the cases that matter. I also have a vague recollection of a triviality result suggesting that no proposition can play the semantic role he needs, which makes me doubt that anything was ever proved here; please tell me if I have that wrong, and if the name I have attached to the slogan is the wrong name.

Act as a philosopher of probability who works on conditionals and on Bayesian updating, and think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming as many candidate readings of that sentence as you can manage, including readings that are mutually inconsistent, readings that are so weak as to be compatible with everything, and pseudo-readings that turn out not to state a claim at all; only then prune them and put them in order. For each survivor, work out whether the conclusion my convenor needed actually follows from it, and whether it survives contact with the farmer's radio call. Structure your reply in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **The job the slogan had to do** (about 150 words): in your own words, the precise conclusion he was after and the premise the slogan was supplying.
2. **A menu of readings** (the bulk of the answer, roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words): numbered readings, each giving a precise formulation of the claim expressed, a label — platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, false, or not a claim at all — and two or three sentences of reasoning behind the label. Wherever a reading holds only for indicative rather than counterfactual conditionals, or only under assumptions about the speaker's own evidence and reliability, say so explicitly rather than letting it pass.
3. **Which readings would licence the dismissal** (about 300 words): whether any of them gets him from the meaning of 'if' to the fixity of my 0.6, and what extra premise he would need if not.
4. **Where my worry survives** (about 250 words): the readings on which reasoning about why he spoke, rather than about what he said, remains legitimate, and the strongest objection to that line of thought.
5. **Two minutes on Thursday** (about 150 words): what I can actually say aloud in the seminar, conceding what deserves to be conceded and holding what I can hold.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind: I have no library access and could only take your word for them. If you are unsure whether some result is genuinely a theorem, or genuinely due to the author I have named, flag the uncertainty plainly instead of dressing it up.
