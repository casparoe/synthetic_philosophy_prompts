I'm putting together a rubric for an interdisciplinary workshop on the epistemic status of long-range predictions — demographic transition, resource scarcity, climate damages, AI timelines — and the first session keeps collapsing into whether Malthus was refuted or merely premature. Before anyone is allowed to defend a prediction, I want the specification: the desiderata any account of being right-but-early must meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as non-negotiable or as the local preference of a camp (falsificationism, the research-programme tradition, and Bayesian calibration each smuggle in a different one).

The list must at minimum handle: a theory that misses its date but gets the mechanism, including Malthus's own later appeal to moral restraint, which reads either as an ad hoc rescue or as the identification of the missing variable; the converse, where the mechanism was wrong for decades and the trajectory right, as with continental drift; the Simon–Ehrlich wager, which was settled on a proxy measure that arguably never tested what either party claimed; predictions that are reflexive, so that their publication changes whether they come true and a failure to materialize is not evidence against the mechanism; near-certain claims stretched over long windows, which cannot be true-by-luck and still informative; and catastrophes averted precisely because they were announced.

Then say which single requirement excludes the most candidate accounts, and — what I most want — whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My hunch is that requiring symmetric risk, so that a missed deadline genuinely counts against a theory, cannot be combined with crediting undated structural tendencies, and that reflexivity defeats the symmetry even where a date is supplied. Argue that rather than assume it. Close with a brief paragraph on how the three camps fare against your list. No citations; I'll supply the literature.
