A cross-party working group on constitutional reform has asked each of its members to file a one-page statement of first principles before we begin arguing about institutional design. Mine concerns the replacement of one legislative chamber with a body chosen by lot, and it runs as follows. I came to this position slowly, largely through jury service, and two colleagues have already told me the package is unstable; neither would say where, and I would like to know before the spring session rather than after it.

1. Elections are not a democratic device but an aristocratic one. They filter for the ambitious, the credentialed, and the well-financed, and they bind an officeholder's attention to the next campaign rather than to the merits of the decision in front of them. I think the older view — Montesquieu's, and behind him the Athenians, who allotted nearly every magistracy but elected their generals — got this right, and that most talk about electoral legitimacy is a way of not noticing it.
2. A chamber of ordinary citizens drawn by lot from the electoral register would beat any elected chamber on the dimension I care most about: it would actually look like the country in class, education, occupation, age, and region, and its members would arrive with no career to protect and no constituency to please.
3. What makes law legitimate is not that it produces good outcomes but that those subject to it can regard themselves as its authors. If a panel of the wisest would govern better than a lottery of the ordinary, I would still object to it. I am neither a technocrat nor an epistocrat.
4. Service must remain voluntary. Anyone summoned may decline without penalty and without giving a reason, and the seat passes to the next name drawn. Compelling citizens to serve would be the kind of claim on a person's life plans that I reject in conscription and compulsory jury service alike.
5. The allotted chamber should hold the power of the purse: money bills — the annual budget, taxation, and borrowing — originate there, and the elected lower house may only delay them.
6. I stop short of randomizing single offices where technical competence is decisive. No allotted judges, prosecutors, central bankers, or military commanders. Ends and priorities can be settled by any fair sample of citizens; means require trained judgment, and it would be reckless to pretend otherwise.
7. Members serve one non-renewable term of three years, are paid a full professional salary, and are supported by a permanent research service that answers to the chamber rather than to any department of the executive.
8. Recognized indigenous nations and two historically persecuted religious communities should not be left to the luck of the draw; they should seat delegates chosen by their own procedures, so that their presence is guaranteed rather than merely probable.

Tell me whether this set hangs together. I want an audit, not an essay: open with a bottom line of two or three sentences on whether the package is jointly tenable, then go through the problems you find in descending order of severity. For each one, name the two commitments that pull against each other, explain the mechanism by which they conflict rather than just asserting that they do, and say what would have to be revised and what that revision would cost me elsewhere. Where two commitments only look inconsistent, say so explicitly and explain why — I would rather be told that an item is fine than have it padded onto the list to make the audit look thorough. Please do not assume I would sacrifice any particular item to save the rest; I genuinely do not yet know which of these I hold most loosely, and I would prefer the answer not be prejudged. Keep the whole response under 650 words, and leave out any pointers to further reading — I want your own reasoning, not a bibliography.
