I cover statistics and research methodology for a general-interest science desk, and after several years of reporting on the replication controversies I keep running into a pattern I have no name for. I would like to know whether the methodological literature has one, and if it does not, what the pattern ought to be called.

Stated plainly: a study establishes something rigorously, and the rigor is then spent in support of a claim the study never established — not because anyone misreports the numbers, but because the quality of the measurement is treated as though it transferred to the reach of the interpretation.

Two instances, both simplified from reporting I have done. First: a large, preregistered, multi-site study finds that scores on a questionnaire administered in a laboratory predict small variations in behavior on a second laboratory task. The finding survives every robustness check anyone has thrown at it. In the argument that follows — about hiring, about policing, about classroom policy — the study is cited with its sample size and its registration front and center, as evidence for how a trait operates outside the laboratory. The trait was never measured outside the laboratory. When I raise that, the reply is a restatement of the design's quality: the sample was enormous, the analysis plan was posted before the data were seen, everything is public.

Second: an economics team runs an unusually well-controlled trial of a training program and produces a very tight estimate of its effect on a standardized exam. The tightness is real and earned. Within a year that estimate is being quoted — by partisans on every side, and by the team's own press office — as the effect of training on earnings, and the question of whether an exam score is the right quantity to be estimating gets answered by re-describing how carefully the trial was run.

In both cases the researchers were, to my knowledge, scrupulous: the limits were flagged in the discussion section, and in interviews some of them objected to the uses their work was being put to. The pattern did not require their cooperation. The credential travelled; the caveat did not.

There is a second feature I want kept in view. The response to an objection about scope is invariably a rejoinder about quality. Nobody disputes the objection's premise. The subject simply never changes to the scope question, and because everything said about quality is true, the exchange leaves the impression that the criticism has been met.

I know the obvious neighbors, and I do not think any of them is it. Construct validity concerns the gap between an operationalization and the construct theorized; external validity concerns the gap between the study setting and the world; statistical versus practical significance concerns the mistake of treating a precisely estimated small effect as an important one. Each of those names a standing property of a study. What I keep meeting is transactional: rigor demonstrated about one quantity being converted, in public, into warrant for a different one, with the conversion never stated and the objection to it answered by pointing back at the rigor.

So, four questions. (a) What are the nearest established concepts, and for each, precisely how does the pattern I have described depart from it? (b) Which of them, if any, is its proper home? (c) Is the second feature — quality rejoinders to scope objections — a distinct phenomenon with a label of its own, a case of some old fallacy, or just the surface of the first? (d) Where are the edges? I want at least three cases you would exclude, and I want to know whether the pattern can occur without the defensive move, or whether the defensive move is what makes it what it is. If no established term fits, coin one, define it so that it cannot collapse back into the neighbors above, and say what you would count as a borderline instance.

Please do not guess at sources, references, or authors' names; I am not asking for a bibliography and I will chase the literature myself once I know what to look for. What I want is the carving rather than the label — a precise account of what this thing is and is not, of which I would rather have too much than a confident name.

Finish with the label on its own line, in the form \answer{...}, containing either the established term you judge the best fit or the term you propose.
