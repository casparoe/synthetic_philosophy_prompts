**Question 3 (25 points).** State Kim's exclusion argument against nonreductive physicalism as precisely as you can. Identify each premise separately and explain what work it does in the argument — in particular, say which premise is doing the excluding. Then set out what you take to be the two most promising responses available to the nonreductive physicalist and say whether either of them succeeds.

**Student answer:**

Kim's target is a position he calls nonreductive physicalism: mental properties are distinct from physical properties but supervene on them, so that there can be no mental difference without a physical difference. The argument has four premises and a conclusion.

(1) *Causal closure*: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. Kim presents this less as an empirical discovery than as a regulative commitment of physical science, though he also thinks the neurophysiological evidence supports it.

(2) *Exclusion*: if some property is sufficient for an effect, no other property distinct from it can also be a cause of that effect, unless we are prepared to call the case genuine overdetermination. Closure and exclusion are really one idea stated two ways: closure says the physical base does all the causal work, and exclusion simply spells out that there is nothing left over for anything else to do.

(3) *Non-identity*: mental properties are not identical to physical properties. This follows from multiple realizability, since one and the same mental property can be realized by different physical properties in different creatures.

(4) *No systematic overdetermination*: it would be an extravagant coincidence if every mental cause were matched by an independent sufficient physical cause, so the overdetermination option is not really available.

The argument then runs: suppose a mental property M causes a physical effect E. By closure there is a sufficient physical cause P of E. Since M is distinct from P, exclusion rules M out unless we swallow (4). So M is not a cause of E. The decisive step, I think, is the move from supervenience: because M is nothing over and above P, M simply has no causal powers of its own to contribute, and that is why Kim's argument is often called a supervenience argument.

Kim's causal inheritance principle — the claim that a mental property inherits the causal powers of its physical realizer — is best read as a concession to his opponents, and it shows that Kim does not really think the mental is causally empty; a nonreductive physicalist can lean on it.

For the responses, I take the two most promising to be the identity response and the proportionality response. On identity, if M just is P then there is no competition, since nothing can exclude itself; Kim is friendly to this but insists it is available only for local, species-by-species identities, which costs the nonreductive physicalist exactly the multiple realizability that motivated the position in the first place. On proportionality, due to Yablo, exclusion is simply the wrong principle when one candidate cause is better proportioned to the effect than another: a pigeon trained to peck at red things will peck at a scarlet one, but what does the explaining is the surface's being red rather than its being that particular shade of scarlet. This is the more interesting reply, because it keeps closure and instead denies that sufficiency excludes.

Kim's conclusion, as I read it, is that we should accept epiphenomenalism about mental properties: the price of being a physicalist is that the mental does no causal work, and Kim thinks a physicalist ought to pay that price rather than give up closure. He also notes that the argument is not really about the mental as such, since any higher-level property that supervenes without being identical to its base will face it.
