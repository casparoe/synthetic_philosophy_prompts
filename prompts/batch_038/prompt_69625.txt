I've been an RN on a step-down unit for like 14 years and I get pulled into ethics consults when they want somebody who actually knows the family, and lately I keep getting stuck on something I can't put into proper words. Our whole decision-making setup goes like this: if the patient can talk, only the patient decides and the family is there for moral support. If they can't talk, we look first for a signed directive naming ONE agent, and what we're taught is that the agent's job is to report what the patient would have wanted — not what the agent wants, not what's easiest on the family. So when a wife stands at the foot of the bed saying he'd want us to keep going, I'm not ready, the consult basically treats her as an obstacle, a bias that has to be filtered out so we can hear the patient through the noise. And if there's no paper, the state hands us a ranked list — spouse, adult children, parents, siblings — and whoever is highest on it and reachable decides, alone, even with four other adult kids in the hallway who think that's insane. Here's the part that bugs me: the same posters in the same hallway say patient- and family-centered care, and everyone on the ward knows which daughter does the actual caring, drives forty minutes, knows the med list, has slept in that chair — and she's frequently nowhere on the list, because she isn't the spouse and she's third among the kids, while the estranged son who hasn't called since 2009 is next in line after the spouse and gets the whole thing. Then flip it: when a family demands we keep treating and the doctors say it won't help, the committee overrides them completely, their wishes count for nothing. So the same group of people has zero standing when they're grieving, total authority when somebody hands them a form, gets overruled when they want more treatment, and decides everything when they're the agent saying he'd want less. Is that actually inconsistant, or is there one idea underneath that makes both halves make sense?

My hunch, and I don't know if it's a real position or just me being tired at 3am: on the unit we behave like the patient is a knot that other people's lives are tied through, so that when you cut the knot the ropes don't just lie there unchanged — but the paperwork behaves like the person is a sealed container with a preference inside it and the family's only legitimate job is to read the label. A nurse I worked with years ago, from Durban, once tried to explain ubuntu to me and I'd be butchering it if I repeated it, but the part that stuck was that you become a person through other people, and personhood is more like something that grows and can wither than a switch that flips at birth. If that's even roughly right, our directive form is close to the exact opposite: it treats a person as finished at the moment of signing and hands that finished version permanent authority over the unfinished one still breathing in the bed. I watch that with the dementia patients, where the woman who signed in 2011 and the woman sitting there now don't look like the same set of preferences to me, and everybody acts like the paper settles it.

So here's what I'm asking, and please don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list — I have a hospital librarian I can badger and I'm not asking you to name-drop. Lay out what this practice assumes about what a person is, and be careful to separate what the forms strictly need from what just happens to fit comfortably with them, because maybe a relational view of the person would generate the identical paperwork for boring reasons: somebody has to sign, decisions have to happen at 2am, a hospital needs one answer it can defend in court. Tell me which parts of the setup require the sealed-container picture and which parts are administrative convenience wearing a philosophy costume. Then say which rival views of a person the practice quietly rules out and whether it rules them out on purpose or by accident of how the form is designed; whether the two-hats thing above is a real contradiction and which half is more defensible; and where the practice doesn't actually settle anything either way, so that a bunch of different theories would produce the same form and fixing the paperwork wouldn't move the question. Last, if a hospital genuinely ran on the relational view, what would change concretely — who's in the room, what gets written down, who signs, what happens when the web disagrees with itself — and what's the honest cost, because I've also been the nurse who called the consult because the family was the problem and I don't want a rosy answer that forgets the patient whose relatives are the reason they can't go home. And please be straight with me about what's settled here and what isn't. If the people who work on this for a living are genuinely split — on whether relational accounts can handle abuse cases, on whether a directive really is a different self exercising authority over this one, on whether the family is even one thing or just a word we use when we don't want to say which people we mean — say so and tell me who leans which way in broad strokes. Don't smooth a contested view into a consensus because I'm not an academic and it'd be easier for me to swallow, and if I've described the practice wrong for hospitals that aren't mine, correct me instead of going along with it.
