I am putting together the final sessions of a graduate seminar on personal identity, and I have run into a difficulty I would like help thinking through before I answer it in class. We have spent most of the term on the reductionist program — psychological continuity, Relation R, the claim that identity is not what matters in survival — and in last week's meeting a student of mine who comes to philosophy from cognitive science objected that this program has already been outflanked by a view she takes to be far more radical: Douglas Hofstadter's proposal in *I Am a Strange Loop* that an "I" is a self-referential pattern which can be instantiated, in degrees, in brains other than the one that originally hosted it. She thinks the discussion of his wife's death in that book is not sentimental garnish on a Parfitian position but the statement of a genuinely different metaphysics, one on which a person literally persists, partially and by degrees, in those who have taken her pattern up. I want to be able to say precisely what turns on that difference, and I am not satisfied with my own current formulation.

Please explain the conceptual issue here: what exactly is the distributed-self claim committed to, and at what point does it cease to be a restatement of Parfit. I would like the explanation to work through several pressure points rather than summarize either author. First, what relation is doing the explanatory work — a causal and psychological continuity of roughly the sort Relation R demands, or something closer to pattern isomorphism that could obtain with no causal connection at all? Second, if it is the latter, how does the claim avoid collapsing into the unremarkable thesis that other people carry accurate models of me, and does it then imply that I partially survive in a stranger who never knew me but happens to instantiate the relevant dispositions? Third, can talk of one "I" being partially present in several brains simultaneously be identity talk at all, given Leibniz's Law and the one-one character of identity — or is the view better read as making no identity claim whatever, in which case what is left of the thought that someone survives? Fourth, what happens in branching and duplication cases, and does the account have any resources for them? Fifth, how does the graded scale of "degrees of souledness" that Hofstadter sketches relate to Parfit's degrees of psychological connectedness: are these commensurable notions, competing answers to the same question, or merely homonyms? If you think one of these pressure points is the real one and the others derivative, say so and justify the ordering.

A few constraints. Please write in American English, and pitch the discussion at readers who know the personal-identity literature well but have never read Hofstadter: gloss his machinery — self-symbols, the level-crossing between patterns and neurons, the autobiographical material about his wife — instead of assuming it. No bibliography, no page references, and no quoted passages from either author; paraphrase throughout, and where you are rationally reconstructing an argument rather than reporting a position someone has actually advanced, mark that explicitly. Somewhere in the range of 1,200 to 1,500 words. Finally, and importantly for my purposes: be candid about the state of play. Separate what is settled among people who know both bodies of work — that Parfit holds identity not to be what matters, for instance — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether Hofstadter's position is a species of reductionism, whether it is a view about survival at all or about representation, and whether it is even intended as an answer to the questions the seminar has been asking. Do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus, and do not quietly accommodate my framing: if you think my student has misdescribed what the pattern-in-others material is meant to do, say so, and explain the alternative reading on its own terms.
