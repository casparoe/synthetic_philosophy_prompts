Acting for this one as a philosopher of science who also happens to know the history of economic thought properly — someone who has actually read the Essay rather than the two-line summary everybody carries around. Think carefully before you start drafting; I'd rather have ten good questions than fifteen padded ones.

Setup: I co-host a small podcast, ~45 minute episodes, audience is smart laypeople plus a fair number of grad students who'll notice if I'm winging it. Next week's guest is a historian of economic thought — I'll just call her G — who has a forthcoming paper arguing that Malthus's principle of population wasn't a failed prediction but a successful one whose domain got narrowed out from under it. Her position, roughly, in four moves:

1. Treat the geometrical-vs-arithmetical business (population doubling every twenty-five years while food creeps along) as a claim about pre-industrial societies, not about the future of England.
2. Read that way, the mechanism was genuinely operative for a couple of thousand years: real wages barely budged, and any time they rose above subsistence, population response knocked them back down. G says this is now much better documented than it was when Malthus asserted it.
3. What broke after roughly 1800 wasn't a refutation of the mechanism. It was two things he had no reason to expect: fertility starting to fall among people who were getting richer rather than poorer, and a food-supply jump that came out of industrial chemistry rather than out of more farmland.
4. So the honest verdict is that he was right about the mechanism and right about the world he actually lived in, and "wrong" only in the sense that the world stopped being his world.

The piece of machinery I most want you to attack, because it's doing all the load-bearing work: G distinguishes a *rescue* — bolting on an auxiliary assumption after the fact to save a theory — from what she calls an *articulation*, which she defines as spelling out a boundary condition that was already implicit in the original claim, and that leaves the theory more precise and more testable inside its domain than it was before. She says the demographic transition is an articulation of Malthus, not a rescue of him. I think that distinction is doing something suspiciously convenient and I want the interview to test whether it survives contact.

Deliverable, in this order:

First, a short brainstorm — bullets, no more than half a page — mapping out what "right but early" could even mean. I want to see you seperate the genuinely different readings (true mechanism, wrong timescale; true mechanism, wrong domain; true claim, untestable in principle; true claim whose supporting evidence only arrived later; etc.) and mark which of them are actually defensible and which are just relabelling a failure as a success. Then throw the useless ones away and build the questions out of what's left.

Then ten to twelve interview questions. For each one give me (i) the answer G is most likely to give on air, and (ii) a follow-up keyed specifically to that answer — not a generic follow-up. The follow-ups should be tighter then the questions they follow.

The questions have to turn on the content of her view, and they have to cover, at minimum, these beats:

- The ratio claim itself. Was it ever a quantitative prediction, or a claim about a tendency? Push on whether a ratio statement with no time parameter and no stated domain can be falsified by any particular stretch of history — if not, what's left of it as a claim?
- Preventive versus positive checks. Build at least one question around the late-marriage pattern in northwest Europe — demographers map it with a line running roughly from Trieste up to St Petersburg, west of which people married late, set up nuclear households, and had fewer children. Malthus credited this to "moral restraint," a prudential response to your prospects. But if marriage age in that region was pinned by inheritance rules and household-formation customs centuries before anyone was doing wage arithmetic, is the preventive check a mechanism or a cultural accident that he rationalized after the fact?
- Origins. The Essay began as a polemic against Godwin and Condorcet and the perfectibilists. Does the motivated origin of a theory bear at all on whether it was right? I want a version of this question she cannot dispose of by saying "genetic fallacy" and moving on.
- The 1980 Simon–Ehrlich wager: Ehrlich picked five metals, and lost when all of them fell in real terms over the decade. Ask G whether a lost bet like that is a rescue-proof data point, or whether prices just aren't the right variable to be betting on — and if she takes the second route, the follow-up should force her to name the variable that *would* be right, and say how it behaved over the same decade.
- The generality test. One question that makes her apply her own rescue/articulation distinction to a case she'd hate — the Club of Rome's 1972 Limits to Growth, or Marxism, or astrology, your pick — and that commits her to a predicted verdict *before* she's allowed to look at how the case actually turned out. This is the one I most expect her to weasel out of; write the follow-up assuming she tries.
- The revision history. Between 1798 and 1803 he massively expanded the book with empirical material and kept revising, six editions through 1826. Is that normal science or immunization? The follow-up needs to corner her on how many rounds of revision a theory gets before the additions stop counting as articulations — and on whether she can state that limit without reference to whether she likes the theory.
- Close on evidence: what observation about a pre-industrial economy, or about a currently developing one, would make G say the *mechanism* claim itself is false — not the timing, not the domain, the mechanism?

Tone: I want to come across as a host who's read the book and is friendly but not soft. No "what first drew you to Malthus?", no softball openers, and no question that just invites her to restate the view she's already stated in the abstract. No question should be answerable with a bare yes or no. Each should be deliverable aloud in about two sentences.

Finally, in two or three sentences at the end, tell me which of your questions she's most likely to survive intact and which one is actually going to draw blood — and why.
