I'm assembling a seminar on the ethics of the future and I keep getting stuck on one page of a national appraisal manual — the sort of document a treasury issues telling departments how to cost out a proposal. Not an eccentric one, a bog-standard one. Here's what it does. Costs and consumption benefits are discounted in real terms at something like 3.5% a year, with the rate stepping down the further out you go. But health effects — lives saved, years of life, QALYs — are supposed to be discounted at roughly 1.5%. And then, in another section of the same manual, you're told to attach a money value to a statistical life, some figure in the millions, and drop it into the benefit stream, where it takes the money rate like everything else.

So: what has to be true about value for that split to be more than a bookkeeping fudge? If you can monetise a life in one chapter, you're treating health as fungible with consumption; if health then gets a gentler rate in the next chapter, you're treating it as not fungible, or at least as not governed by whatever logic drives the 3.5. Which commitment is actually doing the work? My hunch is the low health rate only makes sense if the whole justification for discounting is that the future will be richer than us, so a unit of good delivered then buys its recipient less — and if that's the justification, the practice is commited to health not rising the way consumption does. Is that an empirical bet about future medicine, or a normative claim that a QALY isn't the kind of thing anyone can end up having more of?

Does the split also quietly kill the impatience story? Impatience doesn't care what the good is, so a rate built on impatience would hit health and money alike. Which suggests the manual has rejected pure time preference in all but name while still printing a rate that looks like prudence. And note the rate is calibrated to a growth forecast for a world the policy being appraised may itself change — is that separability something the practice needs, or just something it assumes for convenience?

I'd like a clean map of what's strictly presupposed versus what merely fits: the declining schedule, for instance, seems licensable by several rival views at once. Then tell me what would have to change — in the manual, and in the projects it greenlights — if you swapped the presupposed view for (a) a single rate for everything, (b) no discounting at all, or (c) a person-affecting view on which benefits accruing to people who wouldn't otherwise exist don't count, since a lot of those health gains in year 150 land on people whose existence is downstream of the very policy being scored.
