I teach the philosophy-of-medicine unit and I'm supposed to be writing up my own view of disease, but my verdicts won't line up, so I want you to tell me what principle, if any, is actually sitting underneath them.

Here's what I say. Presbyopia at 48: a disease, even though essentially everyone in the age group has it. Menopause at 52: not a disease. The same loss of ovarian function at 30: a disease. Age-related hearing loss at 78: a disease, same reasoning as the eyes. A marathon runner's resting pulse of 38: not a disease, though it's a wild statistical outlier. A recessive variant that means the standard anaesthetic relaxant would leave you paralysed for hours, in someone who has never been near an operating theatre and never will be: not a disease, just a difference — though I flip the momment they're booked in for surgery. Chronic widespread pain with no lesion anyone can find: a disease. A watchmaker's mild essential tremor: a disease. Being 5ft 2in in a world where the shelves, machines and desks are all built for people 5ft 9in: not a disease, that's a social arrangement rather than a problem with a body. Drapetomania: obviously not a disease. Skin-picking disorder: a disease — and I can't tell you what separates it from the previous one except that I trust the patients' reports of their own suffering.

My own stab so far is roughly Boorse plus a harm clause: a disease is a part of you failing to do what it was built to do, where the failure makes your life go worse. I know that's too quick — the menopause trio suggests I'm smuggling in age-relative statistics, and the height case suggests the harm has to come from the right place.

So: state the principle as precisely as you can, clause by clause, and tell me which of the cases above is doing the work for each clause. Then say what it delivers on three I haven't listed — lactose intolerance in a society with no dairying, an asymptomatic carrier of Huntington's, and severe short stature in a child with no endocrine abnormality. And be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate: I'd sooner have a plain principle that costs me two of them than a baroque one that saves all twelve, so say which I should give up and why.

British English, please.
