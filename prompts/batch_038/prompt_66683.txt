I am auditing an analogy that came up in a graduate seminar, and I want a rigorous, self-contained analysis. Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — everything you need should be reconstructible from the argument itself.

Here is the analogy as a fellow student deployed it. Panpsychism's combination problem — how the experiences of micro-level subjects could amount to one unified human experience — is, they said, exactly like asking how hydrogen and oxygen, neither of which is wet, could possibly amount to water, which is. Chemistry dissolved that puzzle: wetness, transparency, and a boiling point are in none of the parts, and yet nothing is mysterious, because we accept that the laws license combinations of that kind once the microstructure is fixed. So, they concluded, the combination problem is no more mysterious than any other problem of macro-level novelty, and panpsychism therefore carries no explanatory burden that physicalism does not also carry. The parity claim is the load-bearing element: my interlocutor is not merely saying panpsychism is coherent, but that it is no worse off than the physicalist, so the dispute cannot be settled by pointing at combination.

Two complications. First, the analogy was subsequently pushed further, toward a top-down version of the view: if a whole can be more than its parts in the way water is, then perhaps the cosmic whole is the basic subject and human minds are aspects of it — and the same chemistry story is supposed to cover that direction of combination as well. Second, someone in the room asked whether the analogy, taken at face value, also commits us to the claim that large organizations literally have experiences, since a society of subjects seems structurally no different from a lattice of atoms.
