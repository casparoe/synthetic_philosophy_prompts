Our department's joint seminar has been grinding on the same fight for two years and I've decided to make them write down the thing they should have written a year ago.

Side A: a colleague who thinks emotions are basically evaluations — appraisals or judgments about how a situation bears on what you care about. Her whole reason for holding the view is normativity: fear, anger, grief can be fitting or unfitting, appropriate or inappropriate, and she thinks only something with content can be assessed that way at all.

Side B: a colleague in the James line who thinks emotions are perceptions of your own bodily state — heart, gut, breathing, face, viscera, plus whatever interoceptive awareness of all that amounts to. On his view the aboutness gets stitched on afterwards by concepts and narrative, and he thinks "that fear was unfitting" isn't a truth-evaluable claim about the fear; it's a comment about how well calibrated a detection system was and how reliable the person's labeling is.

They have never once stated their disagreement precisely enough that the other would sign it. Please draft the joint statement a real adversarial collaboration between them would produce:

1. A numbered list of claims both can actually sign — substantive ones only, no throat-clearing. I want what both accept about what any theory of emotion owes us, and how each side commits to handling the cases that get thrown around in the seminar: the fear you can't shake while sincerly judging the dog is harmless; moods and free-floating anxiety that seem to have no object; infant and nonhuman animal emotion; grief that keeps going long after you've fully accepted the death; being moved by a character you know doesn't exist; and the panic/phobia cases where self-report and behavior come apart.

2. A disagreements table, with these columns: the wording side A would accept for the dispute, the wording side B would accept, a neutral formulation neither side wrote, and your call on whether it's a genuine disagreement or substantially verbal. Where you say verbal, say which part is verbal and why. My guess is that something around "emotions have representational content" turns out to be verbal and something around normativity doesn't, but convince me either way.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where getting an answer would actually move at least one of them. I suspect one is whether fittingness reduces to the truth or accuracy of some representation, and another is whether a subject can be in an emotional state they have no concept for whatsoever. Tell me if I've mis-diagnosed, and add the ones I'm missing.

4. For each crux, the case, experiment, or body of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. They both already cite the facial-feedback work and how unevenly some of it has held up, and the old reports from people with high spinal cord lesions about how much their feelings changed. Use those if they're genuinely relevant to the cruxes, or say they aren't and say what is. Include what result would force each side to give something up.

5. A short closing section: what each of them is prepared to concede if the evidence goes badly, and whether there is any single question both would accept as decisive. If there isn't, say so plainly and tell me what that implies about the debate.

Also flag anywhere you think one of the two positions is unstable rather than merely wrong — for instance if side A can't say what makes two episodes the same emotion type, or side B can't account for why we argue about whether someone's anger was deserved. I'm not after a verdict on who's right. I want the document both of them could put their names to, with whatever's left over made exact.
