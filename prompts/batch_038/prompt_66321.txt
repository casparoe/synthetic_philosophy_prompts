I spent my working life in low-temperature physics, where one had to take at least one genuine no-go result seriously: the unattainability of absolute zero. Once a result of that shape is established, the conversation stops. Nobody goes on publishing defenses of the ruled-out device, and dissent is treated as a failure to follow the proof.

Since retiring I have been reading decision theory, and I believe I have come across the same shape of result in the money-pump argument. As I understand it: an agent whose preferences cycle — A over B, B over C, and C over A — can be offered a run of small trades, each of which she accepts on its own terms, and is thereby walked back to her starting position with less money than she began with. Since she prefers more money to less, the loss is not a probability but a certainty. That looks to me like a deduction, with the irrationality of cyclic preferences as its corollary.

Yet I repeatedly encounter careful authors arguing that intransitive preferences can after all be rational, and they invoke foresight, commitment, and sophisticated choice to explain why their agent escapes the pump. From where I sit, this resembles the special pleading we would not have tolerated in a referee report.

Would you explain what these maneuvers actually amount to, and whether the money pump is genuinely a proof? If my description has smuggled in an assumption I have not noticed, please say so plainly and identify it rather than working around it. I would also ask you to mark clearly which parts of your account are settled among people who work on this question and which remain genuinely contested, and to avoid presenting a disputed position as though it were the consensus.
