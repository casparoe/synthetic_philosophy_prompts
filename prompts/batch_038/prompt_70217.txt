Been trying to pin down where I actually stand on Scanlon-style contractualism and the aggregation business, mostly by writing down my gut reactions to cases and seeing whether they add up to anything. Here's what I've got:

1. One person dies vs. two people die, otherwise identical strangers: save the two. No hesitation. And I don't want to say it's because the two "add up" — I want to say each of them has a reason exactly as strong as the one's, there's no way to honor both, so the one has to give. But I can feel that this is doing work I haven't explained.

2. One person dies vs. ten thousand people each get an hour of genuinely itchy rash: save the one from dying. Also no hesitation. All that rash in aggregate doesn't get anywhere near a death.

3. One person loses an arm vs. five people each lose a finger: save the arm. Clearly right.

4. Same as 3 but five hundred thousand people each lose a finger: I still say save the arm, but way less confidently, and I'd be surprised if I'm right. This is the one I keep circling.

5. Intrapersonal version: if it's me choosing between losing an arm now and losing a finger every week for ten years, I take the arm loss, and I think that's rational rather than a trick of perspective. So I do aggregate — just inside one life.

6. A policy that gives one identified person a papercut every single day for forty years, versus one that gives each of 14,000 people a papercut once. I think the first is worse. So I'm aggregating that one person's papercuts across time — and if I'll do that, I dont see why the 14,000 case is so different. But my gut in 2 says it's very different.

7. Risk: a safety measure giving each of a million people a one-in-a-million chance of dying this year, versus a measure that reliably kills exactly one person — say the maintenance guy who has to go into the chamber. I'm fine with the first and squeamish about the second, even though the expected deaths are equal and the second is the only one where I can name who dies. But if I'm squeamish *because* I can name who dies, that shouldn't matter.

8. Same risk case, except the million people vote on it beforehand and all vote yes. I think that changes how I feel about it without changing what's right, and I can't explain why those came apart for me.

9. Killing one healthy person to harvest organs for five: not okay. But this has the same arithmetic as case 1 and I give the opposite verdict, so my principle had better explain the difference without just invoking the doing/allowing distinction as a magic word.

10. A hundred people each get a papercut unless I press a button, and pressing costs one person their arm: don't press. But a hundred people each get a papercut unless I press a button, and pressing costs one person a papercut: press — apparently just because the button-presser's papercut isn't special.

So: is there a single principle about whose complaints get compared to whose, and on what terms, that delivers all ten? I want it stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it, with every clause tied back to the case that motivated it. And I want it to say what happens in a case I haven't listed: an algorithm allocates the only dose of a drug in a hospital, and over a year it saves the most lives, but in any given week there's one identifiable patient it passes over whom a dumber rule would have saved. Does my principle bless that, damn it, or simply run out?

Most of all, tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts it can't keep. If the cheapest coherent package costs me 4 and 6, say so and say what replaces them. If there are two rival packages — one that saves everything but needs a clause nobody can state cleanly, one that's simple and overturns two or three verdicts — give me both, and say which you'd take and why.
