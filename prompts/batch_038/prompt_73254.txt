Our philosophy-of-physics reading group ended last term in a standoff I would like to be prepared for when we resume. Two members accept the experimental violations of the Bell inequalities but disagree about what to give up in response. One defends a version of quantum theory in which particles have definite positions at all times, steered by a wave, and cheerfully concedes that the steering acts instantaneously between distant regions; the other denies that anything propagates anywhere, and instead refuses to read the quantum state as a description of an observer-independent situation, treating it as a codification of an agent's expectations. The member chairing the session dismissed the dispute with a single sentence — Bell's theorem refutes local realism — adding that since both of them had already surrendered that package, they were merely quibbling over which half of it to abandon first, and the disagreement was verbal. I suspect the sentence is doing far more work than it can bear.

Please set out, as a numbered menu I can hand round at the next meeting, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be expressing. The ambiguity needs teasing apart on both sides. For locality: is it a condition on the joint probabilities of two outcomes given the settings and whatever lies in their common past; a prohibition on any spacelise influence between events; nothing stronger than the impossibility of using the correlations to signal faster than light; or a constraint on which events may serve as causes of which? For realism: is it the thesis that outcomes are fixed by prior physical states, that measurement results obtain whether or not the relevant measurement is performed, that there is a fact of the matter about the quantum state independent of anyone's information, or merely that the correlations admit of a deeper explanation in terms of local beables? Take the plausible pairings and assign each a verdict: platitude, substantive thesis genuinely refuted by the experimental data, thesis the data leave untouched, or simply false. Where the standard decomposition of the Bell factorisation condition helps — into parameter independence and outcome independence in Shimony's terms, or into locality and completeness in Jarrett's — say which side of the split each of my two colleagues is abandoning, and whether that puts them inside or outside what the experiments actually constrain.

Then address the chair's charge head on. Given that the slogan denies a conjunction, does it really leave open which conjunct goes, so that the two positions are on all fours with one another? Or do some readings oblige one of them into a stance that is not merely unfashionable but incompatible with the observed correlations, or with the relativistic constraint that no usable signal travels outside the light cone? Flag any reading on which the second word turns out to add nothing to the first, so that realism is decoration rather than an independent assumption.

Write in British English. Open with one sentence stating whether you think the chair was right, then give the menu. Keep the entire response under 700 words.
