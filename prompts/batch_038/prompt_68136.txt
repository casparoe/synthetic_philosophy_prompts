I am drafting a methods section for a project in comparative economic history, and I have hit an argument with a colleague over an analogy she wants to build the section around. The territory is the cluster of questions descending from Needham's — why modern science, and later industrial takeoff, happened in Britain rather than in China — and the live issue for us is whether that contrast is well posed at all.

Her analogy, roughly: a properly framed contrastive question in history is like a matched case–control study in epidemiology. In such a study you do not ask why anyone got the disease; you fix an outcome definition, select cases, and select controls matched on the variables you treat as background, and the question of why these cases and not those matched controls is determinate only once the matching and the source population are specified. Change the matching and you get a different but equally legitimate question with a different answer. Her claim is that this is precisely the structure of the Pomeranz-style correction to the divergence debate: "why Britain and not China" is malformed in the same way that "why these patients and not the whole hospital" is malformed, and the remedy is not to abandon the question but to re-specify the comparison — substitute England and the Yangzi delta, taken as the matched pair, for the national units, after which the question becomes answerable. She also wields the analogy offensively, against those who call the whole enterprise arbitrary: on her view, insisting that the contrast class is underdetermined is like insisting that epidemiology is arbitrary because one could have matched on other variables, and underdetermination of the matching is not the absence of an answer.

I am not convinced, but I want an audit rather than a shrug. Please do four things.

First, lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome. What in the historical case plays the role of the source population, the outcome definition, the exposure, the matching variables, the controls, and the exchangeability assumption? Flag any slot in the analogy with no plausible historical counterpart, and any feature of the historical case with no counterpart in the study design.

Second, separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten her conclusion from those that are merely inconvenient. I care especially about: whether the outcome (industrialization) can be defined independently of the units being compared; whether those units are stable objects across the period, given that England in 1800 is partly constituted by the very processes to be explained; whether there is any analogue of a risk set from which both cases and controls could have been drawn; and whether the matching in the historical case is chosen for causal reasons or for narrative ones.

Third, say what the analogy smuggles in. My suspicion is that it quietly imports the assumption that there exists a well-defined space of counterfactually available outcomes — a population at risk, in effect — and that the historian's task is to identify which member of that space was realized and why. If that is right, does her offensive use collapse: does the arbitrariness worry she dismisses reappear in a form the analogy cannot absorb?

Fourth, give me a verdict. Is "why Britain and not China" well posed as it stands, well posed only after re-specification, or not well posed in either form? And is there a better analogy for what comparative historians are actually doing — a natural experiment, a forensic reconstruction, a difference-in-differences design, something else — together with the point at which that substitute breaks down?

Do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the argument. Close with a short paragraph stating what would change your mind: which of your points is most likely to be wrong, and what consideration would flip the verdict in the fourth item. Hard limit of 700 words, table included.
