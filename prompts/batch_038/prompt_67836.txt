I'm in my third year of a joint philosophy–psychology program, and the phrase that has come to dominate my lab is my supervisor's: whenever a disagreement about a perceptual effect threatens to go anywhere, he says there is no such thing as raw perception, that all observation is theory-laden, and moves on. He has used it twice recently in ways that struck me as doing very different jobs. The first was over a referee report on our paper about a size-contrast effect: the referee asked us to show that the effect is in the experience rather than in how subjects decide what to say, and my supervisor declared the demand conceptually confused, since no verbal report can escape the categories the reporter already brings. The second was in a reading group, when someone asked whether a visual experience can justify a belief at all if what you expect shapes what you see, and he said the question simply dissolves for the same reason. A classmate afterwards traced the slogan back to the old value-and-need experiments, and someone else said it was really a philosophy-of-science thesis about theory choice that got smuggled into psychology decades ago and never left. I suspect both things are true and that this is exactly the problem.

What I need is a disambiguation. Could you lay out, as a numbered menu ordered from the most modest to the most radical, the distinct precise theses that "all observation is theory-laden" can be used to assert? For each reading, I want four things: first, exactly what is being claimed and at which level the "lading" happens — whether it concerns which stimuli get sampled at all, where attention is allocated, how the input is categorized, whether the content or phenomenal character of the experience itself is altered online by what the subject believes, or whether any neutral description of an observation is even possible; second, what kind of claim it is — a conceptual truth, a contingent psychological hypothesis about the architecture of vision, or a thesis about the structure of epistemic justification; third, a verdict on its standing, i.e. whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, empirically contested, or just false, together with what would count as evidence for or against it; and fourth, which other readings on the list it is compatible with. I have a strong hunch that one can grant several of the modest readings and still maintain that early visual processing is insulated from a subject's occurrent beliefs, and I want to know whether that combination is stable or whether accepting the modest ones commits you to the radical ones by degrees. Please also flag every point where the slogan slides between a claim about reports and a claim about experiences, since I think that slide is doing most of the work in my supervisor's mouth.

Then tell me which reading, if any, actually earns him the two dismissals. Specifically: does any plausible reading license telling a referee that the distinction between a perceptual effect and a response-bias effect is not worth drawing, or does that demand survive every version of the slogan — and if so, what would satisfying it look like? And does any reading license the conclusion that experiences cannot justify beliefs, or does the most defensible version of the thesis leave that question completely open, perhaps even sharpening it? I would also like you to address the reflexive difficulty I can see but cannot articulate: if all observation is theory-laden, then so is the data in our own paper, and it seems the slogan cannot simultaneously be a hypothesis about how vision works and a reason for refusing to test hypotheses about how vision works — unless there is a reading on which it can, in which case say which and at what cost. You do not need to give me references; I cannot chase them down right now and I want the conceptual map rather than the bibliography. Please write it so that I could hand the list to my supervisor in a lab meeting without it reading like an ambush.
