**The question as set (750 words maximum):**

> In the *Laws*, the Athenian Stranger defends a regulated institution — the supervised drinking party — for a colony whose legislation is supposed to aim at virtue rather than at pleasure. Reconstruct the argument that brings him to this defence. What problem in moral education is wine meant to solve, what restrictions does Plato place on its use, and how should the position be understood in relation to what Plato says about drink elsewhere?

**The rubric issued with the paper:**

**The student's script:**

The Athenian opens by criticising the Cretan and Spartan legislators, whose laws he says are directed only at courage in war. He argues that law ought to aim at the whole of virtue, and that this means courage has to be shown not merely against pain and fear but also against pleasure, which is the harder test and the one the Dorian legislatures neglect. This leads into his account of education: before reason develops in the child, the soul is moved by pleasure and pain, and education is the training of these responses — hence the image of the soul as a puppet pulled by cords of pleasure, pain and expectation. Since virtue consists in having one's pleasures and pains pull in the same direction as one's judgements, habituation must come before instruction.

Wine enters as the solution to a practical difficulty inside this programme. Training against pain is straightforward, because a legislator can arrange for citizens to meet painful and frightening things under supervision, as gymnastic and military exercises do. Training against pleasure is harder, because the legislator cannot manufacture occasions of temptation without exposing the city to real risk. The Athenian's proposal is that wine does the work artificially: it produces a temporary state of fear and disorientation in the drinker, and if it is administered in a controlled setting the citizen can be made to face a manufactured fear repeatedly, at no cost to the city. Drinking thus becomes a safe rehearsal of danger, operating on the same logic that would apply if there were a potion capable of producing terror, which could be given to young men in small doses both to see how they responded and to habituate them to standing firm. The exercise also has diagnostic value: as metal is proved in fire, the drinker's character is disclosed, and the lawgiver learns which of his citizens have the dispositions he needs.

The restrictions are designed to keep the risk contained. The drinking party must have a leader, and the leader must himself be sober, since a drunk man cannot manage either himself or anyone else. Drinking is graded by age: no wine at all before eighteen, moderate tasting between eighteen and thirty, and only from forty may a citizen take part in the Dionysiac symposia. Drunkenness is permitted wherever it is properly supervised, which is why the institution is safe in the new colony and dangerous in existing cities, where the parties are unsupervised and led by whoever is most enthusiastic. In the second book the Athenian adds a gentler justification: wine is the gift of Dionysus to old age, a remedy against the sourness and rigidity of the elderly, who are otherwise too ashamed to sing in public; moderate drinking loosens them and makes them willing to contribute to the choruses, and it renders citizens more sociable and friendly to one another.

The position in the *Laws* is finally consistent with what Plato shows elsewhere. In the *Symposium*, intoxication is already presented as a route by which the soul is carried upward: the ladder of love begins with attraction to a beautiful body and ascends to the Beautiful itself, and Alcibiades' arrival at the party demonstrates that the philosopher can drink without losing command of himself. The *Laws* simply institutionalises that insight. What is depicted dramatically in the earlier dialogue becomes a supervised civic practice in the later one, and wine's role in moral education is confirmed by both.
