Retired physical chemist here — forty years on reaction kinetics, and now I'm the guy who turns up to philosophy seminars and annoys everyone. Next month I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on agency, and I'd rather walk in knowing what's coming at me in the question period.

My thesis, roughly: the coherence arguments (Dutch books, Savage-style representation theorems, that family) don't show that a rational agent has to *have* a probability function and a utility function. They show that if you carve the agent's option set finely enough you can always read one off from its choices — the way thermodynamics is recoverable from mechanics only once you've fixed a coarse-graining. So expected utility maximization isn't a constraint on agents, it's a description you adopt at a grain, and "incoherence" is what you see when the grain and the agent don't line up.

The room: two or three formal decision theorists who know these theorems cold and will not accept a hand-wavy physics analogy as a premise; a behavioral economist who thinks real humans violate the axioms weekly and can't see why re-describing them is interesting; two ML people for whom expected-reward maximization is an engineering fact about their systems rather than a normative claim about anyone; and one philosopher who thinks the entire framework is a category mistake.
