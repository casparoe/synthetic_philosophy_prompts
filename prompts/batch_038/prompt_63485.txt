Here's roughly the account he gave, in my words but tracking what he said:

Smith's starting point is sympathy. When you see someone in distress you don't reason your way into caring about them, you just catch the feeling — he uses the example of flinching when you see a blow about to land on somebody else's arm. That's not self-interest wearing a disguise: Smith was explicit that he's arguing against the Hobbes/Mandeville line that all concern for others is really concern for yourself. Sympathy also happens to feel good, being in tune with other people is pleasant, and that's a big part of why we bother cultivating the virtues at all. Approval, for Smith, is basically just a matter of your feelings lining up with someone else's. But raw sympathy is obviously lopsided — we care more about people close to us than people far away, and most of all about ourselves — so Smith introduces the impartial spectator, which is the voice of your community installed in your head by upbringing. Before you act you picture how it looks to an onlooker, and if you'd blush, you don't do it. Conscience on this view is just the crowd, internalised. Which has an interesting consequence: Smith's moral theory is relativist at the root. Raise a child in a slaveholding society and his impartial spectator will tell him slaveholding is fine — awkward, since Smith himself was no friend of slavery. So the two halves of the man, the moral philosopher and the economist, never really got reconciled.
