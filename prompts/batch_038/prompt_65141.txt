I hold a position about the demandingness objection to consequentialism that I'd like pressure-tested. The objection is usually treated as a direct reductio: consequentialism requires too much of affluent agents, so it must be false. My view is that it proves too much to prove anything. The intuitions it recruits — that keeping what you have earned is permissible, that failing to rescue a distant stranger differs in kind from failing to rescue a child drowning in a shallow pond — are shaped by the particular circumstances of comfortable agents living in societies with weak safety nets and unreliable aid channels, which is exactly the set of circumstances least likely to have produced intuitions that track moral truth rather than the interests of the intuiters. If the debunking line that has been pressed against moral realism in the evolutionary psychology literature is legitimate at all, it applies with unusual force here, and the objection therefore begs the question by assuming the permissive structure of commonsense morality that is precisely what stands disputed.

Tell me what would count against this thesis. I don't want the familiar replies about whether consequentialism can be made less onerous — satisficing versions, scalar or degree-based formulations, split-level theories that let agents keep ordinary permissions in practice while endorsing the maximizing standard in theory. I want the exposures specific to the debunking move: the possibility that it generalizes into a skepticism that strips my own position of any ground to stand on, since if affluent intuition is untrustworthy then so is the intuition that a view is unmotivatedly revisionary, which is much of what recommends consequentialism in the first place; the question of what observable pattern of intuitive verdicts across differently situated people would show that commonsense permissions track something other than the interests of those who hold them; and whether there is any empirical or conceptual finding that would count as evidence, rather than merely as an objection, that the demandingness intuition is reliable. Please rank the defeaters in continuous prose from fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly which of them a committed holder of my view should concede as genuinely damaging rather than explain away. Close with an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into immunity, and whether immunity of that kind is a defect in a thesis of this sort or an acceptable feature of one that is itself a claim about the unreliability of opposing intuitions.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a considered email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italicized text, and no tables. Let the ranking emerge from the order and wording of your paragraphs rather than from any apparatus.
