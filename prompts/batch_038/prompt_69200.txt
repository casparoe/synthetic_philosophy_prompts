Three weeks from now I'm giving a departmental seminar in a microbial evolution group, and I want a proper Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of my view. The room: mostly experimentalists who run Pseudomonas and plasmid-transfer evolution experiments, two theoreticians who fit multilevel-selection models, a bioinformatician who works on metagenome time series, and exactly one philosopher of biology who's clearly read Okasha and will keep trying to drag things back to replicators versus vehicles. They have very little patience for framing. What they want to know is what you'd measure and whether it changes what you do in the lab.

Here's the argument I'm going to present, roughly. The Price equation is available at every level, so any partition can be narrated as really about genes, cells, or groups — which is why the units debate has gone round in circles for decades without anybody losing. It was never a formal question. What settles which level is the unit is an empirical measurement: transmission fidelity across a bottleneck, i.e. how much of the fitness variance at a candidate level actually survives one round of parent-to-offspring transmission. In bacteria, where horizontal transfer is constant and mobile elements hop between lineages, the chromosome and a conjugative plasmid in the very same population can have wildly different transmission fidelity, so the cell stops being the safe default answer. I want to say individuality is bottleneck-relative and comes in degrees you can actually estimate, and that the familiar claim that the gene's-eye view and MLS are empirically equivalent breaks down precisely where transmission is reticulate rather than treelike, because 'the gene' quietly presupposes a lineage to average over. I'm calling it transmission-relative individuality, though I'm open to better names.

Give me the objections this particular audience will raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than how interesting they are, and phrase each one the way a microbial evolutionist or an MLS modeller would actually say it out loud — not the way a philosopher would. For each: a two-minute reply I could give from the lectern, and an honest note on where that reply is softest. Then tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so the semantic fights don't eat the whole hour, which single reply absolutely has to land for the talk to survive, whether any of these objections sinks the claim rather than just embarrassing it, and the question I'm praying nobody asks. Please write the whole thing in British English.
