I've been thinking about how a criminal jury is actually built and something in the design doesn't sit right with me. Twelve strangers, they have to deliberate together, and the verdict has to be unanimous; if they hang, we treat that as a breakdown and just run the whole thing again with a fresh twelve. And when they deadlock, judges in a lot of places send them back out with an instruction telling the minority to ask whether their doubt is still reasonable given that it hasn't impressed so many other equally honest and intelligent people, telling the majority the same thing in reverse, and telling everyone not to give up an honest belief. Meanwhile the same system decides appeals with three judges and a bare majority — nobody has to be persuaded, the loser just writes a dissent and it gets printed.

I know theres a classic result to the effect that a majority vote gets more accurate as the group grows, provided each voter is independently better than a coin flip. What does the jury package actually presuppose about how group judgment works? Does it presuppose that result, or something that contradicts it — because mandatory deliberation plus a deadlock instruction seem to assume the votes are *not* independent, so what's doing the reliability work instead? And is there a single theory that can license both the unanimous criminal jury and the three-judge majority panel, or are they answering different questions entirely (accuracy vs. something else, like legitimacy or error asymmetry)?

Please separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits comfortably with — my suspicion is several rival stories license the same rules — and flag which parts of your answer are settled and which are genuinely argued over by people who work on this. Don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
