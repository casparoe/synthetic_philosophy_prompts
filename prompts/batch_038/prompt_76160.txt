I help run a film-and-philosophy seminar for non-majors at a community college, and next week is Groundhog Day, filed in the syllabus under cooperation and game theory. The obvious line everyone reaches for is that Phil is trapped in a repeated prisoner's dilemma and the movie is a fable about how cooperation evolves. I've been trying to make that precise enough to actually teach, and honestly the more I look at it the less sure I am that it survives contact with the details, so I want a reading that doesn't fudge. My husband thinks I'm overcomplicating a comedy, but it keeps nagging at me.

The problem is the loop resets everybody except Phil. For Rita, for Ned, for the kid who falls out of the tree, for the old homeless man who dies at the end of the day no matter what Phil tries — every one of those is a one-shot interaction with a partner who has no memory of yesterday, so there's no reputation carrying over, no partner choice, no reciprocity, none of the machinery the evolutionary literature says you need before cooperation can get a foothold. From their side there's no future to cast a shadow at all. From Phil's side the future is effectively infinite, but he's the only one who discounts it. On top of that his payoffs aren't stationary: after enough loops money, seduction, and even suicide stop paying anything, and once he knows exactly what everyone will say and do, his partners aren't strategic opponents anymore, they're a script — so is it even a dilemma, or just optimization against a fixed environment? And then the day finally breaks right after he's become a genuinely decent person, which reads like a reward for virtue, and no equilibrium story I know can absorb that.

So what's the honest verdict? Does the movie illustrate the folk theorem in some attenuated sense because at least one player really does face endless repetition, or does it quietly refute the standard story by showing cooperation arising with none of the usual mechanisms present? I'd like you to walk through the specific mechanisms — tit for tat and its variants, direct vs. indirect reciprocity, memory, discounting, partner switching — and say which ones the film actually has and which it's missing, then tell me what's left over to explain Phil's turn. Please don't reduce the movie to an illustration and don't bend the theory to fit it; if the popular reading is just wrong, say so and say what would be a better frame. I have a hunch it's Aristotle, habituation and character, rather than Axelrod, but I want to know whether that's me reaching, and I'd like to hear what, if anything, the film shows about cooperation that the formal literature can't express. No need to quote the movie or cite sources, I mostly want the argument.
