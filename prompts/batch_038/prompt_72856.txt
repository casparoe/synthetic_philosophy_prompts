I am a novelist, and for two books now I have drafted under a rule of my own devising, which I call the reportable line: a scene may render only those interior states its point-of-view character could have put into words had someone asked, and everything else must be carried by behaviour, speech, and the arrangement of the sentences. The rule has served me — readers tell me my characters feel present rather than described, which is precisely the effect I wanted and cannot account for. I have recently discovered a debate in the philosophy of mind that appears to be about my rule, and I want it stated in the vocabulary I actually work in.

Take, first, the distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness, where access is understood as a content's availability to the rest of the system for report, reasoning, and the control of action. Take, second, the global workspace proposal that a content becomes conscious in virtue of being broadcast widely enough to be so available. Take, third, the further claim that phenomenal character overflows whatever is accessible and reportable in this way. Translate all three, as faithfully as you can, into the craft vocabulary of fiction: focalization, showing against telling, free indirect style, what a narrator reports as against what a reader is left to infer, the interior that is dramatized as against the interior that is merely legible. Attend especially to the overflow claim, since my rule denies it by construction; I want to know whether in translation it becomes a claim about prose that carries more than the character could say, or whether it simply fails to land anywhere.

Then give me the residue report, and treat that as the interesting part: which claims come across intact, which come across only if the target vocabulary is allowed to do some violence to them — naming the violence — and which have no counterpart in craft talk at all. Be scrupulous about not smuggling narrative assumptions in and calling the smuggling translation; if you find yourself rendering broadcast as told plainly, or overflow as subtext, stop and ask whether the substitution is doing the arguing rather than the evidence. Conclude with your own reading of the residue: does my rule fail because the craft vocabulary lacks a category the philosophers have identified correctly, or because the phenomenal/access distinction is less clear than it appears and my readers' sense of presence is tracking something describable without it? Please write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — since I mean to read it at my desk rather than skim it.
