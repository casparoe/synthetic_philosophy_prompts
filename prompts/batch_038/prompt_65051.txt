I have been a nurse in a university neuro-ICU for nine years and sit on our ethics consult service, and last spring I ended up on both sides of a single conversation at once: my uncle was declared dead by neurologic criteria after a ruptured aneurysm, and my grandmother came to me rather than to the attending to ask why we intended to stop. What I told her, and what I have told families dozens of times since, is a line I have come to think of as the interface account. Roughly: an organism is alive insofar as it is the kind of thing that maintains itself as one thing, under its own direction, in exchange with an environment that would otherwise run it down; in a human being that self-maintaining exchange has exactly one locus, the brain; when the brain is destroyed the exchange does not slow down or get assisted, it stops, and what the ventilator is then moving is air through a body that is being operated on rather than a body operating. I always pair it with a comparison I found decisive. Nobody says a patient is dead because a dialysis machine performs her kidneys' work, because the kidneys are one organ among many doing one job; the ventilator is different in kind, because the work it substitutes for is the work of being an organism at all. So the position I hold, and want stress-tested, is that death by neurologic criteria is not a legal fiction, not a concession to transplant programs, and not a matter of social agreement, but the recognition of a biological fact about the loss of a specific capacity.

Tell me what would count against it. I want a map of the position's exposure rather than a rebuttal: which observations, which kinds of case, and which conceptual results should move me, ranked from those that would force me to abandon the view to those that would leave it merely awkward. I know some of this territory and I suspect I have been grading it generously. The long-survival cases, in which patients who met the criteria were maintained for years afterward with wounds healing, infections fought, and growth continuing: how much damage do those do to a claim about integration, given that my account never said the parts stop working? The neuroendocrine point: the statutory formulation in most American jurisdictions speaks of irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, yet I have heard colleagues concede that some hypothalamic and pituitary activity can persist after a determination, so that the operative standard is the loss of clinically significant functions; is that concession a repair or a retreat? The 2008 white paper of the President's Council on Bioethics, which concluded that the older somatic-integration rationale had not held up and offered a replacement framed in terms of the fundamental work of an organism: does that substitution rescue my line or concede that it was already untenable? The British practice of determining death on a brainstem basis while American practice runs on a whole-brain basis, so that two jurisdictions with the same medicine declare death in cases the other would call alive: does that show my fact is a convention? The donation-after-circulatory-death protocols, in which death is declared a few minutes after circulation stops on the strength of a decision not to restart it, which looks like permanence rather than irreversibility: if that reading is acceptable there, what becomes of my insistence that the neurologic determination tracks something mind-independent? And the repeated revision of the consensus guideline itself, most recently in 2023: does the fact that the operational criteria keep changing count against the claim that they are tracking a fixed natural kind, or is that just ordinary refinement? I have also built a case I cannot yet answer. Suppose our unit maintained a declared-dead pregnant patient for sixteen weeks and she delivered a viable infant, the body regulating temperature, distributing nutrients, mounting an immune response, and sustaining a second organism throughout. Under the interface account, is that a machine running an extraordinary sequence of exchanges on a corpse, or is it an organism doing the work of an organism with help? Press that case hard rather than reassuring me. Finally, tell me what the account is committed to on the other side: if directed exchange with the environment is the criterion, what happens to the ventilator-dependent patient with a high cervical cord injury, whose body also engages the world only through a machine, and to the patient in a persistent vegetative state, whose brainstem works and who nevertheless seems to have no directed engagement with anything at all? Does my view collapse into the higher-brain account, into the cardiopulmonary account, or into nothing?

Two further requirements. First, separate the defeaters I would personally have to grant from the ones that a committed defender of the neurological standard would already concede as counting against them. I want to know which of these are live internal worries among people who accept the standard and which are pressed only by its critics, and where the standard has been re-described often enough that it no longer risks anything at all; if it has been insulated by qualification, say whether that is a genuine problem for a view that claims to report a biological fact, or an ordinary feature of any clinical criterion, and say what the difference would look like from inside the debate. Second, be explicit about epistemic status throughout. This literature does not speak with one voice, and I would rather not trade one confident story for another: mark clearly what is settled among people who work on the determination of death and what is genuinely contested, and do not present one camp's conclusion as the consensus. Where I have characterized the field in a way that is itself disputed, as I may have with the British and American contrast or with the donation-after-circulatory-death protocols, correct me before you build on it. If the honest conclusion is that my account is defensible but far more exposed than I let my grandmother hear, I would rather learn that now than in front of another family.
