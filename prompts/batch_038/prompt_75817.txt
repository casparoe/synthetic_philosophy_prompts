I'm a novelist, about 40% into a book whose narrator is a cliodynamicist — one of those people who builds mathematical models of long-run political instability and tries to put a date on the next crisis. The plot is that he stakes his career on a public prediction that his own country enters a window of severe internal breakdown inside a specific five-year span, and he's roughly right, which turns out to be a disaster for him, because now nobody — including him — can tell whether he understood something or just got lucky with a curve.

My problem is that on the page he currently reads like a fortune teller with a spreadsheet. I want him to feel like a working researcher: a man with a position on things his colleagues disagree about, and a bad conscience about the parts of his own method he can't defend out loud.

So what I want from you is an agenda for my remaining research time, not a summary of the field. I've got maybe six weeks before I lock the draft, I'm not going to be runing regressions myself, and I have room for one character, not a chorus. Which of the genuinely unresolved questions in cliodynamics and structural-demographic / secular-cycle modeling should I build him around? Give me the questions in priority order and defend the ordering. The criteria I care about, roughly in this order:

- How much a character's answer reveals about who he is. I want questions where the intellectually honest answer and the career-serving answer pull apart — where a man could shade his position without quite admitting it to himself.
- Genuinely open, not settled-but-unpopular. If practitioners have basically converged and only journalists still think it's controversial, it's no use to me.
- Dramatizable. I need something a reader can watch him do — at a conference, in a funding meeting, over a dataset he doesn't want to open. Not something he merely muses about in a car.

Then tell me which open questions I should skip: the real ones that are too technical, too inward-facing, or too dependent on statistical machinery to carry weight in a novel. Be blunt about those. I'd rather cut than pad.

One more thing, and this is the part I most want pressure-tested. I've invented a variable for his model that I'm calling the patience rate: the share of ambitious people in a given generation who expect to reach elite standing within their own lifetime rather than their children's. His claim is that surplus strivers by themselves don't produce breakdown — plenty of societies have them, quietly — and that instability only ignites once the patience rate falls past some threshold, because until then the ambitious keep competing inside the rules instead of against them. Is that a plausible thing for a real practitioner to have built, and does it collide with an argument the field is already having? Or is it the kind of thing that would get him laughed out of a workshop — in which case, is getting laughed out of a workshop a better story than being right?

Please don't give me a reading list or citations. I can't use them and I won't check them; I want the arguments and where the fault lines actually run.

Last thing: if I flip the ending so the prediction fails and the book becomes about a man defending a model that didn't work, tell me wich of your priorities move up and which ones fall away. I want to know how much of the ranking depends on that choice.
