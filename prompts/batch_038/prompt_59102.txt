The question (10 marks, second-year intro course, roughly 250 words expected):

"State the regress problem for epistemic justification. Explain why the coherentist thinks her view escapes it, and consider the objection that coherentism makes justification viciously circular."

The student wrote:

> If a belief is justified only by other beliefs, then each of those needs a justification of its own, and so on down the line. Either the chain stops somewhere, at beliefs which have no justification at all — that's foundationalism, and it's really just dogmatism with a nicer name — or it never stops, which would mean having to complete an infinite number of steps of reasoning before any single belief could count as justified. No finite mind can do that, so that option is out too.
>
> The coherentist escapes the whole thing. She says justification isn't a chain but a web: a belief is justified by cohering with the rest of the system, and the system itself is held in place by being checked against experience. The regress only looks inevitable if you assume justification has to be linear, one belief resting on the next, and that assumption is exactly what she rejects.
>
> On the circularity objection: the worry is that beliefs can't support each other, since a belief that is itself still waiting on support from B can't be the thing giving support to B. The usual reply is that the circle isn't vicious because it's wide — any given belief draws its support from a great many others, not just from the one or two it happens to imply. That seems fair enough. But I think the more serious problem is the one that gets skipped over here. There can be lots of internally coherent systems that contradict each other; a well-told fairy tale is perfectly coherent. So coherence can't be what makes a belief true, and the coherentist is forced to bring experience back in — which, as I said above, is just foundationalism smuggled in through the back door.
