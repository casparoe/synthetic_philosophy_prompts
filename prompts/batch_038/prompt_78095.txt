I teach a small grad seminar on markets and morals, and this term I've been running an exercise where the students have to specify an actual institutional design before they're allowed to argue about it — no more "a market in kidneys" in the abstract. My own worked example this year was the organ case, and honestly, the more I've pushed on it, the more I've come around to thinking my version basically dissolves the whole debate, which is not at all what I expected going in.

Here's the design. There is exactly one legal buyer, a public agency, paying a flat price. No brokers, no advertising, no private negotiation, no bidding. Allocation is strictly by medical urgency and time on the list. And the payment is not cash at the time of donation: the agency puts forty thousand into a deferred trust that the donor can't touch, borrow against, or assign, and it pays out to her designated benificiaries twenty years later, or to her estate if she dies first. The point of the deferral is that it deliberately screens out anyone in immediate financial distress — you can't settle a debt or make rent with it — so the desperate seller, who is the figure doing nearly all the work in the exploitation objection, is designed out of the market rather than merely regulated. The information and safety worries get handled by the single-buyer structure and clinical oversight; the monopoly worry gets handled by the flat public price.

So the line I want to defend in class is this: every objection to organ sales is either about the circumstances of the seller (poverty, coercion, desperation) or about the mechanics of the deal (information, safety, brokers, pricing). Fix both and there's nothing left over except a feeling, and that feeling is either irrational squeamishness or a distributive concern in disguise — people who say they object to the market usually turn out to object to who ends up in it. A colleague thinks I'm collapsing two distinct kinds of objection into one and keeps gesturing at Sandel's corruption point, something about what a market would do to the meaning of donating, and I raised the crowding-out worry myself (that a paid system might shrink the unpaid supply), and he says that's a separate issue too, but my response was that crowding-out is just an empirical prediction about a design that's never existed, and in any case it's the distribution objection wearing a different hat — the people who'd otherwise give freely now wait for the trust. I don't find the third-category talk question-begging exactly, but I can't get any traction on it either, and I don't want to concede something in seminar that turns out to be empty. So: is my dichotomy actually exhaustive, or does my colleague have a coherent category I'm failing to see? And if he does, does it survive the deferred-trust feature, which seems to me to be the part of the design that really does the work?
