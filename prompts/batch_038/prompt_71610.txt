I have spent years arguing with Hume's essay on miracles on rationalist forums, and I would like to run the argument against a novel rather than against the usual apologetics. The novel is Yann Martel's Life of Pi. In its third part, Pi sits in a Mexican hospital bed with two Japanese shipping inspectors who have told him they want a plain account that will not surprise them, and he obliges with a second version of his 227 days adrift: no tiger, no hyena, no zebra, no orangutan — only a cook, a sailor, a mother, and himself, the animal roles mapping onto the human ones and the killings becoming considerably grimmer. He then asks the inspectors which of the two stories they prefer, observes that either way God remains with him, and the report they file records the first.

I want a reading of that ending through Hume's criterion — that testimony establishes a miracle only where the falsehood of the testimony would itself be more miraculous than the event reported — and through the second part of the essay, where Hume explains miracle-belief psychologically rather than evidentially. Three pressures I want applied.

First, does the criterion even reach this case? A tiger surviving seven months on a lifeboat is wildly improbable but transgresses no law of nature, and Hume is careful to separate the miraculous from the merely unusual; if the first story contains no violation at all, the ending is a straight contest of probabilities and testimony, and the standard framing of it as a miracle problem is a category error. Argue this either way, but do not assume it.

Second, a distinction I have been developing and want tested: the surplus element, meaning a detail in the wondrous account with no counterpart in the naturalistic reconstruction, which therefore cannot be absorbed as allegorical translation and has to be weighed on its own. The algae island with its meerkats, where Pi finds a human tooth at the heart of a fruit, is the paradigm case; the blind castaway who tries to murder him may be another, unless he maps onto the cook. Does a surplus raise or lower the credibility of the story containing it, and can you state the mechanism precisely rather than gesturing at it?

Third, this witness announces that he is offering a choice instead of asserting a fact. Hume's whole argument presumes testimony ordinarily aims at truth-conveyance; how much evidential weight survives the speaker's disclosure that it does not, and does Pi's stated preference — plus the frame narrator's discovery that adult Pi has married, fathered children, kept all three religions, and is plainly sane and agreeable — count for or against either story?

Then tell me where the Humean reading overreaches. Does the novel dramatize Hume's diagnosis of the love of wonder and make its reader complicit, or does it escape that diagnosis? Is Ang Lee's film adaptation more exposed, on the grounds that the spectacle is the entire pleasure? Is the ending a genuine option in James's sense — live, forced, momentous — or does it cheat by treating a belief that can simply be withheld as forced? And is the better story doing one job or sliding among three: more probable, more beautiful, more livable?

The deliverable is criticism with a thesis, not a survey. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of Hume, and do not bend Hume into a position he would not recognize in order to make the pairing tidier. Say explicitly what the novel shows about testimonial reasoning that argument alone cannot show, and where it resists the reading. No quotation from the novel, no bibliography, no citations; roughly 1,500 words.
