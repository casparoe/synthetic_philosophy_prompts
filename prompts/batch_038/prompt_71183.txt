I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about whether you have to be able to do otherwise, going case by case, and my verdicts are a mess. Here they are.

1. Chip case, dormant. A surgeon puts a device in Elias's head that will make him take the wallet off the counter if he shows any sign of deciding not to. Elias shows no such sign — he takes it because he wants the money — and the device stays off the whole time. I think he's exactly as blameworthy as he'd be with no device at all.

2. Chip case, fired. Same setup, but Elias pauses for maybe half a second with his hand hovering, and that pause trips the device, which takes over and completes the theft. He'd have taken the wallet anyway if the chip hadn't been there, he was that set on it. Even so, I dont think he's blameworthy for the theft here. The only difference from case 1 is a half-second pause that changed nothing about the outcome, and I can't explain why that pause gets to do all the work.

3. Elias's cousin Dana was raised in a group where disagreeing with the elders produced nausea and panic from the time she could walk. She says she agrees with them, wants what she does, and wouldnt have dissented in any case. She turns in a friend. This looks structuraly identical to case 1 to me — no alternatives, acting on her own endorsed reasons — but I feel much less like blaming her. I can't say why.

4. Two people, same addiction, same real ability to refuse. The one who takes it while hating himself for it: I blame him less. The one who takes it fully on board, no conflict, "this is just who I am": I blame him more. But the first guy had it harder. How can struggling and losing be better than not struggling?

5. My grandmother could no more steal than fly; the option isn't psychologically live for her. I want to say that makes her more praiseworthy than someone who's merely well behaved. But I also want to say the man in my building who's been sober eleven years and fights it every morning is more praiseworthy than someone who's never been tempted. Those two can't both be right and I keep saying both in the same conversation.

My half-formed rule is that blame and praise track what actually came out of you, not what you might have done — unless something got in the way that wasn't really yours. I can't say what "really yours" means, which is probably the whole problem.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely, tell me which case forced each clause, and run it on a case I didn't think of. If some of my verdicts have to go, say which ones and what it costs me to give them up. And tell me what your principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a friend who joined something genuinely awful at nineteen, wasn't coerced, believed it at the time, doesn't much regret it now, and says there was only one road out of that town and she was on it. Some days that sounds like an excuse that works, other days it just sounds like an explanation of why she did it.
