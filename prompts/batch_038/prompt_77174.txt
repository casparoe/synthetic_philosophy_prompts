Second year in an animal welfare science program, and the last eight months of my fieldwork has mostly been riding along with auditors — two marine salmon sites up north, and a whiteleg shrimp operation (hatchery plus grow-out ponds) down south. I keep circling the same thing about how these audits are structured and I can't articulate why it bugs me, so I'm hoping you can help me name it before I write the chapter.

Here's the protocol as I've watched it applied. Welfare gets scored at slaughter. For the fish, the auditor watches a sample come through the stunner and checks reflexes — loss of equilibrium, whether the eye still rotates when you turn the head, gill-cover movement, response to a touch — and records seconds until those stop, with a pass threshold somewhere under a minute. For the shrimp there's no equivalent requirement at all: harvest goes into chilled water, and the audit doesn't time it or score it. But the same shrimp scheme makes a headline commitment out of ablation-free broodstock — no removing the eyestalks of the female breeders to push egg production — and that does get checked and advertised. Meanwhile the biggest single number in the whole grow-out record is pre-harvest mortality, which in a bad cycle takes out a large chunk of the stocked seed, and it shows up in the paperwork as a production loss with no welfare threshold attached. Nobody blinks at 20% mortality; everybody blinks at an unstunned fish taking ninety seconds.

So what I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, not a verdict on whether the auditors are bad people. Which view of welfare, and of harm, does this pattern only make sense under? I can see a few candidate readings and can't choose between them: that welfare just is experience-at-a-moment so only the transition out of life is assessable; that harms have to be gratuitous to count, where killing an animal you're going to eat is non-gratuitous by stipulation but snipping a breeder's eyestalk off isn't; that death isn't a harm to the one who dies, so mortality is genuinely a production variable rather than a welfare one; or that nothing is going on but legibility, i.e. the scheme tracks what a consumer can be shown in a photograph and what an auditor can settle in under a minute. Sort out which of these the practice strictly presupposes versus which it merely sits comfortably with, and say where its underdetermined — where two or three incompatible theories would license exactly the same checklist. Also: is it internally consistent across the three asymmetries (fish versus shrimp, slaughter versus grow-out, broodstock versus food animal)? And what work is the sentience uncertainty doing — the same people who'll tell you decapod sentience isn't settled still treat ablation as needing justification while chill-killing doesn't, so why does the precaution bite on one and not the other?

Last thing, and this is the bit I actually need for the thesis. I've been toying with a QALY-style metric: welfare-adjusted days lived, each day weighted by how good or bad it probably went for that animal, with an explicit sentience-confidence discount. Is the existing audit practice even commensurable with that, or does it presuppose something that rules it out at the root? If we swapped it in, tell me what would have to change on the ground — stocking density, substrate, water quality, cause-of-death coding, who counts as inside the boundary of the scheme — as opposed to just getting one extra box on the form.
