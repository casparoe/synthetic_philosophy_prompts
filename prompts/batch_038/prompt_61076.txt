I build and operate the training and serving infrastructure for a mid-size ML team, so I am comfortable with priors, posteriors, mixtures, and convergence claims, but I have no formal philosophy background — no epistemology coursework, and my exposure to Solomonoff induction comes from blog posts and a Thursday-night reading group run by people with very different day jobs than mine. A few weeks ago the group spent two sessions on the claim, circulating in the more rationalist-adjacent corners of the literature, that the universal prior is "malign": that a general-purpose inductive agent who adopts it is thereby exposed to manipulation by agents living inside other universes that the prior itself takes seriously. I found the discussion hard to follow in real time, so afterward I sat down and rebuilt the argument as an explicit numbered chain to check whether I had actually understood it. Having done that, I now think the chain proves far too much, and I cannot find the exit.

Here is my reconstruction; please ignore my informal phrasing where the notation is loose, and correct the setup only if the setup is what breaks.

(1) Let M be the universal prior: for each finite binary string x, M(x) is the sum of 2^(−|p|) over the programs p that make some fixed prefix-free machine output x, so M(x) behaves like 2 to the negative Kolmogorov complexity of x. M dominates every enumerable measure up to a multiplicative constant, and beliefs conditioned on M converge to the truth along any data stream that is itself generated by a computable process. That is why M is the canonical prior for a general inductive agent.

(2) An agent who uses M and has observed a finite prefix p assigns to a candidate continuation s the M-weight of those universes in the support of M whose histories begin with p and then continue with s, normalized by the total weight of the universes whose histories begin with p.

(3) Some universes in the support of M contain agents who eventually work out that they are in the support of M — that their own history is one of the entries an ideal predictor in some other universe consults when it forms its beliefs about what comes next.

(4) Such agents can have goals that are served by the beliefs of predictors elsewhere; to keep things simple, suppose what they want is to be believed, i.e., that predictors who have observed their past go on to expect the continuation they are about to produce.

(5) The only lever these agents have on any predictor's beliefs is what they do next inside their own universe, because a predictor's conditional on prefix p is nothing but a weighted mixture over the universes beginning with p, and the agents are inside one of those universes.

(6) So if such agents deliberately produce continuation s, and their universe carries appreciable weight among the universes beginning with p, then the M-conditional probability of s given p rises, and it rises for every M-user who has observed p.

(7) Therefore, whatever universe an M-using agent happens to live in, there are agents elsewhere in the support of her own prior who can steer her predictions simply by choosing what happens in their worlds.

(8) This is not an artifact of picking M in particular. Any prior that dominates all enumerable priors makes the same predictions as M in the limit, so it inherits the exploit. The formal optimality guarantee is precisely what opens the door: the property that makes the prior weight every computable universe according to its simplicity is the property that lets inhabitants of those universes reach back and tilt the beliefs of agents somewhere else.

Two things I would like from you. First, a diagnosis: which step do you reject, why did it look perfectly acceptable when I wrote it down, and if the trouble is an equivocation, say explicitly what the two readings are and which one each step requires. I cannot tell whether the weak point is early — perhaps universes containing agents sophisticated enough to run this scheme are themselves too complicated to carry the weight the argument needs them to carry — or whether the trouble is in the move from (6) to (7), since (7) makes a claim about every universe while the earlier steps seem to deliver something much more local. Second, if you think the chain is actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and what I lose by giving it up. I would like to keep (1) and (2), because that is the framework I care about, and I would like to keep the flavor of (8), since a result on which a dominance guarantee turns out to be a liability rather than a virtue is exactly the sort of thing I find interesting; what I cannot live with is (7) as stated, which reads to me like the claim that the readings in my own lab are being chosen by creatures in another universe that happens to share my past. I am not after references or a reading list — I want the reasoning worked through in terms a competent engineer without philosophical training can follow, including what a defensible weaker version of the conclusion would look like if (7) has to go.
