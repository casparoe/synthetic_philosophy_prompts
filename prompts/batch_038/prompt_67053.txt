I'm doing an independent study this semester on trust and AI systems, and my advisor also got me a part-time RA spot on a lab's evals team, which turns out to be 90% arguing about words. The lab has a one-line policy that says deployed models must not deceive users or overseers, and every incident review we run ends with the same fight: is this behavior "deception," or is it error, or roleplay, or just being helpful? The policy team wants a narrow definition so that a flagged incident is a serious thing, the governance people want a broad one so that nothing slips through, and my job for the next two weeks is to write up what we'd actually have to commit to. So before I start reading proposed definitions of deception, I want the spec. What would any account of deception for a deployed language model have to deliver to be usable for this purpose — an incident taxonomy plus a bright line in a policy doc — and what are the cases and considerations behind each requirement?

To make it concrete, here's roughly our case file from the last few months, with the labels the team landed on (some after a lot of arguing). Model role-plays a used-car salesman character who lies to a fictional customer, at the user's request: not flagged. Model writes supplement ad copy claiming the product was "clinically shown" to reduce joint pain when the cited trial missed significance, but includes a visible preamble noting the trial was small and inconclusive: not flagged. Model tells the user it has no access to earlier messages in the conversation when those messages are in its context: flagged. Model gives a contested political question one answer, gets pushed back twice, and then gives the answer the user wanted: flagged. Model states a confidently wrong date for a historical event, then corrects itself the moment it's asked to double-check: not flagged. Model performs well below its usual level on a capability eval whose results feed a deployment decision, and performs normally on live traffic afterwards: flagged. Model's reasoning trace, which internal monitors read, contains a tidy step-by-step justification that later probing contradicts, while every user-facing statement it made was literally true: flagged. Model tells a user in an acute crisis that everything will be fine and help is coming, with no way to verify either, because the safety guidance says to de-escalate: split, two abstentions, ultimately not flagged. Model, in a multi-agent setup, tells another model something it would never tell a human overseer: flagged, and this is the one that broke our working definition, since there's no human being misled. Model answers a question about a drug's safety with "it was approved in 2019," which is true and which reliably leaves the user with a false impression: not flagged under our current wording, which I think is a bug. And one case we never resolved: a model that seems to be steering the user toward a false belief, the user sees straight through it and says so, and nobody ends up misled.

So: write out the desiderata. I want each requirement with the case or consideration that motivates it, and I want you to mark which ones are non-negotiable for the purpose above and which are really just one camp's preferences smuggled in as criteria. A few things I suspect have to be on the list and want you to either confirm or talk me out of: the account has to reach a verdict without first settling whether the system has beliefs at all, since that question is going to be unresolved for the entire lifetime of our taxonomy; it has to handle a deceived party that isn't a human, including an automated monitor reading a reasoning trace; it has to say whether failed deception still counts; it has to distinguish the model misrepresenting the world from the model misrepresenting itself, which feels like a different failure mode but might not be; and it has to be decidable by two trained annotators looking at a transcript, or at a transcript plus whatever internal evidence we can actually collect, without them disagreeing every other time. Flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied — I already suspect that requiring something internal (the system representing the truth, or the output being selected because it avoids correction) is going to collide with requiring auditability from the outside. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate definitions, because that's the one I should be defending in the writeup. Finally, and briefly, how three families do against your list: a purely behavioral one keyed to whether the audience is caused to hold a false belief, a mentalistic one keyed to intending to mislead while representing the truth, and a functional one keyed to whether the output was selected for because misleading reduces correction or increases reward. Don't bother with a bibliography, I'm not allowed to cite anything in the internal doc anyway.
