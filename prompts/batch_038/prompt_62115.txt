I am the senior analyst in the investment appraisal unit of a national treasury board — a mid-sized state whose specifics I'd rather you not try to pin down, since I want the reasoning, not a jurisdiction. We complete roughly thirty-five appraisals a year, everything from port expansions to flood defences to disease eradication programmes, and our standing guidance instructs us to discount all future costs and benefits at a single constant real rate of four per cent, with no exceptions, no horizon-dependent schedule, and no separate treatment of non-market effects. Two recent files have exposed how badly that rule behaves at the long end: a deep geological repository for intermediate-level waste whose radiological hazard profile runs to ten thousand years, and a coastal peatland restoration whose carbon-storage and storm-surge benefits accrue slowly across three centuries. Both looked defensible on every physical and engineering measure we could compute, and both then failed on present value almost entirely because of the discount rate. A parliamentary committee has asked our board whether the four per cent rule should be amended, and each appraisal we produce has to end in a single present-value figure plus a two-page written justification that the finance minister signs. My colleague in the unit argues that it should not be amended: his position is that once analysts are permitted horizon-dependent rates, every one of them will back into whatever rate makes their preferred project pass, and that motivated reasoning with exponentials is worse than a crude constant. I take that objection far more seriously than the literature usually does, and I would like a procedure that answers it head-on rather than assuming it away.

What I need from you is a decision procedure that an analyst with a spreadsheet and no economics training beyond our two-day induction course could actually run on any proposal in half a day: the questions to ask, in order, with a stated threshold or sufficient answer for each; explicit stopping points at which the analyst simply uses the default four per cent and moves on; and a rule for how to combine a time-varying discount factor into the single number our spreadsheet will accept. It must cover, at minimum: how the rate is decomposed into pure time preference, the elasticity of marginal utility, and expected per-capita growth, and where each of those three inputs comes from for a given file; how to treat genuine uncertainty about future growth rather than a point estimate, including what that averaging does to the effective rate over long horizons and what it does not license; how to handle benefits that are non-market and expected to become relatively scarcer, so that the procedure does not quietly double-count the same pessimism in both the rate and the quantity; whether a hazard that itself declines over time should be handled by discounting, by explicit probabilities, or both; and how to deal with irreversibility and the option value of waiting. I also want a short, separate list of escalation triggers: the points at which the remaining disagreement is ethical rather than technical and the analyst is required to stop and hand the file to the board instead of resolving it with arithmetic.

Then run the procedure on three cases. The first is the repository: nine billion in near-term construction and emplacement cost, against a small-probability, very large-consequence release pathway whose likelihood falls away only over millennia. The second is the peatland: two hundred million up front and modest maintenance thereafter, against carbon storage and flood defence whose annual value rises steadily for three hundred years before plateauing. The third is a national antiviral and vaccine-platform stockpile with a fifty-year planning horizon and large replenishment costs every decade. For at least one of these I expect the procedure to return a verdict the parliamentary committee will not welcome — state it plainly rather than hedging it, and tell me which of the three ultimately turns on a value judgment that no amount of technical apparatus can settle, and what exactly the minister would have to decide in that case. Throughout, be candid about which steps of your own procedure are judgment calls wearing the costume of computation. I do not want a literature review, a bibliography, or named authorities to chase down; I want something usable by a person who will never read the underlying debate.
