I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in metaphysics, and I have been invited to contribute a piece of fiction to an anthology our department is assembling on the theme of ordinary objects. I would like you to write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words that dramatizes the special composition question. I should be precise about what I mean, because the genre is full of stories that re-stage the Ship of Theseus with a melancholy robot, and that is exactly the version I cannot use: the question in my story must be whether there is one thing there at all, never whether it is the same thing it used to be.

The premise is my own invention, and I would ask that you build on it rather than substituting a more familiar one.

A deep-space salvage outfit works a device it calls a keeper. Clamped onto a dispersed field of debris, the keeper holds the fragments in tensioned adjacency: nothing touches anything else, but every piece is continuously corrected so that it maintains a fixed distance and orientation relative to its neighbours, and the array as a whole moves when any one of its members is pushed. The company's legal code, inherited from an Earth jurisdiction and never properly revised, provides that engaging a keeper "brings a vessel into being" and that disengaging one "destroys" it — with consequences for taxation, inheritance, and, in at least one contested precedent, for homicide.

Your story should concern a crew that has kept one such array bound for eleven years, and a decision about it — to bind, to release, or to swap out a failing member — that the code cannot describe coherently.

Constraints, in rough order of importance:

1. No character may articulate a metaphysical position as a position. Nobody uses the word "composition," nobody wonders aloud when many things become one thing, nobody has encountered the literature. Each character's view must be legible only in what they do with and to the array: they salute it, they invoice for it, they decline to sign for it, they grieve a component and are embarrassed about it afterwards.

2. Between them, the crew should embody exactly three candidate answers to the question — for instance, that the fastening is what matters; that what matters is a continuous coordinated response to force; that nothing composes anything except living things, so the code is a fiction everyone is paid to sustain; or that there is no answer to give, the array simply is a whole, and no reason can be produced for why. Choose three, and make at least one of them genuinely attractive rather than a straw position.

3. Include a scene in which someone asks, in complete sincerity, what would have to be added to the array for it to stop being many pieces and start being one vessel. The answer offered must be concrete and physical — a cable, a shared signal, a common history of impacts, a shared injury — and it must visibly fail to satisfy the person asking.

4. Narrate from the point of view of someone whose work makes the question unavoidable and whose vocabulary does not extend to it: a keeper technician, a claims assessor, a cargo priest, an apprentice. That narrator should be entirely reliable about the machinery and quietly unreliable about everything else.

5. Coin the institutional vocabulary yourself and use it consistently — the particular clause of the code being invoked, the trade name for the failure mode when a fragment drifts out of tolerance, whatever the crew calls an array that has been bound too long. Do not gloss these terms.

6. No revelation in the final paragraph, no gesture toward mystery, no sentence that could be quoted on the anthology's back cover. End on something mundane and faintly annoying.

Please do not name any philosopher, and do not append a note explaining how the story maps onto the various positions. I would rather the mapping stay invisible; I can do it myself.

Above all, what I care about is the prose. The constraints are there to keep the story honest, not to turn it into a puzzle.
