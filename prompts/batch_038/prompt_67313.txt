I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm drafting a piece about the argument over whether off-world settlement should be treated primarily as an insurance policy against human extinction. Last month I sat in on a closed session at a space-policy conference where two of the participants got into a sustained exchange: a reliability engineer who has spent her career designing redundant systems for spacecraft, and a political philosopher who works on the long-term future. I transcribed it as closely as I could, and I want an adjudication before any of it goes in print, because I don't want to reproduce a point that merely sounded good in the room.

Here is the exchange, move by move.

1. The engineer opened: "The real case for a self-sustaining colony has nothing to do with inspiration or minerals. It's that humanity is currently a single point of failure. A second, independent population is a backup, and backups are worth buying when what they protect is everything. Nothing else you could do with the same money hedges a total loss."

2. The philosopher: "That's an engineering intuition imported without its enabling assumption. Redundancy adds reliability only when failures are independent. In your own field you'd ask for the beta factor — the share of a component's failures attributable to causes shared with the rest of its redundant group — and when that share is high, two copies are one copy counted twice. The catastrophes worth worrying about at civilizational scale are precisely the ones that propagate: an engineered pathogen, a runaway artificial intelligence, an institutional or ideological collapse. The colony would be staffed by people from this civilization, running its software, reading its papers, and staying in communication with it. So the correlation approaches one in exactly the cases that matter. The only threats a colony genuinely hedges are the uncorrelated local ones — a large impact, a supervolcano — and those already carry very low annual probabilities."

3. The engineer: "Correlated redundancy is still redundancy, because it changes the shape of the failure rather than only its probability. A threat that reaches both worlds doesn't reach them simultaneously or with equal force: the distance, the different environment and the different resource base mean the colony is hit later and less hard. That converts a hard failure into a soft one, and the gap between a bottleneck and an extinction is the whole gap. Also, you quietly shrank the case — for the local, uncorrelated catastrophes you just dismissed as improbable, a colony is about as close to perfect reliability as anyone will ever get."

4. The philosopher: "Two problems. First, 'later and less hard' assumes the threat travels as a signal; if the threat is a capability, the colony has the capability from the same knowledge base, and you have merely arranged two simultaneous failures on different clocks. Second, and worse, your argument proves too much. If the value of the backup scales with the magnitude of what is lost, the same logic says we should pour resources into building backups rather than into making the primary safe, since hedging beats prevention whenever prevention is less than certain. That is an allocation policy nobody would actually defend, which tells me a premise is wrong."

5. The engineer: "It's the ordinary portfolio result, not an absurdity. You diversify in proportion to how unreliable your prevention is; if prevention were certain you wouldn't need the backup. And there's an asymmetry you've skipped. A separate settlement also hedges the failure of Earth's own prevention institutions — a captured regulator, a race dynamic, a political movement that switches the safeguards off. That is a threat correlated with institutions, and a physically and politically separate population is insulated from it in a way no amount of spending at home can achieve."

6. The philosopher: "You've changed the target. A colony insulated from Earth's institutions is not insulated from Earth's knowledge, and the failure mode you now need protection from crosses the distance as information, which no government on Mars can filter. Meanwhile the founding population is not a neutral archive of better values. It is a selection effect: the people who volunteer for a high-mortality frontier are not a representative sample, and the conditions their descendants face will reward hard choices about who gets oxygen. So the colony plausibly hedges institutional failure on Earth while creating a fresh instance of value lock-in — freezing the outlook of a small, unrepresentative and possibly hardened founding group into the deep future. That is what I would most want to avoid in any long-run scenario."

7. The engineer's reply, which the moderator cut short: "Which is an argument for choosing the founders carefully and keeping the colony dependent on Earth for as long as possible — and if that's your position, you're describing a backup you can control, which is what I've been arguing for all along."

What I need from you:

(a) Take moves 1 through 7 in order and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per move. The rating should track the argument actually made at that step, not the general plausibility of its conclusion.

(b) For each side, separate the claims that are conceptual — right or wrong in virtue of how the reasoning works — from the claims that are empirical and hostage to contested quantities: the strength of the correlation, annual catastrophe rates, how long a founding population could remain self-sustaining. Where a move depends on an empirical premise neither of them defended, say so, and say which way the move would cut at the high end and at the low end of that premise.

(c) Tell me what both of them are missing. I noticed that neither said what self-sustaining is supposed to mean, and neither asked whether the correlation the philosopher objects to could be deliberately engineered down rather than merely measured — but I want your list, not mine, and I want to know which missing consideration would most change the verdict if it were supplied.

(d) Finally: who is more right overall, and is there a version of the engineer's insurance case that survives the philosopher's objections intact? If there is, state it in a sentence or two precise enough that I could print it without either of them objecting.

I should be clear about what I'm not asking for. No bibliography, and no list of who has argued what — I can't verify any of that from where I'm sitting, and names would only tempt me into attributing views to people who may not hold them. I want the reasoning: which moves hold up under pressure and which merely sound decisive in a conference room.
