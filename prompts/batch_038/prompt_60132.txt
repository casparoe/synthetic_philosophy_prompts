Our company's lab techs went on strike three weeks ago — about 40 of them, seperate bargaining unit from engineering. Management sent an email to my org asking for volunteers to cover the techs' on-call escalation queue while they're out. Two people on my team got into it about this in the channel and then again at lunch, and somehow I got picked to be the tiebreaker. For what it's worth I dont have a stake: different rotation, wasn't asked to sign up, not in the unit, not a union member, and neither of them are. I just want to know who's actually right. Here's the exchange as close to in-order as I can reconstruct it.

1. Dana: volunteering is strike-breaking and it's wrong, full stop. A strike only means anything if the work actually stops. If anyone willing to do the job can just walk in, then a "right to strike" is a right to quit with extra steps, and we already had that. So the right to withdraw your labor has to carry with it some right to make the withdrawal stick, and that's what honoring a picket line is.

2. Marcus: that proves way too much. If "making it stick" is the source of the legitimacy, then a human chain across the loading dock is strictly more legitimate than a polite sign, and Dana agrees that's assault. The strikers have rights over their own labor and nothing else. Me accepting a shift is my labor, not theirs.

3. Dana: your principle is symmetric on paper and totally asymmetric in practice. The company has a balance sheet to outlast the strike fund, lawyers, and an endless supply of people who need work. The techs have exactly one lever. A rule saying nobody may apply pressure beyond their own person only ever binds the side with no other assets.

4. Marcus: fine, but the queue I'd be covering isn't leverage, it's the safety escalations — instrument failures, that sort of thing. Covering those doesn't weaken their position on wages, it stops a third party from getting hurt, and that third party isn't at the table and never chose to be.

5. Dana: and who made those escalations load-bearing on the people they're underpaying? The company did, on purpose, by not staffing redundancy. Any essential-work exception you grant is an exception whose size the employer picks, and they will pick it to be the whole job.

6. Marcus: the union answered this one themselves. Their rep emailed and said they have no objection to engineers covering genuine safety escalations, only to us taking over the routine backlog. So the size of the exception was set by the strikers, not the company. Which is your objection in 5, resolved.

7. Dana: that's my point, not my objection. The party that owns the labor gets to say how far the withdrawal goes. But notice that's a different principle from the one you opened with in 2, where individuals have rights only over their own labor and everyone else's is up for grabs. Under 2, the union rep's permission is worth nothing, because it isn't theirs to give.

At which point Marcus said something like "so we agree," and Dana said no, because he's only happy with the carve-out because it happens to land exactly where he wanted to volunteer anyway, and he'd have argued the opposite if the rep had said no. Neither budged after that.

Can you go through move by move and rate each one — who's right, who's wrong, who's changing the subject — and then tell me whether 7 actually settles the disagreement or just moves it somewhere else? I'm not after a both-sides shrug. I'd like to know if either of their principles survives the exchange intact, and if neither does, what the thing is that would actually decide whether covering that queue is permissible.
