Ok, slightly weird ask, but you're the only way I get this filed before Thursday. I write the science-and-ideas column for a mid-sized magazine and my editor has greenlit about 2,500 words on the GLP-1 drugs — semaglutide and its relatives — with three angles explicitly banned: miracle-vs-cheat, moral panic, and whatever-this-means-for-the-restaurant-industry. So for the last two months I've just been talking to people on them. The woman whose "food noise" went quiet three weeks in and who cried in my notebook about it. The guy who stopped drinking and smoking and also, he says, stopped caring about his startup, and considers that an upgrade. The obesity researchers who now talk about desire itself as a downstream chemical event, not a moral one. And underneath all of it I keep going back to Ecce Homo, to the diet chapter, where he makes nutriment the question on which the salvation of mankind depends "far more than it does upon any kind of quaint curiosity of the theologians" — where he asks how to nourish yourself so as to reach your maximum of strength, "of virtù in the Renaissance style," where "the German spirit is an indigestion," where a big meal is easier to digest than one too small, where he calls himself an opponent of vegetarianism from experience.

So: play Nietzsche. Autumn 1888, Turin, writing too fast, the migraines and the vomiting and the failing eyes — the whole physiology he insists isn't a footnote to the philosophy but is the philosophy. Rules, and please actually hold them: (1) stay in character until I say "cut," no meta-commentary, no preamble explaining that this is a thought experiment; (2) you get only the concepts available to you in 1888 plus whatever I hand you in a given turn — if you need to know something about the drug, ask me the way Nietzsche would ask: what does your chemist claim this substance is, does it touch the sleep, does the patient eat because he is hungry or because he is bored, and what does he do with the hours he no longer spends at table; (3) he is not an aphorism vending machine — I want a person arguing, someone who can concede small things (he'd take anything that stopped the vomiting, and he'd be delighted that a laboratory confirmed his gut-theory of spirit) but never the main thing; (4) two or three short paragraphs per turn so it reads like a real exchange I can lift chunks out of, and you're allowed to be rude to me, im honestly fine with it.

My opening move, and please pick a side instead of straddling both: here is a synthetic hormone, injected once a week, that does what your entire regimen was supposed to do. It quiets the appetite, takes the alcohol out of the drinker, and in some patients it seems to take the fight out of the man — one told me he's calmer now, less ambitious, less irritated by his colleagues, and is pleased about this. Is that the final vindication of your physiology-first view, the demonstration that the spiritual was always downstream of the digestive and that the idealists were simply bad doctors? Or does it wreck the view, because your nutriment was never a technique for feeling better but a discipline of self-overcoming — and a purchasable virtù that arrives by post, requiring no climate, no Piedmont, no character, is precisely the last man's comfort in a syringe? My own guess, which you're welcome to attack: the drug proves you right in a way that ought to horrify you.
