I serve on the nine-member Competence Allocation Panel of our federation's second chamber, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance of our next sitting. The panel's mandate is narrow but consequential: when the constituent states and the federal government disagree about where an authority belongs, we recommend an allocation, and the plenary votes on it. Our constitution contains two clauses that pull against one another — a subsidiarity clause, under which authority rests at the lowest level capable of discharging it effectively, and a parity clause, under which the federation must secure roughly equivalent conditions of life across its territory. Most of our disputes are really about which clause governs.

The case before us concerns long-term residential care for the elderly. At present the competence is shared: federal framework standards fix minimum staffing ratios and the inspection regime, the states administer and co-finance, and municipalities deliver or contract out the service. Two states have petitioned for full authority over standard-setting and financing. Those two are also the ones whose facilities fared worst in the most recent federal audit cycle — 31 and 27 percent of residents in institutions rated inadequate, against a federation-wide figure of 12. The state with the best record, at 4 percent, opposes devolution, arguing that a federal floor becomes a ceiling: once states set their own ratios, pressure to lower the floor to what a majority of states can afford will make its own higher standard look like an unaffordable advantage open to challenge. The treasury puts the fiscal transfer at about 1.9 percent of state GDP, and an independent expert commission has found that a stand-alone inspectorate is viable only above roughly 700,000 inhabitants; three of our nine states fall below that line, at 340,000, 510,000 and 690,000. Meanwhile about one recipient in four moves to a different state within five years of admission, usually to be nearer family, and their entitlements are portable today precisely because the standards are federal.

Our standing rules require that any recommendation treat three things separately rather than as one switch: who provides the service, who raises and bears the money, and who sets the norms and inspects compliance. It must also name the smallest level competent for each, and include a reversal clause stating the measurable conditions on which the allocation reverts. Adoption requires a two-thirds majority, and the panel is currently split on whether the petitioning states' poor outcomes count against them — as evidence of incapacity — or for them, as evidence that uniform standards have failed where they were most needed.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend devolution, retention, or a specific conditional allocation; state expressly which justification of subsidiarity it rests on, the epistemic one (local knowledge, variation as a source of learning) or the political one (self-government as a good in itself, and the protection of distinct ways of life), and indicate how the recommendation would have to change if it rested on the other; address the parity clause objection at its strongest rather than as a footnote; supply a short test the panel could reuse on the next allocation question; and say what evidence from the next two audit cycles would show the recommendation to have been wrong. Conclude with a brief view on whether the European Union's subsidiarity control mechanism — national parliaments issuing reasoned opinions within eight weeks, with a third of them compelling the Commission to review — is a workable model for policing an allocation of this kind, or too weak to transplant. Roughly 1,200 words, with headings, written for members who will disagree; please make it self-contained rather than relying on references to literature.
