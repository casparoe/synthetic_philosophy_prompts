Bit of an odd one, this. I'm a distributed systems engineer, twelve years in, mostly consensus protocols, gossip, service discovery — the general misery of getting a load of nodes to agree on something when nobody's in charge. Last year our team got dragged into a nine-month fight over wire formats: two of our services had independently settled on incompatible schemas, everyone agreed one of them had to go, nobody could agree which, and in the end the question just sort of evaporated. One accumulated more services depending on it, the other quietly died, and no decision was ever actually made — which is the part that still nags at me. A mate told me this was exactly what Lewis's *Convention* is about and I've now been through the first couple of chapters twice on the train without being confident I've got it.

So what I want is a translation rather than a summary. Take Lewis's account of what a convention is — the regularity in a coordination problem, the common knowledge, the preference for conformity that's conditional on everyone else conforming, the claim that the regularity is arbitrary because some alternative would have served just as well, the role of salience or focal points, and the seperate business he does with precedent — and restate the whole apparatus in the vocabulary I actually work in: converged states, quorums, gossip and its convergence guarantees, backwards compatibility, deprecation paths, switching costs, tie-breaking rules, leader election, crash versus Byzantine failure. Then do the same, more briefly, for the people who came after him and rebuilt conventions on learning dynamics and repeated interaction instead of common knowledge (the Skyrms-style stag hunt line, the evolutionary accounts of how a population settles on one equilibrium), and for the norms-with-normative-expectations accounts that add something Lewis deliberately leaves out. As faithful as you can manage, and honest about where you're straining.

The residue report is the bit I actually care about, so weight it heavily: for each piece, does it map cleanly, does it only map if you distort it, or does it have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — and in each case is the gap a defect in how engineers talk or a sign that the philosophical claim wasn't as clear as it looked. Two specific things. My hunch is that common knowledge is doing less work than Lewis thinks, that it's basically quorum-plus-gossip-convergence, bootstrapped by the same mechanism as everything else in my day job; if that's right say so, and if it's wrong tell me what common knowledge carries that convergence doesn't. And the arbitrariness claim looks obviously false from where I sit, because which schema wins has massive distributional consequences for whoever has to migrate — so either Lewis means something narrower by arbitrary than I'm assuming or that's a real hole. Please don't smuggle my framework's assumptions in and dress them up as translation: if you render common knowledge as 'all nodes have acked' you've made a substantive claim, not a gloss, and I'd rather you flagged it as such.
