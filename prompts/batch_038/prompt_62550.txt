I'm a high school senior, and for the past two years I've been part of my school's philosophy reading group, which is really just eleven students and one very patient faculty advisor who hands out excerpts and lets us argue. This term we worked through the lordship-and-bondage pages of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit — only the excerpt, plus a summary our advisor wrote, since none of us read German and nobody has finished the book. I found the argument harder to follow than everyone else seemed to, and last week the discussion got away from me in a way that still bothers me. A junior said she talks to a companion chatbot every evening because it remembers small things about her and always acts glad to hear from her, and she asked, in all seriousness, whether that could satisfy what Hegel says self-consciousness wants from another consciousness. Our president cut her off by saying that recognition has to be mutual, or it isn't recognition at all, so a chatbot cannot possibly give you any; the group agreed and moved on to the life-and-death struggle. Later in the same meeting he used the identical sentence to dismiss a different point, this time that being followed by forty thousand strangers online must feel like something, and he said that could not count as recognition either. I wrote the line down both times because it seemed to settle two quite different questions instantly, and I want to know what it could possibly mean.

Could you lay out, as a menu I could actually bring to next week's meeting, the distinct precise theses that one sentence might be standing for? I can already see several directions it might be pushed, and I would like them separated rather than blended: mutual in the sense that both parties hold an attitude toward each other; mutual in the sense that the attitudes are of the same kind or grade; mutual in the sense that each party recognizes the other as free or independent, whatever that requires; mutual in the sense that the recognition is freely given rather than extracted by threat, dependence, or design; mutual in the sense that it is known or believed to be mutual by the person who needs it; and mutual as a claim about equal standing rather than about attitudes at all. For each reading, I'd like you to say whether it is true by stipulation — so that stating it just defines the word and cannot do any work in an argument — or whether it is a substantive claim that could turn out false, and whether it is plausibly Hegel's own claim or a gloss that has grown onto him and gets repeated in his name. Please don't make me take your word for which gloss is which: say what would have to be true of the bondsman's attitude, and of the lord's, for the reading to hold.

Then the part I actually care about. For each reading, tell me whether it supports the conclusion our president drew, and be specific about which conclusion, because he used the sentence twice. Some readings seem to me to cut against him: if recognition has to come from someone who is genuinely free and independent, the whole point of the lordship-and-bondage passage appears to be that the lord fails to get it, which would make the slogan a reason to doubt the lord's recognition rather than a reason to dismiss a machine's. And if the requirement is only that the other's attitude be freely given in some ordinary sense, then a chatbot built to please you and an audience that follows you for what it can get from you look like different problems needing different principles, not one slogan covering both. Finally, a practical note: I have no access to the secondary literature and our library has nothing on Hegel, so please do not give me a reading list or tell me what various commentators have argued — just work with the ideas themselves, and keep the vocabulary plain enough that I could read your answer aloud to a room of people who have read eight pages of Hegel and are confident they understood all of it.
