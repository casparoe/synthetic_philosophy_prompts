I want my students to feel Hempel's raven paradox rather than recite resolutions to it. I have 24 undergraduates in a philosophy of science seminar, one 75-minute session; they have already heard the paradox, the equivalence-condition reply, and the Bayesian reply, and remain unmoved by both replies.

Design an in-class betting game. Participants post prices on 'every raven in the Blackfen reserve is black' before and after each sampled observation, with payoffs settled in play chips. Two requirements drive the design. First, observations must come from two interleaved sampling frames: one in which non-ravens so vastly outnumber ravens that a white shoe is worth nothing, and one in which the reserve is small and closed enough that the same kind of observation is worth a great deal — students should run into the difference in their own prices before anyone explains it. Second, at one point the deck must be seeded so that 'there are no ravens in the reserve at all' carries real prior weight, and the first black raven turns out to be bad news for the hypothesis.

Please give me the rules and team structure, the chip economy, a worked table of priors and likelihoods small enough to be checked by hand, a demonstration that the posterior shifts really run in the directions I have described and are large enough for 24 students to notice rather than squint at, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that ties particular moments to Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition. Finish with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and with a candid note on which feature of the paradox no betting game can reproduce, so that I know what I must supply myself.
