I am preparing a session for a research seminar on the history of philosophy of science, and I should like to set my students an exercise in disciplined reconstruction. It concerns Henry Sidgwick — Knightbridge Professor of Moral Philosophy at Cambridge, a founder and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, who died in August 1900 — and it asks how he would respond to a body of evidence that did not exist in his lifetime.

Suppose Sidgwick were presented, today, with the following five cases, together with the surrounding methodological argument in each:

1. Daryl Bem's 2011 paper in a leading journal of personality and social psychology reporting nine experiments that appeared to show effects of future, not-yet-presented stimuli on present behaviour, the failed direct replications that followed within months, and the subsequent meta-analytic claim that the effect survives once the failed replications are pooled in.
2. The Ganzfeld protocol: decades of sessions in which a receiver, under mild sensory deprivation, attempts to identify material being viewed by a distant sender, forced-choice scored against matched decoys; the running meta-analytic exchange between proponents and sceptics; the 1996 joint statement on a new protocol; and the replication attempts conducted under that protocol.
3. The Princeton Global Consciousness Project's claim that a network of continuously running random number generators deviates from chance around the time of major world events, and the sceptical objection that, once the event windows and the test statistics are chosen after the fact, nothing remains that could have counted against the hypothesis in advance.
4. The AWARE study of cardiac-arrest survivors: reports of veridical perception during clinical death, one widely discussed case in which a patient's account of a period of awareness was checked against the hospital record, and the concealed visual targets placed on high shelves in resuscitation bays, which no patient was reported to have seen.
5. The 2023 paper, published in a flagship journal, reporting a quantum-like effect in plant microtubules, its retraction a year later, and the allegations that the randomisation of conditions had failed — an episode in which the critics were not professional sceptics but members of the same research community.

I should also like the reconstruction to engage with the wider setting: the replication crisis in psychology; pre-registration and registered reports; the migration from significance testing toward Bayesian reasoning and effect sizes; and the awkward fact that parapsychologists were among the first to adopt some of these reforms, and among those whose results the reforms most conspicuously did not rescue.

Sidgwick's own working commitments should be brought to bear on all of this, insofar as they can be reconstructed from his practice and reported views rather than from his prose: his insistence on first-hand witnesses and his impatience with hearsay amplified in retelling; his defence of the evidential value of large numbers of individually trivial reports, against the objection that such a census merely multiplies the opportunities for error; his refusal to dismiss a phenomenon in advance as impossible; his readiness to sit through fraudulent mediumship and say so publicly; and his hope — widely reported, and important here — that a scientifically respectable demonstration of survival might supply some part of the evidential foundation that scriptural criticism had removed from religion. Students should consider whether that last commitment would incline him toward credulity or, on the contrary, toward a suspicion of his own hopes that he would demand of others.

The essay I want should reach conclusions and defend them, not survey. Specifically: for each of the five cases, what verdict would Sidgwick actually return, and on what grounds drawn from his own methodological commitments rather than from ours? Where would he sit with respect to the methodological reformers — as an ancestor of pre-registration, or as someone who would find it merely a new name for an old discipline? Would he now grant that some psi effect has been demonstrated at all? And is the century-long impasse in this field, on his reckoning, a dispute about evidence or a dispute about what would count as evidence? Where I expect the exercise to bite is in identifying the places where Sidgwick would part company with present-day scientific consensus in either direction: cases in which he would be markedly less sceptical than a contemporary psychologist, and cases in which he would be markedly more so, together with an explanation of why the direction differs.
