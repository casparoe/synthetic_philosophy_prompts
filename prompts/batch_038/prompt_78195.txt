I have spent the better part of a week in a forum thread defending Hume's essay on suicide — the one he kept out of print during his lifetime — and the exchange has produced a reconstruction that now alarms me, because I cannot locate the defective step. It runs as follows:

(1) Hume's answer to the charge of usurping the Almighty's office is that nothing occurs outside the general laws by which providence governs matter and motion: a person's own act in ending their life is an event in nature like a fever or a falling tile, and if altering the course of nature were presumptuous in that instance it would be presumptuous in every exercise of human industry.

(2) If every event falls under those laws, then no human being is the ultimate source of any event; every death, whoever brings it about, is equally a disposition of providence.

(3) Blame and impermissibility attach only where the agent is the ultimate source of what occurs; absent that, the act is no more the agent's than the tile's.

(4) Therefore no killing is blameworthy or impermissible — not the assassin's, not the intoxicated driver's, not the magistrate's.

(5) Hume denies this. The essay retains duties to one's neighbour, and his moral theory makes vice and virtue turn on qualities of mind to which an observer's sentiments respond.

(6) So (1) cannot be the premise carrying the essay's first argument: either that argument is unsound or the moral theory is false.

(7) Hume defends the necessity of human actions at length elsewhere, so he cannot disown (1) and (2) without paying twice.

(8) Hence the essay's central argument collapses on Hume's own commitments.

I am not prepared to give up (5); ordinary blame does too much work in my own thinking. Which step do you reject, why did it look unobjectionable at the point where it entered, and what is the price of the rejection? For each candidate repair, say whether the essay's conclusion still stands and what Hume is left with if it does not.
