For thirty-one years I worked on the instrumentation side of life detection: first in a national laboratory, developing assays for organic and isotopic biosignatures, and later on several mission-definition panels, where the awkward task fell to us of writing down, for engineers and programme officers, what a mission was actually supposed to look for. I retired eighteen months ago and have since been reading the philosophy of biology more carefully than I once thought it warranted. I have now written out the position I have arrived at, and before I circulate it to a small group of former colleagues — two of whom are philosophers — I should like an unsentimental audit of it.

My commitments are these.

1. I am an anti-essentialist about life. There is no natural kind answering to the word; the term picks out a cluster of capacities, and living systems shade into non-living ones by degrees. I want to be precise about what I am claiming: I take that gradation to be a feature of the world and not merely a measure of our present ignorance. Better instruments would not remove it.

2. When the panels pressed me for a working criterion, I defended the one I still think best, and I hold it as a biconditional: a system is alive if and only if it is capable of open-ended Darwinian evolution — heritable variation, differential replication, and no built-in ceiling on the complexity the process can generate.

3. The criterion must be substrate-neutral. Were we to encounter a population of self-replicating machines, or of programs running in some simulation, exhibiting heritable variation under selection with no ceiling, I would call it alive. I have consistently refused to write chemistry or thermodynamics into the definition, on the grounds that those are merely facts about the particular sample of life we happen to have.

4. Aliveness is nonetheless a property of individual organisms here and now. A mule is alive. A dormant seed that will never germinate is alive. The last surviving member of a species is alive. This is no marginal concession on my part: the practice I supervised for three decades consisted of assaying individual samples for individual living organisms, and it presupposed throughout that the question whether this particular one is alive has an answer.

5. I am a scientific realist about the underlying matter. There is a determinate fact as to whether the ocean beneath the ice on Europa contains life; that fact is a fact about the world rather than about our concepts; and sufficiently good instrumentation would in principle disclose it.

6. Viruses are not alive — not because they fail to evolve, which they plainly do, but because they cannot evolve except as passengers within cellular systems that are themselves evolving; they are components of living systems rather than living systems. At the same time I hold that no account of life is adequate unless it assigns them a central place, since they have shaped the evolution of every cellular lineage on this planet.

7. I distrust definitions constructed from intuitions about imaginary cases. A definition must be answerable to how life actually is, and the only life we know is the one in front of us. Much of the recent literature strikes me as unmoored for exactly this reason.

8. Where a definition and my considered verdicts about particular cases come apart, I revise the definition. I have never accepted that cases should be sacrificed to a theory.

Here is what I should like. First, an ordered list of the genuine tensions, most severe first, with a sentence or two on why each is a real inconsistency rather than a difference of emphasis, and on what would have to be given up to remove it. Second, and equally important, an identification of the pairs that only appear to conflict, with an explanation of why they dissolve once stated carefully; I would rather be told that two of my commitments are unremarkable than have everything flagged. Third, the cheapest single revision that would render the set jointly tenable, together with an honest account of what that revision costs me, since I expect the cheap fix will require me to abandon something I have been defending for thirty years. I do not know where the worst of it lies, and I have deliberately not indicated which of the eight I would be most reluctant to surrender — part of what I want from you is a view on that question. Please treat the list as my own position and trace what commits me to what, rather than surveying the general problem of defining life.
