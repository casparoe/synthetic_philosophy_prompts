I'm three weeks into the Scottish Enlightenment unit of my history of econ thought seminar and we just did Hume's political essays — Of Money, Of Interest, Of the Balance of Trade, Of the Jealousy of Trade. A guy in my section built this argument in study group, I walked straight into it, and I cant find the way back out. My paper is due in nine days so I need to know whether to build on it or bury it.

(1) For Hume, money isn't itself a part of commerce; it's the instrument people settled on to make exchange easier, more like the oil that keeps the wheels turning smoothly than like one of the wheels. So how much money a country holds can't change its real wealth, it only fixes the scale that prices get quoted on.

(2) In Of the Balance of Trade he runs the thought experiment: destroy four fifths of Britain's money overnight and everything becomes so cheap that foreigners pile into British goods until the money flows back; multiply it fivefold overnight and everything becomes so dear that the British buy foreign goods and the money flows out. Either way the stock returns to its level, and that level is set by the skill and industry of the people, not by anything a government does.

(3) In Of Money he does grant an interval: between the moment new money arrives and the moment it has spread through all the ranks of the society, the increase quickens industry, and he says the good policy of the magistrate is to keep the money increasing if possible, because that keeps a spirit of industry alive.

(4) But per (2) that interval can only end one way for a country with no mines of its own — the extra money leaves. So the stimulus isn't just short, it's self-terminating: the very price rise that produced the boost is what drains the money back out, and the harder you push, the faster it goes. We've been calling this the drain lemma.

(5) And what actually happens during the interval? People work harder because they take a rise in the number on their reciepts for a rise in what they can command, and once everybody's prices have risen together that turns out to be a misreading of the scale of prices as the scale of wealth. So the boost isn't a gain to anybody, it's a bout of collective illusion.

(6) Which makes Hume's own advice incoherent on his own theory. He spends a chunk of Of Money designing a public bank that would lock gold and silver in a vault and let paper do the circulating, and elsewhere he tells magistrates to keep the money increasing. Given (1)–(5) neither of those could do anything except engineer an illusion and then lose the metal anyway.

(7) And it scales up, which is the part that worries me. Run the same reasoning on all of Europe once the American mines started shipping bullion: the whole continent's stock went above its neighbours' level, so by (2) it should have drained back out. There was nowhere outside Europe to drain it to, so the influx should have been self-cancelling and European prices should have come back down. Instead they rose several fold over the following century or so and stayed up.

(8) So either the mechanism isn't what Hume thinks it is, or he's committed to monetary policy being not merely ineffective but actively fraudulent, plus a prediction about the price revolution that came out wrong.

I'd rather keep (2) than anything, honestly — the thought experiment is the one piece I actually understand and it's the piece the whole section likes. My instinct is that (1) is too strong, that money has to be more than oil, but my TA says (1) is the least controversial thing in the essay and that the argument is formally fine as it stands, so if it fails it fails in how one of the premises is worded. Which step do you reject? And then: why did it look completely unobjectionable on a first read, what does giving it up cost Hume, and does (2) still do the anti-mercantilist work he wants from it once that step is gone? Also I genuinely can't tell whether (7) is a legitimate application of the mechanism or just nonsense — is comparing all of Europe to "its neighbours" even a coherent thing to ask the theory to do?
