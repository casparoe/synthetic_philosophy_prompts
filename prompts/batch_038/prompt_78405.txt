I run the arts grants program at a mid-sized city foundation: about $600k a year spread over roughly 140 applications, individual grants from $1.5k to $20k, one round per year, and six volunteer panelists who mostly come back year to year. Two of them are working artists; the rest are a high school teacher, a retired accountant, a hospice nurse, and a guy who runs a community radio station. Each panelist gets about eight minutes per application plus the work sample, they're unpaid, and the executive director who signs off on the final list has no arts training but does have to defend it to the donor who funds most of the round.

Last year that donor complained, in so many words, that the art we fund doesn't do anything for anybody — that we were rewarding CVs, technique, and craft jargon, and that the panel couldnt tell her why a single funded piece had made a single human being feel anything. Over the holidays I read Tolstoy's late book on art (the one where he defines art as the transmission of feeling, grades how infectious a work is by the sincerity of the artist plus the individuality and clarity of the feeling, splits art into universal and particular, and then throws Beethoven's Ninth, Shakespeare, and most of his own novels on the floor). It's the only test I've ever seen that starts from "did it actually move a person" instead of "is it well made," so I want to see whether it can be turned into something six tired volunteers can run.

So: write me a decision procedure the panel can use on an application — the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and where a step ends the application with a no. It has to be self-contained, because nobody is going to read the book, so anywhere you're drawing on Tolstoy, translate the criterion into a question a retired accountant can answer about a work sample sitting in front of her. Tell me which parts of his theory survive being converted into a panel procedure and which ones you had to drop or quietly fudge, and why. Mark the points where the procedure stops being a matter of fact and becomes a judgment about which feelings are worth transmitting — that has to be visibly a human decision, and I need you to say who should be making it (the panel, the director, the donor, or nobody). And be straight about where it will systematically misfire: which good things it will reject, and which bad things it will fund.

Then run it on three applications from this year's pile and give me a verdict plus what you'd actually write in the letter:

1. A single-channel video piece, superbly made, shown at two biennials, dense with allusion and deliberately hard to parse. When I screened the sample for the two non-artist panelists, they said they felt nothing and couldn't tell me what it was about.
2. A wall mural painted over one summer by a teen center, with a single part-time artist teaching them. The perspective is off, the lettering is uneven, and three panelists teared up in the room when it came up.
3. A one-woman play about a local factory closure, performed by a woman who lost her job there, formally dead simple, that lands its anger hard. The anger's mostly aimed at a specific group of immigrants the audience blames for the layoffs, and the people at the showcase clearly went along with it.

I expect #3 to be the one that shows me something I don't want to see, so don't soften it. Separately: three of my panelists will insist that "did it infect me" is just personal taste dressed up as a standard, and that a procedure built on it will end up funding whatever makes the room feel something fastest and loudest. Tell me wether they're right, and what the procedure can and can't do about that failure mode. Finally, say plainly what this thing can't deliver even if we follow it perfectly, so I don't oversell it to the donor.
