I'm doing a short (~20 min) intro talk before my department's film club screens Groundhog Day next month — February 2nd, obviously, someone in the society thought that was funny enough to lock in the date. Half the room will be epistemology grad students who have argued about the Sleeping Beauty problem a hundred times and will check out instantly if I rehash it, and the other half will be undergrads plus a few partners who've never heard of it and will check out if I get technical. So what I want is a reading of the film through the lens of self-locating belief that serves both: the film does the heavy lifting and the philosophy falls out of specific scenes rather than getting stapled on top.

My own hunch, which I'd like you to either build out properly or shoot down in print, is that the loop is the Beauty setup run backwards. Beauty's memory gets wiped so her waking evidence carries no indexical content at all, and the whole halfer/thirder fight is over whether that's enough to move her credence. Phil is the mirror image: everyone and everything in Punxsutawney resets except him, so he's the one agent in town for whom "which iteration am I in?" is a live, accumulating fact. He can count. He can learn the piano, memorize the day's events, predict the flat tire and the kid falling out of the tree — all of which only makes sense if he treats each morning as one more sample from a long sequence he's personally tracking. That seems to me to expose something the halfer argument leans on without saying it out loud: "you learn nothing new when you wake up" only looks plausible because the drug guarantees you don't learn anything *indexical* either. Take the drug away and the centered evidence is real and does exactly the work the thirders say it does. Is that a legitimate reading of what the film dramatizes, or am I just decorating my pet view with Bill Murray?

Then there are the bits that don't fit and I'd much rather hear them from you now than from a grad student in the Q&A. First, the film never tells us how many times the day repeats, and I don't think Phil could ever find out — he presumably started keeping track only after the loop had already run some unknown number of times, and there's no external record, so his iteration count is a fact about his own bookkeeping rather than about the world. Does that put the loop in a category where self-locating credence isn't so much contested as undefined — you can't reason about your place in a sequence if you have no idea what's generating the sequence or how long it runs — which would make Phil's problem closer to the Doomsday-style stuff about sampling assumptions than to Beauty's? Second, and this one might just be me being cute: the groundhog. Phil kidnaps the same Punxsutawney Phil and drives them both off a cliff, over and over. If the world genuinely resets, is the animal in the truck on iteration four hundred the *same* groundhog or a fresh one that has never been kidnapped? The film clearly wants the first answer and the town treats it as the one true Phil, but a strict reset implies the second, and that looks like a real puzzle about what persists across centered worlds. Third: what if two people remembered? Palm Springs gets to play with that, and there's even a third looper who turns up holding a grudge — but in Groundhog Day Phil's total isolation is what makes the structure work at all, and I can't tell whether that's an accident of the romantic-comedy genre or something philosophically load-bearing about self-locating belief needing a single rememberer to be a problem.

I'd also like your take on the ending, since that's where I suspect the reading either pays off or collapses. The loop breaks the morning after the day Phil spends almost entirely on other people — the choking mayor, the boy in the tree, the old man he keeps failing to save no matter how many loops he spends trying, who dies that night anyway — and the film invites you to read that as the exit condition. Earlier he'd tried to end the sequence outright, repeatedly, and just woke at six again, which is the closest the film gets to Beauty's actual structure: the sequence of awakenings is what's real, and no single one of them is privileged or final. Does his eventual decision to stop trying to escape and start behaving well amount to adopting one side of the halfer/thirder dispute as a way of life — treating each day as one among indefinitely many, which is what makes patience, practice, and caring about strangers rational at all? And please be honest about where the reading breaks: is there a point at which the critic has to admit the film is about despair and redemption and only accidentally about indexicals? I'd genuinely like to know if there's something the film shows about living inside a centered-world problem that the formal setups can't convey, since that's the one slide I want people to remember.
