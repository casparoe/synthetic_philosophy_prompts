**DRAFT — "The Sliding Violinist"**

1. Thomson's violinist and Marquis's future-like-ours are almost always taught as opponents, and the classroom framing is that you pick a side. I think that framing hides something. Look at the direction of the deprivation each argument is actually about. Marquis's claim is that what makes killing wrong is that it deprives the victim of a future of value — a future like ours. That is an account of the wrongness of *taking away*. Thomson's claim is that a right to life is not a right to the use of someone else's body, so unplugging does not violate the violinist's right to life; it merely withholds something he never had a claim to. That is an account of *not giving*. Nothing in Marquis's account tells you that a valuable future generates an entitlement to somebody else's kidneys, and nothing in Thomson's account tells you that death is not a terrible loss. You can hold both, in full, without tension. The badness of a death is a reason to prevent it; reasons to prevent do not, all by themselves, become claims on a particular person's body.

2. So they're compatible. But I don't think the compatibility is a truce. I think it's a pincer, and the reason is that Marquis supplies exactly the variable Thomson's examples are built to suppress: magnitude. Every one of Thomson's cases is constructed so that the stakes for the dependent party are total and fixed. The violinist dies if you unplug. The expanding child crushes you. There is no case in which the dependent party's future is a matter of degree, because if there were, the example would invite a weighing, and the whole point of the example is that there is nothing to weigh against a right. Marquis's account, by contrast, makes the thing at stake scalar, and it makes it scalar in two independent ways — how much future there is, and how much that future is *like ours*.

3. So: the sliding violinist. Hold everything constant — same kidneys, same Society of Music Lovers, same nine months, same total loss of your liberty and health for the duration — and vary only the violinist's remaining future.

   - A: unplugging kills him; staying plugged cures him and he has fifty years of a full, valuable life.
   - B: unplugging kills him; staying plugged cures the ailment, but he has an unrelated condition that gives him five more years regardless.
   - C: same, but five weeks.
   - D: staying plugged keeps him biologically alive, but irreversibly without anything that makes a future valuable — no experience of anything he cares about, no projects, no relationships. He survives into a future that is not like ours.

4. The question I want to press is whether the duty to stay plugged in varies across A–D. Both answers cost something.

   If it doesn't vary, then FLO is doing no work at all in the applied case, and the right to refuse is a bright-line right against use. That is a fine place for a pro-choice partisan to land, but it's not free. It means you cannot explain why so many people's intuitions about abortion shift as pregnancy advances — a shift that is not merely private squeamishness but is written into basically every regulatory scheme that permits anything at all, where the permissible window narrows as gestation proceeds. An account that has to treat that entire pattern as mass confusion is paying a real price.

   If it does vary, then the right to refuse is weighted rather than bright-line, and can be overridden by the size of the future at stake. That is a much worse place to land, because it concedes the thing the violinist was invented to block: a sufficiently large future-like-ours could ground an enforceable duty to gestate. And then you owe an exchange rate — how many years of somebody else's future buys one month of your body — which nobody has ever offered and which I don't think can be offered without collapsing into utilitarianism about body parts.

5. Case D is interesting on its own, and it's where I'm least sure of myself. If FLO is what makes killing wrong, and D has no FLO, then unplugging in D seems clearly permissible — but it seems permissible *for Marquis's reason*, not Thomson's. In D there's nothing to take. So here is a case where the two accounts converge, and they converge by way of the same consideration, which suggests they aren't really independent in the way section 1 claimed. If that's right, my compatibility thesis undermines my own pincer.

6. One wrinkle I want to include but don't know how to defend. Notice that what varies most plausibly across real pregnancies is not the *amount* of future but the *likeness* of it. Early on, the future in question is indeterminate in a way it later isn't: there is no psychologically continuous subject yet to whom that future belongs. If likeness is what does the scalar work, then the intuition shift tracks similarity to a person's future rather than duration, which is a genuinely different explanation from the standard viability talk. The obvious objection is that this smuggles a personhood requirement back in through the window, which is the exact move Marquis's account was designed to avoid — the point of FLO is that it doesn't care whether the victim is a person. So I may have to cut this section.

7. Where I want to land, and can't yet: Thomson and Marquis are compatible, and their compatibility generates a dilemma about whether the right to refuse has a magnitude. Whichever horn you take, you give up something you wanted — either the phenomenology and the law of late pregnancy, or the bright line against compelled gestation.

8. My own biggest worry is that this is a merely verbal problem. Thomson herself gestures at a spectrum of decency rather than a right/not-right binary — the minimally decent Samaritan, and her suggestion that terminating late for a trivial reason would be indecent rather than unjust. If she already concedes that the assessment is graded, then the pincer in section 4 dissolves, because the weighting I'm demanding lives in the register of decency, not of rights, and rights were never supposed to be weighed against futures in the first place. I don't know how to press my point without conceding the piece.
