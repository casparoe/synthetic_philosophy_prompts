I have spent rather more years than I would care to admit on the alignment fora, and next month I am running a session for a reading group that is half ML engineers and half philosophers. The text is Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982), and I want it read against mechanistic anomaly detection: the ARC/Christiano programme that tries to spot a dangerous internal mechanism by comparing a suspect model's workings with a reference model's, rather than by watching what the model says and does. Work from the later cuts, i.e. no voiceover and the unicorn daydream present, and tell me where the 1982 theatrical version would change the verdict.

The programme's unstated premise is that "same mechanism" is a determinate relation. Press on that premise through the film:

1. The Voight–Kampff test is behavioural detection dressed up as mechanistic detection: it reads the involuntary traces of emotion and the time they take, then infers a different interior. Does the film ever give us grounds for thinking Rachael's mechanism differs from a human's, or only her provenance — manufactured rather than born, memories installed rather than lived? Sort out the candidate criteria of mechanism identity (sameness of causal role under intervention, similarity of internal representation, sameness of algorithmic description, shared developmental history) and say which she satisfies and which she fails. Multiple realisability is unavoidable here; engage with it properly rather than invoking it as a talisman.

2. Roy Batty's last act towards Deckard is an anomaly running the other way: the suspect system behaves better than the reference. What does that pairing show about the distance between flagging an anomaly and having any reason to act on it?

3. Deckard's own status. Gaff apparently knowing his dream is the film's evidence that he is one of them. Is that mechanism-level evidence or a provenance inference, and does the fact that the film's answer shifts between cuts suggest anything about whether mechanism identity is settled by the facts at all?

Where the film resists the reading — the noir fatalism, the romance, the voiceover's insistence that there is a fact of the matter — say so plainly rather than forcing the fit. Do not quote the film verbatim and give me no bibliography; I want roughly two thousand words of criticism that ends by stating what the film adds to the "what counts as the same mechanism?" debate that argument alone would not.
