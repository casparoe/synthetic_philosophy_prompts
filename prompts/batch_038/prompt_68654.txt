Third year in a sociology PhD, sort of on the social-ontology end of things, and I'm stuck on a pattern I keep running into in my chapter on generational categories. I've floated it with a couple people in my department and the response is always some version of "yeah, generations are socially constructed," which isnt really an answer, because I'm not asking whether they're constructed — I'm asking about a specific move that happens after they're constructed.

Here's the pattern. The research org that basically everyone downstream cites picks a birth range — for millennials it's 1981 to 1996 — and is fairly upfront that the cutoffs are chosen because of a bundle of political, economic, and technological events landing in people's formative years, i.e. a working convenience rather than a finding, and the Census itself says something like there's no official start and end. Then out in the wild the hedge evaporates: newspaper graphics, marketing decks, HR onboarding slides, and other academics' lit reviews all write "millennials (b. 1981–1996)" as if that were a settled fact. That part alone is just sloppy citation practice, not interesting. The part I can't name comes next. All the survey data gets aggregated using that range, which produces the trait list — delays homebuying, changes jobs a lot, prefers experiences, whatever. And then when the boundary gets challenged, usually by someone born 1980 or 1997 saying they don't fit at all or that they fit perfectly, the defense of the boundary appeals to the traits: 1996 is where the genuinely internet-native cohort stops, look how different the 1997-born are on this measure. But that difference was produced by sorting people into bins at 1996 in the first place. The range generates the evidence that then gets cited to justify the range. Same shape with the cusp microgenerations — when the term for the 1977–83 people took off, it was recieved as a discovery about a previously unnoticed group rather than as a symptom that the parent boundaries were arbitrary.

I know it's in the neighborhood of reification, and one person told me it's just a self-fulfilling prophecy, but neither seems right to me: nobody's behavior is changing because they got labeled, and the category isn't coming true. What happens is that a stipulation loses its provenance and then its own downstream output gets used as support for it — the direction of justification flips somewhere along the chain, without anyone arguing in a circle out loud. So does this have a name, in sociology or in philosophy of science / social ontology? I care way more about the carving than the label: what exactly is the mechanism, what counts as a case, and what doesn't. Does it require that the stipulation was flagged at the start and then unflagged, or can it happen when everyone always assumed the boundary was natural? Does it extend to BMI cutoffs, or the poverty line, or "middle class," which look structurally similar but might be doing something different? And where's the line between this and ordinary circular reasoning, given that here the circle is spread across several institutions over a couple of decades so no single person is committed to it? No need for references, just tell me what you think the thing is.
