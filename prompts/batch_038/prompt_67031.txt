Please compose a diptych in verse on the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist reply to it: eighteen lines in all, made of two octaves followed by a closing couplet.

**The situation.** A speaker, wakeful in a dark house in the small hours, twice sees the same thing at the far end of the hall — the door open, light behind it, a person standing in the doorway. In one octave this is a perception: someone really has got up and come to check on them. In the other octave there is nothing — the door is shut, the hall is dark, no one is there. The two nights are otherwise alike in every respect the speaker could have reported at the time. Label the octaves I and II and leave the couplet unnumbered.

**The formal demands.**

1. Both octaves in strict iambic tetrameter, rhyming ABABCDCD.
2. The second octave must rhyme on the same four sounds as the first, but in a different scheme — no sound added, none dropped, the pairings rearranged — so that the second stanza is built entirely out of the first one's materials without repeating its pattern.
3. The fifth line of each octave must be word-for-word identical: a flat perceptual report the speaker would sincerely have made on either night, something on the order of "There's someone standing in the door." Because the scheme has changed, this refrain will sit in a different rhyme pair the second time; leave it where it is regardless. It is the only thing the two octaves share verbatim, and it must be a sentence about the world rather than about how the world strikes the speaker.
4. Inside the octaves, nothing may hint at which night is which. Ban the hedging register — *seems, appears, looks as if, as though, almost, merely, only, really, truly* — and ban equally the vocabulary that would smuggle in an inner object to stand where the missing one would be: no phantoms, ghosts, dreams, visions, mirages, afterimages, no mind's eye. Each octave, taken from inside, must be an equally faithful account of what is given to the speaker.
5. Nowhere in the poem may the technical vocabulary appear: no sense-data, no disjunctivism, no highest common factor, no qualia, no veridical, no indistinguishable. The argument has to be carried by the form.

**The couplet**, where the symmetry fails — and it must fail deliberately.

- Its two lines must be unequal in length, one long and one much shorter, breaking the tetrameter for the first time in the poem.
- They must rhyme with each other on a sound used nowhere in the octaves: the fact that settles which night was which is not made of the materials the two stanzas share.
- The difference must be located wholly outside the experience — in a cause running before it (a bulb that had burned out that week, a fever, the hour, a body asleep in the next room) or in what the speaker's feet or hands find afterward (a cold floor, a shut door, a partner who never stirred). Nothing available to the speaker in the moment may decide it.
- The second line must be grammatically dependent on the first — a relative or subordinate clause, or a participial phrase, unable to stand alone — and the dependence should run in the philosophically correct direction: the empty case is characterized only as the case that could not have been told from the other, never the reverse.

The poem should hold both halves of the point at once: that from where the speaker sits there is no difference to report, and that this licenses no inference that the two nights had anything mental in common. One is a case of the world being seen; the other is a case of nothing being seen, and it is what it is only because it matched the first. It should nonetheless read first as a poem about being awake in a house at night, not as a schematic exercise.

After the poem, in no more than two sentences of prose, say which octave is the perception and what in the couplet settles it.
