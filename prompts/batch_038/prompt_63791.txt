I write a weekly column on markets and the science of collective behavior, and the following passage is my draft of the section that explains information cascades. It is built out of a background conversation with an economist and a popular book I read over the weekend:

"Rational people can end up ignoring everything they know. In the canonical model, decision-makers act in sequence, and each sees what the people ahead chose but not what those people knew. Once two or three have gone the same way, everyone behind them has good reason to follow: the accumulated choices are stronger evidence than anything they could learn on their own. From that point the sequence stops transmitting information, so a million people can line up behind a decision that rested on almost nothing — a crowd of that size can end up knowing less than a crowd of three. The same logic explains why fund managers all pile into the same trades: nobody wants to be the one who was wrong alone. Cascades are fragile, though. A single well-informed person who acts against the current can shatter the pattern, which is why markets eventually correct: the crowd's unanimity is only ever as good as the first few movers. It follows that transparency is the remedy. The more public information in play — disclosure rules, analyst reports, open data — the more each person has to go on besides everyone else, and the less likely a cascade becomes."
