I am two-thirds of the way through a novel whose narrator is a foley artist: she makes the footsteps, the rustle of cloth, and the breaking bone for other people's films, celery stalks snapping in front of a microphone for a femur, shoes filled with gravel for a walk home. The hinge of the book is an accident that costs her the use of her hands. She can still tell the director and the assistant precisely what to do, can still hear at once when a footstep is wrong for the gait of the actor on screen, but she can no longer make the sound herself, and the last third of the novel is supposed to turn on whether what she still has is the same thing she had before. Because so much of the book depends on getting that question right, I read some philosophy of skill while I was preparing, and the piece that has stuck with me is an essay a novelist friend sent on, from a general-interest literary magazine. I want to know whether it is fair before I let it shape the character, and before I put it in the mouth of a philosopher who appears at a dinner party in chapter eleven, where I had drafted her repeating roughly this account to the narrator's husband:

"Ryle's insight was that knowing how to do something and knowing that something is the case are two different kinds of knowledge, and that the first cannot be assembled out of any quantity of the second: no pile of true propositions about balance, torque, and steering adds up to riding a bicycle. What marks knowing-how is precisely that it resists articulation — the expert acts without consulting rules, and to the degree that she can recite the rules she has lapsed back into the lesser, propositional sort of knowledge. His argument for this is a regress: if every intelligent performance required a prior act of contemplating a rule, then applying that rule would itself require contemplating a further rule about how to apply it, and intelligent action could never get started. Ryle's own positive account is that knowing-how is a disposition, and since dispositions are just settled patterns of bodily behaviour, what he was really showing is that skill lives in the body rather than in the mind. Intellectualists have since replied that knowing-how is after all a species of knowing-that — one knows the relevant propositions under a practical mode of presentation, which is to say that the knowledge is held in the muscles — and cases like a ski instructor who can describe to his students a stunt he can no longer perform himself show that Ryle was simply wrong, so the matter is now closed."

My unease is that the essay is too neat, and that its neatness is exactly what I found seductive: it makes my narrator's articulacy a symptom of decline, which is a beautiful idea and may be a false one. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what to trust — what someone who works on this debate would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong — and whether the essay's conclusion, that the ability to explain one's craft is evidence that one no longer has it, survives once the summary is corrected. I am not looking for citations or suggestions of further reading, and I know I may be inclined to believe the essay because it flatters the book. Give me exactly five numbered items, one for each sentence of the passage in order, each a sentence or two long: the verdict on that sentence, and the single correction that matters most; in items two and five, add what the sentence, once corrected, would license me to write about a character who can lecture fluently on her own technique and who, after the accident, keeps the knowledge but loses the ability. No introduction, no conclusion, nothing before item one or after item five.
