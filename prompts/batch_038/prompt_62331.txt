I am a doctoral candidate in political theory working on the prehistory of what Walzer called the moral equality of combatants, and I have hit an interpretive difficulty in Grotius that I cannot resolve. In *De Jure Belli ac Pacis*, Book III, ch. 4, §§II–IV (A. C. Campbell's 1814 English translation), he writes:

§II: "On some occasions when it is said, that men may LAWFULLY do a thing, the expression only means that doing such act will not subject them to human and legal penalties, but it by no means indicates that the action is strictly conformable to the rule of religion and morality. Thus among the Lacedaemonians and Egyptians stealing was allowed: an indulgence that by no means took away the GUILT of theft."

§III: "Thus qualified, the annoyance of an enemy, either in his person or property, is lawful. This right extends not only to the power engaged in a just war, and who in her hostilities confines herself within the practice established by the law of nature, but each side without distinction has a right to employ the same means of annoyance. So that any one taken in arms, even in another's territory, cannot be treated as a robber, malefactor, or murderer, nor can even that neutral power, in whose territory he is taken, treat him as an enemy, for being found in arms."

§IV: "This principle was established by nations to prevent others from interfering in their disputes, or giving the law to them respecting the rights of war."

My seminar group reads §III as a straightforward anticipation of the claim that combatants on both sides are equally permitted to fight, since Grotius grants "each side without distinction" the same right of annoyance, irrespective of whose cause is just. That reading troubles me: it looks flatly inconsistent with Book II, where the right to wage war attaches only to the party with a just cause, and it would make Grotius a proponent of moral equality avant la lettre. Could you help me work out what the lawfulness invoked in §III actually consists in, and what role §II's discussion of the word "lawful" plays in settling that question? In particular, I would like to understand whether §III is making any claim at all about the moral permissibility of fighting for the unjust side — and if not, what §III and §IV do establish about who may treat captured enemy soldiers as something other than criminals, and on what grounds.
