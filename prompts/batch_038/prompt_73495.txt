So for about two years I've been running an informal betting pool in our lab. I'm a third-year PhD student in distributed systems and it started as a joke at a group dinner — everyone puts in something small like $20 a round, and we bet on questions nobody in the room actually knows the answer to. Early on those were things like "will the conference take Alice's paper" or "will the cluster die during the demo," but over time it drifted toward mathematical-ish claims: whether some open conjecture is true, whether a protocol we designed really has no attack below a certain length, whether a lemma in a preprint we all cite is actually correct. The house rule is that a claim only settles when somebody produces a proof or a counterexample that checks out in a proof assistant; otherwise it rolls over and the pot keeps growing.

I've been losing steadily for like fourteen months and I'm pretty sure I know why. There's a guy in the group — call him D — who has almost no teaching load and access to a fat machine, and he just grinds. Before posting prices he runs searches, checks the small cases, sometimes leaves a solver going overnight. So when he offers to buy "the conjecture is true" off me at 60 cents, it's usually because he's already seen something I haven't. I set my prices by feel: how surprised I'd be, how much I trust the people who stated the result, whether the pattern holds in the toy cases I can do in my head. My labmate says this makes me straightforwardly irrational and I should be embarassed, since my prices are guaranteed to bleed money against anyone who thinks harder. But the first-year, who did a philosophy undergrad, defends me — she says she can't find any actual contradiction in my beliefs, I'm just slow, and that the "you'll get bled dry" argument my labmate is leaning on only works if you assume a person can see everything their own beliefs commit them to, which no one can. Meanwhile a third person has proposed a rule for next year banning anyone from using a computer to inform their prices, "to keep the pool honest," which I suspect is exactly backwards but can't articulate why.

Can you help me sort this out? Concretely: is my labmate right that I'm irrational, or is the first-year right that the standard he's invoking is impossible for a human to meet — and if she's right, what standard should I be measured against instead? And practically, what should I change about how I deal with D and about the no-computers proposal? One more thing that keeps coming up and genuinely confuses me: somebody at dinner said the entire pool is a category error, because the conjecture is either true or false independently of us, so the only correct prices are 0 and 1 and anything in between is just a measurement of our ignorance rather than of the question. Does that objection land, or is there a real sense in which I can be better or worse at pricing these things?
