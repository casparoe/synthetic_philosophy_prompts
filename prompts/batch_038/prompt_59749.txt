For twenty-two years I ran a volunteer emergency service in a rural county — the kind where a crew is whoever answers the pager — and lately I have been trying to write down, for my own satisfaction, what I actually believe about groups doing things. I have no academic training in this and nobody local to check my thinking against, so I would like an honest audit of whether my views hang together. Here they are, as plainly as I can state them.

First: when I say the service decided to keep the north station open, or that we intended to reach the ridge before dark, I am not speaking loosely. Those are real intentions of ours, not a façon de parler for some bundle of what each of us separately happened to think.

Second: what makes such an intention real is nothing mysterious. It is that we had an accepted way of settling things — a vote at the monthly meeting, a standing order in the ops book — and that each of us was disposed to go along with whatever that way produced. There is nothing in the service that is not in its members and in the rules they follow.

Third: in the winter of 2019–20 the north station question simply went quiet. The standing order was never repealed, but it had become a dead letter: every one of us privately assumed the station would close, nobody would have acted on the old commitment, and no procedure for revisiting it was live. I still think it was true at that time that we intended to keep it open, and that nobody had changed his mind. A member who had gone to the county and announced that we were closing the station would have been speaking for a commitment that was ours even though not one of us held it.

Fourth: what separates genuine acting-together from a mere coincidence of behaviour is mutual responsiveness — each participant tracking the others and adjusting what he does in light of them. A crowd surging through a gate in a panic may look perfectly coordinated and still not be doing anything together in the sense I care about.

Fifth: the best operations I ever took part in were run by a single incident commander giving orders. Most of the crew never saw the whole plan; two of them thought the operation was a waste of a night and said so out loud; everyone did his part anyway. I count those as joint action in the fullest sense. The crew pulled that man out of the culvert, not merely the commander.

Sixth: when a member walks away from an operation in the middle of a shift without a word, the rest of us were wronged, not merely inconvenienced. He owed us his part, and our resentment has a standing that resentment against a stranger who changed his plans would not.

Seventh: I will not accept any account on which a person is obliged to keep participating. Anyone may drop out of any joint activity at any moment, for any reason. A commitment you cannot exit is not a commitment but a trap.

I do not know where the trouble lies in this set, and I am deliberately not telling you which of these I would be most willing to give up, because I do not know that either. What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial: which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do; for the real conflicts, an ordering by severity rather than a flat list treating every strain as equally bad; and for each, the cheapest revision that would restore consistency together with what that revision would actually cost me — including whether the cost falls on my account of what the service was or on my description of what I saw it do. Where an apparent conflict dissolves, say why it dissolves and say what would have to be different for it not to. Finish by telling me which single change would clean up the most. Please write all of this as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
