I am preparing a worked example for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, and next week we take up the units-of-selection debate. What I want is a single constructed case that either forces the competing frameworks apart or, if they cannot be forced apart, shows precisely where the descriptive difference lives. Please build the case to the following specifications.

**Setting.** A real, non-human organism with a plausible natural history: no engineered creatures, no science fiction, no human cultural institutions. Discrete, ephemeral resource patches — a carcass, a ripening fruit, a rotting log, a tidepool, your choice — are colonized each season by a fixed number of juveniles drawn at random from a single well-mixed global pool. Patches do not reproduce as wholes, do not go extinct, and do not found daughter patches; at the end of the season every patch is abandoned and all adults return to the global pool to mate. The only respect in which patches differ is the per-capita number of offspring their residents leave behind.

**The trait.** One heritable, discretely expressed trait that leads its bearer to do something raising the reproductive output of its patch-mates at a cost to itself. No kin recognition, no tagging or "greenbeard" mechanism, no repeated interactions, no partner choice, no reciprocity, no punishment or social enforcement of any kind. Individuals interact with their patch-mates and with no one else.

**Two conditions that must both hold, and must be shown to hold by arithmetic rather than asserted.**

1. *Within-patch disadvantage.* In any patch containing both types, a bearer has strictly fewer expected offspring than a non-bearer in that same patch. State the cost as a definite number.
2. *Global increase.* At the starting frequency you choose, bearers have strictly higher average fitness than non-bearers across the whole population, and the trait's frequency rises from one generation to the next. Report both frequencies.

These two conditions cannot both be met if the fitness effects within a patch combine additively, so you will need patch output to depend on the number of bearers in a non-additive, threshold-or-synergy fashion. Do not merely announce that the dependence is non-additive: build it into the natural history, so that a reader can see why two bearers together accomplish what one bearer cannot. If your pool is finite, tell me what the slight negative relatedness induced by sampling without replacement does to your numbers, or make the pool large enough that it can be ignored.

Keep everything checkable by hand: discrete generations, patch sizes under ten, integer or simple fractional offspring counts, no continuous-time machinery. One paragraph of scenario plus a payoff table is all the setup I want; my students are first-years who have read only introductory material.

**Then the demonstration, in this order.**

(a) Check each specification above against your own construction, one at a time, showing the relevant computation. In particular verify that relatedness among patch-mates, measured relative to the global pool, really is zero, and say which sense of "unrelated" you are using.

(b) Decompose the change in trait frequency into a within-patch component and a between-patch component. Give both numbers and confirm that they sum to the total change reported in condition 2.

(c) State what three accounts each say about the case: a gene's-eye or individual-selection account; a multilevel account of the MLS1 sort, on which patches constitute a level at which selection occurs; and a Hamilton's-rule account. For the third, insert the relatedness, cost, and benefit your case actually has and report what the rule predicts. If it predicts the wrong direction, say so plainly and diagnose what has gone wrong — whether the rule has been violated, whether its cost-and-benefit terms have been mis-specified because the effects are non-additive, or whether something else is happening. I want a diagnosis, not a rescue by relabeling.

(d) Tell me whether the three accounts disagree about any observable fact: the trajectory of the frequency, the equilibrium it settles at, the response to a perturbation. If they are numerically equivalent and differ only in description, locate exactly where the descriptive difference sits and whether anything hangs on it.

(e) Report the equilibria. Does the trait fix, is it lost, or does it settle at an intermediate frequency, and is there an unstable threshold below which it disappears? Give values.

**Finally, the honest notes, which matter to me more than the scenario.** Which single feature of your construction is doing the work? Identify it, then remove it: rebuild the payoff table so that patch output depends additively on the number of bearers while the cost to the bearer stays exactly the same, and show what becomes of conditions 1 and 2. Next, give the strongest attack a defender of the gene's-eye view would mount — not a denial of your arithmetic but the claim that the case has been misdescribed, for instance that a trait favored on average is not altruistic at all and that the "levels" here do no causal work — and say whether you think the attack succeeds. Last, say whether your patches would count as Darwinian individuals on the usual criteria (a reproductive bottleneck, division of labour, no reproduction of the group as such), and whether that verdict changes whether this is a case of group selection.

So that you know what would count as cheating: the point is to have the verdicts generated by the numbers rather than stipulated by the adjectives. A scenario that gets its result by describing the trait as beneficial for the patch, or by smuggling assortment in through the back door — patch-mates who turn out to be relatives, or patches colonized non-randomly — fails. So does one in which the between-patch component turns out on inspection to be nothing but relatedness under another name.
