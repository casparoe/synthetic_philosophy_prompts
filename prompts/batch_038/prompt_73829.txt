I am teaching one 80-minute seminar in a master's-level methodology sequence on models of rational choice, with fourteen students who have all completed a term of expected utility theory and know the axioms well enough to recite them. Next week's session is Herbert Simon's critique: satisficing and bounded rationality. I need a teaching plan, not an exposition — I can reconstruct the material myself, and the reading is already assigned. What I lack is the dependency structure, and this is the part I would get wrong on my own.

Here is my difficulty. The failure mode I expect is the cheap one: students hear "Simon says people do not maximize, they take the first option good enough" and file it as a psychology trivia item about laziness, or conclude that Simon is simply saying people are irrational, and then the session turns into anecdotes about their own apartment hunting. My colleague warns that the sharpest student in the group will arrive with a more damaging objection already formed, roughly that once you charge the agent for the time and effort of searching, the search process and its stopping rule are just ordinary maximization over an enlarged option set, so satisficing names no distinct model — and that my colleague's counter, which I find persuasive but cannot yet articulate crisply, turns on the aspiration level being adjusted in response to how the search is going rather than fixed in advance. Build the sequence so that this objection lands as a productive turn instead of a demolition: tell me precisely where in the order the idea of an endogenous threshold has to be planted so that the objection is available to be answered rather than fatal, and flag every step at which a student could form a belief that will make a later step impossible. I also want the fork handled explicitly, because students will arrive assuming that Simon and the heuristics-and-biases tradition are one continuous project, which I think is wrong in a way that matters for what the normative upshot of satisficing is supposed to be.

On the carrying example: I had drafted a hiring committee screening applicants, but I suspect it fails, because the case comes with so much institutional furniture — job descriptions, reference letters, anti-discrimination constraints — that students will spend the whole time on whether the committee is behaving fairly and never notice the stopping rule as the object of attention. Tell me what the example must have and what it must lack for this idea to be the only thing visible in it, then either nominate a better candidate and justify the choice against those criteria, or defend the hiring case and explain how to strip it down. Finally, give me one closing question I can put to the room that would distinguish students who have the idea from students who can recite it; my own draft is whether an agent who has costless access to the full option set but follows a fixed threshold rule anyway is satisficing, and I want your view on whether that question actually separates the two groups or quietly begs the point. Note anything you would cut at this level — the secretary problem, search-theory machinery, optimality results — and say so out loud in the room rather than leaving the gap unmarked.

Open with a single sentence giving me the bottom line on the order and on which misconception must be headed off first. Then the sequence with rough timings, then the verdict on the example, then the misconception map keyed to the step at which each one gets defused, then the closing question and what a good versus a merely fluent answer sounds like. Please keep the whole thing under 700 words.
