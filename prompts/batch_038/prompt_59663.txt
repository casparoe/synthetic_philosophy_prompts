**Question:** Explain what a deflationist about truth means by saying that truth is not a substantive property. Why, on this view, do we need a truth predicate at all? Finish by saying how the deflationist should handle the Liar.

**Answer:**

> The deflationist's core claim is that truth isn't a substantive property. When we call a sentence true we aren't describing it as having some deep feature, like corresponding to reality or cohering with the rest of our beliefs; we're just reasserting it. "It is true that grass is green" says exactly what "grass is green" says, so on this view "true" adds nothing and could in principle be struck from the language without any loss. This goes back to Ramsey's redundancy theory, and the modern version is normally stated with the equivalence schema: for any sentence s, "s" is true if and only if s. The left side mentions the sentence and the right side uses it, and that's the whole story — truth just is disquotation, the device for cancelling quotation marks.
>
> Tarski is usually credited with proving the deflationist right. His semantic definition reduces "true in L" to a set of recursive clauses over the parts of the language, and what it delivers at the end is only the instances of the schema, so the famous definition is really a way of making disquotation rigorous rather than a rival theory with content of its own. Correspondence theories, by contrast, owe us an account of a relation between language and world that does explanatory work, and deflationists point out that nobody has ever supplied one that isn't either trivial or mysterious.
>
> The obvious objection is that if "true" adds nothing we shouldn't need the word at all, and yet we do. The deflationist's answer is generalization. I can't list everything the witness said, but I can say "everything she said is true," and the truth predicate lets me do that in one go — it's a device for getting infinite conjunctions and disjunctions out in finite space. So "true" does have a job, but the job is logical rather than descriptive. It's a bit of machinery for endorsing lots of claims at once, not the name of a property those claims all share.
>
> The hard case is the Liar. Take "this sentence is not true." If it's true then it isn't, and if it isn't then it is. Deflationists deal with it by restricting the schema: it applies only to sentences that actually have a truth value, and the Liar doesn't have one, so the schema never licenses the contradiction. Truth stays fully deflationary everywhere that it applies at all.
