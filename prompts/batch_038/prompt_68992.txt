I'm a fourth-year political theory PhD student, and because I'm the only person on our graduate student association's elections commission with any background in this stuff, everyone defers to me on a proposal I actually find pretty confusing.

Here's the situation. Our spring budget referendum keeps going badly. Turnout was 11% last year and members voted down a small fee increase that would have kept the student legal clinic open. Afterwards basically everyone, including the people who'd campaigned against it, agreed that almost nobody had read what the measure actually said, and that a lot of the "no" votes came from people who assumed the money was going to the administration. So a caucus is proposing the following for next spring: before your ballot on the budget measure counts, you take a 20-question check drawn from a published pool of 60 items about the association's own finances — where the money comes from, what the fee would fund, what the bylaws require us to do with a surplus. Unlimited retakes with a 24-hour cooldown. Your vote on the budget question then counts for between 1.0 and 2.5 votes depending on your score, scaled linearly. Officer elections stay one-person-one-vote; the sponsors are adamant those be kept seperate.

The commission is split and I need to circulate something before Monday. The complications I keep coming back to: the 60-item pool was written by the same caucus sponsoring the proposal, and there's no procedure for checking whether some items nudge you toward the answer the sponsors want. On the practice version, international students and students at the partner community-college programs averaged a weight around 1.4, while domestic students in the STEM doctoral programs averaged around 2.2 — that's roughly a 1.6x difference in raw voting power between two groups of the same membership. One member with a diagnosed reading disability has asked that her weight be set by attendance at an oral briefing instead of a written test, and nobody can tell me whether granting that is a fair accommodation or an open door, since if we recieve a few more requests like it the written check stops mattering. And one commissioner wants to know why, if we accept the principle here, we shouldn't next year be weighting by degree or by year in the program.

Write the memo I'll circulate: approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions the commission could actually put into a motion. Bottom line in the first two sentences, then the reasoning. Take the sponsors' case seriously rather than just knocking it down — the thing they keep saying is that the point of a referendum is to find out what members would want if they knew what they were voting on, and last year is proof they didn't. No citations or references; nobody on this commission is going to look anything up and neither am I. Hard cap of 500 words.
