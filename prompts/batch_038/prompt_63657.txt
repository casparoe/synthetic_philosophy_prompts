I work on the ML platform side of a mid-size software company: deployment, orchestration, permissions, budgets, on-call. I have no formal philosophy training — I read argumentatively and I can follow a careful argument, but I don't know the literature's vocabulary and I don't want it used on me unexplained.

Here is my problem. Our team argues constantly about whether the systems we build are tools or agents, and the argument never resolves, because I've become convinced we're using the words to carry at least four different claims:

1. A design-policy claim about what we ought to build — that we should build systems that only answer when invoked, that recommend rather than act, and that never get to choose what to work on, rather than systems that run continuously and pick their own tasks.
2. A metaphysical or classification claim about what makes anything an agent at all, which is what people are usually reaching for when they say a sufficiently capable system will behave agentically no matter how we package it.
3. A claim about the systems we actually ship today — whether an LLM behind a function-calling loop with a scratchpad, a vector store, a cron trigger, and a tool budget already counts.
4. A claim about how much weight to put on the standard AI-risk argument that any competent optimizer with a persistent objective will drift toward self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to shutdown.

What I want is a classification instrument that sorts a person's answers to concrete cases onto a position, rather than sorting the labels they say they like.

Deliverable: a decision tree.

Starting point, fixed: "I accept that the systems in this taxonomy are implemented as software on ordinary hardware, and I accept that some of them take actions in the world with consequences nobody explicitly chose." Do not spend a question getting me to this starting point.

Structure: six to eight internal nodes, each one a question built out of a concrete case or an operational claim — a CI job that only runs when triggered; a chatbot with function calling and a tool budget; a long-running process with persistent memory, its own task queue, and the ability to spin up subprocesses and spend money; a trained policy in a fixed environment; an autoscaler or a market-making bot; a human operator. Every question must be answerable by someone who has never heard the name of the position it leads toward, so no node may use the words "agency," "agent," "tool," or "goal-directed" in a way that telegraphs which answer is the agentic one. Give each node two or three answer options, lettered, and make sure each option is one that some articulate person would actually defend rather than a strawman nobody picks.

Leaves: seven to ten, each with

- its path signature, e.g. Q1a / Q2c / Q3b / ...
- a short name for the position — a descriptive name is fine, and I would rather you name it yourself than guess at who holds it. Please include somewhere in the tree: the classical optimizer view on which a persistent objective plus competence makes convergent instrumental subgoals near-automatic; the deflationist view on which today's language models are stochastic predictors wearing an agentic surface and the appearance of goals is a training artifact; the view on which genuine goal-directedness requires a system that maintains its own existence as a bounded, self-sustaining process, so that ascription is doing all the work otherwise; the view on which agenthood is entirely relative to an interpreter's predictive strategy; the view on which the tool/agent design distinction collapses past some capability threshold; and the view on which the whole dispute is verbal and should be retired in favor of specified, checkable properties.
- the position's credo in one sentence, in a form its own defenders would sign.
- its most awkward commitment: what it has to say about the case that hurts it most, or the empirical bet it is quietly making.
- if a leaf is unoccupied in practice, say so, and diagnose why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Then: name every pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate, and for each pair give the additional question or case that would separate them — or state plainly that no such question exists and the two collapse into one position.

Finally, a short postscript, clearly after the tree and clearly marked, telling me which of the four claims from my list above the tree has exposed as a merely verbal disagreement, and which are genuine cruxes that our team could in principle settle by looking at systems we have actually deployed.

No citations, no reading list, no bibliography, and no attributions to named authors — I could not check them and they would only add noise. Write for an engineer: where you need a term of art, define it in the sentence where it first appears. Begin your reply with the decision tree itself: no preface, no restating my request, no summary of what you are about to do, and no commentary of any kind before the tree. The postscript is the only place any meta-level remark belongs, and it comes last.
