I have been a reader of the rationalist blogosphere for the better part of fifteen years, and the one thing that corpus never gave me is a precise statement of what reductionism actually amounts to. "The map is not the territory" and "reality doesn't care about your intuitions" are moods, not doctrines, and I got tired of gesturing at them. So over the past year I have been working through the literature on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality — Fine, Schaffer, Bennett, Rosen, plus the exchanges between the priority monists and the atomists — and trying to write down a version of my own view that I could defend sentence by sentence. I do evaluation work on machine learning systems by day, so this is an evenings project, and the document is currently about four pages. A friend who did a PhD in metaphysics has agreed to tear it apart sometime next month, and I would like a rehearsal before he does. To be explicit about what I am not asking for: no survey of the field, no reading list, no suggestions of names or papers to check, and no reconstruction of what any particular author would say about me. I want an audit of my own commitments — which of them are jointly unsatisfiable, which only look unsatisfiable, and what the cheapest repair is in each case.

Here is the package, stated as directly as I can manage.

1. Grounding is primitive and hyperintensional. I reject any analysis of it in terms of supervenience, counterfactual dependence, or necessary implication; "necessarily, if A obtains then B obtains" is not what a grounding claim says, and I do not think grounding can be recovered from modal resources.
2. Nevertheless I hold that grounding is necessitating: if some facts fully ground a fact, then necessarily, if they obtain, it obtains. I also want grounding claims to license the obvious counterfactuals — had the grounds been otherwise, the grounded would have been otherwise.
3. Grounding is a strict partial order on facts: irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive. It is also well-founded — every chain of full grounds terminates, and there are no infinite descending chains.
4. Maximalism: every truth is either fundamental or has a full ground. I do not accept free-floating truths, i.e. truths that obtain with nothing to account for their obtaining.
5. The fundamental tier consists exclusively of a finite set of positive, contingent, particular facts: roughly, the distribution of physical magnitudes over spacetime regions. I specifically refuse negative facts, absences, and totality facts at the base. Part of my motivation is aesthetic and part is practical — I want the base to be *writable*, a finite description one could in principle complete.
6. I define fundamentality uniformly, for entities as well as for facts: x is fundamental if and only if x has no full ground. On my definition, being partly grounded is compatible with being fundamental.
7. Two verdicts I want to keep. First, the truth that there are no unicorns: I would say it is partly grounded in the base facts, since each of them is individually incompatible with a unicorn being around, but I deny that any finite plurality of base facts fully grounds it, and I will not buy a totality fact to finish the job. Second, the truth that nothing grounds the base facts: I am inclined to say its full ground is just the base facts themselves, since each of them is exactly the sort of thing that nothing could ground. I do not want to call either of these truths fundamental; on my picture the base facts are the fundamental ones and these are not among them.
8. I am a grounding physicalist: mental facts are fully grounded in the base facts together with the relevant grounding facts, and there is nothing over and above.
9. I want a single fact to be able to have two or more distinct full grounds — for instance, a surface's looking red to me might be fully grounded either in facts about its reflectance profile or in facts about its molecular constitution. I worry that the transitivity and asymmetry in (3) forbid this. Tell me whether they do.
10. Mathematical truths: I do not want them grounded in the base facts, and I do not want them fundamental. My preferred move is to call them *zero-grounded* — derivative, but grounded in nothing at all, with the empty plurality doing the work. Separately, I hold that everything non-fundamental is grounded, ultimately, in the fundamental facts; that is what I mean when I say the base is complete.

Three requests. First, sort these into genuine conflicts and merely apparent ones, and be explicit about the apparent ones rather than simply leaving them off the list: I suspect that at least two of my own anxieties are confusions — the one in (9), and a standing worry, pressed by my friend, that (2) simply smuggles the modality I banished in (1) back in through the door — but I want the reasoning, not just the verdict, and I want to be told if I am wrong to dismiss them. Second, rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than listing them flat: which can be fixed by rewording a definition, which require abandoning something I have stated as central, and which would take down the whole package if left in place. Note in particular whether a repair to one item creates a new problem elsewhere, since I expect several of these interact. Third, for each genuine conflict, state the cheapest repair and exactly what it costs me, and then name the single revision that would resolve the largest number of problems at once, even if it is not the one I would instinctively reach for. Be concrete about which numbered items have to go. I am not attached to any of them in advance, and I would much rather find out now than in front of him.
