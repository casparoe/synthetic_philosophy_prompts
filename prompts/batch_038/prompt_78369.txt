I'm in my last year of a Kant seminar that has quietly turned into an animal ethics seminar, and there's a fight in it that hasn't moved an inch since week three. Roughly two camps.

Camp A says Kant's own position is a dead end: on his account animals can only be things we have duties *regarding*, never beings duties are owed *to*, because obligation runs between creatures that can obligate one another, and the whole thing leans on a spillover argument about how being cruel to animals hardens you towards humans. They grant that's internally coherent, but say it can't survive a case where an animal is clearly wronged and nobody's character is thereby damaged — so they want to keep the machinery (humanity as an end in itself, the whole kingdom-of-ends picture) and swap out the criterion, Korsgaard-style, so that having a good of your own rather than being rational is what gets you in the door.

Camp B says the indirect-duty reading is a caricature. They point at the bit where Kant says you can't just dispose of an animal that has served you for years the moment it can't work anymore, and at how much work he lets analogies with duties to persons do, and they argue the texts already leave room for something close to direct standing, so the repair is internal rather than a rebuild.

Nobody is budging and I've started to think they're not arguing about the same question. My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: A is doing reconstruction and B is doing exegesis, and each side's best evidence is exactly the kind the other refuses to count as evidence — A says "fine, Kant was wrong," B says your not actually reading him. And underneath that I suspect a second split about which Kantian claim is load-bearing: the reciprocity condition (only a being that can be obligated can be a party to duty) versus the humanity formula's talk of ends in itself. Those two come apart pretty sharply and I think people slide between them without noticing, which would make half the exchanges non-sequiturs.

There's also a related thing I keep wanting to raise and can't get a grip on. Both camps talk as if this were a binary status question — does the animal have standing or not. But look at what actually gets argued: whether a Kantian can object to eating meat from an animal that never suffered at any point, whether a shelter can put down a healthy but unadoptable dog, whether a horse gets kept alive past its usefulness. Those fights seem to be about who is permitted to bring an animal's good into deliberation and in what capacity — representation and authorization rather than status. Would reframing the debate that way break the deadlock, or just relocate the same fight into new vocabulary?

So, diagnose it for me. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what the phrase "end in itself" is supposed to pick out, two different projects (historical vs. systematic) sharing a single label, or a disguised empirical question about animal agency that comparative psychology could in principle settle? Given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress — a distinction, a decisive textual case, some formal result about whether an obligation-relation can admit non-rational beings without wrecking reciprocity, or just a change of question? If your answer is that no progress should be expected, make that case explicitly and say why the seminar keeps going in circles. I don't want a survey of the positions, I don't want a reading list, and I definately don't want you to tell me who's right about animals — I want the structure of the disagreement.
