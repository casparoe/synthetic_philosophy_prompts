I am a fourth-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, drafting a chapter on credit and priority disputes. I want you to argue for a position I have come to hold but cannot yet defend properly.

The position is this: the multiples phenomenon cannot bear the anti-heroic weight routinely placed on it. Merton's claim — that independent, near-simultaneous discovery is the common pattern in science rather than the exception — is usually taken to show that discoveries are determined by the state of a field and would have been made anyway, whoever got there first. But what makes a case a multiple is that two or more investigators made *the same* discovery, and there is no principled way to fix the grain of that sameness. Individuated finely, Newton's fluxional method, with its nascent quantities and geometric idiom, is not Leibniz's differential algorithm, with its notation, its handling of the inverse problem, and its generality as a technique; and Wallace's Ternate argument is not Darwin's theory, which carries the principle of divergence, the analogy with artificial selection, and a mass of accumulated evidence alongside the selection mechanism itself. So construed, the paradigm multiples dissolve into overlapping but distinct achievements, and real multiples become rare and unremarkable. Individuated coarsely — both produced a calculus, both arrived at selection as the mechanism of adaptation — multiples become ubiquitous to the point of triviality, since almost any two adjacent contributions in a maturing field will count as the same discovery. The frequency claim that does the evidential work is therefore never pinned down, and multiples cannot displace the heroic picture; they are merely consistent with its negation.

Please argue for this as strongly and as committedly as you can, in your own voice, rather than presenting it as one consideration among others; I can do the balancing myself. In doing so:

- Work through the Newton–Leibniz and Darwin–Wallace cases in enough detail that the grain question becomes concrete. Test the candidate criteria for fixing it — same problem solved, same location in the field's problem-space, mutual translatability of results, same downstream consequences for research — and show what each does and fails to do in each case.
- Answer the objection that this is vagueness or the sorites restated, and so a general feature of counting anything, with no special bite against Merton.
- Answer the objection that the argument proves too much: if grain-relativity defeats multiples, it equally defeats singletons, and I have explained away the explanandum rather than deflated the explanation. Say whether any asymmetry is available to me here.
- Tell me whether the event-identity literature helps: Davidson's coarse-grained events, Kim's property exemplifications. Either show how one of them fixes the grain, or say what discoveries are if they are not events at all.
- Close with what survives. Does the multiples phenomenon retain any evidential value for the question of whether discoveries are inevitable? And if there is no determinate fact about whether two people made the same discovery, are priority disputes merely bitter, or are they rationally interminable?

I am not after a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature — I want the argument itself. At the end, tell me plainly where you think it is weakest and whether those weak points are repairable, or whether the thesis has to be narrowed or abandoned. And if you think the whole thing rests on a confusion — that I have run together a point about counting with a point about causation, or that the sameness of a discovery is determinate in every respect that matters and my worry is idle — say so at the outset and tell me where I have gone wrong, instead of producing a polished defense of something you take to be false.
