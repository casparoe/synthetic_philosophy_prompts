I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, mostly AI policy and the research culture around it, and I'm drafting a long feature on what happens to oversight once the systems being supervised are cleverer than the supervisors. My editor wants the piece to hang on one clean claim rather than a survey of techniques, and after several weeks of reading I've settled on a spine that I'd like you to stress-test before I build four thousand words on top of it.

The spine is this. The debate proposal — the arrangement in which two models argue over a question or a proposed action and a weak human judge decides which of them gave the better account — is usually pitched as resting on a proof rather than a mood, and that's precisely what makes it attractive. Because the setup is a zero-sum game, a model that asserts something false hands its opponent an exploitable opening, so with debaters playing anywhere near optimally, honesty is simply the dominant strategy and deception gets punished in expectation; that part is mathematics, not a hope about what training will happen to produce. What remains over, on this picture, is the judge: a human who can't follow the argument will still be taken in by a confident and well-organized case, however honestly it was argued. So the open problems in this area are all problems about judge quality — how to aggregate many weak judges, how much deliberation time and how much supporting material to give them, whether to let them consult tools or convene a panel of cheaper models — and the strategic question facing a lab is essentially how far to invest in scaling the judge rather than the debaters. A researcher I spoke with in the course of reporting characterized the situation as the field having already solved the incentive problem and now merely working through the perception problem, which struck me as a crisp formulation and which I'd like to use for my nut graf.

Please take on the role of a researcher who works on the formal side of scalable oversight: someone fluent in the complexity-theoretic analysis of debate protocols, in what those results do and don't license, and in the distance between an idealized game and models actually trained to play it. Work through the question carefully rather than answering on first impression — I'd suggest you begin by writing out, for yourself, what the theorem about optimal debaters facing a bounded judge actually establishes and under what conditions, before turning to my framing. Then tell me which parts of the spine above will survive contact with the researchers I'm about to interview and which will not, and, if the central claim is simply mistaken, what the correct version of it is and how much of my argument can be salvaged once the claim is replaced.
