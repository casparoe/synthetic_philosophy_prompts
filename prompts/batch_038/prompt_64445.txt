I am in the fourth year of a political theory doctorate and am preparing a paper for a departmental work-in-progress workshop in a fortnight; the working title is something like "Two Mills: Fallibilism and Confidence in the Posthumous Chapters on Socialism." The paper takes up the chapters Mill began drafting around 1869 and left unfinished at his death in 1873, which Helen Taylor published in a periodical in 1879, alongside the revisions he made to the *Principles of Political Economy* in the late 1860s — above all the addition to the chapter on the probable futurity of the labouring classes in which the form of association he expects ultimately to predominate becomes that of the labourers themselves, on equal terms, jointly owning the capital with which they work and appointing managers whom they can remove. One reader of my draft told me, unhelpfully, that I seem to be "running two different Mills through the same argument," and although I think that is unfair I cannot yet say precisely where the seams are. So rather than another round of exegesis, what I want is an audit of the commitments I have written down, since they are the load-bearing walls of the paper. I am not after sources, the state of the literature, or a summary of Mill's position; I have read him closely and my supervisor has already commented on the scholarship. I want to know which of these claims commit me to others I would rather not be committed to.

Here is the package, in the order it appears in the paper. (1) Interpretively, Mill's late position is a substantive change of view and not a change of register: the passages on associated labour added at the end of the 1860s say something the first edition of 1848 did not, and readings that deflate the shift into a continuity of tone, or that discount it as a late-life softness, are mistaken. (2) The shift happens entirely inside utilitarianism. Mill never adopts a doctrine of natural right and never concedes the socialist maxim that the labourer is entitled, as a matter of pre-institutional justice, to the whole produce of his labour; what changes is his forecast about which institutions best serve the general good and how much of the socialists' indictment of the existing order he takes to be correct. (3) Nevertheless, I want to say that Mill makes a genuinely *moral* concession and not merely an economic one: in the opening chapter of Book II on property he treats the existing distribution as disfigured by its origins in conquest and spoliation and as an institution that has never in fact been put to the test in anything like a fair form, and in the posthumous chapters he grants a great deal of the socialists' diagnosis of the miseries the present arrangement produces. (4) Throughout the paper I gloss Mill's limit on legitimate interference in the standard way and apply it as a bright line: the state may coerce an individual only to prevent harm to others, and a person's own good, whether material or moral, is never by itself a sufficient warrant. (5) I hold that the distinctively Millian case for taking socialism seriously in the posthumous chapters is epistemic rather than doctrinal — whether any of the schemes would actually work is a question of experience that cannot be settled by reasoning in advance, so the schemes should be permitted a trial and the results compared, and this is the same fallibilism that underwrites the case for experiments in living in *On Liberty*. (6) At the same time I hold that Mill's own confidence is settled rather than provisional: the displacement of the theory of dependence and protection by that of self-dependence, and the rising sense of independence among labourers, are for him facts about the direction of moral progress that warrant the judgment that associated labour must in the end predominate, and they do part of the work in his surprisingly welcoming treatment of the stationary state. (7) Normatively I want to inherit that anti-paternalism and to make it do critical work: I object to any transition to socialism that is administered *for* the labouring classes instead of achieved *by* them, and I think state direction of industry is objectionable in principle because it casts the worst-off as wards. (8) I also endorse, and cite Mill as an authority for endorsing, two specific measures: that the state may appropriate without compensating the owner the rise in the value of land produced by the growth of the community rather than by the owner's own exertion or outlay, and that there should be strict limits on what may pass by bequest and inheritance.

Please go through the list and tell me which pairs are in real tension and how badly, and which merely look like tensions and dissolve once the underlying distinctions are drawn — I strongly suspect the two classes are not equally populated, and I would rather be told that one of my apparent problems is a non-problem than have everything flagged at equal severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the argument, since several of these commitments are doing independent work in later sections. In particular I want to know whether my gloss at (4) can actually deliver the verdicts at (8) for the landowner who has done nothing wrong and is nevertheless dispossessed, and whether (5) and (6) can both be true of one author without my having quietly replaced Mill's fallibilism with his confidence. Finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most, and by ranking the tensions by severity rather than listing them in the order I stated them.
