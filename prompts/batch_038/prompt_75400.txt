I've been going back and forth with a colleague about Braudel and the longue durée and I'd like you to write an essay that actually adjudicates it instead of summarizing the debate. The setup: someone proposes a test for the longue durée programme — go hunting for "geographically matched pairs," regions with near-identical slow-moving endowments (climate, navigable water, soils, disease burden, distance to markets) that nonetheless ended up on visibly different developmental trajectories. If you can produce enough such pairs, my colleague says the whole project is refuted, or at best demoted to background music: slow structure can't be doing explanatory work if outcomes vary freely while the structure is held fixed. I think that's too quick — that a matched-pairs result of exactly that shape might be perfectly consistent with what the longue durée is actually claiming, since I read the thesis as being about the timescale on which things move rather than a law linking geography to prosperity. Write an essay on whether the matched-pairs test, assuming it came out the way my colleague wants, would refute the longue durée thesis. Give the strongest version of both readings, be explicit about what the thesis would have to be asserting in order to be falsifiable by this kind of evidence at all, and consider whether the objection secretly assumes the thesis is doing something its proponents disclaim. Dont settle for "both sides have a point" — reach a verdict and defend it. Also please mark clearly which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which parts are live disagreements; if you think my reading of Braudel is a minority or self-serving one, say so rather than treating it as the consensus. End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" giving your bottom line in fifteen words or fewer.
