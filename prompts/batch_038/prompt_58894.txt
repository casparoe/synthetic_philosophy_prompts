I co-teach an upper-level seminar cross-listed between our philosophy department and the history department — twelve students, one eighty-minute session, three groups of four — and next week the reading is on non-human causes in history: pathogens, droughts, eruption-driven cold summers, and whether it is legitimate at all to call such things actors. Half the room are history majors who treat any formal apparatus as a category error; two are economics graduate students who want to turn everything into a regression. I have spent more years than I care to admit on rationalist blogs and forums reading about credit assignment, causal graphs, counterfactual dependence, and decomposing a joint outcome into per-contributor shares, and I would like the session to let the students feel exactly where those tools grip and where they slip on a historical case. So: design a runnable in-class activity in which the students must apportion a single historical outcome — a coastal garrison town that capitulates after a siege — across a fixed roster of candidate factors, some of them human (the commander's decision to hold three weeks past the point the granaries were empty, a defector, a relief fleet that never sailed) and some of them emphatically not (an enteric infection running through the defenders, a multi-year drought that had already hollowed out the hinterland, a volcanic summer that ruined the second harvest, a grain fungus in the stored reserves). I would rather you invent the town, the century, and the details than borrow a famous real episode, because anything the class already knows arrives with opinions attached.

Here is the shape I have in mind, though you should improve on it where you can. You hold, as facilitator, a sealed set of counterfactual cards: for each factor, what happens to the outcome if it is removed. In round one the groups get cards for a subset only and must post a ledger that sums to one hundred percent, defending each entry against a group member whose role is counterfactual auditor and who may demand, of any allocation, that it be stated as a removal claim rather than an importance claim. In round two you introduce interaction — one environmental factor turns out to change the effect of another, so that removing either alone changes little and removing both changes everything — and the ledgers have to be revised under a rule you specify. In round three a card reveals that the commander's decision to hold was itself downstream of the environmental variable, and I want the students to notice, without being told, what happens to the human share when that inference is allowed to run. Two further constraints. First, the rules should be arranged so that the group runs into two things on their own: that the choice of contrast class and the demotion of standing background conditions to mere conditions is doing more work in their ledger than any evidence is, and that pushing the non-human share toward completeness quietly evacuates the human one. Second, nobody says the phrase environmental determinism, and nobody hears it from me, until the debrief; no lecture beforehand, and the formal vocabulary comes out only afterwards.

For deliverables, please give me the phase-by-phase timing across the eighty minutes, the exact wording of the instructions handed to the students, the role cards including at least one student per group whose only permitted contribution is some variant of asking what produced that decision, the full sealed card set with what each card says, a scoring or standing scheme that creates real pressure without any actual money and without touching grades, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the conceptual point it was staged to expose — including the moment where the additive decomposition smuggles in the assumption that everyone in the room shares a single measure of the outcome, which I suspect is the part the historians will resist hardest. Tell me frankly what will go wrong the first time I run this: my own prediction is that they will allocate by moral blame rather than causal contribution and give the pathogen close to nothing on the grounds that it did not decide anything, and that one group will simply hand everything to the drought to be provocative. Tell me how to handle both without breaking the no-naming rule. And close with an honest note on which feature of the real historiographical situation the activity cannot reproduce — my suspicion is that handing them sealed counterfactuals is precisely the luxury no historian has, since the counterfactuals are what the argument is about — and what I should say in the room to make up for it. One logistics note: the building's printer is unreliable and the room has a single whiteboard, so anything that requires more than index cards, markers, and that one board will not survive contact with the session.
