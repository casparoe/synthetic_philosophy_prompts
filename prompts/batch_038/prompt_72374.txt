I am in the second year of my second term on the city council of a mid-sized city of roughly two hundred thousand — an old rail and manufacturing core, a university that has been buying up houses for a decade, and a rental market that went from slack to painful somewhere around 2021. Rezonings and discretionary use permits that the planning commission cannot resolve come to us for a final vote, and I have been keeping a record of my own votes with the reasons I gave on the floor at the time, because a colleague now claims he can no longer predict how I will vote and suspects I have been reasoning backwards from outcomes. I would like to test that claim rigorously. The record, in order: a proposal to build forty market-rate units on an infill lot in a single-family district, with no off-street parking, which I opposed, saying the street was already at capacity and that two hundred organized residents were owed an answer rather than an override; twelve units on that same street, thirty percent of them deed-restricted at sixty percent of area median income with a setback variance, which I supported, saying the scale was unobjectionable and the restriction was doing real work; a homeowner in the city's most expensive quadrant seeking to legalize a garage apartment her caretaker had occupied for nine years, over the block association's formal objection, which I supported, saying the prohibition was protecting nobody and merely collecting a toll from the person with the least bargaining power; a church's plan to convert half its lot into sixty units of permanent supportive housing for people leaving shelter, against near-unanimous turnout from surrounding renters about parking and sightlines, which I supported, saying the turnout was large and the objections thin and that the people who would be housed were not in the room; a three-hundred-unit market-rate tower within four hundred feet of a transit stop, with no affordable units but a substantial fee in lieu, which I supported, saying I refused to demand both density and its price and end up with neither; and a rezoning of a light-industrial strip where eleven immigrant-owned repair shops and a produce warehouse employ about two hundred people, to mixed-use residential, with the developer offering twenty percent affordable and no relocation plan of any kind, which I opposed, saying that affordable units at the edge of the city were not a substitute for the wages that strip was producing and that nobody had offered those two hundred workers anything at all.

The case comes up in three weeks. A single family-owned LLC holds a contiguous block of nine walk-ups, eighty-four households in total, at rents well below anything else in the city, occupied overwhelmingly by recent immigrants and by seniors on fixed incomes; the boiler is failing, the roofs are past due, and the owner has stopped doing non-emergency repairs. The proposal rezones the block for six stories and one hundred eighty units, with fifteen percent affordable at sixty percent of area median income, relocation assistance during construction, and a right of first return for existing households. The catch, from the tenant association's analysis: return rents would run roughly double what households pay now, and because of the household-size provisions in the draft agreement, about twenty of the eighty-four households would not fit into the units they would return to. The ground-floor redesign also eliminates the block's laundromat and a corner grocery that employs eight people, neither of which has another site within a mile. The single-family association two blocks away supports the rezoning enthusiastically, and I have read their newsletter carefully enough to know that they mean the future population of the block at least as much as its buildings. The owner states plainly that a no vote means a sale to a buyer who will raise rents and do no repairs, and I have no particular reason to think this is a bluff. There is a transit stop two blocks away.

What I want is not my vote but an account of the rule I am running. Reconstruct the decision procedure that best explains all six precedents, and say honestly whether any of my stated floor reasons look like post-hoc rationalizations, or whether one vote is better treated as an outlier that should be set aside. Then give me the other rules that fit the same six votes about as well — I can see at least a housing-supply rule, a distributional rule keyed to affordability, a rule about protecting the existing users and workers of a place rather than its built form, and something in the neighborhood of Lefebvre's right to the city, understood as a claim by people who inhabit and produce urban space but hold no entitlement in it — and show me where those rules actually diverge on the new case rather than merely gesturing at them. Identify which of my precedents is the closest analog and precisely where the analogy breaks down, since the new case seems to have a relocation plan where the industrial strip had none, and to have the neighborhood association on the project's side for reasons I find objectionable. Tell me which single further proposal, if it came before us, would most cleanly discriminate between the competing readings of my record. And if the six precedents genuinely underdetermine this one, say so plainly and lay out what principle I would be adopting by voting yes and what I would be adopting by voting no — in particular, what each choice assumes about whether the city's remaining low-rent stock is a commons to be defended or a decaying asset to be traded for something better, and what that assumption would commit me to the next time a similar block comes forward.
