My sister teaches high-school chemistry, and over Thanksgiving we got into an argument she still wont let go of: whether water could have turned out not to be H2O. I said no — if the stuff in the lakes and rivers had been something else, then that stuff just wouldn't have been water, and there'd be no water around. She said that's cheating, that I'm defining my way to a necessity, and honestly I don't have an answer. I've been sitting in on a philosophy of language seminar this term (not for credit; I write software for a living), and after that dinner I started keeping a list of every case that comes up along with what I actually think about it, because I suspect my verdicts don't fit together and I'd rather find that out than win an argument with my sister. Here's the list. I've numbered them so you can point at them by number:

1. Hesperus is Phosphorus — necessary, if true at all, and I only know it by looking. No wobble here.
2. Water is H2O — same shape as 1. Necessary a posteriori.
3. But: if the lakes had been full of that twin-earth liquid (XYZ), I really want to say water would have been XYZ. That looks flatly inconsistent with 2 and I can't make myself give it up.
4. Heat is molecular motion — necessary. Though I feel the pull of "heat is whatever makes things feel hot to us," so in a world where molecular motion didn't produce that sensation, is it still heat? I say yes, still heat, still molecular motion.
5. Pain is C-fiber firing — not necessary. I can genuinely conceive of the firing with no pain at all. But 4 and 5 look like the same sort of claim about the same sort of thing (something we pick out partly by how it registers with us) and I'm ruling on them differently.
6. Cicero is Tully — necessary.
7. The standard meter bar is one meter long — contingent, and yet knowable without measuring anything, which I find wierd but I'll accept both halves of that.
8. This table is made of that hunk of wood — I accept it couldn't have been made from a completely different hunk, though I'm happy to allow a splinter's worth of substitution.
9. Same for me: I accept I couldn't have come from a totally different sperm and egg. Except — this is what blew up the dinner — my sister is adopted, and when I said it to her she said that felt like being told her origins were second-class. And now I notice I hold 9 much more loosely than 8. I'd drop 9 before I'd drop 8, and I don't think that difference should be there.
10. Gold is yellow — contingent. Gold has atomic number 79 — necessary. Fine, no complaint.
11. Cats are animals — necessary. But if cats turned out to be Martian robots I'd say cats aren't animals. So I seem to think a necessary truth can be overturned by evidence, which I know I'm not supposed to think.
12. The number of planets is nine — obviously contingent. But add "actually" and it becomes necessary, and now it looks like I can manufacture necessities just by inserting a word. That bothers me more than it should.
13. Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street — I don't want to call it necessary, and I don't want to call it true either.
14. Two of my own. (a) A physics paper coins "zaylon" for whichever single particle produces a certain detector signature; later it turns out two different particles produce it. I think "zaylon" names nothing at all, and there's no world where it names one of them. (b) A village christens a peak "Karak" by pointing at it through fog, and they're actually pointing at a lower ridge; from then on everybody uses "Karak" for the real peak. I think that one succeeds and names the peak. Both are baptisms where the description used goes wrong, and I rule on them differently.

My tentative principle, which I don't trust: a term is rigid iff what it picks out in any world is settled by the thing we actually latched onto rather than by the description we used to latch onto it; and any true identity between two rigid terms is necessary. That seems to handle 1, 2, 6, 8 and 10 and to fall apart on 3, 5, 11, 12 and 14.

What I want is the principle, or the small set of rival candidates, that does the best job on the whole list. Please give me a single JSON object and nothing outside it, with exactly these fields: "best_principle" — an object with "statement" (a sentence or two, precise enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen) and "clauses", an array of objects each with "clause" and "motivating_cases" (which of my numbers that clause exists to handle); "alternatives" — up to two objects in the same shape; "scorecard" — an array of objects, one per case number 1 through 14, each with "case", "verdict" (one of "saved", "overturned", "reinterpreted") and "note" (one sentence on what your principle says about it); "the_pair_you_cant_both_keep" — an object with "pair" (the two case numbers you think are genuinely incompatible, I'm guessing 2/3 or 4/5 but tell me if it's another), "which_goes", and "what_it_costs"; "new_cases" — two cases I haven't listed where your principle makes a definite prediction, ideally a surprising one, each with "case", "prediction", and "why_it_tests_the_principle"; "sister" — one or two sentences in plain English, no jargon, that I could actually say at the next family dinner about whether water could have been XYZ; and "bottom_line" — one sentence saying whether my tentative principle is salvageable with a patch or has to be replaced.
