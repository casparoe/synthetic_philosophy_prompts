A grad student brought this to office hours and I spent twenty-odd minutes failing to break it, which bothers me, because the conclusion cant be right.

(1) On Williams's account, what's objectionable about impartial moralities is that they make the agent step outside their own projects and rank them as one option among others — that's the damage to integrity.
(2) But promising does exactly the same: the commitment binds me even when breaking it would serve my projects better, so any morality with promises in it also requires me to step outside my projects and hold them conditional on a ranking that isn't mine.
(3) So either promise-keeping damages integrity in just the same way, or (1) has misidentified what the objection is about.
(4) Promise-keeping doesn't damage anyone's integrity — it's close to the most integrity-preserving thing an agent does.
(5) So (1) is wrong about the target.
(6) And there's nothing else for the objection to target, because weighing one's own projects against other people's claims is all that impartiality ever asks of an agent.
(7) So the integrity objection fails, and the drowning-husband point with it: a man who checks whether saving his wife rather than the stranger is impartially justified hasn't had one thought too many, he's had the one thought that was required.

I want to hold onto the negation of (7) — I still think the drowning case shows something. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked innocent going in, and what the retreat costs me; I have a feeling I'll end up defending something thinner than Williams actually wanted.
