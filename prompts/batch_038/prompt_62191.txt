QUESTION 3 (25 points). In "Equality of What?" Sen argues that the disputes between utilitarians, Rawlsians, and resourcists like Dworkin are fundamentally disputes about the space in which interpersonal comparisons are made, and that this question is both prior to and separable from the question of what pattern to aim at in that space. (a) Explain what Sen means by the informational basis of a theory of justice, and why he thinks utility and Rawlsian primary goods are each a deficient basis. (b) What is the capability metric, and how is it supposed to avoid those deficiencies? (c) Could Dworkin's equality of resources meet Sen's objection without abandoning the resource metric altogether? Explain.

STUDENT ANSWER:

(a) Sen's claim is that every theory of justice has an informational basis, a fixed set of variables in terms of which it makes comparisons between people, and that by restricting itself to those variables the theory makes certain facts about people's lives structurally invisible to it. So the debate about equality of what is not really a debate about how much people should get but about what a theory is even able to see. Utilitarianism takes utility, meaning happiness or desire-fulfilment, as its basis. Rawls takes primary goods: rights and liberties, powers and opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect, all of them means that anyone would want whatever else they happen to want. Both are deficient. Utility is deficient partly because it is a private mental state that cannot really be compared between people, and partly because people adapt. Someone deprived for long enough lowers her expectations and sincerely reports herself contented, so the utilitarian metric registers no injustice in exactly the place where there is the most of it. Rawls's list is deficient because it treats goods as though they mattered in themselves rather than for what people can actually do with them.

(b) The capability metric compares people by their capabilities, which are the real freedoms they have to achieve various combinations of functionings, where functionings are the things a person can be or do: being adequately nourished, being literate, taking part in the life of the community. Sen distinguishes the capability set from the achieved functioning, and what matters morally is the freedom rather than the realised outcome, which is why the person who fasts and the person who starves have the same functioning but very different capabilities. Sen's positive answer to the deficiencies in (a) is to supply a list of the central capabilities, weighted according to what people in different societies have reason to value, so that comparisons are made on a scale that is objective without being blind to what people's lives are actually like.

(c) Dworkin could absorb the objection. His view gets equality of resources out of two devices: the auction, where the shipwreck survivors bid for the island's resources until nobody prefers anyone else's bundle to their own, and hypothetical insurance, where people who know the odds of being born with a given handicap but not their own endowment buy cover against the handicaps they might turn out to have, and the average cover purchased sets the level of tax and transfer. That second device is precisely where Dworkin handles disability. So Sen's objection amounts to the observation that health and native talent are missing from the resource bundle, and Dworkin can just put them in. He would also rightly resist Sen on a further point: any metric keyed to what people can do with what they have has to face the expensive tastes problem, and a capability metric gives no clean way of separating a person's circumstances from her choices, which is exactly the separation Dworkin's whole apparatus is built to preserve.
