My father has been in a residential care facility for fourteen months, following a stroke and a diagnosis of a rapidly progressive dementia. Last week my sister circulated to the rest of the family a photocopied chapter from a popular book about the brain and the self — she has lost the cover and cannot tell me the title or the author — saying that it explained why we should stop talking as though Dad were still in there. The chapter's philosophical content is drawn almost entirely from Daniel Dennett's account of the physical, design, and intentional stances and from his idea of real patterns. She has since invoked it twice in family discussions, most recently as grounds for cancelling the expensive video-calling arrangement at the facility, on the reasoning that nothing is lost when the story stops.

I studied some philosophy as an undergraduate: enough to recognize Dennett's name and roughly what the three stances are, not enough to judge whether this chapter represents him fairly or has quietly converted him into something else. I should declare at the outset that I am not a disinterested reader. I want the chapter to be wrong, and I know that makes me likelier to see errors that are not there — so I would rather you tell me where my suspicion is unfounded than flatter it.

Here is the passage in question, transcribed as faithfully as I can manage:

"Modern philosophy of mind spent most of the twentieth century assuming that if you wanted to explain a person's behavior you had to find the mechanism inside them. Daniel Dennett inverted the problem. On his account there are exactly three strategies open to a predictor, distinguished not by what they are about but by how much they assume. At the bottom is the physical stance: you treat the thing as a lump of matter, work out its structure and the laws governing it, and calculate what it will do next. This is the only stance that describes what is actually there, but it is almost never usable in practice — nobody predicts a chess program's moves from the physics of its silicon, still less a patient's from the physics of their tissue. In the middle is the design stance: you assume the thing was built to do something and predict accordingly, which serves well for thermostats, livers and word processors, and breaks down the moment the artifact is defective or the design was poor. At the top is the intentional stance: you treat the thing as a rational agent, work out what it ought to believe and want given its situation, and predict what any rational agent so placed would do. Crucially, the stance is a choice made by the predictor and not a discovery about the world; the same system can be handled in any of the three ways, and nothing in the world settles which treatment is correct. What this reveals is that beliefs and desires are not items inside the head at all. They are what Dennett calls real patterns — real only in the sense of being useful summaries of behavior, the way lines of latitude are useful summaries of the earth's surface. Hence his comparison of a person to a center of narrative gravity: an abstraction posited to organize a story, not a thing that could ever be located. The position is therefore eliminativist in all but name; Dennett disputes that label, but the quarrel is largely verbal, since on his own account there is no fact about what anyone believes except insofar as an attribution helps us predict. This has a consequence easily mistaken for coldness which is in fact its opposite. Someone whose behavior can no longer be predicted by ascribing beliefs and desires has not lost anything real: the beliefs were never there in the sense in which the neurons were there. What is left, for those who loved them, is not a loss but the cost of switching stances — an adjustment in the family's own predictive habits — which is why elaborate efforts to sustain contact with a person far gone in dementia are sentimental rather than kind."

What I would like from you is an accuracy audit I can act on, not a general essay about Dennett:
