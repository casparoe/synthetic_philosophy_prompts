I'm TAing a second-year value theory course and Thursday I get the students for fifty minutes on my own — whiteboard, no slides. The prof did the front end of Principia Ethica (naturalistic fallacy, open question argument) and then jumped straight to metaethics elsewhere, so nobody in the room has met the positive half of the book: the stuff about what's actually good in itself, organic unities, and the claim that the best things are states of consciousness that combine affection for people with the enjoyment of beautiful things. They've done one intro course. They will walk in thinking Moore is the guy who says you just know good when you see it, and half of them think that's an insult to philosophy.

Plan the session for me — I don't want the material explained back to me, I want the order and the traps. Specifically: which piece has to be in place before the next one is even intelligible (I genuinely can't tell whether organic unity has to come before or after the account of what's valuable, since he seems to need both at once), and what I have to say about intrinsic value up front so that the states-of-consciousness claim doesn't collapse for them into pretty experiences are nice, so who cares. Then the one example that carries the whole thing: what it needs to contain and what it must not. I'd been planning to open with his isolation move — a beautiful world with nobody in it versus a hideous one — but my worry is that it either sets off the is-beauty-just-in-the-eye fight in minute three, or it convinces them he thinks scenery matters with no one around, which I then have to spend twenty minutes walking back. Tell me if that's right and what to use instead.

Then map the misconceptions they'll actually form and the exact point in the sequence to head each one off. I'm confident about three: he's a hedonist with extra steps; intuitionism means anything goes; and affection for a person just means liking them, which makes his view of friendship sound bizzarely cold, like he loves the qualities and not the person. I suspect there's a fourth one about organic unity being some kind of vague emergence, but I don't know how to catch it before it forms. Finish with one question I can put to them at the end that separates students who got the structure from students who just liked the mood, and tell me what I should leave out at this level — and say out loud in the room that I'm leaving it out.
