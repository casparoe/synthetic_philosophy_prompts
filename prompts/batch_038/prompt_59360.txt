Some background, because I think it changes how you should answer. I spent thirty-four years running an analytical chemistry lab for a state environmental agency — trace metals in water and soil, mostly — and I retired two years ago. Since then I've been working through philosophy of technology on my own, slowly, and last month I finally read that Langdon Winner piece everybody points at about whether artifacts have politics, plus a couple of the replies to it. The trouble is that my own gut verdicts about the physical objects I spent my career around don't line up with the way the debate is usually staged, and I can't tell whether my intuitions are muddled or whether the standard framing is. So I'm going to give you my verdicts on a batch of cases and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, actually generates them — or tell me one of them has to go. You're the expert here; assume you're a professional philosopher of technology who has taught this argument for twenty years and has your own view about it.

The cases, roughly in the order I thought of them:

1. The low Long Island overpasses everyone uses as the textbook example. Political, obviously. But a historian friend of mine insists the story about the buses was never properly documented, and let's grant him that — suppose the bridges were just built cheap to a standard clearance. I'd still call them political, only for a different reason, and I can't say what that reason is.
2. The sign at a tunnel mouth giving the clearance in metres, which keeps buses out just as reliably as concrete does. Not political, to my mind; it's information. Add a camera and a fine behind the same sign and now I do want to call it political. So: roof yes, sign no, sign-plus-camera yes. I cannot state the difference and it bothers me.
3. Where our air-monitoring stations got sited back in the nineties. Partly convenience — near existing state buildings, near mains power, somewhere a technician could actually reach in February. Consequence: for about a decade the neighbourhoods with the worst air had no monitor at all, and the numbers used in enforcement came from a station four miles upwind. Verdict: more political than anything else on this list, and nobody ever decided it.
4. The child-resistant cap on a solvent bottle. Not political, I'd say — it's a safety standard everyone signed up to. Except our senior technician had arthritis and spent her last six years asking people to open her bottles. Arguably the same shape as case 1, and I still don't want to call it political.
5. The autosampler on our mass spectrometer: one fixed 96-position rack, forty seconds a position for the wash. Which meant an overnight run had to be planned by four in the afternoon and could carry one project's samples only. Verdict: political. It decided that a particular postdoc's slow-reaction experiments were impossible, and it turned sample prep into somebody's whole job. No vote, no memo, no villain.
6. The move from paper lab notebooks to an electronic system with timestamped entries you can't quietly revise afterwards. Political, and also better science. I want to be able to hold both of those at once and I'd like to know how.
7. The old two-pan analytical balance, which needed two people — one to load, one to read the vernier — versus the electronic balance that replaced it and one person runs. The old instrument created an assistant's post; the new one removed it. Verdict: the old one was political, the new one is just better. I can hear how bad that sounds.
8. Firmware on a spectrometer we bought new that outputs processed spectra only and never the raw ones, with no way to switch it off. Verdict: political. But I can't say who decided or when — the salesman, the engineer who wrote the default, the year it was compiled?
9. A deliberately political artifact that flopped. Our agency put a grand "collaboration" staircase through the middle of the building to force people off different floors to run into each other. Everybody takes the lift. Verdict: an attempt at politics that isn't politics. Though a repealed-in-practice law is still a law, so maybe I'm wrong.

My own tentative principle, which I want you to break rather than flatter: an artifact is political when it goes on making a decision after the people who made it have left the room — when a choice that would otherwise have to be renewed every day gets frozen into a thing. That seems to handle 3, 5, 7-old and maybe 8. It plainly doesn't separate 4 from 5, since both are frozen, and it makes the tunnel sign in 2 political every time somebody repaints it, which I don't want. I'm also fuzzy on whether "frozen" is doing any work at all, or whether I've just redescribed "has consequences."

Here's what I'd like back, and please think hard before you write. Start by sketching two or three genuinely different candidate principles — call them P1, P2, P3 — a paragraph each, and for each say which of my verdicts it reproduces and which it contradicts. Then commit. Pick the one you'd actually defend in a seminar, state it precisely enough that I could apply it to something I've never seen, say explicitly which of my nine verdicts it forces me to give up, and defend giving it up rather than apologising for it. I don't want a menu at the end and I don't want "it depends on what you mean by political" — give me a position and reasons for it, and flag honestly the one or two places where your principle is buying the right verdict with an ad-hoc clause. Finally, run it on three cases I haven't listed and tell me what it says: a sea wall built to a crest height chosen from a particular return period; a hospital triage score that uses a patient's past healthcare spending as one of its inputs; and a town that removes the benches from a bus shelter. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list — I'm at the lake with one bar of signal and I can't look anything up. Names are fine if you need them, but don't tell me to go read something.
