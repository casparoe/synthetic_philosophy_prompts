My department runs an annual workshop at which each graduate student posts a one-page statement of the view they actually hold in their own area, and the rest of the room spends the following hour trying to break it. Mine is in epistemology, and it concerns higher-order evidence and whether a believer can rationally hold a belief she takes to be inadequately supported. Rather than wait for the room, I would like the audit in advance. Here is the package exactly as I intend to post it:

1. Evidentialism, strictly: what it is rational for me to believe is fixed entirely by my total evidence. I do not think there is any separate standard of rationality that could come apart from fit with evidence.
2. Higher-order defeat is real. Evidence that I was impaired, rushed, or badly positioned when I formed a belief can make it rational to abandon or downgrade that belief even when the first-order evidence, taken on its own, still supports it exactly as strongly as before.
3. The enkratic requirement is exceptionless: it is never rationally permissible for me to believe P while believing that believing P is not supported by my total evidence. I do not treat this as a requirement binding only on ideal agents; I take it to bind me.
4. About disagreement I am steadfast. If I have assessed the evidence correctly and I know that I have, a peer who has seen the same evidence and reached the contrary conclusion gives me no reason to change my mind; the bare fact of disagreement carries no evidential weight.
5. Transparency: I find out what I believe about P by attending to P rather than by inspecting myself, and correspondingly the fact that a conclusion still strikes me as compelling when I go back over the reasoning is a genuine reason for me to keep believing it.
6. In the case set out below, the right verdict is level-splitting — and not as a permissible concession to human frailty, but as what the evidence requires. I retain the confident first-order belief and simultaneously believe that the confidence is unsupported. I have been calling this posture *deferred endorsement*.
7. Ordinary people, myself included, are in akratic states — believing P while believing that believing P is unsupported — reasonably often, and I do not find that scandalous.

The case in (6): I audit a large quantitative model for a research group that keeps its own error log. The log shows, from my own entries, that after roughly twenty-four hours awake I mis-handle intermediate results at a substantial rate; several figures re-derived carefully the following morning came out differently, and in every recorded instance the night version was the wrong one. Two weeks ago I spent most of two nights reconciling a set of projections, and around five in the morning I re-derived the pivotal number from scratch, got the model's answer, and found it compelling. I believed it then and I believe it now. I also believe, on the strength of the log, that my confidence at that hour is not supported by anything I was then in a position to assess. Nothing has changed since: no one has located an error in the work, and re-running the derivation in the same state would only reproduce the performance the log warns me about. So the two beliefs are both still standing, and I intend to defend both.

What I want is an audit rather than a tour of the debate: tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which only look as though they do, and what the cheapest repair is in each case together with what that repair costs me elsewhere in the package. Please treat all seven as equally detachable — I have no prior sense of which is the weak link, and you should not read a preference into the order in which I listed them or into the fact that (6) is my headline view. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or attributions of particular papers; if a tension has a standard name or turns on a standard distinction, name it, but do not build the answer around who holds what.

Please return the result as a single JSON object with the following schema:

- `package_verdict`: one of `consistent`, `consistent_after_a_stated_restriction`, or `inconsistent`, plus one sentence of justification.
- `tensions`: an array ordered by descending severity, one entry per genuine tension, each with `id` (e.g. `T1`), `commitments` (the numbers involved), `kind` (one of `strict_contradiction`, `jointly_unsatisfiable_in_some_case`, `incompatible_motivations`), `severity` (integer 1–5), `diagnosis` (two to four sentences locating precisely where the pressure falls, including which commitment does the pushing and which is pushed), `cheapest_fix` (the minimal revision that removes the tension), and `cost_of_fix` (what that revision forfeits elsewhere in the package).
- `dissolutions`: an array of pairs that look like tensions but are not, each with `commitments`, `why_it_looks_like_a_tension`, and `why_it_isnt`.
- `single_most_productive_revision`: an object with `commitment` (the number), `revised_to` (the replacement claim, stated precisely enough that I could paste it into the handout), `tensions_resolved` (the `id`s it clears), and `new_problems_created`.
- `irreducible_residue`: what still fails to hang together after that revision, or `none` if the package can be made coherent.
