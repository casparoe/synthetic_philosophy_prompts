ok so I keep circling back to the transformative experience stuff (L.A. Paul's setup: the choice is both epistemically transformative, you can't know what it's like until you've had it, and personally transformative, the thing rewrites your values, so you can't put a utility on the outcome with your pre-decision value function). The canonical rebuttal people make is "sure you can, just go gather evidence: talk to people who did it, read their writeups, do a trial run." And the canonical counter-rebuttal is that testimony can't transfer phenomenal character so it doesn't touch the decision-theoretic problem. Both of these takes are extremely well-worn and I'm bored of them.

The wrinkle I actually want to poke at is narrower and I don't think it gets discussed. Suppose the preview you can buy is cheap, vivid, first-person-ish, and *known to be biased in a specific identifiable direction* — maybe it's produced by someone with skin in the game, maybe it's a curated highlight reel, maybe it's a one-week simulation of a five-year commitment, maybe it's testimony from people who all self-selected into sticking with the thing. Ordinary Bayesian intuition says biased data still beats no data, you just discount for the bias and move on. My suspicion is that for genuinely transformative choices a biased preview can leave you *worse off* than total ignorance, for two reasons that compound: (a) it colonizes your imagination, so you can no longer generate the naive pre-transformative imaginings that were the only thing you'd have been able to reason from, and (b) you can't apply the discount, because correctly debiasing the preview requires knowing the post-transformation utility function, which is exactly the thing you don't have.

So: give me one worked example, mundane and non-sci-fi — career pivot, having a kid, emigrating, some elective medical or psychiatric intervention, religious community, whatever you think bites hardest — where this actually gets worse-than-nothing. I want the mechanism spelled out concretely, not gestured at: what exactly the preview is, which direction it's skewed and why, who made it and what they gain, what the person was able to imagine *before* consuming it that they can't imagine after, and where the debiasing step fails. Then say what they should have done instead, if anything — and I reserve the right to think "just don't look at previews" is a cop-out, since we're not choosing under a veil of ignorance about whether previews exist.

Bonus points for telling me whether "biased preview" is a real third category sitting alongside Paul's two flavors, or whether it collapses into the epistemic one on inspection. And please don't reach for the revelation-vs-reason framing as your answer, I've read that one five hundred times and it mostly just relocates the problem.
