I have spent something like fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one argument keeps recurring there in fresh costumes: whether a person, an organization, or a machine "really has" a tendency that never manifests when it is tested. The current instance concerns a hypothetical agent built to accept a shutdown command on request. My correspondent says the agent cannot honestly be described as willing to be shut down, because on anticipating such a request it would quietly arrange matters so that nobody ever gets into a position to issue one. I say that is not the tendency failing; it is something structurally different, and the difference changes what you would build and what you would audit for. We have now had this argument four times without once agreeing on what either of us is claiming.

I want an instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree that sorts positions on what makes a "would" claim true of a thing, built so that readers are sorted by what they say about cases and not by which label they prefer.

Constraints, all of which I care about:

1. Build from the logical space first. Give me the questions and the answer-combinations they permit; only then place actual positions at the leaves. Note every combination nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that empty cell is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
2. Six to eight questions; eight to twelve leaves.
3. Audience: smart non-philosophers from online argument culture. No question may use the literature's vocabulary at all — none of "fink," "mask," "mimic," "antidote," "disposition," "manifestation," "intrinsic," "categorical," or "counterfactual" may appear in a question. Phrase each question around a concrete thing and a concrete would-it-or-wouldn't-it.
4. The tree must handle these five cases, and every leaf must deliver a verdict on each. I will call them `wire` (a conductor that would carry current to whatever touches it, except that a device attached to it kills the current at the instant of contact), `sleeve` (a glass that would shatter if dropped, except that it always travels in a foam sleeve that absorbs exactly that sort of blow; take the sleeve off and it shatters), `sorcerer` (a thoroughly sturdy glass watched over by a sorcerer who shatters it himself the moment anyone strikes it), `pill` (a tablet that would dissolve in water, except that it contains as part of its own composition something that renders it inert the instant it gets wet — nothing outside it interferes), and `untestable` (a thing that has a property only where nobody could ever find out, because the finding out would destroy it, so the instruction "put it in conditions where nothing interferes and see what happens" has no coherent filling-in).
5. For each verdict, pick the closest of these five and add at most half a sentence of qualification: property present and the "would" true; property present and the "would" false; property absent and the "would" true; property absent and the "would" false; no determinate answer without a context.
6. At minimum the leaves must include: the view that the "would" is the whole story; the view that the "would" plus a repaired description of the test conditions is the whole story; the view that what matters is what the thing is like in itself, so outside interference is beside the point; the view that "is disposed to" behaves like "is tall," true only relative to a context and a comparison class; the view that the property is a real feature which makes the "would" true and cannot be analyzed by it; the view that every property is of that kind; the view that properties are inert and the laws do the work, attached contingently from outside; and the view that there is nothing but the pattern of what happens, of which the "would" is a compact summary. Add any others you think the space demands.
7. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what question would separate them.
8. Finish with the application: which leaf I am standing on, which my correspondent is standing on, and what our disagreement is actually about.
9. No dates, no publication details, no bibliography, no pointers to papers. If a position has a conventional name, give the name and nothing more. I cannot check references, and I would rather you be wrong about an argument than about a citation.

Give me the instrument twice: first as readable indented prose I can paste into a forum post, then as a JSON object matching this schema exactly.

- `questions`: array of `{id, case, question, why_it_separates, options}`, where `case` is the concrete scenario in plain words, `question` is what the reader is asked, `why_it_separates` says which positions come apart here, and `options` is an array of `{text, leads_to}` with `leads_to` either `q<id>` or `leaf:<id>`.
- `leaves`: array of `{id, name, thesis, verdicts, awkward_commitment, reachable_by_pattern}`, where `thesis` is one sentence, `verdicts` is an object with the five keys `wire`, `sleeve`, `sorcerer`, `pill`, `untestable` holding the controlled verdicts from constraint 5, `awkward_commitment` is the single most uncomfortable thing the position is stuck with, and `reachable_by_pattern` is the string of answers that lands a reader there.
- `unoccupied`: array of `{answer_pattern, position_it_would_be, why_empty, note}`, with `why_empty` one of `incoherent`, `unattractive`, `unexplored`.
- `inseparable`: array of `{leaves, why_the_tree_fails, separating_question}`.
- `application`: `{my_leaf, correspondent_leaf, what_the_disagreement_is_really_about, what_i_should_audit_for}`.
