Four months ago I became executive director of a family foundation: roughly forty million dollars in endowment, eleven trustees, and a forty-year habit of making unrestricted multi-year grants to small, thinly capitalized organizations. Last autumn, in the space of six weeks, the donor who had informally committed to replenishing the corpus over a decade died without a will and her estate went into litigation, and a regional health system that had co-funded our flagship program withdrew its share. We cut grantmaking by a third inside a single quarter. I was three weeks into the job when I signed those letters, and one of them used the chair's own image of the river.

The chair — a retired litigator who reads a great deal of history — has opened every meeting since with the same reference: Machiavelli's comparison of fortune to a raging river, which no one can resist when it is actually in flood, but which in quiet weather can be given dikes and embankments so that when it next rises it is carried off through a channel instead of destroying the plain. He draws three conclusions from it, and they have become the foundation's de facto policy: hold two years of grantmaking in cash, make no commitment longer than one year, and never let a single program exceed a modest share of annual spending. His reasoning is that an institution which built its dikes in calm weather can afford to be generous when the water comes, while one that did not is simply at fortune's mercy.

One of the younger trustees — the founder's granddaughter, ten years in venture capital — accepts the same chapter but points at its other half: the judgment that it is better to be impetuous than cautious, because fortune yields to the bold, and the example of the pope Machiavelli credits with carrying off what no amount of ordinary prudence would have managed. She says the chair's dikes are the posture of exactly the man Machiavelli describes as ruined, ruined because he cannot change his method when the times change; she wants two large, concentrated, multi-year commitments made now, to organizations being hollowed out by the same shock that hit us. Several trustees find both positions persuasive and cannot articulate why. The retreat is in three weeks, and I have been asked to circulate a framing paper in advance.

Please write the analytical core of that paper, as an audit of the river analogy rather than as a defense of either trustee. Specifically:

- Give the mapping explicitly. What in our situation plays the river, the flood, the plain that gets destroyed, the quiet weather, the dike, the channel, and the builder? Where does the virtù that must be ordered in advance against fortune correspond, if anywhere, to an endowment policy?
- Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the chair's use of the image from the ones that are merely decorative. I want you to take seriously at least these: whether a river's flooding is recurrent in a way that licenses building to a magnitude, and whether our shocks are; whether a board can tell, at the moment it must decide, that it is in quiet weather rather than already in the flood; whether the builder survives to see the next flood, given that our trustees and our horizon are not the same length; and what the image does once there are many builders working the same bank at once, each acting on the same reading of it.
- Then the harder question, which is the one I care about most: has the chair kept the metaphor while discarding the argument Machiavelli attaches to it in that same chapter — the diagnosis of the cautious man who prospers while the times suit his temperament and is destroyed when they stop suiting it, and the accompanying claim that no one can change his nature? If so, does the granddaughter's bold reading fare any better, or is it the same selectivity inverted? I need this handled even-handedly, because she is right that his position has a disposition and not only a policy inside it.
- Say plainly whether either conclusion survives the audit. If neither does, say what does: which of the two proposals, or what third thing, you would put to the eleven of us, in terms we could vote on at the retreat.
- If the river is simply the wrong image for what our foundation faces, offer a better one and state what it licenses that the river does not.

Two constraints. I need no scholarship, no bibliography, and no reading list — I need the mapping done honestly from the argument itself. And one trustee is a retired flood-defense engineer who has sat through four meetings saying nothing about any of this; whatever you write has to be able to stand up to him.
