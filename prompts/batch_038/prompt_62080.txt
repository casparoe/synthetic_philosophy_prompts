I write a fortnightly column on ideas for a general-interest magazine, and rather than run yet another summary of the quarrel over whether a reason must be anchored in something the agent already cares about, I want to publish the document the two camps ought to have produced jointly. Please draft, in good faith, the statement that a convinced internalist in Bernard Williams's mould and a convinced external reason realist could both put their names to after a real adversarial collaboration. It should open with the claims both can sign — what the sound deliberative route does and does not establish, what a reason-explanation is supposed to explain, what follows about deliberation that enlarges an agent's motivations rather than merely rearranging them — and each such claim should be substantive enough that a reader learns something, with anything that looks like agreement merely on the surface explicitly flagged as such. Then the residual disagreements, each stated twice: once in wording the internalist accepts as a fair statement of her own position and once in wording the externalist accepts as a fair statement of his, so that a reader can judge how much of the dispute survives the reformulation. If you conclude that some part of it is merely verbal, say which part and why. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions whose settlement would actually move at least one side, with, for each, a case or argument both agree bears on it and each side's prediction written down in advance, including what each would concede about their own view if the prediction failed. Please build the cruxes out of at least three concrete situations rather than abstractions: an agent who, fully informed, unconfused, and given unhurried reflection, remains entirely unmoved by a value she understands perfectly well; an agent whose motivations would be expanded by deliberation so that she ends up holding reasons she could not have articulated beforehand; and a reproacher who blames someone for a failure the agent insists she had no reason to avoid, where the question is whether the blame itself presupposes a reason. Aim for nine hundred to twelve hundred words in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the piece should read like a jointly signed letter, in the first person plural throughout.
