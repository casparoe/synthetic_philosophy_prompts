My sister is in the forth year of a philosophy PhD and for over a year now I've been making her play a game at dinner: I describe someone with some bit of information and she has to say, flat out, whether they know it or not. No hedging allowed. Her record so far:

- A guy reads one email saying his interview is in Room 4B, and he's got ninety minutes to spare and nothing else going on. Does he know where to go? "Obviously."
- Same guy, same single email, but the panel started late, it's the only slot they'll ever give him, and if he walks into the wrong room he's blown it. "No, he doesn't know. He needs to stop someone and ask."
- I then asked whether he'd know in that second version if he'd checked with two different people. "Then yes."
- A midwife draws up a drug after one look at the label, routine delivery, nothing unusual about the case. "Knows." Same midwife, same single look, but it's one of those drugs where the wrong strength kills the baby. "Doesn't know. Someone has to verify it."
- A guy on the sofa on a Sunday who's just watched a programme about how memory quietly rewrites itself, and "remembers" shutting the back window. I said out loud that nothing whatsoever turns on it, he's not leaving the house, worst case he's a bit cold in the night. I was sure she'd say no. She said "he knows, he's just been spooked."
- Me: I sniffed the milk, it's fine, but I've had a bad stomach all week and if I'm wrong I'm useless for days. Her answer was "you know it's fine, you just shouldn't drink it" — which felt like a completely different shape of answer from the ones above, and she wouldn't say why.

The one I'm sending her tomorrow: night guard at a tiny gallery. He glances at the humidity guage on the wall, reads 48%, believes it. Absolutely nothing rides on him — he's not permitted to touch the climate system, the place runs itself, he clocks off in ten minutes and couldn't care less about the job. Meanwhile I've spent the evening reading a maintenance report saying that model of sensor drifts badly and was overdue for recalibration, and the room he's standing in has a painting in it that's finished at 55%. I'm also the one who has to decide in the next five minutes whether to wake a conservator up. And let's say by the time I'm deciding, his shift has ended and he's gone home — there's no decision left for him to make at all.

What's she going to say? I want the candidate rules that fit her whole record laid out — I've gathered there's a fight between people who think it's the subject's own practical situation that moves knowledge around, people who think it's the situation of whoever's saying the word, and people who think knowledge never moves and everyone's just being loose — plus anything else her answers point to, like whether the milk case means she isn't really talking about knowledge at all half the time. For each rule, what it predicts for the guard, then your single best guess and the reason you'd bet on it. Two bits I can't get a handle on: does it make any difference that the high stakes are mine and not his, and should "his shift's over" matter at all? Finish with one more scenario I could send her afterwards that would split the rules better than this one does.

And be straight with me about the state of play here — what's actually settled versus what people who work on this full time still can't agree on. Don't hand me one camp's answer wearing a consensus costume.
