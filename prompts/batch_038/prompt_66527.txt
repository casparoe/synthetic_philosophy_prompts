I'm putting together a session for a film-and-philosophy reading group on Nolan's Memento, and I want a proper critical reading of it through Dennett's idea of the self as a centre of narrative gravity — not the "memory is identity" thing everyone reaches for with this film, though I'd like you to say outright why the narrative lens is the better one, and whether that's just my prejudice. Set the lens up in your own words first: the self as an abstract posit, like a centre of gravity in mechanics, a useful theoretical abstraction that isn't a thing inside anyone and that no deeper fact has to correspond to. Then bring in the rival I think the film actually puts under pressure — the view, Schechtman-ish, that narrative doesn't merely describe a self but constitutes it, answerable to constraints: the story has to hang together and it has to fit the world well enough to be lived and articulated. And keep a third position in the mix as a spoiler: Strawson's case against narrativity, the Diachronic/Episodic split, and his claim that a good life doesn't require self-narration at all.

Here's the thing I really want tested, because its my own hypothesis and I may be over-reading. The film runs on two interleaved strands — the black-and-white motel-room and phone-call scenes moving forward in time, the colour scenes moving backwards — and they fuse at the moment the Polaroid of Jimmy's body finishes developing. My claim is that the strand that runs forward is the *telling* and the strand that runs backward is the *living*: that Nolan has arranged it so narrative time only flows in its normal direction when somebody is narrating, and that this is a formal argument for the centre-of-narrative-gravity picture rather than a directorial gimmick that happens to resemble one. Push hard on that. What would have to be true of the film for it to be more than a nice story I'm telling about a story Leonard is telling?

Then the scene work. Point to specific moments and say what the lens does with them: Sammy Jankis as recounted down the phone, and Teddy's claim that Sammy is really Leonard's own history; Jimmy whispering the name Sammy as he dies, which is what first makes Leonard doubt; the annotations on the Polaroids, especially Leonard writing on Teddy's photo that he shouldn't be believed; and the closing stretch where Leonard burns the photographs and deliberately manufactures the clue his future self will follow. On that last one I want a straight answer: is it an act of self-authorship that only a narrative view of the self can make sense of, or is it the refutation of one — a self that knowingly corrupts its own evidence isn't constituting itself, it's dismantling itself? And can a narrative-gravity theorist tell the difference between self-narration and mere fiction spun about someone else, or does the Sammy story show that distinction collapsing?

Be honest about two objections, because the reading has to survive them. One, someone in my group will say the amnesia is medically cartoonish, that no real anterograde amnesiac behaves like Leonard, so the film is a puzzle box using a disease as a plot device — say whether that undercuts the philosophical reading or is simply beside the point, and dont fudge it. Two, Leonard is far too coherent for a centre of narrative gravity that's this badly wrong: stable style, stable habits, stable investigative competence, all intact while the content is false. If the self is just an abstraction generated by a story, why is the abstraction so robust, and what does that tell us about where the self is supposed to live on this picture?

End by saying what the film adds to the debate that argument alone doesn't — if it does. Does watching the colour strand in reverse give the viewer anything a thought experiment in a paper can't, or is that an aesthetic effect I'm mistaking for evidence? British English throughout, please, and no bibliography or further-reading list — I want the criticism itself, with the philosophy stated rather than cited.
