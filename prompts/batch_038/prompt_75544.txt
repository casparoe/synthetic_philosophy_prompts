**Question.** Assertion is often said to be governed by a norm. Consider three candidates:

- **(K)** One must assert that *p* only if one knows that *p*.
- **(T)** One must assert that *p* only if *p* is true.
- **(JB)** One must assert that *p* only if one justifiably believes that *p*.

Suppose a speaker tells a colleague, "Your flight landed safely." She has checked a tracking app she has always found reliable. Unbeknownst to her, the app's feed has stalled and is displaying the previous day's arrival, which happened to land at the same time; the flight she is asking about has not yet been confirmed by any source she has. In fact it did land safely.

(a) Which of K, T, and JB, if any, does the speaker violate? (b) Is she subject to criticism, and if so of what kind? (c) Do lottery assertions — "Your ticket didn't win," said before the draw, where the ticket did in fact lose — help to decide between K and T? Defend your answers.

**Candidate's script.** The three norms differ in what they make a condition on the act of asserting. T is the weakest, requiring only that what one says be the case; JB adds that the speaker's belief be well founded; K is the strongest, since knowledge entails both truth and justified belief. K is factive in a way JB is not: one cannot know a falsehood, although one can justifiably believe one. It is worth saying at the outset that a speaker can violate a norm of assertion without saying anything false, and this is what makes K an interesting proposal rather than a restatement of the obvious requirement that assertions be true.

In the flight case the speaker has violated both K and T. She violated K because her true belief is a matter of luck: the app was showing yesterday's arrival, so although things turned out as she said, she was in no position to ensure that they would. And she violated T for the same reason, since she had no way of guaranteeing the truth of what she was asserting, and a norm of assertion that can be satisfied by accident is not being satisfied at all. Her belief, on the other hand, was perfectly reasonable given the evidence available to her. She consulted a source she had every reason to trust, and there was nothing in the display to alert her to the stalled feed. So JB is met. That JB and K come apart here is exactly the lesson of the Gettier problem: justification, however good, does not amount to knowledge when the truth of the belief owes something to luck.

Whether the speaker is criticisable is a harder question, and I think the answer tells against K. She did nothing epistemically reprehensible. She checked a reliable source, she had no reason to suspect a delay, and nobody could have expected better of her in the circumstances. If she is not blameworthy, then it is difficult to see in what sense she asserted wrongly at all, and a norm that condemns ordinary, conscientious assertion-making is not a norm that speakers could realistically follow. The fact that we cannot criticise her in any useful sense therefore counts against K rather than in its favour.

The lottery case is more helpful, because here the norms genuinely disagree. "Your ticket didn't win," said before the draw to someone whose ticket did in fact lose, satisfies T, since the proposition asserted is true. It also arguably satisfies JB, since the odds make the belief highly reasonable. Yet the assertion strikes us as improper: we feel that the speaker should have kept quiet, and that she would owe a retraction had the ticket won. K explains this directly, because the speaker does not know and so may not assert. That is a strong reason to prefer K to T, and it shows that T is not merely a weaker version of the same requirement. The two are not tracking the same feature of assertions at all, since T can be satisfied in cases where K is violated.
