Write a short story of about 1,800 words that stages the Meno problem: Plato's question of why knowledge is worth more than true belief, given that a true belief about the road to Larissa gets you there just as well as knowledge does, and given his suggestion that true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus — valuable, but liable to wander off unless they are tied down by working out the reason for them.

The setting and cast are these. A terraced coastal city in the third year of a slow drought. Each morning the Water Authority posts one-line bulletins — which cisterns will hold, which springs will go brackish, which wells will fail by a given day — generated by a hydraulic model that nobody outside the Authority may inspect and that has never yet been wrong. Your protagonist is a water-carrier, celebrated for never once having gone to a dry well, who believes the bulletins and nothing else. Her rival is a former surveyor who has spent a decade rebuilding a crude model of the aquifer from public scraps (tide tables, salinity readings, chalked water-lines) and who is often a day late and sometimes simply wrong.

The story must do four things. First, show at length that under ordinary conditions the two are indistinguishable in their consequences. Second, put the swamping objection in a character's mouth and make it a strong argument rather than a straw man: once the bulletin has delivered the truth, what further value could the process that produced it contribute? Third, introduce a perturbation — the bulletins change format, the Authority goes silent for a week, the aquifer enters a regime in which yesterday's truth no longer licenses tomorrow's — where tethered and untethered belief come apart, while also letting the surveyor's understanding mislead her at least once, so that knowledge is not simply handed the trophy. Fourth, include a scene in which someone asks why the city does not go and look at the model itself, and let the answer brush against the puzzle about inquiry that opens the dialogue: one cannot search for what one already knows, and if one did not know it, one would not recognize it upon finding it.

Craft constraints: carry the philosophy through objects, procedure, and dialogue, not exposition; no character names a philosopher or a text; let one recurring physical object — a knotted cord, a float, a chalk line — bear the tethering motif. Avoid the obvious resolutions: the bulletins must not turn out to be false or fraudulent, the Authority must not be unmasked as a charlatan, and the ending must not be the surveyor converting the city.

After the story, add a note of roughly 150 words stating which answer to the Meno problem the fiction implicitly endorses — stability under perturbation, transferability to new situations, a shift from knowledge to understanding, or a revisionist denial that knowledge is worth more at all — together with how confident you are that this is the best available answer, which single point in your own reasoning you are least sure of, and where the story takes liberties with the philosophy or leans on a contested reading of it.
