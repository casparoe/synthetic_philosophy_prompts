I am drafting a section of a paper on material constitution whose aim is diagnostic rather than merely illustrative. The familiar statue-and-lump case persuades most people that two objects occupy one place largely because the lump seems able to survive being flattened and re-rolled while the statue does not; the anti-monist intuition therefore looks modality-driven. I want a case that tests whether that is really where the intuition lives, by disabling the reshaping argument and seeing what, if anything, is left to sustain a pluralist verdict.

Please construct such a case to the following specification.

**Setting.** Ordinary circumstances: no exotic physics, no divine intervention, no vague or gerrymandered boundaries, no appeal to bare thisness. The artefact must be produced from a material that undergoes an irreversible transformation in the making — firing clay is the natural choice, since a fired piece can never be reduced again to workable plastic clay — with the consequence that no available intervention destroys the artefact while leaving the candidate lump intact, or vice versa. If firing will not serve the purpose, substitute another genuinely irreversible process, but say why.

**Timeline.** Give at least four dated moments (preparation and shaping, drying, firing, and a later destruction or transfer of ownership). The parties must disagree about how many objects are present at exactly one of those moments and agree at the rest. The disputed moment may not be disputed merely because it is indeterminate when the artefact came into being: the relevant facts (when shaping was completed, when the kiln reached temperature, when the piece was signed) must be stipulated crisply. If you judge the constraints jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and explain why, rather than quietly relaxing one of them.

**Stakes and length.** The count of objects must settle a practical question — I have in mind an estate dispute in which two clauses of a will pull in different directions, one bequeathing the potter's finished work and the other bequeathing all her materials. The scenario proper should run to roughly 250 words, with the timeline appended.

**Verdicts.** Then report what four positions say: (i) monism, on which the artefact just is its matter; (ii) constitution without identity, on which one object constitutes another without being identical to it; (iii) coincidence pluralism, on which two numerically distinct objects are wholly present in the same place at the same time; (iv) a stage- or counterpart-theoretic treatment, on which how many objects there are depends on the sortal in play and the counterpart relation it licenses. For each, state the count at each moment and identify which of that view's own principles generates the answer, including where a dominant-kinds strategy would be invoked. The verdicts must be derived from the positions rather than built into the description; close this part with a self-audit pointing to any wording in your scenario that pre-judges the count.

**Analysis.** Identify the single feature doing the work — it cannot be a difference in what the objects could survive being reshaped into, since that is excluded by construction — and give the minimal edit that removes it, confirming that all four positions then agree. State the strongest monist attack, addressing in particular the objection that firing does not destroy the lump but merely changes it qualitatively, so that the case never had two candidates in the first place. Say whether your case remains one of synchronic material constitution or has quietly become a diachronic identity puzzle about successive objects, and whether that shift matters for the diagnostic aim. Finally, deliver the verdict I actually want: does the anti-monist intuition survive the loss of the reshaping argument, and if some residue remains, is it historical, sortal, or of some other kind?
