Reading group on the evolution of religion, and I've built an arguement against costly-signalling accounts that I think holds together but whose conclusion I don't believe, so either I've made a mistake or a lot of people have. Everything below is my own reconstruction — no paper runs it in this order — so please take the steps exactly as I've stated them rather than as somebody else's view.

(1) A costly religious practice works as an honest signal of commitment only if, for a person who isn't committed, the cost of performing it outweighs what they get from being taken as committed.
(2) The cost that matters is residual: what's left after you subtract whatever the practice directly gives the performer — the calories at the communal meal, the discipline or health effects, the rehearsal that makes coordinated group action smoother, the prestige that attaches to any conspicuous role. I need this stipulation, because otherwise the theory can always locate some cost somewhere and stops being falsifiable.
(3) What a faker gets is the trust, preferential dealing and informal insurance the group extends to apparent members.
(4) So if the signal is honest, residual cost exceeds that payoff for every non-member who performs the practice.
(5) Meanwhile the committed member pays the same residual cost, and must be getting at least as much out of the arrangement as the faker stood to get — if loyal members were netting less than a con artist would have, why would anybody stay loyal? So for them, payoff ≥ residual cost.
(6) But it's one practice, performed by one body, in front of the same audience, and the audience can't see inside anyone's head. Nothing in that situation could make the very same public act cost one performer more than another, or pay one performer more than another.
(7) So the differential (1) needs doesn't exist: either residual cost exceeds the payoff for everyone, in which case nobody performs the practice and there's no signal, or it does for no one, in which case everybody performs it and the signal carries no information.
(8) The theory's best evidence doesn't rescue it. Strictness predicts group survival, but strict groups also select — people who won't or can't comply never join, or drift out — and selection on its own generates that correlation with no information transmitted at any point.
(9) Therefore the costs work as a filter, not a signal, and costly-signalling accounts of religion should be replaced by screening-and-selection accounts.

Which step do you give up, and what does giving it up cost? Please don't tell me its sound: the conclusion can't be right, since if it were, forty years of behavioural ecology would have been describing something other than what it says it describes. I'd rather keep (2) and (8) than anything else — (2) because dropping it seems to leave the theory unfalsifiable, and (8) because the selection worry looks empirically real whatever the theory claims. I'm least attached to (9) and half suspicious of it already.

I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey of the places it might slip: pick one step, name the ambiguity or scope slide that made it look acceptable to a careful reader, and then price it — what the theory loses, and what the neighbouring positions have to say about that step. In particular give me your ruling on the cheap-talk camp (honesty needs aligned interests at least as much as it needs cost), on the differential-marginal-cost condition in the Grafen-style models, on the credibility-enhancing-displays move (what's signalled is sincere belief rather than coalition loyalty), and on whether filter-versus-signal in (9) is a real distinction or a relabelling — I suspect it's just the economics distinction between the informed party moving first and the uninformed party moving first, with separating equilibria either way.
