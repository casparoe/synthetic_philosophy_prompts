I have been reading LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and their successors for something like fifteen years, and the habit I took away from all of it is that a confident, fluent answer is a thing to be taken apart rather than admired. I am now working as a TA for an upper-division philosophy of language course, and I am grading the short-answer section of the final. I have finished the rest of the batch and want this one response assessed on its own before I write my comments.

The question, as it appeared on the exam:

> Consider the following four sentences, any of which a speaker might utter sincerely and with full understanding.
>
> (A) It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining.
> (B) It was raining, but I did not believe that it was raining.
> (C) It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining.
> (D) It is raining, but she does not believe that it is raining.
>
> Part 1. For each of (A) through (D), say whether a sincere utterance of it would be absurd in the way characteristic of Moore's paradox, and explain why. Where you can, give one account that yields all four of your verdicts rather than four separate remarks.
>
> Part 2. Evaluate the following proposed diagnosis: "The absurdity of (A) is that in asserting that p a speaker represents herself as believing that p, while the second conjunct of (A) denies precisely what that act of assertion represents. The act therefore defeats itself, and the absurdity is pragmatic rather than logical." Does this diagnosis succeed? In particular, does it explain why merely believing the content of (A) — never uttering it, never writing it, never implying it to anyone — seems absurd as well? And does it account for your verdicts on (B) through (D)?
>
> Part 3. State what your answer to Part 2 implies about whether a person could believe the content of (A) at all, and defend that implication rather than simply asserting it.

The student's answer:

> (A) is absurd, and it is the paradigm case. Note first what the absurdity is not: (A) is not a contradiction. Both conjuncts can be true together, since it may be raining while I have formed no view about the weather, and the conjunction is therefore perfectly consistent and often true. The absurdity lies in the performance. In asserting the first conjunct I present myself as believing that it is raining; the second conjunct denies exactly what that presentation commits me to. The act of assertion defeats itself, and the failure is pragmatic rather than logical.
>
> (B) is not absurd. The second conjunct concerns a belief I no longer hold, so nothing in my present performance is undercut: I can coherently assert now that it was raining and that at the time I did not believe it was. Such assertions are frequently true and unremarkable — if it rained all afternoon while I slept in a windowless room, I can say on waking that it was raining but I did not believe it.
>
> (C) is not absurd, or at any rate not absurd in the way (A) is. Here the second conjunct does not deny that I believe it is raining; it reports a further and contrary belief. There is no self-defeat, because a speaker may acknowledge that she is in error: she says what she takes to be the case about the weather and then adds, truthfully, that her own doxastic state is at odds with it. If anything, a sincere utterance of (C) is a frank confession of akrasia — the speaker knows better but cannot help believing otherwise — which is a recognizable human condition rather than a paradox.
>
> (D) is plainly not absurd. What another person believes has no bearing on what my assertion commits me to, so there is nothing for the performance to defeat. (D) can be true and can be known: from the window I can see both the downpour and that my colleague, facing the other way with headphones on, has not noticed it.
>
> The proposed diagnosis is correct as far as it goes. It is the explanation I gave for (A), and it also predicts my verdicts on (B) and (D), since in neither case does the speaker's present act represent her as holding a belief that the content she asserts denies. Its limitation is that it is confined to speech acts, and so it says nothing directly about believing the content of (A), which strikes many people as equally absurd. But the limitation can be removed. Belief is standardly understood as a kind of inner assertion: to believe that p is to endorse p, to take a stand on how things are. Endorsement has the same self-defeating structure as assertion when its content denies the endorsement, so the diagnosis carries over to belief without amendment. Evans's observation that belief is transparent — that one answers the question whether to believe that p by attending solely to the question whether p — confirms the result, since a subject who follows that route outward to the weather can never assemble the conjunction. The Moore-paradoxical belief is not merely difficult to form but impossible, which is what the diagnosis should predict.
