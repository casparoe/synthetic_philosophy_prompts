Last month I was appointed chair of the diagnostic-error review committee at the hospital where I practice radiology, and at our second meeting I ran into an argument I cannot untangle. I had proposed that a second reader of certain studies be blinded to the clinical history, on the grounds that knowing the referring clinician's suspected diagnosis shapes what a reader reports. A senior colleague objected that the proposal is naive, invoking the slogan "all observation is theory-laden": no reader ever encounters a film neutrally, he said, so blinding cannot remove a bias that perception itself already supplies. Two weeks later he reached for the same line to dismiss a separate question about whether our structured reporting template changes what readers notice in the first place. I suspect something has gone badly wrong there, but I am a physician rather than a philosopher, and I have been unable to say what.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that sentence might be making? For each entry I would like the following: a statement of exactly what the claim asserts and about what — the character of the experience itself, the wording of the report the observer produces, the availability of a neutral arbiter between rival theories, or the causal influence of expectation on visual processing (what I believe is sometimes called the penetrability of perception, though I am not certain I have the term right); a verdict on whether it is a platitude no informed person disputes, a substantive and contested thesis, or simply false; and an assessment of whether it would actually support my colleague's conclusion that blinding the second reader buys nothing.

Two further things. Please mark where the menu conflates the phenomenology of expert recognition with a claim about how the visual system works, and say what kind of evidence would discriminate between those. And note which readings are vulnerable to the objection that what looks like altered seeing is really altered judging, reporting, or remembering. I do not need references, attributions, or a reading list — I need the distinctions and the verdicts, in plain American English, so that I can bring them to the next committee meeting.
