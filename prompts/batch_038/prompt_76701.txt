So I work at a firm that sells political-risk analysis to institutional investors, and about two years ago we started running an internal forecasting program. Nothing fancy: roughly forty questions a quarter with fixed resolution dates, people log probabilities, everything gets scored with Brier scores, small accuracy bonus at the end of the year. There's one protocol rule I've started to find strange. If two registered forecasters land more than 20 points apart on the same question, they have to have a conversation before the numbers get locked, and the protocol doc says the point of the conversation is to establish which forecaster has information the other lacks. Except that's almost never what happens. About half the time they talk for twenty minutes, discover they read the same three newsletters and have no private information whatsoever, and then split the difference and write "compromise" in the log. Nobody treats that as the protocol failing. It's basically what the protocol is for.

Meanwhile the risk committee one floor down runs the opposite norm. They have to name a devil's advocate for every major call, they do premortems where you assume the position blew up and write the obituary in advance, they pay outsiders to come argue against the house view, and a meeting that ends in consensus gets flagged as needing a second meeting. Same firm, sometimes literally the same people in both rooms.

I've been asked to write a one page internal note explaining why convergence is the right norm in one room and divergence is the right norm in the other, and I've hit a wall, because trying to write it made me realize I dont know what our forecasting protocol actually believes about probability. Does it think there is a single correct number out there for "will the incumbent be out of office by March," one that a well-informed person could in principle land on, so that disagreement is just a symptom of somebody missing something? I vaguely remember a game theory lecture about Aumann's result that two rational agents with a common prior can't agree to disagree once they know each other's beliefs, and about the assumption underneath it being contentious — the common prior assumption, where you claim priors only ever reflect information, so anyone with the same information must have the same prior. If our protocol quietly assumes that, then splitting the difference is incoherent on its own terms: two equally informed, equally rational people shouldn't have had different numbers to begin with, and if they do, one of them is wrong, and averaging with a wrong number just dilutes the right one.

So what I want is a careful reconstruction of the theory of probability, or of belief, that the forecasting side actually presupposes — and then a separation of which parts the practice strictly requires from which parts it merely sits comfortably with. My suspicion is that a Savage-style subjectivist, someone who thinks priors encode upbringing and taste and not just information, could run the identical Brier-score program without changing a single rule, in which case the protocol isn't committed to the thing I thought it was committed to. Is that right? And is there some variant of the practice — a different scoring rule, a different handling of the disagreement conversations, an aggregation policy — that really would force the common prior on you, so I can tell whether we've stumbled into it or could still avoid it? Also: which rival views does the current setup quietly rule out, especially the forecasters who mutter that there's no fact of the matter whether a coup happens and that 55 versus 45 is just a matter of how you read it? Our program manager tells them to pick a number anyway because the score needs one. Is that a neutral administrative move or a substantive philosophical one?

Then the committee. Are the two rooms actually inconsistent, or are they answering different questions — one about belief in a resolvable event, one about action under a one-shot strategic situation — such that a single coherent theory licenses both norms? If they're inconsistent, which one should give? And if we replaced the forecasting side's implicit view with the subjectivist one, what would concretely have to change in how we run the thing: the mandatory conversations, the scoring, the bonuses, the way we treat two people who stay 20 points apart for a year and both end up well calibrated?

Please answer in plain continuous prose, like an email or a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Lead with your verdict on whether the two rooms contradict each other, then work through the rest.
