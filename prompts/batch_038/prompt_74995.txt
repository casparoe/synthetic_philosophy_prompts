I've been trying to work out when, if ever, a ceremonial head of state should act against ministerial advice, and I've got a pile of verdicts I mostly trust but can't get to line up. Roughly in the order I thought of them:

Kerr dismissing Whitlam in 1975: wrong. The government still had its majority in the House, the deadlock was real but the Senate blocking supply wasn't obviously a constitutional crime, and a head of state who picks that moment to intervene has put himself into politics permanently. Though I'll admit I can't say what Kerr should have done instead, and I don't think that's just squeamishness.

Haakon in Norway in 1940, telling his cabinet he would abdicate rather than accept a Quisling government: right, obviously right. Which is embarrassing, because formally it's the same shape of act as Kerr's — a head of state staking the office on a substantive outcome against the advice he's being given.

Juan Carlos going on television in 1981 to tell the armed forces to stand down: right. But I suspect I'd call it a coup attempt if it had failed, and I don't like that my verdict tracks how things turned out.

Baudouin in 1990 letting his own government declare him unable to reign for a day and a half so the ministers could promulgate the abortion bill themselves: wrong, and worse than a flat refusal would have been, because it dressed up a personal veto as a formality and made everyone complicit in pretending.

George V's secret undertaking in 1910 to create enough peers to push the Parliament Bill through if the Lords kept blocking: right, even though it was secret, even though it bound a future monarch, and even though it worked only because it was never used.

King's consent — the requirement that a bill touching the Crown's prerogative or the royal estates have consent signified before parliament can even debate it, plus the reporting a few years back that the mechanism gets used to get draft bills rewritten before introduction: the worst item on the list by a distance. Nobody sees it happen, nobody has to justify it, and a Commons committee concluded it's pure House procedure that Parliament could abolish by resolution without any legislation at all.

The Queen granting the 2019 prorogation on advice a court later found unlawful: blameless, she had no choice. And 'she had no choice' is the fact that ought to worry me rather than comfort me.

Japan, where the constitution says the Emperor has no powers related to government at all and even an abdication needs a special act of the Diet: I admire this and also think its a loss, because an institution that can never act wrongly can never act as a check either.

My tentative principle is: act against advice only when following the advice would disable the mechanism that makes advice authoritative — parliament's capacity to hold the government to account, or the electorate's. That gets Haakon, Juan Carlos, George V and the 2019 case, and it condemns Baudouin, since signing a bill you hate disables nothing. But it also says Kerr was right, which I dont want, and it is completely silent on king's consent, the one I care about most.

So is there a principle that does better? I have a suspicion the missing ingredient is publicity — that the act has to be one the head of state can avow and be answered for — except that would condemn George V's secret pledge, which I want to keep, so I'm not sure I actually believe it.

And tell me what your principle says about three cases I can't call. A PM who has plainly lost a confidence vote refuses to resign and asks for a dissolution, and a viable alternative government exists: refuse? Then the same PM, same request, but no alternative exists — people always cite Lascelles' 1950 letter here, and it makes the availability of a replacement PM with a working majority one of the conditions for refusing, but I don't see why the existence of a substitute should decide whether an improper request gets granted. Finally: a monarch's private secretary mentions informally to a minister that the palace would prefer a bill delayed, the minister complies, and the monarch never knew and never would have asked. I think that's worse than the monarch doing it himself, which seems backwards, since it involves less agency, not more.

Please commit. Pick the principle you think is right — one, or at most two with a clear account of which governs when — state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case neither of us has heard of, say which of my verdicts each clause is there to catch, work out what it implies for the three cases above, and be straight about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up rather than quietly reinterpreting them. Don't hand me a menu of options with pros and cons and leave me to choose; argue for yours. Keep it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
