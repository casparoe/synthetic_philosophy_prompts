I teach philosophy of science and formal epistemology, so I'm completely fluent in the estimation-theory way of talking about everything — estimands, identification, priors, loss functions, bias-variance, sufficiency, missing-data mechanisms, out-of-sample validation, the whole toolkit. What I'm not fluent in is the theory-of-history literature. I know the slogans — Ranke's line about the historian's job being just to show what actually happened, Carr's insistence that the facts never reach us except already selected and framed by whoever is writing, Hayden White's claim that the story-shape a history has (romance, tragedy, comedy, irony) is something the historian brings rather than finds — but I've never done the close reading and I dont trust my own paraphrase of any of them.

So here's what I want: translate those three positions into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me the residue report. Don't cheat, and by that I mean this — my vocabulary comes with a built-in assumption, namely that there is some well-defined thing out there being estimated and that getting it right is a matter of fit to it. White is denying precisely that. So if you translate him by quietly reinstating the assumption, you've smuggled, and I'd rather you said where you had to.

A few things I especially want stress-tested. First, my hobby-horse: I've been telling my seminar that the archival problem is just the missing-not-at-random problem, that Carr is making a selection-bias point in fancier clothes, and that White's emplotment business reduces to the observation that you cant write down an estimator without first committing to an estimand and a loss function — which would make him either trivially right or wrong about something other than what he thinks he's wrong about. Am I onto something, or is that the kind of translation that feels like insight only because it's lossy? Second, what happens to the word objectivity under translation — does it come through as one notion or does it split into four or five things the debate keeps conflating? Third, run one concrete case through the machinery: two historians, same documents, same episode, one plots it as tragedy and one as farce. What is that disagreement in my terms, and is it a disagreement about accuracy at all, or has accuracy dropped out as a possible description of it?

Then the report proper. Which claims come across clean, which only come across bent out of shape, and which have no expression whatsoever in estimation talk. And for the inexpressible ones, tell me which diagnosis you actually favor — that my vocabulary is missing something real and important, or that the original claims were never as clear as they sounded when I was reading the slogans. No bibliography and no reading list, please; I know the names, I want the mapping and the damage assessment.
