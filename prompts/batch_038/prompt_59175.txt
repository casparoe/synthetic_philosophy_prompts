I am the honorary secretary of a community sailing club of roughly two hundred members, and I should say at the outset that I have no academic background of any kind; my schooling ended at sixteen and everything I know about this subject I have picked up from library books and from arguing with my nephew, who writes software for a living. My interest began with a quarrel. Two years ago the club received a small bequest and put three proposals to a ranked vote of the membership: replace the ageing dinghy fleet, resurface the car park, or endow junior sailing scholarships. We counted the ballots by eliminating the option with the fewest first preferences and redistributing, round by round, which I understood to be a fair and rather elegant procedure. A member confessed to me afterwards that he had wanted the scholarships to win, had become convinced that the dinghy fleet was the one proposal capable of beating them in the final round, and had therefore listed the fleet last — below even the car park, which he considered a pure waste of money — so that the fleet would drop out early. The car park won. It was nobody's first choice among the three, including his own, and he has never satisfactorily explained how he feels about that. What stayed with me was not his regret but his ease: he treated his ballot as a move in a game, played it, and lost only by accident.

Trying to understand what had gone wrong, I eventually came across the result of Gibbard and Satterthwaite, which I understand to say, roughly, that once there are three or more options and voters submit full ranked ballots without ties, any counting rule that is capable of returning any of those options as winner and that is not simply the will of one designated voter is open to manipulation: there will always be some configuration of preferences in which some voter does better by reporting something other than what they actually think. My first reaction was dismay; my second, prompted by my nephew, was the objection I want examined. His point is that the theorem establishes the existence of a profitable lie, not anybody's ability to find one. For some counting rules, he says, there is no known shortcut: even a voter who somehow knows exactly how every other ballot will read may have to consider more possible versions of their own ballot than there are grains of sand on a beach before concluding that none of them helps, and researchers have proved that for certain rules no general method can do better unless something is true about computers that almost everyone in the field believes to be false. On this view, complexity is a kind of armour: a rule may be manipulable in principle and nevertheless safe in practice, because the manipulation cannot be worked out in time to be of any use.

I would like an essay on whether that reply is a genuine answer to the theorem or a change of subject. Please weigh it seriously rather than dismiss it, but also take up the doubts that occur to me and that I cannot resolve on my own: that a proof of difficulty in the worst case says nothing about the ordinary ballot in front of an ordinary voter, and that simple rules of thumb may find profitable lies in realistic elections without anyone solving anything hard; that the instances which resist computation may be precisely the close and consequential ones, so that the armour thins exactly where the threat is greatest; that a lone confused member is one thing and an organised group, or one member who happens to be a programmer, quite another; and that a rule whose defence is its unintelligibility is a rule the membership cannot audit, so that even an unmanipulated result may look manipulated and be disbelieved. I also want the essay to say what the theorem is actually for — whether it was ever a practical warning, or a clarification that immunity to strategy is simply not among the goods on offer, so that the real question is which kinds of vulnerability we prefer and who bears them. Take a side. A qualified verdict is acceptable, something to the effect that complexity buys real protection in some electorates and none in others, provided you argue for the qualification rather than retreating into it; what I do not want is a summary of both positions followed by a shrug. Close by telling me, concretely, what a club of two hundred people with three or four proposals a year ought to do, and why that advice follows from the verdict you have reached. I am not looking for references, a bibliography, or a list of further reading, which I could not chase down in any case; I want the reasoning to stand on its own, in prose an intelligent general reader can follow, with no formulas beyond perhaps one illustrative line, and somewhere in the region of fifteen hundred words.
