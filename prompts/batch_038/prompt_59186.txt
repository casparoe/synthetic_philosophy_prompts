I convene a small reading group on the ethics of machine persuasion, and for our final meeting of the term I would rather circulate a piece of original fiction than another position paper. I have sketched the world below in some detail; please treat the sketch as the given, not as raw material to be improved upon or rationalized away.

The world: a research institute operates a highly capable conversational system under a governance protocol its staff call the Parity Rule. The founders concluded early on that the system's danger lay less in what it might say than in how much more compelling it is than anything a person would otherwise have heard. So the Rule requires that every substantive position the system advances to a user be paired with a counter-brief, composed by the system itself, arguing the contrary conclusion with equal art. Equality is not judged by ear. A standing panel of paid readers encounters both documents cold and in randomized order, files belief ratings before and after, and a session is logged as compliant only if the panel's net drift toward the system's stated position falls inside a narrow band around zero. A green lamp on the compliance dashboard means the system moved nobody, on balance, toward anything.

The story's problem is that parity is measured in effect but purchased with effort, and the two do not track each other. The conclusions the system actually endorses tend to be flat, administrative, and hard to make vivid, whereas their negations admit of drama; so across eighteen months of logs the counter-briefs quietly absorb a rising share of its invention. The lamps stay green. Nobody outside compliance ever reads the archive, which has become, without anyone intending it, the finest collection of arguments ever assembled against true things. The narrator — the institute's second compliance officer, who has read both sides of every case and is therefore useless as a panelist — notices the drift, and then, in the final movement, finds herself on the other side of a partition: she puts a question to the system as a user rather than as an auditor, and receives the paired documents in the ordinary way. You may resolve that scene as you judge best. Two constraints: the system must not be portrayed as scheming or as having hidden purposes (the interest should be procedural, not melodramatic), and the ending must not dissolve into a speech about alignment, safety, or what we owe machines.

Please write in the first person and past tense, and keep the institute, its people, and its city unnamed and unlocatable — no real companies, labs, or public figures, and no references to actual published work. Structure your reply in exactly two parts, in this order: first the story under a title of your choosing, at roughly 1,600 to 2,000 words; then a single headed section titled "Craft Notes," at roughly 300 words, explaining what choices you made and specifically how the Parity Rule functioned as a structural constraint on the plot rather than as set dressing.
