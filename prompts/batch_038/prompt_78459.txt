so I never studied any of this, I'm self employed in a fairly boring line of work, but I read that essay of Keynes's about the economic possibilities for his grandchildren a few years back and it lodged in my brain, and its been read again a couple of times since, plus bits and pieces I've picked up about his own crowd in London and how they thought the point of being alive was people and art and seeing things clearly, apparently from some old philosophy book they'd all got hold of at university. Anyway, without deciding to I seem to have assembled a whole position on the good life and money, and I want somebody who actually knows the arguments to go through it and tell me whether it hangs together, because I have a feeling parts of it can't all be true and I can't see which parts. Here's what I hold.

(1) The material condition Keynes was waiting for has basically arrived. Real living standards in the rich countries are several times what they were when he wrote, so the hard scarcity that made the money motive look respectable is gone. What didn't come true was the fifteen hour week, and I read that as a failure of what people want, not of what we can make.

(2) The leftover hours are explained by the relative needs rather than the absolute ones — wanting to be a step ahead of the people around you. That's why getting richer doesn't buy you out of it: that kind of wanting has no ceiling, so the rat race survives abundance.

(3) But I also don't think the line between absolute and relative stays put. Things that were luxuries in his day are things you can't hold down a job or raise kids without now — a phone, broadband, a car unless you live in a big city, the shoes the other kids have. So the absolute side keeps quietly swallowing stuff from the relative side, and the threshold moves.

(4) On what the good life actually consists in, I'm with Keynes's friends: the people you're close to, and giving real attention to beautiful or true things. You don't need equipment for it, you don't need to be talented at it, and you don't have to earn it first.

(5) At the same time I think most people come apart without something definite they have to do. My dad took early retirement at fifty eight and it wrecked him, two years of it. Give a person all the time in the world and no task and it goes badly far more often than it goes well.

(6) I think cutting the working week — four days now, three later — is the right lever, and I want it as public policy rather than as something a generous employer offers and takes back. Partly for the hours it hands back, partly because it forces the gain to be shared out.

(7) And I think you can't switch the accumulation habit off on a timetable. The idea that we keep pretending greed is a virtue for another hundred years and then drop it once we're comfortably off assumes there's a switch somewhere, and I don't believe there is. People who've spent forty years being rewarded for wanting more dont stop wanting more because the calendar says the waiting period is over.

So: go through those and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and which ones only look like they do. Please don't just flag everything as equally troubled, because then I learn nothing from you. Rank the real conflicts by how bad they are, and for each say what has to be given up, what the cheapest repair looks like, and what that repair costs me — I'd rather hear the cost than be told there isn't one. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most of the mess at once. And don't guess at which of these I'm fondest of or would defend hardest; I genuinely don't know yet, I might drop any of them including the ones I'd have bet on.

One more thing on how you answer: where this is actually settled among people who've thought about it properly, say so plainly, and where it's genuinely still contested — even contested between people who agree on all the facts — say that too and don't hand me one side of a live argument as though everyone accepted it. And please write it all as continuous prose, like an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Numbers in brackets are fine when you're pointing back at one of mine.
