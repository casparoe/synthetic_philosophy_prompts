Been teaching myself about inverse reinforcement learning on and off for a couple months (mostly blog posts and lecture videos, never took a class in any of this) and I've talked myself into an argument that seems to prove way more than I want it to. I can't find the exit.

(1) A preference is only ever revealed through behavior. There's no other channel — you can't read it off someone's brain, and what they *say* they prefer is just more behavior.
(2) For any chunk of behavior you like, you can find a reward function plus some story about noise, mistaken beliefs, or a shifting state that makes that behavior look optimal. You can always bend the reward — add a constant, rescale it, tack on a shaping term that depends on where the agent is heading — and the choices come out identical.
(3) So the same behavior is compatible with a lot of different preferences, and nothing in the behavior picks one of them out as *the* right one.
(4) So when an IRL algorithm reports a reward function, it hasn't learned the agent's preference. It's produced a rationalization of the data — one that was available before anyone watched the agent do anything.
(5) Worse, the whole procedure can't fail. If the learned reward predicts something dumb, you say the noise model was wrong, or the feature set was incomplete, or the human's preference was non-stationary and you needed a different state description. There's always a fix that leaves the method untouched.
(6) A method that can't be broken by any possible evidence isn't telling you about the world, it's just a story you tell about whatever happens.
(7) So preference attribution is unfalsifiable.
(8) And notice (7) isn't about computers. When I say my sister prefers the window seat, I've watched her behavior and built a story, and if she does something that doesn't fit I'll say she was tired, or she didn't know the flight was long, or she's changed her mind. Same moves, same immunity from evidence.
(9) So if (7) is a fatal objection to IRL, it's a fatal objection to the whole practice of assigning preferences to people.
(10) And I'm not willing to bite that bullet, because there's a case where I'm obviously learning something and not just retconning. My nephew has a stuffed rabbit. I've watched him with it enough times to make calls about situations I've never seen him in — that he'll go straight into a muddy puddle rather than leave it on the sidewalk, that he won't nap at a house where he doesn't have it, that he'll scream if I pretend to toss it in the trash. I make the call *before* and I'm right more often than chance, and when I'm wrong I actually update. So either that isn't knowledge, in which case (1) is doing something sneaky, or one of the steps between (2) and (6) is broken.

I keep circling back to the move from (3) to (4) and I can't tell if it's a real gap or if I'm just uncomfortable. If a reward function that explains every choice I've seen doesn't tell me the preference, what would? Which step do you actually reject, and why did it look fine to me? And if the answer is that I have to keep most of the argument and give up (1) instead, tell me what giving up (1) costs — because (1) feels like the one premise I can't imagine dropping.
