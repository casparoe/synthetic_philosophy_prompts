For nineteen years I have worked for a small water district in a mountain valley. I write the shift schedules, keep the maintenance logs, and order parts. I have no university training in anything, but I have turned one question over in my mind almost daily for the past two years, and I want a careful judgment on it rather than a survey of what other people have written. I am not asking you to send me off to read anything. I have come across the expression "Chesterton's fence," and I know the general conservative claim that long-standing arrangements carry a presumption in their favor; I do not want either of those explained to me. I want your reasoning about a particular argument that I watched happen in my own workplace.

The district operates an earthfill dam built in the early 1960s. Since shortly after construction, the standing order has been that at the start of each shift two operators walk the full length of the crest and back — about forty minutes — with no instrument, no checklist, and no written report, though they are supposed to mention anything unusual at handover. We now have piezometers, a seepage weir with a continuous recorder, and cameras covering the crest. Our operations manager, Diane, who joined two years ago with an engineering degree, has proposed abolishing the walk. Our senior operator, Emmett, who has been here thirty-one years, opposes it. I was in the room for most of their argument. Neither is stupid and neither was arguing in bad faith.

Here is how it went, as nearly as I can reproduce it:

1. Diane said the walk consumes roughly five hundred operator-hours a year, that the crew tells her they experience it as pointless, and that anything it could detect is now detected continuously and more sensitively by instruments that do not tire or lose attention.

2. Emmett said the walk has twice found what the instruments did not: muddy seepage at the downstream toe in 1994, and a slough on the upstream face in 2011, both noticed by someone walking and both confirmed afterward. He said instruments report only on the exact places they were installed, chosen by people who had to guess where trouble would come from.

3. Diane said both catches predate the present instrumentation and the present weir; that if the real worry is spatial coverage gaps, the remedy is to instrument the gaps; and that a person walking at a fixed pace along a crest sees strictly less than a camera sweep of the same crest.

4. Emmett then said the walk is also where apprentices learn the dam — every anomaly gets named and pointed at, in place — and the only occasion on which the outgoing and incoming shifts are together outdoors. Diane said that if those are the goods at stake they should be claimed openly and produced deliberately: a structured orientation and a scheduled handover meeting would deliver both, more reliably, and could actually be evaluated. Emmett said she could not produce them deliberately, because the value of the walk depends on its not being announced as training. The moment an apprentice knows he is being taught, he performs for the teacher instead of looking at the dam; and a handover held at a table substitutes a report about the dam for contact with it.

5. Diane said that last move makes his position untestable. If every failure to find evidence that the walk earns its cost is itself explained away as evidence that its worth is the kind of thing evidence cannot reach, then no practice could ever be shown to be worthless, and his argument is doing no work.

6. Emmett said he had no need of that, because the burden runs the other way. Sixty years of continuous operation is a fact about the world that Diane's proposal has to answer for. Keeping the walk and being wrong costs time; ending it and being wrong may not be recoverable at all.

7. A few weeks later, pulling files for an unrelated matter, I found the 1962 order that instituted the walk. It was written by the district's first engineer, and the reason it gives has nothing to do with either safety or training: the walk was ordered to create a twice-daily record of visual observation that could be produced if a claim arose, following a drowning suit against the district that had been settled the year before. Our counsel says such a record would carry no weight today. Diane said this ends the matter — the fence was erected as legal cover, and even that purpose is gone. Emmett said the memo proves the opposite of what she thinks: the reason a practice was first written down is not the reason it went on being done for sixty years, and what the walk was for in 1962 says nothing about what it does now.

8. Diane's final move is the one I have not been able to answer. She said the walk's survival is not evidence of anything, because it persisted for the reason useless things usually persist: ending it would have required one identifiable person to sign a name to ending it, while continuing it cost each individual operator almost nothing personally. That is drift, not selection, and drift leaves no trace of value behind it.

Neither budged. The proposal is on hold pending the next board meeting.

What I want is an adjudication of the argument as it was actually made, not a general essay about tradition. Please take the eight moves in order and say who was right in each, rating every move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Then address these questions, which are the ones I keep circling. Whose burden is it here, and does the answer shift depending on whether the cost of the practice falls in hours or in dollars? Does the 1962 memo matter at all, and if so, whom does it favor? Is Emmett's claim in move 4 — that a good can be destroyed by being aimed at directly — a real claim about how institutions work, or a way of stepping around Diane's challenge? Is Diane's unfalsifiability charge in move 5 fair, given that Emmett disclaimed that reading in move 6? And is her drift-versus-selection point in move 8 genuinely decisive against the argument from sixty years of survival, or does it only show that survival is weaker evidence than Emmett treats it as being?

Two things I have been unable to settle alone. First, I keep a habit I invented for myself: before retiring any procedure in my own work, I write in a binder what I believe that procedure does and why, date the entry, and re-read it a year later to see whether I was right. Does that habit engage the disagreement between Diane and Emmett, or does it miss what they were actually fighting about? Second, tell me what both of them overlooked, and tell me whether some fact about this dam would settle the argument if we possessed it — or whether the disagreement would survive any quantity of data, in which case I would like to know what sort of disagreement it really is.
