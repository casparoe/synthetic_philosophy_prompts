You are an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and in particular someone with a talent for locating the exact joint at which an otherwise plausible package of views stops holding together. Please work carefully and in order: first restate my package in your own words and work through the concrete case in (7) explicitly, writing out the relevant conditional probabilities rather than relying on how puzzles of this shape usually go; only then render a verdict.

Context. I am giving a position statement at a small workshop on paradoxes of uncertainty. The house rule is that the presenter circulates a numbered list of commitments in advance and the other participants spend the session trying to break it; presenters are expected not to indicate which item they intend to defend to the end, so that the attacks land where they will. The room is mostly subjective Bayesians, plus one frequentist who considers the entire puzzle a category error. My list is as follows.

1. The familiar switch argument — the other envelope is equally likely to hold half of what I am holding or twice it, so switching gains me a quarter in expectation — is unsound. I want to be explicit that I am not resting this on the complaint that the relevant expectations are undefined or infinite: I think the argument fails even when every expectation involved is finite and perfectly well defined.

2. Any prior over the smaller amount that is doing real work in the puzzle must be proper. I reject improper priors, and uniform distributions over all the positive amounts, as ways of running the puzzle. This is a restriction on the puzzle only: I use improper priors cheerfully elsewhere, as limits of proper ones in estimation problems and in statistical mechanics, and I see nothing incoherent in that.

3. Rational choice is a matter of maximizing expected value conditional on everything the agent actually knows, and the amount I observe on opening my envelope is genuine evidence about what the other envelope holds. I am an evidentialist in the ordinary sense of updating on what I learn, and I see nothing suspicious about doing it here.

4. Both amounts were settled before I ever looked, and nothing I do now alters them. I do not see how an act that cannot change what is in the other envelope could turn out better than refraining from it.

5. Nothing in the description of the puzzle fixes a unit or a scale. Doubling every amount in the story leaves the story unchanged, so whatever the right policy is, it has to look the same at every amount: if switching is right when I see X, it must be right when I see 2X. I cannot accept that the rational thing to do turns on crossing some particular monetary threshold, since nothing in the setup picks one out. I will grant that a bounded prior forces some amount at which switching stops paying, out at the very edge of the prior's support, where the arithmetic simply runs out; what I deny is that a threshold can sit anywhere in the interior, where it would have to be a fact about the world rather than an artifact of my ignorance.

6. Before I open anything, switching and keeping are exactly equally good: the expected gain from a blind switch is zero. This follows from the symmetry of the setup and I regard it as a constraint any account must respect.

7. Concretely: suppose the smaller amount is equally likely to be any of $1, $2, $4, $8, …, $512, with one envelope receiving that amount and the other twice it. Working this through on a train, I get that switching is favored if I see any amount from $1 up to $512 — for $2 through $512 the other envelope is equally likely to hold half or double, and at $1 it can only hold $2 — and that if I see $1024 I must be holding the larger amount of the top pair, so I keep. Hence: switch at everything except $1024. I take this as confirming (5), since the cutoff falls at the edge of the support rather than somewhere arbitrary in the middle.

8. The blind switch has expected gain zero by (6), yet on the arithmetic in (7) the only case in which switching loses money is seeing $1024, which has probability 1/20 and costs me $512. So the entire balance of the puzzle seems to rest on one low-probability state, while every other state pulls toward switching. I confess I find this uncomfortable and half-suspect I have set the case up wrong, though I cannot see where.

9. Dominance is binding on me: if one available act is never worse than another in any possible state of the world and is better in some, then it is not merely permissible but mandatory. I think a blind switch meets this condition relative to blind keeping, and so is required of me.

10. And yet I also think that a policy of switching blind and then switching blind again, when offered the chance, refutes any account that recommends it. My evidence at the second offer is exactly what it was at the first, so the reasoning cannot have changed; if an account tells me to switch twice and end where I began, I regard that as a reductio rather than a curiosity.

For what it is worth, I do not know which of these is the weak link. Each one looks defensible to me when I consider it on its own, which is precisely why I want an outsider to go through them pairwise.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Please verify the arithmetic in (7) and (8) rather than taking my word for it, and note explicitly if either of my calculations is wrong.
