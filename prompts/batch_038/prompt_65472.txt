The Act that created our commission's Amnesty Chamber conditions amnesty on two limbs: the applicant must make a full disclosure of all facts relevant to the act, and the act must be associated with a political objective and have been committed in the course of the conflicts of the past. Proportionality between the means used and the objective pursued is listed among the considerations bearing on the second limb, but the Act assigns it no determinate weight, and the Chamber has never settled how much it carries. I am legal secretary to the Chamber. The determination below is the one on which we are most likely to divide, and I want the reasoning worked through before the hearing rather than after it. Treat the eight prior determinations I summarise as the whole of the relevant authority; I am not asking for a comparison with any other country's practice, and nothing in our record depends on one.

The record to date:

- Granted. A police intelligence sergeant who beat detainees to extract information about an armed underground cell, acting under a standing directive from his commanding officer; disclosure complete and corroborated.
- Refused. A police constable who beat a detainee for three days during the routine arrest of a person with no established armed connection, and who omitted the names of two colleagues present from his statement; disclosure incomplete.
- Granted. A liberation cadre who bombed the records annexe of a police station after hours, killing a contract cleaner working inside; the operation was authorised by the movement's command and publicly claimed by it.
- Refused. A cadre who executed a man denounced as an informer after a township "people's court," on full and truthful disclosure; the Chamber found the execution unsanctioned by any command structure and substantially the product of a private feud with the informer's brother.
- Granted. An army major who called an artillery strike on a village held on intelligence to shelter an insurgent column, killing eleven civilians; the Chamber recorded that the target was military, the casualties unintended, and the means not grossly disproportionate to the objective as it was understood at the time.
- Refused. A member of a security death squad who abducted and shot a prominent human-rights lawyer with no armed ties, disclosing fully and testifying that he believed the lawyer sat on the movement's leadership; the Chamber held the belief was not one he could reasonably have held, and that the purpose was the destruction of civilian political opposition rather than the pursuit of any objective of the conflict.
- Granted, by a divided vote. A security officer who burned the body and destroyed the file of a detainee who had died in custody from causes for which the applicant was not responsible; the concealment was authorised and formed part of a standing practice.
- Refused. An officer who ran a smuggling operation under the cover of counter-insurgency work, disclosing fully; the Chamber held personal enrichment to be the operative objective.

The application now before us has these features. The applicant was a senior intelligence analyst who was never present at any scene. He drafted a classification memorandum listing a trade-union organiser as a priority target and circulated it to an operational unit known within the branch to practise what it called "special handling." The unit abducted and killed the organiser. His disclosure is complete and independently corroborated; the family's counsel has conceded as much, so the first limb is not live. He maintains that his purpose was to initiate surveillance, that elimination went beyond anything he authorised, and that "special handling" meant interrogation as far as he was concerned. Two facts cut against him: an internal briefing note in his own handwriting glosses "special handling" as "removal," and he had read the unit's after-action reports on two earlier operations. One fact cuts for him: after the killing it emerged that the organiser had in fact been co-ordinating logistics for three armed cells, which the applicant neither knew nor had any means of knowing when he wrote the memorandum. Two members of the unit who carried the organiser away have already been granted amnesty for that killing.

Three things, please, in order.

First, reconstruct the candidate principles that could account for the record as a whole. My own reading is that at least four are in play — a functional-contribution principle (an act that knowingly enabled a violation in furtherance of a party's objective falls within the second limb), an actual-purpose principle (the political objective must have been the applicant's own operative aim), a reasonable-belief principle (the applicant's appreciation of the facts must have been one he could defensibly have held), and a legitimate-target principle (what matters is the status of the victim and the character of the objective, not the applicant's mind). State each sharply enough that it makes a determinate ruling on all eight determinations, and tell me which survive the whole record without ad hoc repair and which do not. If one of my four collapses into another, or if some determination is explicable only by a fifth principle I have missed, say so rather than accommodating it.

Second, give the ruling each surviving principle yields on the new application, and name the single prior determination that is the closest analog under each. Then commit to a recommendation — the determination that best coheres with the Chamber's own record, not a compromise or a remand for further evidence. Give your reasons for preferring the principle you prefer, and say what a reader who accepts the runner-up principle should say in dissent. If you conclude that the eight determinations genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state that plainly and identify what properly breaks the tie: the statutory purpose of national reconciliation, even-handedness between applicants from the state and applicants from the movement, the interests of the victim's family, the deterrent or expressive effects of the ruling, or something further. Say whether the tiebreaker you select is a legal one available inside the Act or a frankly political one the Chamber is usurping.

Third, tell me what the Chamber would be legislating whichever way it goes. A grant converts a desk officer's classification memorandum into an act of the conflict, and does so in a case where the memorandum was not itself a violation. A refusal commits us to holding that remote contributions fall outside the Act even where the proximate killers were amnestied for the very same killing — so that the men who drove the organiser away are immune and the man who put his name on a list is not. Is that asymmetry a coherent position, an incoherent one, or a defensible one at a cost we should name? Address, finally, the mirrored application already in our queue: a movement operative who passed a name at a regional meeting with no knowledge of what the receiving unit did with it. Does consistency compel the same verdict in both cases, or is a principled difference available to us — and if a difference is available, specify the further application that would most cleanly separate the competing principles and thereby force the Chamber to choose between them openly.
