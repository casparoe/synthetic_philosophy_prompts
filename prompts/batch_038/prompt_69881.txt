You are a decision theorist and philosopher of rational choice who has also served on real selection committees, and I want your help with a live decision rather than a textbook exercise. Please think this through carefully before answering, and start by tabulating the precedents rather than reasoning about the new case first.

I sit on the acquisitions committee of a regional land trust in the northeastern United States; we buy parcels that buffer and connect the roughly 4,100 acres we already hold. Our written policy sets a minimum standard for any parcel — acreage, a habitat-quality score, connectivity to existing holdings, feasibility of purchase — and our mandate says only that the committee "should act when the standard is met." Two members read that as a stopping rule, one reads it as a mere permission, and we have never settled the difference. Our decisions over the past six years:

1. **2019.** A 140-acre parcel cleared the standard. A larger, better-connected parcel was already listed at the time, priced about 40 percent higher, and buying it would have required drawing on our stewardship reserve. We bought the first parcel. Recorded rationale: the standard had been met and the alternative was not affordable within the year's budget.
2. **2021.** A parcel cleared the standard. The adjoining parcel that would have completed a corridor was already under purchase-and-sale agreement with a buyer who had told us in writing that he would resell to a land trust at his own cost. We declined the first parcel, waited five months, and bought the corridor parcel at his price. The minutes record the majority saying that buying in week one "would have cost the mission a parcel we could have had for the same money." Our chair dissented, arguing we had no business declining a parcel that met the standard.
3. **2022.** A parcel failed one element of the standard — no public-access potential — but scored exceptionally on a single feature, direct frontage on the river we had spent a decade assembling. Our normal practice is a weighted composite score. We bought anyway, against the score, on the strength of that one feature. It has held up.
4. **2023.** A parcel cleared the standard. Our part-time analyst estimated a 40 percent chance that a better parcel would come to market within the year. We bought. Nothing better appeared. The chair's rationale: there was no way to check the 40 percent, and the standard had been met.
5. **2023 (later).** A parcel cleared the standard; the analyst estimated a 55 percent chance of a superior parcel within the season. We declined, to keep our options open. The owner took a developer's offer three weeks later and the superior parcel never surfaced. A member moved to record that the decision itself had been wrong, not merely unlucky; the motion failed.
6. **2024 (January).** We realized the standard had been calibrated three years earlier for a much hotter market and that parcels were now clearing it partly through score drift. We recalibrated upward, and then bought a parcel that would have failed the old standard and passes the new one.

Our chair has served twenty-two years and voted on all of these. She wrote the rationale in case 1, dissented in case 2, wrote the rationale in case 4, voted to buy in cases 3 and 5, and championed the recalibration in case 6. She has said on the record that her objection is not to arithmetic as such but to arithmetic nobody can check.

Here is the case in front of us Thursday. Parcel A is 96 acres with riparian frontage on the creek that runs through our existing preserve; it clears every element of the standard we adopted in January, and its habitat score sits at about the 70th percentile of parcels we have bought in the last decade. Asking price is $390,000, roughly 18 percent below our per-acre average for that watershed. Our remaining acquisition budget for the fiscal year is $425,000, so buying A leaves us about $35,000. Meanwhile two adjoining parcels — 61 acres and 210 acres — that would complete the corridor have been quietly listed by an estate. The analyst's read: a 35 percent chance both become available to us within six weeks at a combined price around $780,000, which would require drawing roughly $355,000 from the stewardship reserve; a 25 percent chance only the 210-acre parcel becomes available at around $490,000, requiring a $65,000 draw; a 40 percent chance neither does. The reserve is what funds trail work and invasive-species removal on the land we already own, and we have never agreed on how to convert deferred stewardship into something commensurable with acres acquired. Waiting costs about $12,000 in staff time and re-appraisal, and the analyst puts the chance that Parcel A's owner loses patience and takes her standing developer's offer at about 15 percent; her agent has confirmed in writing that she will hold for six weeks. The analyst is a part-time consultant we have used for two years whose estimates have never been back-tested, and his two relevant data points are our own cases 2 and 5.

I have been arguing with a fellow board member about how to describe what we do. My read is that we are running one of four rules, and that our record does not yet settle which: a pre-committed aspiration level that licenses stopping the moment the standard is met (Simon); a marginal cost-benefit search calculus that says stop only when another round of looking costs more than it is worth (the search-theory line); a Slote-style view on which taking a good-enough option is *permissible* even when a better one is available, so that no decision of ours could ever be condemned as long as the standard was met; or a Gigerenzer-style view on which a simple, robust stopping rule is not a second-best compromise but the right answer when the inputs to any calculation are noisy. If you think that taxonomy is wrong or incomplete — if what we are really tracking is regret-avoidance, or option value, or the *verifiability* of the better alternative rather than its expected value, or some accidental approximation of an optimal-stopping rule — say so and give me the better taxonomy.
