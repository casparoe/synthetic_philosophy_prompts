THE QUESTION

Pain asymbolia: patients, typically with damage to the insular cortex and surrounding regions, report that they can feel pains and can say where they are and how intense they are, but that the pains don't bother them. They shrug, laugh, or keep talking when pricked or burned, don't withdraw the limb, and don't ask for analgesia. Some say they know they ought to avoid the stimulus but that they just don't care. Call these patients group A.

Now consider a second group. Group B is hypothetical, but you should treat it as coherent and possible for the purposes of this question. B-patients match A-patients on behaviour — no withdrawal, no avoidance, no analgesia-seeking, flat or amused facial behaviour — but differ on report: they say the pain is genuinely awful, that they dislike it intensely and want it to stop right now, and that their indifference is an indifference of the body rather than of the experience.

Three views about what makes a pain unpleasant:

- Imperative view: an experience is a pain, and is unpleasant to the degree that it is, in virtue of carrying imperative content — content to the effect that the subject should protect or stop using some body part.
- Evaluative view: unpleasantness consists in the experience representing a bodily disturbance as bad for the subject.
- Desire view: unpleasantness consists in the subject's desire, typically a higher-order one, that the experience cease.

(a) For each of the three views, say what group A is evidence for and what it leaves open. Which view is most directly threatened by group A, and which accommodates it best?
(b) Which view or views does group B discriminate between, and how? Would anything turn on whether group B is merely conceivable as opposed to actually instantiated?

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

Group A matters because it shows that the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain and the affective-motivational dimension can come apart. This isn't the same thing as congenital analgesia. A-patients aren't numb, they can localize the stimulus and rate its intensity, so their nociceptive processing and the discriminative side of pain are intact. What's missing is the part that makes pain hurt. So group A is a real problem for any view that builds unpleasantness into the sensory character of pain, because here we seem to have the sensory character without the unpleasantness.

The imperative view is the one most directly threatened. If a pain just is a command to protect the body part, then A-patients, who have no commanding element whatsoever — they shrug, they don't withdraw, they don't care — don't have pains in the relevant sense. The imperativist either has to say that asymbolic patients feel no pain, which is a hard sell given that they report pains and rate them on scales, or has to say the command is present but somehow cut off from behaviour, and that second option is basically to give up the view, since the whole point of imperative content was to explain why pain moves us. So group A leaves the imperativist only the choice of which horn to take, and neither is attractive.

The evaluative view does better, or at least it has a move available. It can say the A-patient retains an interoceptive awareness of the bodily disturbance but has lost the representation of that disturbance as bad. That's a natural fit for a lesion in a region thought to be involved in representing the body's internal state. But this costs the evaluativist something: on their account the A-patient has a full sensory experience of damage that is not bad for them at all, which is a strange thing to say about a person with a burn on their hand, and it gives up the idea that pain is essentially bad.

The desire view accommodates group A most easily. No desire that the experience cease, no unpleasantness. And it doesn't need to reinterpret anything in the patient's testimony, it takes the reports at face value: the patient feels something, and doesn't mind it. Group A leaves open, for all three views, whether the damage is in the experience itself or downstream of it, and none of the views can settle that from reports alone.

What group A shows overall, then, is that the felt badness of pain can't be what motivates us, because here are patients who feel every bit as much pain as the rest of us and are simply unmoved by it. That's a clean counterexample to motivational internalism about unpleasantness.

(b) Group B refutes the desire view. B-patients want the pain to stop, they say so explicitly, they dislike it intensely, and yet they do nothing about it. So wanting a pain to stop is clearly not sufficient for the kind of unpleasantness that does any explanatory work, and the desire theorist has nothing left to say about them. Group B is meanwhile neutral between the imperative and the evaluative view, because both of these locate unpleasantness in representational content, and content doesn't have to motivate anything. Really imperativism and evaluativism just disagree about how to notate the same representational relation, one with a world-to-mind direction of fit and the other with a mind-to-world fit, so no neurological case could tell them apart, group B included.

On the last bit, whether group B is real or merely conceivable doesn't matter much either way. What does the work is the coherence of the case, and a coherent possibility is enough to show that a view's explanation isn't necessary. Actual instantiation would matter for the imperativist's empirical credibility but not for the philosophy.
