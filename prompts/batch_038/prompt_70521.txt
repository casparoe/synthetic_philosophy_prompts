I am a junior at a public high school in a mid-sized city, and for the past year I have been following our planning commission's deliberations as part of a student civic-engagement group that submitted a letter on a proposed rezoning. The proposal would allow fourplexes along a corridor of single-family streets within a ten-minute walk of our commuter rail station, and would permit a roughly sixty-unit building on a parcel that is currently a surface parking lot and a self-storage facility. At the second public hearing, a member of a neighborhood association stood up and said, twice, that the commission was fooling itself: "you can't build your way out of a housing shortage." She then compared the rezoning to widening a highway to relieve congestion, arguing that the new lanes simply attract more drivers until the road is as jammed as before, and that new apartments likewise attract newcomers until rents are as high as ever. Her conclusion was that the commission should vote the rezoning down and instead devote the city's money to preserving and subsidizing the affordable units that already exist.

I find myself broadly sympathetic to permitting more housing, but I am not confident that I understood what she was actually asserting, and several people on my side of the debate treated her remark as merely confused, which struck me as too quick. The sentence seems capable of meaning quite different things depending on how it is unpacked. Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a single favored interpretation, the distinct precise claims that "you can't build your way out of a housing shortage" might be expressing? For each reading, please state it in a way that pins down the variables that are usually left vague: which market is at issue (the city proper, the metropolitan region, the nation), which segment of the price distribution (newly built market-rate units, or the bottom of the existing rental market), whose demand (current residents or prospective in-migrants), over what time horizon, and holding what else fixed. Then give a verdict on each — whether it is a conceptual or definitional truth, an empirical claim that could in principle be tested with local data, a platitude that nobody disputes, a substantive and contested position, or simply false as stated. Most importantly, say for each reading whether it would in fact support the conclusion she drew, that this specific rezoning should be voted down and the money redirected to subsidized units, since I suspect some readings that sound damning leave that conclusion untouched while others, which sound modest, would carry a great deal of weight.

Two further requests. First, please assess the highway analogy on its own terms. Induced demand for road capacity is often presented as settled science, but I have an intuition that road space is unpriced at the margin while apartments are not, and that this might break the analogy even if everything she said about highways is correct. Tell me where the analogy does hold, where it fails, and what would have to be true about the housing market for the traffic result to transfer across. Second, because I would like to respond at the next hearing without being dismissive of a neighbor who has lived here for thirty years, tell me which reading you judge she most likely meant given the rest of her argument, and give me one or two short questions I could put to her in public that would reveal which reading she is actually committed to. I am not looking for references, studies, or a reading list; what I want is the logical structure, worked through carefully enough that I could explain it to another student on the bus ride home.
