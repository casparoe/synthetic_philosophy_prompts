I have been collecting historical analogies as they are deployed in foreign-policy argument, and recording my own judgment on each: warranted, unwarranted, or indeterminate. The difficulty is that the judgments do not sort along any line I can name. They do not track surface similarity; they do not track how recent the source case is; they do not track whether the source case ended in catastrophe or in muddle. I want a principle — or a small set of competing principles — that reproduces the pattern, and I want to be told where the pattern itself has to give.

Here are the verdicts, with the reason I give for each when pressed.

1. **Munich 1938, applied to Vietnam in 1965 — unwarranted.** The analogy smuggled in a claim about the adversary (Hanoi as an expansionist power whose appetite grows with every concession) that the source case could not license, and it mapped a civil conflict in a recently decolonized state onto a great-power territorial revision in the center of Europe.

2. **Munich 1938, applied to the limited military action against Georgia in 2008 and then to Crimea in 2014 — warranted**, or at any rate markedly better than the Vietnam use. The mechanism is the same: an incremental seizure met with no consequential response, followed years later by a larger one, with the interval spent consolidating.

3. **Vietnam as a cautionary analogy for Iraq in 2003 — warranted**, even though almost no descriptive feature matches. What carries is the structure of the commitment: an open-ended occupation, a political settlement presented as a military objective, no defined condition for leaving.

4. **The Anglo-German naval rivalry of the 1890s to 1914 as a model for US-China competition — unwarranted.** This troubles me most, because the descriptive match is the closest on the list: rising power, incumbent hegemon, naval construction programs, mutual anxiety. But the source case's war ran through rigid alliance commitments, mobilization timetables that outran political decision-making, and the absence of any credible second-strike deterrent. None of those is present.

5. **The same source case — the summer of 1914 — as a model for India and Pakistan: warranted.** Compressed decision times, pre-delegated authority over forces, a triggering event in contested territory, third parties drawn in by commitments resembling alliances.

6. **The printing press as a model for the internet and social media: warranted**, despite the absence of any shared political structure. When the cost of reproducing and circulating text collapses, the gatekeeping authority of established institutions collapses with it, and the adjustment takes generations rather than years.

7. **The Winter War of 1939–40 as a model for the 2022 invasion of Ukraine: warranted** and, unusually, predictive. The same aggressor, the same declared rationale about the security of a border buffer, the same underestimate of local resistance. The analogy identified the failure mode in advance.

8. **The domino theory applied to Southeast Asia after 1954 — unwarranted as description.** The cascade stopped short of Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia. But I am reluctant to file it simply as false, because belief in the cascade generated the policy that produced much of what in fact happened.

9. **A public university's budget shortfall described as a sovereign debt crisis — unwarranted**, though the arithmetic is identical. One of the two has an electorate and a currency; the other has neither, and the causal story runs entirely through that difference. And yet I accepted case 6 on a mechanism at least as loose as this one, which is a tension I cannot dissolve.

10. **Present AI development described as a Manhattan Project — indeterminate, and the case I most want help with.** For it: concentrated capability, direct state interest, race dynamics, the argument that control at a chokepoint is decisive. Against it: no single deliverable device, no verification regime, capability diffusing rather than concentrating. And the wrinkle: the analogy has already begun to shape its own object, licensing secrecy and export restrictions that were not in place when the comparison was first made.

My tentative principle, which I want tested and repaired rather than endorsed: an analogy is warranted in proportion to the degree to which source and target share the operative mechanism, and not merely the descriptive properties, where the operative mechanism is the causal structure that produced the outcome in the source case and that would, if present, produce a comparable outcome in the target. Cases 3, 5, 6, and 7 pass on mechanism; cases 1, 4, and 9 fail because their resemblance is descriptive. That accounts for most of the list. It does not account for 8 or 10, and I suspect the omission is this: an analogy in public use is not only a representation of a situation but an intervention in it, and once the relevant actors know which analogy is being applied to them, the payoffs attached to the roles it assigns change. I do not know whether that belongs as a clause in a principle of analogical warrant, or whether it is a reason to think no such principle can be complete.

What I would like:

(a) State the principle — or, if my verdicts require more than one, state the candidates — precisely enough that a colleague could apply it to a case without consulting me. Give it clause by clause, and for each clause say which of my cases motivated it and what work that clause does which the others cannot do.

(b) Apply it to two cases I have not listed: the comparison between current semiconductor export controls and the interwar embargoes on trade and raw materials, and the comparison between the policy response to the 2008 financial crisis and the deflationary spiral of the early 1930s. Give me your verdict on each, and then tell me whether the principle delivered that verdict or had to be stretched to reach it.

(c) Say which of my ten verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should therefore surrender. Cases 4 and 5 share a source and split; cases 6 and 9 share a structure and split. If a mechanism-based principle has to buy one of those splits at the cost of the other, say so plainly instead of reconciling them.

(d) Say whether the reflexivity problem — that employing an analogy alters its object — admits of principled treatment, or whether any clause addressing it must be either trivial, since it is true of every public argument, or self-undermining, since it would counsel against using precisely those analogies whose use carries the greatest consequences. If you think it can be handled, state the clause and run it against cases 8 and 10.

(e) Finally, say whether the principle you end with is specific to statecraft or general to analogical reasoning, given that cases 6 and 9 involve neither states nor wars; and whether it is the familiar account of analogical inference in terms of relevant similarity with a modification attached, or whether the modification is carrying the whole weight.

I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for references. I am looking for something with edges sharp enough to cut with.
