Satellite navigation clocks are deliberately detuned before launch, because once in orbit — moving fast and sitting higher in Earth's gravity well — they tick about 38 microseconds per day faster than identical clocks on the ground, and uncorrected that drift would smear reported positions by kilometres within a day. Deep-space navigation works in the same spirit: nobody asks where Mars is, the software computes whole worldlines and reads off where the transmitted signal's light cone intersects the planet's path, since one-way light time runs from several minutes to over twenty and the spacecraft executes commands stamped for an onboard time the team predicted in advance. All of this bookkeeping is done in a single Earth-centered coordinate time that the system treats as the master clock, against which every other clock in the constellation is steered.

And yet the same engineers will tell you, rightly, that relativity makes simultaneity frame-relative: there is no fact of the matter about which distant events are happening now.

What metaphysics of time does this practice presuppose? Does maintaining a shared, nanosecond-accurate "now" across orbit and ground require that there be an objective present for it to be tracking, and does the choice of one master frame amount to smuggling in a preferred foliation? Or could a presentist, a growing-block theorist, and an eternalist each license exactly the same engineering? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly commits its builders to from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say what — if anything — would have to change in the practice were one of those views settled.
