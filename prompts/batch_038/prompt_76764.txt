So I'm TA'ing a 300-level philosophy of mind seminar and next week's slot is "the emotions" — 14 students, 75 minutes, a whiteboard, and a pack of index cards I already stole from the department office. I really don't want to stand at the front and narrate the three candidate answers (emotion as a kind of judgment, emotion as a kind of perception, emotion as a raw bodily feel), because half the section will just take notes and pick one, and the two people who've read stuff on emotion already will dominate. I want an activity where the three answers come apart in the students' own mouths: they try to describe a single emotional episode inside a deliberately restricted vocabulary, discover what the restriction costs them, and can't just retreat to "well obviously it's all three at once." No theory names up front, no prereading required (they've got a textbook chapter and maybe a third will have done it), and I want them to be the ones who notice the gap rather than me pointing at it.

Here's the mechanic I've been toying with — improve it or tell me it's dumb. Groups of 3 or 4, every group gets the *same* vignette card. My working example: a doctoral student opens a reviewer report on her paper, reads two pages without much reaction at all, then notices her hands are shaking and her coffee has gone stone cold, and only at that point realizes she has been furious for twenty minutes — furious at a reviewer she has never met and whose name she doesn't even have. Then each group gets a secret house rule about what they're allowed to say in their written description of what happened. One house may talk only about bodies, arousal, and behavioral dispositions, and may not mention anything the emotion is *about*. One house may talk only about evaluations, appraisals, and commitments, and may not mention any felt quality whatsoever. One house may talk freely about the report "striking her as" dismissive and about her "seeing it as" a slight, but may not cash that out as either a belief or a bodily state. And the part I like: a house loses any word from its allowed list if another house can catch it doing covert work the rule was supposed to exclude — so the evaluation-only house gets dinged the moment someone points out that "she took it to be unfair" is smuggling in a claim about what it feels like from the inside. After that comes an attack phase where each house has to produce a case, real or invented, that some other house's vocabulary flat-out cannot describe, specific enough that the accused house can't paraphrase its way around it.

What I need from you is the whole runnable thing. The vignette cards — I'd like at least four, including one where the emotion is disavowed on good evidence (the person knows perfectly well the thing in the bathtub isn't going to hurt her and says so sincerely while backing out of the room), one where theres no object that actually exists, which I want you to think hard about because I suspect it puts the "seeing it as" house in a bind about whether you can see-as something that isn't there, and one where the person reports the feeling vividly but has no idea what it's about or whether it's about anything. Then the exact wording of the house rules and of the word-catching rule so it doesn't dissolve into a shouting match about synonyms; a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes including when I shut the attack phase down; what goes on the board and in what order; and the debrief, where I finally get to name the three seperate positions and map each house's breakdown onto a precise pressure point on a precise theory — not "they all have problems," but which family of case defeats which account and why, plus what a theory has to give up to survive it. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time someone runs this and how to handle it (my guess: the body-only house gets bored and starts cheating, and some student says "but isn't the shaking just the anger?" and short-circuits everything at minute 12). And I want one honest paragraph about what a paper-and-cards exercise structurally cannot reproduce about the very thing the whole debate is supposed to be about, so I can say that out loud instead of letting them leave thinking they've settled it. If you think the word-catching rule is too cute and will eat the session, say so and hand me a cheaper version of it. Don't send me a reading list — I know what to read, I just need something that works on a Tuesday.
