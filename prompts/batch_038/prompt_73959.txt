I'm the philosopher on our university's Strategic Foresight Panel — six of us — and every year we run an internal forecasting tournament: faculty, staff, grad students and a handful of undergrads submit probability forecasts on about thirty questions (will the state restore the appropriation, will fall enrollment land inside this bracket, will the garage bond measure pass, that sort of thing). We average everyone's Brier scores, the top scorer gets a $1,500 prize out of a donor fund, and — the part everybody forgets — the panel also lifts the pooled median across participants and puts it straight into the actual strategic plan. So we care about the aggregate forecast, not just about crowning a winner.

Now we're having a fight. Our economist says a fixed prize for best rank isn't really a proper scoring rule at all: if you only get paid for beating the field, you should shade away from what you actually believe whenever you expect the crowd to bunch up, and he has a slide about it. My colleague in statistics wants to switch to log scores to keep people honest, which I suspect just means nobody will ever dare write 0.95 again. There's a third proposal to score each person relative to the crowd's mean on that question. And a grad student wants to kill the prize entirely and pay everyone proportional to their absolute score.

Could you write the memo I'll circulate before the panel votes Tuesday? Readable by non-philosophers, but don't sand down the actual issue — I want it to explain why propriety is sensitive to whether payoffs are absolute or relative, and what the immodesty of a proper rule does and doesn't buy us in a tournament like this one.

Please use these sections, in this order:

- Bottom line — 3 or 4 sentences saying what I should vote for
- What the tournament is actually for — ~150 words on the tension between rewarding an individual winner and producing a good pooled forecast
- Why relative payoffs break propriety — ~250 words, walking through the contrarian-shading mechanism concretely on one invented question, so the economist's slide gets answered rather than waved at
- The three alternatives, assessed honestly — ~300 words on log score, crowd-relative scoring, and no prize with proportional payouts; say which ones survive and which ones just move the distortion somewhere else
- The strongest case for keeping winner-take-all — ~150 words, genuinely steelmanned; if the prize is what makes people bother forecasting at all, say so
- Recommendation and implementation — ~200 words, including the seperate problem that two participants (the registrar and someone in the budget office) have inside information on three of the questions

Roughly 1,100 words is plenty. No citations or reading lists needed, I just want the reasoning.
