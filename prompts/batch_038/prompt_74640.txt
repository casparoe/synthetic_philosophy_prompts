Three years ago I retired from a national metrology institute, where I spent most of my career on precision measurement, and I have since taken up philosophy with more time than discipline. I now sit on the technical advisory board of a small firm that builds quantum random number generators for gaming regulators, and the board is deadlocked over a question I have come to suspect is philosophical rather than engineering.

Each cycle of the device yields one of several output patterns. On the firm's account — derived from the prepared state and the Hamiltonian, and confirmed in the type-approval tests — the probability of one particular flagged pattern is 0.214. Our chief engineer maintains that this figure is a fact about the physical world rather than anybody's estimate, and that the board's only business is to adopt it unadjusted. He is not being careless, and I do not think he is hiding anything: he regards any departure from it as a refusal to take indeterminism seriously.

Two things trouble me. First, in the last two production runs the observed rate of the flagged pattern was 0.19 and 0.18, and our own people trace the discrepancy to a slow calibration drift in a component the theoretical model does not describe. Second, the board must decide this week whether to recall those units, and the figure we publish will be read by the regulator as a property of the devices actually in service, not of an idealised source.

Is the engineer right that the model's number simply is the probability, so that my unease is metaphysical squeamishness? If he is not right, what exactly is the error — and which figure should the board use, and on what grounds? I would rather have the reasoning than a map of the rival interpretations of probability.
