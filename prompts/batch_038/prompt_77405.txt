**Context.** I am preparing a two-page handout for a first-year graduate seminar in political philosophy. The session is on the problem of dirty hands: the suggestion that political actors sometimes must do what is, by ordinary moral standards, wrong in order to prevent a greater harm, and that they are not thereby excused but remain morally tainted by the deed. My students arrive having read a standard treatment (Walzer's discussion of political action, Nagel's account of ruthlessness in public life, Weber's contrast between an ethic of responsibility and an ethic of ultimate ends, and Sartre's play), and they immediately sort themselves by slogan: "I'm a consequentialist," "I'm basically a deontologist," "I think the whole problem is overblown." That sorting is useless for the discussion, because the interesting disagreements are almost entirely second-order. Nearly everyone concedes that the minister, the chief, or the diplomat had decisive reason to act as they did. What they disagree about is what *survives* the verdict: whether guilt persists after the act is judged right, whether an apology is owed to the person who was used, whether the deed may be repeated once conditions recur, whether the agent may seek office again, whether the beneficiaries may keep what the wrongdoing produced, and whether disclosing the deed is itself a further duty or a further harm.

**Deliverable.** Build a decision tree, roughly nine to eleven questions with at most three answer options each, that sorts a reader by these second-order commitments into a named position on the dirty-hands debate. Two structural rules govern it. First, no question — and above all not the opening question — may ask whether the act was permissible, right, or justified; the tree must begin from a question about what follows the act. Second, no question may use vocabulary that advertises a label: the words "dirty hands," "tragedy," "remainder," "residue," "role morality," and "agent-regret" must not appear in any question or option. Everything is to be phrased in the plain practical register of what someone should feel, say, owe, or do next, so that a reader who has never encountered the literature can still answer honestly.

**Materials.** Anchor the questions in the following five vignettes, which the students have already discussed and which are yours to distribute across the tree as you see fit. (i) During a supply collapse, a health minister forges a transfer order to move ventilators from a rural district to the capital, knowing this will kill some forty patients who would otherwise have survived and save some three hundred; she forges rather than litigate because a legal challenge would take longer than the patients have. (ii) A police chief authorizes an unlawful entry into the home of a man whose abducted niece has perhaps thirty hours of breathable air left; the entry yields nothing admissible, the family is compensated, and the chief never faces charges. (iii) A rebel commander orders the execution of a captured informer — a conscripted teenager — because his brigade is on the point of disintegrating and its collapse would cost far more civilian lives than the boy's. (iv) A mayor accepts campaign money from a developer she knows to be corrupt, wins the election, delivers none of the promised favors, and uses the term to enact a housing policy that materially improves the lives of some twenty thousand poor residents. (v) A diplomat agrees to ceasefire language granting amnesty to a war criminal, ending fighting that is killing five hundred people a week and foreclosing any future prosecution of atrocities committed against one small group.

**Leaves.** The tree must terminate in at least six and no more eight positions, and must include, however you choose to label them: the view that the act is both justified and wrong with guilt that no justification dissolves; the view that political office generates its own normative standards so that nothing ordinary-moral was violated at all; the view that there is no distinct problem because a correct theory of what to do absorbs the supposed conflict; the view that prohibitions hold until a threshold, past which they lapse cleanly; the view that the agent's felt distress is not a leftover to be tolerated but the very mark of virtue, so that the untroubled actor is the one who has failed; the abstentionist view that an official who cannot act without wrongdoing should leave the office to someone else; and the critical view that talk of dirty hands is largely an exculpatory ideology deployed after the fact by people who wanted to do the thing anyway. Coin short descriptive labels of your own; name a philosopher only where a position is standardly identified with one, and give no dates, titles, or references.

**For each leaf, supply:** one sentence on its most awkward commitment; and which of the five vignettes it handles least well, with a clause saying why.

**Then supply:** a scoring key mapping answer patterns to leaves; an explicit flag on any pair of leaves your questions cannot in fact separate, with a note on what additional question would be needed; and at least two logically available cells of the space that no position occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive to anyone who has considered it, or because it has simply gone unexplored. The delegation move — the official who keeps her own hands clean by having a subordinate do the deed — should appear somewhere in the tree either as a question or as a diagnosed empty cell; say which you chose and why.
