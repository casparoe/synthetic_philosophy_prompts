Analyze the "neuropsychological model" controversy in rock-art studies through the lens of Deborah Mayo's severe-testing account of evidence, and then tell me where that lens goes silent.

The facts I'll stipulate, so you need not look anything up. In 1988 David Lewis-Williams and Thomas Dowson proposed that the recurrent geometric motifs of southern San rock art correspond to the entoptic imagery produced by the human visual system in altered states of consciousness — the same "form constants" Heinrich Klüver catalogued in 1926 from mescaline subjects (lattices and grids, cobwebs, tunnels and funnels, spirals). Their three-stage model runs from raw entoptic forms, through a stage in which the subject tries to make sense of them, to full iconic hallucination and participation. The hypothesis was anchored not only in neurology but in the ǀXam San dictation archive recorded in the 1870s, which Lewis-Williams read as describing a trance dance central to San ritual. In the late 1990s he and the French prehistorian Jean Clottes extended the framework to the Upper Paleolithic decorated caves of the Franco-Cantabrian region, treating the imagery there as shamanistic. Critics objected that the neurology is universalizing and therefore explains too much, that the analogy leaps from nineteenth-century southern Africa to Ice Age Europe with nothing bridging the gap, that "shamanism" is an imported category, and that the model can absorb every anomaly. Defenders replied that Lewis-Williams distinguishes art made *in* an altered state from art merely *of* one, never claimed to explain all imagery, and holds that the ethnographic texts carry the argument independently of the neurology.

Now the analysis. State the severity criterion and apply it: what would a test of the entoptic hypothesis look like that had a high probability of detecting error if the hypothesis were false — a motif distribution that came out wrong, an ethnographic passage that contradicted it, a cave whose geometry failed to correlate? Did the evidence actually offered clear that bar, and if not, was the shortfall a matter of the tests being too easy to pass, or of the hypothesis being so flexible that no test could be severe for it? Then run the comparison in the other direction: Mayo built severity for statistical hypotheses with well-defined error probabilities, and singular claims about the mental states of people who died twenty thousand years ago have no such structure. Say plainly whether severe testing yields a verdict here or is simply inapplicable, and whether inference to the best explanation does better work on this material. Finish with what the episode suggests about how archaeology demarcates a legitimate reading of objects from a compelling but untestable one.
