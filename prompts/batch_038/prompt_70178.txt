I run a tiny podcast about language and institutions — 40-minute episodes, audience is mostly grad students and a few lawyers listens. Next week's guest is a philosopher of language who argues that Austin's declarations aren't a real class of speech act at all. Her view, roughly: when a judge says "case dismissed," a chair says "the motion carries," an employer says "you're fired," or an officiant says "I pronounce you," no new state of affairs is being produced by the utterance. The change was already sitting there in what she calls the substrate — the standing rules, the records, and everyone's dispositions to update — and the sentence is just an announcement of that change, aimed at whoever needs to hear it. Her diagnostic is the reversal test: for any alleged declaration, ask whether the change can be undone by an utterance from the same mouth with the same standing. She says the answer is systematically no, that undoing always needs a fresh procedure, a different authority, or a change in the world, and that this shows the words were never the mechanism, only the report.

Write me nine interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — be specific about what that answer actually is, don't just write "if she pushes back." Order them so the pressure builds, and tell me which single one you'd keep if the conversation goes sideways and I only get to land one blow.

Needs to hit:

- the resignation problem. "I resign" undoes a status by words alone, same mouth, no new procedure. Does that break the reversal test, or does she get to say resignation is also just announcing a shift in the substrate — and if the latter, what stops the test from being consistent with everything?
- Austin's misfire/abuse split. Her account turns misfires into false announcements, which is a redescription, but the abuse cases cut the other way: standing present, procedure right, both parties joking around, and the marriage still lands on the record. Ask what a mechanism that runs regardless of the speaker's intentions does to her claim that these utterances report.
- the "hereby" insertion. It slots in happily to a naming or firing sentence but does nothing when you put it in a report of the same content. If "hereby" is just a precision hedge on her story, that asymmetry needs an explanation — and ask her for one that doesn't smuggle the act back in.
- promises nobody can enforce: the deathbed promise, the promise the promisee never hears, the promise to someone with zero sanctioning power. If the obligation lives in the substrate as other people's dispositions to hold you to it, these should be inert. Are they, and if not, what's carrying them?
- retroactive validation. I'm fairly sure there's a common-law rule under which acts done by someone who turns out to have lacked authority are still treated as binding on people who relied on them, and there are self-declared governments whose earlier acts get ratified afterwards. On her view all of that should just fail. Ask how the substrate reaches backward in time.
- the notational variance objection: if every declaration can be rewritten as announcement-plus-backstage-machinery, is there any observation her view makes that Austin's doesn't? Her likely answer is that hers predicts where declarations fail — no standing, no audience, no record — so follow up with the point that felicitiy conditions already predict exactly those failures, and ask what's left over.
- one applied question: can a chatbot or a self-executing contract issue a declaration? Does her view make machine declarations easier to swallow than Austin's does, and does she want that consequence.

No warm-ups, nothing about what drew her to this, and don't burn a question on "so explain the view" — my intro handles that. Each question has to be sayable out loud in under three sentences.
