I'm sitting on a working group that's drafting the franchise rules for a new binational metropolitan authority — a transit/water/port body covering a city that spilled across an international border sometime last century. We can't agree on who the electorate is, and every proposal on the table quietly answers that question before arguing about anything else. Residents on both sides, obviously; but also people who commute in daily and vote somewhere else, a seasonal workforce with no local residence at all, an airport enclave staffed by a third-country carrier, and — according to the hydrologists — everybody downstream whose water table our extraction decisions move.

So before we look at another proposal I want the spec. Write the desiderata for a theory of the boundaries of the demos: the requirements any account of who constitutes the demos has to meet to count as adequate *for our purposes* — that is, usable as a rule for actually constituting a decision-making body, not just a standard for criticizing existing ones from the outside. The purpose matters here, so if you think some requirement binds on a critic but not on a constitution-drafter, or the reverse, say so explicitly.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — the concrete situation that shows a theory failing without it. At minimum I want the list to cover: the circularity worry (a demos can't democratically settle who's in the demos without already being one); the difference between being affected by a decision and being subject to the authority that makes it, and what happens to a theory that runs the two together; the case where the unit itself splits, merges, or dissolves; the fact that present borders are historical accidents that often trace back to conquest, so a theory that simply reads off the existing jurisdiction looks conservative by construction; whether the output is a set of persons or a distribution of weights, since "how much say" is live for us and not only "who's in"; and whether the rule can be operated at all by the people who have to administer it.

Then mark which of these you'd call non-negotiable and which are really one camp's commitments dressed up as criteria — that distinction is half of what I'm after. And the part I most want: if some subset of the list can't be jointly satisfied, name the subset, show why, and say what we're left choosing between — which requirement to drop, or a reason to think the whole framing is misconceived. Tell me also which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out.

Last thing, one paragraph only: given the list you just wrote, does it favor a residence-based binary franchise, or something that hands out fractional shares of a vote proportional to how much of a person's life the authority actually governs? Commit, dont hedge — if my own list turns out to license the weighted thing I'll have to eat it in front of the group.

No bibliography, no footnotes, no name-dropping. I've read the all-affected and all-subjected crowd; what I havent got is a checklist I can hand to a civil engineer and a municipal lawyer.
