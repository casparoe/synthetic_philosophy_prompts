I am a novelist, and for the better part of two years I have been at work on a book told in five voices, only one of which belongs to an ordinary adult human being; the others are a common octopus kept briefly in a research tank on the Adriatic, a border collie, a six-month-old infant, and a retired lexicographer now deep into dementia. To keep the voices from bleeding into one another — the perennial hazard of animal narration, which so easily becomes a human being in a fur or mantle suit — I have maintained a card file on each narrator, and on the lesser creatures that populate their chapters, in which I tag every mind with one of the four grades Dennett distinguished: the purely Darwinian creature, whose entire repertoire was settled before it hatched; the Skinnerian creature, which learns by consequence within its own lifetime; the Popperian creature, which carries an inner model of the world and can therefore try things out in imagination before trying them in flesh; and the Gregorian creature, whose thinking is built out of borrowed external tools — words, tallies, maps, calendars, the accumulated contrivances of other minds. I have been tagging with a single rule of my own devising, which I want examined rather than admired: assign a mind to the highest grade at which it can presently operate unaided.

My verdicts are as follows, and I suspect they will not all survive. (1) The octopus I tagged Darwinian, on the grounds that everything remarkable about her was in place at hatching; I hedged only because she opens unfamiliar jars and because there is a much-argued-over report of octopuses acquiring a colour preference merely by watching another octopus attack a ball. (2) The collie I tagged twice over: Skinnerian in her architecture, but Gregorian by courtesy, since she follows a pointed finger, waits on the meaning of several hundred spoken words, and reads a household's moods — none of which she would have acquired alone in a field. (3) A scrub jay in the garden of the institute I tagged Popperian, because, having been watched while burying a nut, she returned and moved it, which looks less like learning from punishment than like weighing what a rival would think. (4) The infant I tagged Darwinian-plus-Skinnerian and refused Gregorian, even though she will be reasoning with words inside two years; latent capacity seemed to me to count for nothing at the moment of description. (5) The lexicographer I tagged Gregorian and will not budge on, although by the tests I cheerfully applied to the jay and the collie he would now fail them all; his wife still addresses him as a person, and the entire fourth voice depends on my continuing to do so. (6) The near-contemporary language model that appears in the fifth voice, and which the lexicographer consults in the manner of an oracle, I tagged Gregorian outright — a creature that seems to enter the ladder at the top, with no body of its own to have been designed and no lifetime of rewards and punishments quite like anyone else's, although its training consisted of little else.

What I am after is the principle, if there is one, that these six verdicts add up to: please state it precisely, clause by clause, indicating which of my cases forces each clause, and tell me plainly which verdicts I must surrender and which I may keep at the price of complication. I would like you to address three knots in particular — whether the four grades are exclusive categories or nested ones, such that a Gregorian creature must also be a Popperian, Skinnerian, and Darwinian one; whether the tagging is synchronic (what the mind can do now) or diachronic (what it could do, or once could, or was built by); and how much work the word unaided in my rule can bear, given that the collie's pointing and the lexicographer's recollections are alike capacities sustained from outside. Then apply whatever principle you settle on to cases I have not raised, including one I have been circling and avoiding: a character who loses speech entirely after a stroke yet goes on planning, deceiving, and using tools with undiminished cunning — is he demoted, and does my rule commit me to narrating him as a lesser mind? I should add that I cannot consult the literature and am not asking for references or a bibliography; I want the reasoning, and I would like to know, finally, whether these four grades are the sort of thing one discovers in a creature or the sort of thing one brings to it, since that determines whether my card file is research or merely an elaborate form of self-indulgence.
