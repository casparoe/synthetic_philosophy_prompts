I am preparing a reply for a county policy seminar and have found myself unable to defeat an argument I am confident is unsound. The case: our county has an ordinance, adopted in 1911, requiring every road bridge to be repainted each year. The engineering report accompanying it was lost in a 1938 courthouse fire and its author left no notes. The reformers have done precisely the homework the ordinance's defenders demand — they commissioned a study identifying three purposes the annual repaint could plausibly have served (frequent recoating of a lead-based coating, the yearly deployment of a crew that thereby inspected every structure, and periodic closures that rationed traffic over bridges then thought weak) and showing each to be obsolete, since modern coatings last a decade or more, inspection is now separately funded and separately scheduled, and the weak structures were replaced in the 1970s. The ordinance costs several million dollars a year and closes each bridge for weeks.

The conservative on the panel argues from Chesterton's fence as follows.

(1) One may not remove an institution unless one can say why it was put there: a reformer who cannot see the use of a gate has no business clearing it away.
(2) To say why a practice was put there is to know the full range of functions its founders had in view and that it has in fact discharged; part of that range may be latent, and the practice's long survival is itself evidence that functions remain which nobody has yet identified.
(3) Where the record of adoption is destroyed and its authors are dead, that range cannot be known — as with the repainting ordinance.
(4) So the ordinance may not be repealed however diligent the reformers' study, since such a study can establish only those functions its authors thought to look for.
(5) The same holds for any institution whose founding rationale is undocumented, which is most institutions, and the older a practice is, the likelier its record is to have been lost.
(6) Therefore the more obscure an institution's origins the more securely it is entrenched, and any group can render a practice permanently irremovable by destroying the evidence of its purpose.

I cannot accept (6). It converts the loss of institutional memory into an asset, makes vandalism a technique of entrenchment, and — most awkwardly for my opponent — it protects most absolutely those practices that were never deliberately instituted by anyone at all, which are exactly the ones the tradition being invoked claims to revere. I would rather salvage (1) in some form than surrender it, since without something like it the conservative position collapses into a mere preference for the familiar.

Tell me which step fails, and why it was able to look acceptable where it stands. Say explicitly how (1) and (2) must be read for the inference to (4) to go through, and whether that reading is one a conservative should want. If you think instead that the argument is valid and a premise must simply be given up, identify the cheapest one to surrender and price the surrender: what the position loses, what it retains, and whether the reduced principle still delivers any verdict at all on the bridge ordinance.
