Next month I am giving a twenty-five-minute talk with a full question period at an interdisciplinary consciousness workshop. The room is not a philosophy department. Roughly two-thirds are cognitive neuroscientists and computational modelers who work in report-based and no-report paradigms — rivalry, masking, blindsight, attentional blink — and who are functionalists by default rather than by argument; several build predictive-processing or global-workspace models; there is one higher-order theorist, one committed illusionist, and a handful of people from machine learning who would like a measurable criterion for consciousness in trained systems. The chair is a methodologist whose recurring question to every speaker is what their position forbids.

My thesis is that epiphenomenalism is not refuted by the paradox of phenomenal judgment, in the form Chalmers gave that objection, because the paradox silently assumes that first-person warrant must be causally mediated. My positive view, which I call lockstep reliabilism: a judgment about my experience is warranted not because the experience caused it but because the neural state that produces the judgment is the very state that, under the psychophysical laws I am positing, entails the experience the judgment reports. Judgment and experience are parallel effects of one physical antecedent, like two instruments wired in parallel to the same shaft, and neither causes the other. Warrant in such a configuration is the ordinary warrant we already grant in common-cause inference, where a belief about X is caused not by X but by a shared cause of X and of the belief.

The argument sketch I will present runs: first, the paradox moves from the premise that experience causes nothing to the conclusion that the accuracy of our judgments about experience is inexplicable; second, that inference requires a linking premise tying accuracy to causation, which is a substantive reliabilist commitment and not a truism; third, reliability can be secured by lawful covariation without causal contact, as uncontroversial common-cause cases show; fourth, what therefore remains against the epiphenomenalist is not an epistemic paradox but the metaphysical complaint that the covariation is brute — which is the price of the view rather than a refutation of it. I have already decided to concede three things in the talk: the brute-law complaint stands; I have no evolutionary story for why the laws should map onto precisely those states that got recruited for self-report; and my account makes warrant externally individuated, so a zombie duplicate would produce identical sentences without warrant, undetectably from the inside.

I want a prep sheet, not a defense of my position. Give me the eight or so objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if it landed unanswered, each phrased in the register of the sub-audience most likely to raise it — a neuroscientist's version of the idleness complaint is not an illusionist's version, and neither is a machine-learning person's — and for each: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; an explicit note on where that reply is softest; and a verdict on whether to concede, partially concede, or hold. Then tell me what I should concede in the opening two minutes so that the session does not spend itself entirely on falsifiability; which single reply has to land for the view to survive the afternoon; which two objections will sound devastating and are not; and which one I am hoping nobody asks. I have a suspicion about the last: something to the effect that my covariation is not merely unexplained but improbable, since nothing could have selected for a law connecting an inert property to whichever neural state happened to be recruited for reporting it. Tell me whether that is the right thing to dread or whether there is something worse I have not seen, and if there is, what I can say about it in two minutes without abandoning the view.

One note on tone. I have spent more than a decade in blog comment threads where this debate gets compressed into a ritual, one side observing that qualia do no causal work and might as well not exist, the other replying that this is simply the hard problem restated. Assume your reader is past that. I want the objections as practitioners of these methods would genuinely put them, including the ones that turn on what their experimental designs can and cannot distinguish.
