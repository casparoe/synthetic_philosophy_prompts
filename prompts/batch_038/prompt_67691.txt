Our reading group has been working through Hume's monetary essays — "Of Money," "Of Interest," and "Of the Balance of Trade" — and one member, Daniel, keeps cutting across the discussion with the slogan "money is only a veil." He has deployed it in support of three quite different conclusions: (i) that the mercantilists were simply confused in treating a favourable balance of trade as a source of national wealth; (ii) that the post-2008 rounds of quantitative easing were, in his phrase, "an elaborate way of changing the units"; and (iii) that a country inside a currency union has nothing to gain from leaving and devaluing, because relative prices would readjust automatically in any case — the point he says Hume's specie-flow argument already establishes.

I suspect the slogan is doing different work in each case, and that at least one of the three rests on a reading Hume himself would not accept. Could you lay out, as a list I can bring to next week's meeting, the distinct precise theses that "money is only a veil" might stand for? I have in mind at least the following distinctions, though please add any I have missed and correct my formulations:

- a claim about long-run proportionality between the money stock and the price level, as against a claim that real variables are determined independently of nominal ones;
- neutrality with respect to the level of money as against superneutrality with respect to its rate of growth, and what standard models imply about each;
- a claim about the steady state as against a claim about the transition, including Hume's observation about the interval between an inflow of specie and the resulting rise in prices, and the distributional consequences of who receives the new money first;
- a claim that the value of money rests on convention rather than on the commodity embodying it, which may be ontological rather than quantitative;
- a claim that the money stock adjusts endogenously to the needs of trade, so that exogenous control of it is illusory;
- a policy claim, that no monetary intervention can improve on what would happen anyway.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive thesis that is defensible under specifiable conditions, or false — and where the conditions come from. Then indicate where Hume's own arguments in these essays support the reading, where they go beyond it, and where they cut against it, taking account of his treatment of the balance of trade and of his unease about paper credit. Finally, go through Daniel's three conclusions one at a time: state which reading each requires, whether that reading is in fact available to him, whether Hume is entitled to it, and — given how loosely he uses the phrase — which of the readings he most likely means when he says it.
