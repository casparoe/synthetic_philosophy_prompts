I am a third-year doctoral student jointly supervised between philosophy and linguistics, and our university's interdisciplinary fund has an annual bridging-grant call: twelve months, a modest budget, and a requirement that the project involve a partner outside the home discipline. I want to pitch a project on the machine-translation question my reading group has been circling all year, the thesis we have taken to calling "the shallowness of machine translation" — Hofstadter's long-running insistence that translating correctly demands a vast and actively deployed body of knowledge about how the world works, so that systems which merely shuffle textual form are bound to be shallow however fluent they look. The obvious rejoinder, which a friend of mine working on production translation systems repeats to me at every opportunity, is that contemporary neural systems translate far better than anything that existed when the argument was first pressed, so the point has been empirically settled against him. That rejoinder has never struck me as decisive, though I cannot yet say why. My half-formed idea is that it assumes the claim is a prediction about output quality, when it is really a claim about the kind of capacity that produces the output; if so, output quality is the wrong test, and the right test would be something like whether a system's translation competence is *surface-fragile* — whether it survives reformulations of the source that preserve meaning while disturbing the cues a form-matching system would lean on. Please take on the role of a philosopher of mind who is also fluent in the computational literature on translation and its evaluation, think carefully before drafting, and treat this as a framing problem rather than a write-up exercise.

Before the proposal proper, spend a short paragraph brainstorming three or four candidate formulations of the research question and state which you would choose and why. I suspect the debate runs together at least three distinct claims: that translation requires a large body of world knowledge; that this knowledge must be deployed in producing the translation rather than merely stored somewhere in the system; and that deploying it in this way constitutes understanding. The interesting question, as I see it, is whether the middle claim can be given a determinate sense at all, or whether it collapses into the first — and whether a system that passes ordinary quality evaluations while failing systematically under meaning-preserving perturbation would vindicate or refute the thesis.

Then write the proposal itself, roughly two pages, with the following components. First, a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer and so that a competent critic could tell what would settle it. Second, an account of why the two existing camps — call them the world-model camp and the benchmark camp — both leave the question open, including an honest treatment of the worry that the question is merely verbal or unfalsifiable and of what would make it non-vacuous. Third, the method, laid out phase by phase across the twelve months: what the conceptual work consists in, what a diagnostic that a linguistics lab partner could actually build and run would look like, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives (more benchmark scores, or a purely armchair analysis of what understanding amounts to). Fourth, what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing, including whether showing that the question is empirically undecidable would itself be a fundable result. Fifth, a realistic assessment of scope, naming explicitly what I would have to cut to finish in a year.

Finally, the reviewers always ask one particular thing, so build in an answer: why should a bridging grant fund philosophy here rather than simply paying for more evaluation runs? Give me two or three sentences suitable for the closing section that answer this without conceding that the conceptual component is decorative. No bibliography and no citations, please — I will supply those myself, and I would rather the argument not depend on my getting a reference right.
