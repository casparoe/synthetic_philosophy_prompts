The question was:

"Short answer, 25 minutes. Patients with pain asymbolia, typically after insular damage, say they feel pain but that it does not bother them, and they show little aversive behavior. Stipulate the following: the patient localizes and rates intensity normally, describes the pain as sharp and burning, says flatly that he does not mind it, and is otherwise emotionally intact — he minds loud noise and foul tastes as much as anyone does. Does this case show that pain's unpleasantness is separable from its sensory character, and what does it imply about whether pain is bad in virtue of how it feels? Address at least one account of unpleasantness and say what the case predicts for it."

Student's answer:

"Unlike congenital insensitivity, where there is no pain signal at all, the asymbolic does receive and process the signal: he can say where it is, how intense it is, and what quality it has — sharp, burning. The term goes back to Schilder and Stengel. So this looks like a clean dissociation between the sensory-discriminative side of pain and the affective-motivational side, which is exactly the split the two-component picture predicts.

What it seems to show is that unpleasantness is not built into the felt quality of pain, since the quality is intact and the unpleasantness is gone. That is bad news for the motivational accounts. Bain's imperativism and Klein's evaluativism hold that a pain is unpleasant in virtue of carrying a command, or a negative evaluation of a bodily condition; asymbolia is normally presented as a refutation of those views, because the patient still has a pain without the command, so the command can't be what the unpleasantness consists in. A hedonist about pain does better here: on that view badness is a separate mental property that can be attached to a sensation or detached from it, and asymbolia is just the detached case.

There's a worry about how much weight the reports can bear. Saying 'I feel pain but I don't mind it' may be conceptually confused — if it doesn't bother you, in what sense is it pain? — so we shouldn't take these patients at their word about what their experience is like. But the descriptions of location, intensity and quality are detailed and consistent across cases, so the sensory side of the dissociation is solid even if the affective self-reports are shaky.

Also, the stipulation that he minds loud noise doesn't rule out that the lesion has dulled negative affect generally; a brain that has lost the insula may simply have a reduced capacity for finding anything aversive, which would explain the missing unpleasantness without any interesting lesson about the nature of pain. So my conclusion is that unpleasantness is extrinsic to pain — it depends on caring — but the case is too weird to lean much on. Either way, it cannot show that pain is intrinsically bad."
