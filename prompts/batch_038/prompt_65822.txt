I head the measurement and incentives team at a regional social-services authority. We write the performance measures and payment targets that go into our contracts with outside providers — employment placement, supported housing for people leaving homelessness, home-care visits for older adults — and those measures move real money and real provider behaviour. Roughly thirty proposals reach me each quarter: some from programme managers who want a measure, some from providers who want an easy one, some from my own analysts who think they have found something better. What I need is a screening procedure my analysts can run on a proposal in under an hour, before it comes to me for sign-off, and that they can run without a background in statistics or philosophy.

The procedure should be built on a working view of mine, which I want you to develop and stress-test rather than accept as settled. The four-way taxonomy of Goodhart failures that circulates in the alignment literature — regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial — sorts failures by their statistical structure, and that is not the axis on which my office can act. What I can act on is the remedy. My suspicion is that at least three distinct failures get reported to me under the single heading "this measure can be gamed," and that the reporting is useless precisely because the heading hides them:

- Gaming proper: the provider can raise the score without raising the client outcome. The remedy is verification, audit, clawback.
- Leakage: the improvement is genuine, but it is bought by shifting effort from an unmeasured part of the caseload or the task to the measured part, so that outcomes rise for some clients and fall for others while the aggregate score improves. The remedy is widening or pairing the measure; nobody has done anything wrong and an audit would be a waste.
- Drift: everyone behaves honestly, and the proxy–outcome relationship decays anyway because the caseload, the labour market, the technology or the service model changes underneath it. The remedy is re-baselining and a shorter review cycle, and no amount of contracting cleverness prevents it.

These three have different symptoms, different responsible parties and different contractual consequences. I want the procedure to force a classification rather than allow an analyst to stop at "at risk of Goodharting."

Against that background, the deliverable should contain:

1. The questions in the order an analyst should ask them, and for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer — not "consider whether the proxy tracks the outcome," but the specific evidence or line of reasoning that entitles a non-expert to move on. Where a question cannot be answered from data we would plausibly hold (we get quarterly provider returns and a small client experience survey; we do not get anything randomised, and we rarely get outcome data beyond twelve months), say so, and give the fallback and its cost.

2. A sharpening of the test I currently apply by hand: the analyst must write down the three cheapest ways a provider could lift the score substantially within one contract year, and then state for each whether the client outcome moves with it. Tell me how to make that step something other than an exercise in imagination — what to prompt for, whose testimony counts, how to check the list is not padded with implausible scenarios — and what the procedure should conclude when the analyst genuinely cannot think of any.

3. The exit conditions. Where the procedure terminates in reject; where in accept with named monitoring; where in accept outright; and where it stops being a factual question and hands the decision to me because it turns on values — for instance, how much unmeasured harm to a subgroup we are willing to accept in exchange for a measure we can actually verify at reasonable cost. Mark those handoffs explicitly, and say what information I need at each one.

4. Three worked applications, showing the questions being answered and not merely listed: (a) an employment provider measured by the number of clients in work at the six-month point; (b) a supported-housing provider measured by the proportion of residents who have not returned to homelessness at twelve months; (c) home-care provision measured by a vendor's algorithmic risk score predicting which clients will need emergency admission in the coming year, with payment attached to reductions in predicted risk. I expect (c) to break the procedure, since the object being optimised is a model whose own training data we generate, and I would rather see exactly how it breaks than see it handled smoothly. At least one of the three must come out against my interests: I have publicly committed to the employment measure, so if the procedure rejects or substantially rewrites it, say so without softening.

5. An account of the reflexive problem. Once this procedure exists it becomes a target in its own right — programme managers and vendors will learn to fill in the boxes in whatever way gets their measure approved, and my analysts will learn which answers shorten the meeting. Tell me what to build in against that, and be candid if the honest answer is that no checklist can be made robust to being checklisted.

6. A closing statement of what the procedure cannot do at all, together with your own assessment of how confident you are in the central claims you have made: in particular, how confident you are that gaming, leakage and drift form a real division rather than three descriptions of one underlying failure, and which single step of the whole procedure you are least sure about and why.

Please do not include a bibliography or a reading list; refer to positions and arguments by their content and I will attach references myself if we publish this.
