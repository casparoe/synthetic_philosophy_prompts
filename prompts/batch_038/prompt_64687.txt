I cover research integrity and the sociology of science for a general-interest magazine, which means a disproportionate share of my stories begin with one frightened person: a postdoc, a lab manager, a departed co-author, a technician who will speak only on the condition that nothing identifying appears in print. Corroboration usually exists but is unpublishable — a spreadsheet someone photographed and then deleted, a colleague who confirms the atmosphere but not the act. My editor now wants a written protocol for deciding when a single anonymous source is enough to run an allegation, because at present the decision is made by whoever sounds most confident in a hallway. I have been reading Nozick's tracking account of knowledge, and it struck me that the newsroom question is close to a literal instance of it: what I want to know is whether the source's report counterfactually tracks the facts — whether they would have produced this report had the allegation been false, and whether they would have found and surfaced it had it been true but shaped differently than they suppose. I would like you to build the protocol on that foundation rather than on the usual journalism-school vocabulary of reliability and credibility, which I find does no real work.

The deliverable should be a sequence of questions a reporter can actually ask in order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the points at which the procedure terminates in "we do not run this," and the points at which it stops being about truth and hands the decision to an editor because the live issue is harm to identifiable people or our own institutional interest. Two features of the tracking framework need explicit handling. First, method: I take the lesson of Nozick's grandmother case — her belief that the child is well looks insensitive, since she would have been lied to had he been unwell, until you pin down the route by which she forms it — to be that sensitivity is only evaluable relative to a specified method of belief-formation, and my sources are frequently unable or unwilling to specify theirs; they "know from the lab," or "everyone knew," or "you could see it in the raw files." Tell me how to pin the route, what to do when the source cannot, and whether an unpinnable route is automatically disqualifying or merely costly. Second, closure: tracking theories give up closure under known entailment, and I have watched that pattern play out badly in practice, where a reporter is confident about the narrow act and then writes confident sentences about what the senior author knew, what the institution tolerated, and what should happen to the paper. I want the protocol to state precisely which inferences I may carry forward from a sensitively-held allegation and which must be re-earned separately, and to justify the line rather than merely assert it.

Some constraints on the ground: I typically have forty-eight hours between a source going on the record and an editor needing a recommendation; I cannot disclose to a source what we intend to publish or what we have verified elsewhere; nothing in the protocol may require a document I can legally print, though it may require documents to exist; and legal review is mandatory before publication but sits outside my discretion. There is also a rule of thumb currently circulating on my desk that I want tested rather than inherited: if being wrong would cost the source their job, their funding, or their visa, we treat their report as tracking, on the theory that high stakes buy honesty. My suspicion is that this inverts the actual analysis, and that exposure is what makes a report sensitive to the source's own interests rather than to the world; I would like the protocol to either dismantle it or defend it, and to say which of the two it is doing. Then please run the procedure end to end on three cases: a postdoc alleging duplicated images in a celebrated paper who volunteers that they were competing with the accused lab for the same grant; a lab manager reporting that a prominent investigator repeatedly ran studies without required approvals, corroborated by two colleagues who will confirm only under an anonymity we cannot characterize in print; and a source I have used twice before and who was right both times, now alleging something that would upend a subfield, offering no documents and declining to say how they came to know. For the third I expect the procedure to tell me to stand down, and I want it to say so in terms I can hand to an editor without embarrassment rather than hedging into a maybe. Close by stating what the protocol cannot do: the cases where it will misfire, the ways a determined fabricator or a self-deceived whistleblower defeats it, and which of its steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing. I am not looking for references or further reading — I need something I can run on Tuesday.
