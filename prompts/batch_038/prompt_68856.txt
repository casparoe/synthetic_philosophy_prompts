I retired from a national lab four years ago — most of my career was materials characterization, which is a polite way of saying I spent decades deciding whether two samples were the same stuff. Lately I've been reading philosophy on my own, mostly badly, and I've gotten stuck on the persistence debate: endurantism versus perdurantism, three-dimensional objects wholly present at each moment versus four-dimensional worms with temporal parts. I follow the arguments more or less, but they always feel like their being run on toy examples — statues and lumps of clay, cats on mats, that sort of thing. So I want to try one on a real building with all the mess left in.

The case is the Frauenkirche in Dresden. Here are the facts as I have them; correct me where I'm wrong. Bähr's baroque church went up between 1726 and 1743, and it was already the third church building on that site. It held out against the firebombing of 13–14 February 1945 for about two days and then the dome came down on the 15th. The East German authorities decided to leave the wreck standing as a war memorial, and it sat that way for nearly fifty years. After reunification the reconstruction began in 1994 and the church was reconsecrated in October 2005. Alot of the original stone had been catalogued and stored — I've seen a figure of something over eight thousand blocks put back roughly where they came from — and the rest is new sandstone cut to match, so the walls are visibly a patchwork of dark old stone and pale new. The cross on top of the dome is, I believe, a new one made by a British goldsmith whose father flew in the raids, and the bent original, pulled out of the rubble, is kept inside.

What I want from you:

First, tell me what the object even is under each view. Under endurantism, what is standing on the Neumarkt in 1975 — a church, a ruin that counts as a church, or a heap of stone that will become one? Is a forty-five year gap survivable, and if so what carries the identity across it, given that for a good stretch there wasn't even an assembled object, just sorted blocks on a field? Under perdurantism, do I have one worm running from 1726 to today with a badly damaged stretch of temporal parts in the middle, or two worms, or one worm plus a later one that overlaps it in the 1990s? Be concrete about which relation is doing the work — causal continuity, closest continuer, whatever it is — rather than gesturing at it.

Second, run the details through the two views. Does it matter that some of the stone is literally the old stone and some isn't, and does it matter differently to each? What about the fact that the original cross still exists, is preserved, and is not on the building — is the thing on the dome a part of the church or a replacement part, and who decides? And the third-church-on-the-site point strikes me as awkward for both theories: if 'the Frauenkirche' could already survive being torn down and rebuilt in 1727, maybe the name picks out a lineage or a location rather than an object, in which case both views are answering a question nobody in Dresden was asking.

Third, the part I actually care about. The fight in the 1990s was not a fight about numerical identity. The conservation doctrine that grew out of the 1964 Venice Charter basically forbids rebuilding a destroyed monument and only permits reassembling the fragments that are actually there; UNESCO has loosened that in the last decade or so for exceptional cases with complete documentation — the wrecked mausoleums at Timbuktu were rebuilt, for instance. The words people used were authenticity, memory, copy, Disneyfication. So tell me honestly whether endurantism or perdurantism has anything at all to say about authenticity, or whether they are silent on it and the metaphysics does no work in the real dispute. If they're silent, say so plainly, and then say what the case does show: a problem the theories never anticipated, a place where one of them quietly changes the subject, or a reason to think the whole quarrel is about how we carve up buildings rather than about how buildings persist.

My first instinct, as a lab man, was that you could settle this with measurements — date the stone, check the quarry, count the original blocks. Then I realized the measurements answer a question nobody in that argument was asking, and I'd like you to say whether that's a confession of defeat or the actual point.

No reading list, please; I can't chase papers down anyway and I'd rather you spend the words on reasoning. But don't give me a survey of the two positions either, I've read the survey. Take the building seriously and show me where each theory starts to strain.
