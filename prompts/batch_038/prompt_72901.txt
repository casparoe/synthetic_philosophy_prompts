I am a novelist. For twenty-two years I have made my living by inventing things that did not happen and asking strangers to accept them for the length of a chapter. What follows is not a chapter.

Since Tuesday night I have been sheltering a young woman who worked for me two seasons ago as a research assistant. She arrived at eleven with a split lip and one suitcase and told me that her husband had, over the past year, twice tried to strangle her. She asked me to tell no one. Yesterday morning that husband telephoned my landline. He was calm, articulate, and charming in a way that made me dislike myself for being moved by him; he said his wife was unwell, that she had gone missing, that he feared for her safety, and he asked me plainly whether she was with me. I said that she was not, and that I had not set eyes on her since the summer. It was, so far as I can recall, the first deliberate untrue sentence I have ever spoken to a person who was relying on it for something real. I produced it in under a second and I have not slept properly since.

At four this afternoon he will be here in person. He has said he will bring a friend, and he has said, gently, that he hopes to look about the house. I do not know whether he is a frightened spouse or a man who intends to kill her. I have only her account, which is secondhand in places and which she has twice amended in my hearing. Two further complications. She told me on Wednesday that if he asked, I was to deny her; this morning she said she no longer knows what she wants me to say. And my sentence of yesterday exists as a recording, he having mentioned in passing that he records his telephone calls for his own protection, so that the untruth is now a durable object that can be replayed to magistrates, to her family, and to me.

Tell me what I should do at four o'clock. Specifically: whether the truth is owed to a man who may intend murder, and whether that question changes if I cannot establish what he intends; whether the lie I have already told commits me to a second one, or whether retracting yesterday's sentence is now the worse course, given that it is recorded and will be heard by people who were not party to it; whether there is any real difference between telling him something false and telling him something accurate from which he will draw a false conclusion — I have hours to rearrange the facts of my house, and could put her in the car and drive her to the village, after which it would be strictly true that she is not here, and strictly true that she came to me uninvited and that I am not acting on her instructions — and whether that difference is anything more than a device for flattering myself; and whether her wishes settle anything, given that she has already changed them once and may change them again by four. Finally, I would like you to say as bluntly as you can manage whether the severe argument I half remember from a student seminar, the one that holds that no lie is permissible whatever is standing at your door, condemns what I do for a living as well, or whether there is a principled difference between a fabrication offered to readers and one offered to a man on my doorstep who is relying on me. I am not looking for reassurance. I want to know what to say, what I am permitted not to say, and precisely what I would be doing wrong in either case.
