I am in my final year of high school and I have drafted an essay for a philosophy essay competition that is open to secondary-school students. The limit is 2,000 words and my draft runs to about 1,700. I have left the references out on purpose, because I intend to add them myself once the argument is settled — so please do not send me a reading list, do not suggest sources to go and look up, and do not offer a polished rewrite. What I want is criticism.

The draft is below, followed by six specific requests.

---

**Who Gets to Fix Whose Mistakes?**

**1. The question.** People who write about joint action want to know what makes it the case that you and I are doing something together rather than each doing our own thing side by side. The familiar shape of an answer is that each of us has the right kind of attitude toward the same goal, and that each of us knows something about the other's attitude. I want to propose a different test. Instead of asking what each participant intends, ask who is allowed to fix whose mistakes, and when.

**2. What I mean.** I will call it a repair right: a standing, un-negotiated entitlement of one participant to intervene in another participant's part of the activity while it is still going on, where the intervention does not count as interference and does not count as a criticism of the person. Where repair rights are in place, a mistake by one member stops being a fact about that member and becomes a task for everyone. I will call this error pooling. Where they are absent, a mistake stays exactly where it happened: it belongs to the person who made it, and other participants can register it only afterwards, and then only as a grievance.

**3. Three cases.** (a) I play cello in the pit orchestra for my school's musical. In one number the violinist beside me comes in a bar early. If I hold to what I planned, we diverge; if I stop, the number collapses. What actually happens is that I move with her. Nobody asked me to and nobody owes me an explanation afterwards for having done it. The permission was already inside the activity.

(b) My history teacher runs group projects in which each member of a four-person group is assigned a role: researcher, writer, designer, presenter. Last term our designer noticed that the writer's argument contradicted a source the researcher had found. She said nothing, because it was not her role and because saying it would have read as a criticism of two other people's work. The project was submitted, was internally inconsistent, and got a good grade. Everyone did their part. I do not think they acted together.

(c) Two people carrying a sofa up a narrow staircase. When one of them gets a bad angle on a landing, the other does not wait to be told; they both re-grip. The re-gripping is not a favour and not a complaint.

**4. Where I think the accounts I have read miss this.** On the planning account, a shared intention is a structure of interlocking individual intentions plus sub-plans that mesh, and the parties need common knowledge of all of this. My worry is about timing. Meshing is something that has to be achieved, and the interesting question is what makes it possible in the two seconds when there is no opportunity to negotiate anything — which is exactly the situation in (a) and (c). I also think this account is committed to treating the four students in (b) as a case of shared intention that went wrong, when I want to say they were never jointly acting at all.

On the view that treats a we-intention as a distinctive state in each individual's head, irreducible to any combination of I-intentions plus beliefs about other people, my worry is that jointness has been located in a kind of psychological state. Each of the four students in (b) could have had the strongest we-intention available and the project would still have failed the same way.

On the plural subject view, the parties form a joint commitment, and that commitment generates obligations toward one another and the standing to rebuke a member who does not do their part. This is the closest to what I want, and I have to be careful here. But rebuke looks backwards: it is what you are owed after a failure. A repair right is exercised during the activity, before the failure has happened, and it is not a form of blame at all. In (a) there is nothing to rebuke anyone for, and yet something has to happen.

On the view that distinguishes acting in the we-mode from acting in the I-mode, I take the claim to be that in the we-mode I adopt the group's reason as my reason, and that this attitude is sufficient by itself, without any of us needing to know what the others are thinking. If that is right, my account is redundant, because adopting the group's reason already explains why I shift with the violinist. But I do not think the attitude is sufficient. The designer in (b) plausibly adopted the group's reason — she wanted the project to be good — and that is precisely why she stayed quiet.

**5. Scale.** I want to say that repair rights get thin as a group gets bigger, and that this is why a platoon can act jointly while a country going to war cannot, even though we describe both as acting. But I am not confident this is a claim about joint action rather than a claim about how loudly I can hear a group. I may cut this section.

**6. Objections I have thought of.** (i) Circularity: if repair rights only exist because of a joint commitment, then they cannot be what joint action consists in; I have explained the thing by the thing. (ii) Role division: my group-project case suggests that assigned roles destroy joint action, but an orchestra and a surgical team are divided by role to an extreme degree and are the clearest cases of joint action anyone has. My answer is that roles destroy joint action only when they are handed down from outside the activity, which I admit is fast. (iii) Asymmetry: a conductor and a coxswain have repair rights over everyone and nobody has any over them. I want to say this is still joint action provided the distribution is known to all, but then I seem to be saying that a hierarchy is joint action, and I cannot tell whether hierarchy is compatible with jointness or is a rival to it.

**7. What I want to defend.** Joint action is not primarily a matter of what sits in each participant's head. It is a matter of what participants are licensed to do to one another's part of the activity while the activity is still going on.

---

**What I would like from you.** Please address these six points and not much else.

1. Are "repair right" and "error pooling" doing real work, or are they new labels for something the views in section 4 already contain? In particular, tell me plainly whether my contrast with rebuke in the plural subject view is a distinction without a difference. If it is, I would rather know now.

2. Accuracy. Go through section 4 and tell me where I have misstated or overstated a position. I am least sure about the knowledge condition I attribute to the planning account, and about the sufficiency claim I attribute at the end of section 4.

3. Give me the strongest objection to my thesis that a defender of the planning account could make, which is not one of the three I list in section 6.

4. My cases. Do the pit orchestra and the history group project actually support the thesis, or would they come out the same way on the accounts I am criticising? If one of the three cases is weaker than I think it is, say which and why.

5. Section 5. Tell me whether a 2,000-word essay can defend a claim about group size, or whether it should go.

6. Cuts. Tell me what to remove and in what order, and then give me your verdict in one sentence on whether the claim in section 7 is true, false, or not the sort of claim that can be either.

Bluntness is much more useful to me than encouragement, and I am not looking for help with sentence-level style.
