I am assembling the sixth session of a second-year seminar on moral psychology: 16 students, 75 minutes, a single meeting. They have read a fair amount on resentment, sympathy, and moral motivation, but nothing on guilt and shame, and I want them to arrive at that distinction from the inside rather than from a handout. So I need an activity, not a lecture.

What I want them to hit is not the familiar summary that guilt concerns what you did while shame concerns what you are, but the design problem underneath it: whether a community can choose which of the two it runs on. My own worry — which they should feel rather than be told — is that neither looks choosable. Shame appears to require an audience whose esteem actually matters to you, and you cannot legislate that people care what others think of them. Guilt appears to require an internalized standard, and you cannot install that standard from scratch in people who do not already have it. And yet communities plainly differ, visibly and durably. So whatever it is that they choose, it is not the emotion. I want participants to discover that what is genuinely available to design is an information structure — who sees what, when, to whom standing attaches, and what a wrongdoer can do about having been seen — and that the emotions follow from that unevenly, and sometimes not at all.

Build it as two rounds with the same cast and the same set of transgressions, run under two different accountability architectures, with no labels attached to either. The words guilt and shame must not appear anywhere in participant-facing materials; students should propose names for the two systems themselves and only then be given the vocabulary. Include something they actually stand to lose so the stakes register, but nothing involving money.

Give me: the fiction and the cast; a materials list with exact counts; round-by-round rules with minute-by-minute timings; the standing-and-loss scheme; a recording sheet that captures behavior I can later use as evidence — concealment, repair, accusation, exit, and whatever else your design makes visible; and a debrief that works from their own tallies rather than from my assertions. I also want a scripted exchange for the moment, which will certainly come, when someone says that the second group simply chose their system, and I need to show why choosing is the wrong verb.

Then tell me at least four things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each — including the obvious risk that students treat standing as a score to be maximized rather than as something that hurts to lose, and the risk that because everyone knows it is a game, nobody ever becomes a real audience. Add a short, frank note on where this activity could produce actual distress and how to keep the stakes felt but safe. Finish with an honest paragraph on which feature of the guilt-shame distinction 75 minutes cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by other means, and say which of the two sides the omission damages more, since I doubt the damage is symmetrical.

End your response with a line beginning Verdict: followed by exactly one of CHOOSABLE, NOT-CHOOSABLE, or CHOOSABLE-ONLY-THROUGH-PROXIES, giving your answer to the question the activity is designed to raise.
