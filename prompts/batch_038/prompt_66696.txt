I'm the comms lead at a county health department and I'm still chewing over an argument I had with one of our epidemiologists last week. A reporter from the local paper emailed asking whether the drop in teen vaping we've been seeing in our youth survey three years running is real, or whether it's just kids being less willing to admit it now that the topic is more stigmatized. I pulled the survey data myself — response rates, the wording changes over the years, all of it — and then told her flat out: it's a real decline, not a reporting artifact. My colleague (call her Dana) wasnt happy. Her point was that I don't know that, I can't rule out that the teens simply stopped admitting it, and so I had no business saying it without a pile of hedges; apparently the right answer was something like "our data is consistent with a genuine decline." And here's the part that really grinds me: there is no further check that would settle it. Any follow-up survey you run has exactly the same problem, you're still asking kids who might be lying to you. So on her rule I could never say the thing, ever, no matter how much work I put in.

My pushback is that if you're not allowed to state flatly the things you're most confident about just because you can't demonstrate them beyond all doubt, then nobody can tell anybody anything. My own doctor tells me stuff all the time that she read in one study. Dana herself told a school board, with zero hedging, that a particular intersection was the most dangerous one in the county. So I think she's not actually living by the rule she's holding me to. But then there's a wrinkle that messes up my "she's just moving goalposts" story: about a month ago she was ALSO annoyed at me for telling a coworker flat-out that her grant had been declined. In that case I knew it, first hand — I'd been cc'd on the decision letter, I read it with my own eyes before she did. So in case one I was less than certain and got told to shut up about it, and in case two I was certain and also got told to shut up about it. Which is why I can't just wave her off as inconsistent.

So, two questions I guess. Was I actually wrong to say it flat-out to the reporter, or is Dana applying a standard nobody can meet? And is there something genuinely different going on in the grant case that I'm missing — like maybe the problem there wasn't that I didn't know, just that I blurted it out in a hallway when it wasn't my news to deliver? If there's a single principle that explains both of her complaints without making ordinary assertion impossible, I'd love to hear it, because then I can use it on her (we are a very argumentative department, sorry this is so long). Lay out your reasoning however you want, but please end with a last line starting "Verdict:" followed by just ME, HER, or NEITHER — meaning whose argument about the reporter is stronger.
