I'm three years into a philosophy PhD — my thesis is on precedent and analogical reasoning in the common law, so value theory is very much not my home turf — and our department's journal has asked me to write a short critical notice on a chapter in an edited collection that came out last year. The chapter is by a legal academic, and it deploys the incommensurability literature to make an argument about regulatory trade-offs; the running example is a transport authority choosing between routing a light rail line through a neighbourhood with bad asthma outcomes and routing it through a medieval street pattern the city has formally promised to protect. I liked the chapter and I think the case study is genuinely good. But its closing recap of the value theory left me uneasy, and I can't tell whether that's because it's actually unfair to the people it names or because I'm out of my depth. I've read the recap about five times now and I keep flip-flopping between thinking its a caricature and thinking I've missed something obvious. I should flag my own bias up front: I'm inclined to think the whole incommensurability business is a pseudo-problem wearing technical vocabulary, and that it's all really just vagueness — so please push back on me wherever I'd be writing as a philistine.

Here's the recap, more or less as the author states it. I typed it out from the book rather than paraphrasing it, but treat it as the author's summary of the literature, not as mine:

'Incommensurability is the problem that some values cannot be measured against one another on a common scale. Ruth Chang's account of hard choices says that when an agent faces a genuine choice between two options — a career in academia or a better-paid one in finance, say — the options are incomparable: neither is better than the other, and they are not exactly equally good either. Since there is no fact of the matter about which of them is better, no amount of reasoning can settle the choice, and the agent's only recourse is to commit, exercising her will rather than her judgement. This is what distinguishes hard choices from tragic dilemmas, where the options do conflict but one of them is straightforwardly worse. John Broome's objection is that the appearance of incomparability is really vagueness: the options are equal, but only roughly so, and we cannot tell where the borderline falls; once you see that, the puzzle dissolves. The practical upshot is that incommensurable values rule out cost-benefit analysis in principle, since a life, a species or a culture cannot be assigned a number.'

What I want is an accuracy audit I can actually use in the notice, not a lecture on the debate. Go through it sentence by sentence and, for each, tell me what Chang, Broome and Raz would each happily sign up to, what is fair-but-lossy simplification of the sort you'd forgive in a chapter aimed at lawyers, and what the people named would flatly deny having said. In particular I want an answer on: (a) whether 'incommensurability' in the first sentence is being used in the technical sense the philosophers intend or in something closer to the everyday 'no common yardstick' sense, and whether that slippage infects what follows; (b) whether attributing incomparability to Chang is right, because I have a vague memory of her making a big fuss about a fourth comparative relation that is supposed to be something other than incomparability, and if so whether the chapter has collapsed two things she keeps apart; (c) whether the 'only recourse is to commit, exercising will rather than judgement' sentence is a fair rendering of what she does say about commitment, or a straw man she'd object to — and if it's the latter, what the actual view is and what work the will is doing in it; (d) whether the tragic-dilemma sentence is even coherent, since it reads to me as though the comparative relations have been put the wrong way round; and (e) whether Broome is being characterised fairly, since 'we cannot tell where the borderline falls' strikes me as an epistemic reading of vagueness that may well not be his, and I don't know whether his argument is meant to establish that there is no incomparability or only that a particular argument for it fails. Then, seperately: does the cost-benefit conclusion survive the corrections? I'd like you to say which part of it, if any, is licensed by the accurate material and which part is doing independent work of its own — the chapter seems to slide between 'cannot be ranked' and 'cannot be monetised' and 'cannot be traded off at all', and I can't tell whether those come apart or whether the slide is harmless. If the correct version of the claim turns out to be weaker than the author thinks but still worth making, tell me that plainly, because it would change the tone of my notice quite a lot. Please write the whole thing in British English, and don't bother with a bibliography or a list of where to read more — I've got the papers. What I can't yet do is tell the difference between the real position and a plausible-sounding imitation of it.
