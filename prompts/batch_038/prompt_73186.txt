I am the lecturer for a final-year undergraduate paper on the epistemology of testimony, and I am setting this year's two-hour final. Of the four questions on the paper, one must be a single essay question worth 25 marks, answerable in roughly 45 minutes. Its purpose is to separate candidates who can reason about the structure of testimonial warrant from candidates who have memorised which labels attach to which slogans. A question that can be answered competently by reproducing a standard summary of the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony is, by my lights, a failed question, whatever else it does well.

Please write that question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question must present the candidate with the following stipulated case. You may polish the prose and choose a different name for the speaker, but every stipulation must survive intact, and the case must be set out as a numbered list of stipulations so that the candidate can refer to clauses by number rather than paraphrasing at length.

Vera addresses you, and says:

(i) In the ten years we have known each other, I have never said anything to you about a matter you were in a position to check independently which turned out to be false.

(ii) I am generally reliable about facts of this kind — about what I have and have not said to you in the past, and about whether those things were true.

Further stipulations: you have never once checked a claim of Vera's against independent evidence, so you hold no observations that could serve as data for (i); you have no information about Vera's track record with anyone else; no one else has vouched for her; nothing you possess counts as a defeater for either clause; you are otherwise perfectly situated to understand her utterance. And you are aware that if you were to accept (ii) on her word, you could then treat (i) as your inductive evidence for her reliability — which in turn would license further trust in what she tells you, including (ii) itself.

The question you write must ask the candidate to do three things, in this order. First, to explain precisely what goes wrong with the attempt to build a non-circular positive reason for believing (i) out of the materials the case supplies, and to say what kind of premise would be needed to repair the attempt. Second, to explain whether (ii) is any help, by distinguishing two readings of it: as a first-order claim about how Vera's past statements have come out, and as a second-order claim about her reliability concerning that very record — and to say what follows on each reading. Third, to reach and defend a verdict on whether, given the stipulations, you are epistemically permitted to believe (i). The candidate must be told explicitly that no philosopher need be named, that names carry no marks, and that all marks are awarded for reasoning that engages the stipulations.

The model answer should be about 750 words and should itself satisfy the standard you set in the marking scheme. It must commit to a verdict rather than balancing the considerations, and it must state the principle of entitlement to which it is implicitly appealing and explain why that principle is not simply an arbitrary line drawn where the case happens to become uncomfortable.

The marking scheme should allocate all 25 marks across six must-hit moves. For each move, give the marks it carries, a one- or two-sentence description of what earning those marks looks like in a script, and a one-sentence note on the error that most reliably forfeits them. Please design at least one of the six moves so that it cannot be earned by a candidate who has correctly identified the two broad positions and applied their names to the case — that is, make it impossible to reach full marks by correct labelling alone. Conclude with a short paragraph stating what distinguishes a first-class script from a good-but-unremarkable one, expressed in terms of the moves rather than in terms of general qualities such as clarity or originality.

One further constraint on everything you produce: do not name any philosopher, cite any work, or gesture at the secondary literature anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme. My candidates have been taught the debate, and I want the deliverable to be usable without any appeal to authority.
