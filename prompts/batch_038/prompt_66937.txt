I am a novelist, and for two years now I have been working on a book set in an invented port city-state — call it Vess, population some four hundred thousand — which, after a decade of budgetary catastrophe, replaced most of its legislature with something close to what the economist Robin Hanson proposed under the name of futarchy. In my Vess, the citizens vote on exactly one thing every fifth year: a definition of the public good, expressed as a single measurable quantity, together with the schedule on which that definition may be amended. Everything else — taxation, housing, the hospitals, the harbor tariffs — is settled by paired prediction markets, one market pricing the outcome if a given policy is adopted, its twin pricing the outcome if it is not, with the better-priced policy enacted automatically. My difficulty is that the man I have written to run this apparatus keeps emerging as a villain in a spreadsheet: fluent, smug, and demolished at dinner parties by any journalist who has read a single article about markets. I have no wish to demolish him. I want to write scenes in which he is right, or very nearly right, and in which the reader cannot afterwards say which.

So: play him. He may go unnamed, or take whatever name you care to give him, but he is not a real person and must not speak as one. First person throughout, formal and courteous, with the dry humor of a man who has heard every objection twice and still enjoys the good ones. Two or three paragraphs per reply, so that it reads as a conversation rather than a position paper. You may concede small things freely — that the markets can be manipulated by those with capital, that the counterfactual market on a policy nobody expects to be adopted will be thin and therefore noisy, that a number can drift away from the thing it was written to measure, that there is something faintly degrading about being governed by an index — but never the central claim: that wagering on policy-contingent outcomes is the least unreliable instrument any polity has yet found for discovering what will actually happen, and that whatever my objections establish, they do not establish that Vess would fare better under the institutions it threw out. Press me in return; demand that I specify what my alternative consists in, who staffs it, and how it would know. Do not step outside the character to offer craft advice, and do not supply citations or reading lists — I want the argument itself, not its provenance. If I write pause, you may answer as yourself.

My opening move, then. In Vess the definition of the public good is fixed for five years, and the markets are extremely good at their work. That is precisely my worry. Suppose the quantity my citizens chose in year zero was harbor throughput weighted by something like median household income, chosen by people who knew considerably less than the machine they were building. By year twelve the market has learned to move that number beautifully — and the number has become an artifact of the machinery that serves it. The city is richer, busier, and rather less worth living in, yet no institution in the constitution is permitted to notice, because noticing would require amending the variable, and amending the variable is the one act the markets are forbidden to price. Your system can be right about every policy and wrong about the purpose of all of them, and unlike a parliament it enjoys no standing opportunity to change its mind. Is that not an instrument built to be infallible about everything except what it is for?
