I spent thirty-one years running a water quality lab for the state — bioassays, dose-response curves, chasing contaminants down to parts per billion — and I retired two years ago and immediatly fell down the philosophy rabbit hole, mostly ethics, through the public library's lecture series. In three weeks I'm giving a talk to a monthly reading group that meets on the university campus and is open to the public, and I want to argue against the inefficacy objection to individual action on climate and on meat. My argument is basically the one I'd have made as a chemist, and I dont think I've seen it laid out this way in the philosophy books I've read, so please treat it as mine rather than telling me what Parfit or Kagan already said.

Here's the argument. Harm in the physical world comes on a curve, not a step. In three decades of assays I never once saw a genuine dose-response relation with a true flat region and then a jump; what look like plateaus are plateaus relative to the noise floor of the instrument. Below the limit of quantitation the slope isn't zero, you just can't resolve it. So when someone says one flight or one hamburger makes no difference, they're smuggling in a discontinuity that my whole professional life says isn't in the world. The imperceptibility of an effect is a fact about our instruments, not a fact about the effect. Second half, and I'll say this out loud to the room: I can't show the marginal harm is measurable, so I'll have to lean on expected value — a real but unresolvably small effect still counts. And I'll concede that voting doesn't fit my picture at all, since a vote isn't a dose, it's a discrete thing that shifts a probability, and I don't have a good answer for why that case should be handled differently. For what it's worth I still fly to see my grandchildren four times a year, so I'm not arguing from a clean conscience.

The room is about fifteen people. Two graduate students in climate ethics who are dead-set on "individual acts are a distraction, it's institutions or nothing," and who will have read Kagan and the replies to him. One Kant scholar who thinks the whole expected-harm calculation is beside the point, because wrongness attaches to maxims and not to consequences. One fellow who's an economist by training and will want to know whether my expected-value move commits me to caring about infinitesimal probabilities, and whether my continuity argument is doing any work at all beyond that move. A couple of local activists who'll cheer for the conclusion without following the argument, and possibly one physicist who'll say something about the world being quantized. I get 25 minutes plus about 20 of questions, so my answers have to run thirty seconds, not three minutes.

Give me the prep sheet: the six or seven objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that particular person would put it (the Kantian doesn't sound like the economist), with a thirty-second reply and one honest line on where the reply is soft. Tell me which of them I should concede in the first five minutes so they don't keep coming back, and end with the objection I'm hoping nobody raises — I have a pretty good guess what it is, since my own continuity logic seems like it should prove that abstaining is every bit as futile as emitting, but I want to see it stated cold. Keep the whole thing under 700 words; if you have to choose, give me fewer objections with better replies.
