In a just-war seminar my instructor settled an argument with the domestic analogy. Aggression between states is armed robbery writ large; a defensive war is the victim's self-defense; and the citizen who refuses to fight is the bystander on the street who watches a mugging and says he will not get involved. Since that bystander's refusal is shameful rather than merely free, he concluded, pacifism is complicity. He granted that his chosen case is awkward: an outside power arming one side of a civil war, where who counts as the victim is exactly what is in dispute.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a defense or refutation of pacifism. First, the correspondence table — what plays the individual, the robber, the victim's body and property, the bystander, the police and the courts — flagging any slot that has no occupant at all in the international case. Second, sort the disanalogies into those that bear on the complicity charge and those that are only local color. Third, say what the source quietly lends the target: a single body to defend, a harm that ends when the robber is stopped, a proportionality calculation the bystander can actually perform, a legal order waiting behind the scene, an observer who stands safely outside it. Then: does the charge survive once those are on the table? If it does not, what analogy would carry an anti-pacifist conclusion better, and what precisely would that one license — a duty to fight, or only a permission that the pacifist may decline?

Close with a line in exactly this form: Verdict: [survives | survives only as a permission, not a duty | fails] followed by one clause giving the reason.
