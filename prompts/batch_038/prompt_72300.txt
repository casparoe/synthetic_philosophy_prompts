Here's the question as it appeared, worth 20 points:

> Two positions on what counts as adequate assurance for a deployed AI system. (A): it is enough to show that the system's expected harm under the deployment distribution is acceptably low, as estimated from held-out evaluation data. (B): only a guarantee that holds regardless of the input distribution — a worst-case bound — should count. Explain the difference in the kind of support each offers and what each has to assume. Then say explicitly whether a PAC-style generalization bound belongs on the (A) side or the (B) side and why. Finally, evaluate this thesis: whenever a worst-case guarantee is attainable, it is strictly preferable to (A), so the disagreement between the two camps is about budget rather than about what assurance is.

And here's the student's answer, unedited:

> (A) is a statistical claim and (B) isn't. Under (A) you run the system on evaluation data and average the harm; the conclusion is only as good as the assumption that this data is representative of what the system will meet in deployment, and that assumption is precisely what fails under distribution shift. Averaging also hides the tail: expected harm can be low while some small slice of inputs produces catastrophic harm, and the tail is arguably the case safety is actually about. (B) doesn't need any of that. A worst-case bound makes a claim about every input in the specified set, so there's no representativeness assumption to attack and no averaging to hide anything in. This is what formal verification is supposed to deliver: you prove a property over a whole region of input space instead of sampling from it, and it's also the only kind of guarantee that survives an adversary, since an adversary by definition picks from the tail.
>
> On the PAC bound: it belongs on the (B) side. PAC guarantees are distribution-free — they hold for any distribution D with no assumption about what D is — so unlike a plain empirical estimate they aren't buying their conclusion from representativeness. There's a probability in the statement, but it's the probability of drawing a bad training sample, not a probability over inputs, which is a different sort of caveat. And because the claim is quantified over all D, it is a worst-case claim, just worst-case about the distribution instead of about the input. That's the sense of worst-case that matters when your worry is shift.
>
> On the thesis: a worst-case bound entails the corresponding average-case one, since if harm is at most c on every input then expected harm is at most c under any distribution at all. So if (B) is attainable you get (A) for free, and (B) is strictly preferable. Which means the two camps aren't really disagreeing about the nature of assurance; they're disagreeing about how much you can spend. The genuine cost is conservatism: verify against a large perturbation set and you may certify something that is trivially robust and useless, though you can shrink the set or gather more data to tighten the bound. Of course you prove the property of the model rather than of the physical system, and that gap is a real limitation, but it applies to any modelling exercise. Also worth saying: neither (A) nor (B) is about whether the objective is right. A system can be perfectly robust to input perturbation and still be optimizing something we didn't want, so both positions are downstream of the specification problem.
