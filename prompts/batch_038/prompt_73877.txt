I am drafting the framework chapter of a dissertation on individual rights and social choice theory, and before I circulate it to my committee I would like a consistency audit of the position I have written down. My concern is that I have assembled these commitments from different parts of the literature at different times and never actually checked them against one another. I am not asking for a survey of the debate, a summary of the standard responses, or a recommendation about which orthodoxy to adopt; I want to know whether this particular package hangs together, and if it does not, precisely where it comes apart.

Here is the package.

(1) The correct way to represent a right inside a social choice framework is as a protected sphere of decisiveness over outcomes: for each person there is at least one pair of personal alternatives such that, whenever that person strictly prefers one member of the pair to the other, the social ranking must rank it the same way. I take rights on this account to be properties of the social ordering, not limits on the strategies individuals are permitted to pursue. This is the Sen-style formalization of minimal liberalism, and I regard it as getting the concept right rather than as one option among several.

(2) I accept the Pareto condition in full generality. The familiar move of restricting it to preferences that concern only the preferrer's own situation strikes me as circular: to sort preferences into self-regarding and meddlesome, one already needs an account of whose business which matters are, and supplying that account is exactly the moral work the appeal to rights was meant to do. So Pareto stays unrestricted, and the impossibility has to be answered somewhere else.

(3) My preferred answer is Gibbard's reformulation. Sen's condition is not genuinely libertarian, because it makes a right a matter of how outcomes get ranked rather than of what an individual is unconditionally able to bring about; if a right is instead the person's guaranteed capacity to secure some feature of the outcome no matter what others choose, then there is no logical conflict with Pareto and the paradox dissolves. I take Gibbard's own illustration to confirm this: two neighbors, each free to paint their own house, each harboring a spiteful preference for the other's living in an ugly color, end up in a Pareto-inferior outcome. That is a failure of the agents, not a contradiction in the idea of a right. So I hold that Sen mis-stated the libertarian claim, and that once it is stated correctly the tension disappears.

(4) I do not think the Lady Chatterley's Lover case is an embarrassment for the rights-based view. It is a case in which two people's protected spheres happen to interact badly, with the result that the social ranking places an outcome nobody prefers at the top. That the outcome is unattractive, or that everyone would rather have a different one, does not show that either person's protected pair was wrongly specified in the first place.

(5) Two verdicts of my own on cases I constructed. (a) Three flatmates: A alone controls whether her bedroom window is open overnight; the cold air reaches the shared hallway and disturbs B and C, who unanimously prefer it shut, while A prefers it open. I hold that the social ranking must still place window-open above window-shut, since A's decisiveness over her own pair cannot be a function of how many people dislike the result or how strongly. (b) Same arrangement, except that what A does in her room is smoking, the smoke is carried by the shared ventilation into B's and C's rooms, and both have severe asthma. A prefers to smoke; B and C unanimously prefer that she not. Here I hold that the unanimous preference of B and C should prevail and that A's right is not decisive. I do not regard this as my converting a difference of degree into a difference of kind by fiat: air that damages other people's breathing is a different sort of thing from a draft in a hallway.

(6) I also think Sen's own escape route deserves better treatment than it usually gets. Restricting the domain of preference profiles the theorem must handle, for instance by setting aside profiles in which people have preferences about how others exercise their protected choices, is not obviously the ad hoc maneuver it is taken to be, and I do not want to reject it out of hand.

(7) At the meta level: the decisiveness condition in (1) is a technical device for representing rights in a formal model, not itself a moral claim about what rights are, and nothing in my position depends on the formalism carrying moral weight. At the same time, I hold that the impossibility result is normatively serious, telling us something real about the limits of institutional design rather than merely about a modeling choice.

Please treat these as a set rather than commenting on them one at a time, and do the following.

- Identify every pair or larger group of commitments that cannot all be held together, and rank the conflicts by severity rather than listing them as equally damaging.
- Distinguish genuine conflicts from merely apparent ones. Where two commitments only look inconsistent, say what distinction dissolves the appearance and whether that distinction is one I am entitled to given the rest of the package.
- For each of my two verdicts in (5), say whether it is actually reachable from (1) through (3) as stated. If a verdict is not reachable, identify the missing premise, and say whether I can supply it without running afoul of the circularity objection I press in (2).
- For each genuine conflict, state which commitment has to give, what the cheapest revision would look like, and what it costs me downstream, including whether it undermines anything else in the list that I have not flagged.
- Finally, name the single revision that would restore the most consistency across the whole set, and explain what I would lose by making it.

Reason from the commitments exactly as I have stated them; I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or pointers to further literature, and you should not assume I have any particular text in front of me. Please write the audit in American English.
