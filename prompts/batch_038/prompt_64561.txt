My dad died in March of a neurological thing that came on late and moved fast. The genetic counselor sat me down afterward and said it's one of the familial kinds, that its roughly a coin flip for me, that there's a predictive test, and that there's nothing anyone can do about the result either way. I haven't tested. Since then I've been making a pile of decisions in the shadow of that coin flip, and I've noticed I keep giving myself advice that can't all be right. I don't know if any of this has technical names — I never got past one stats class — so I'll just give you the cases and my gut on each.

1. Long-term care insurance. If I test positive I'm basically uninsurable, so the window is now. The broker told me straight out that people in my situation (family history, no test yet) apply at a much higher carrier rate than the general population, obviously they do. My gut: apply anyway, max coverage. The fact that applying is a sign I probably have it doesn't count against applying at all. My genes don't care what paperwork I file.

2. Testing. Two things I can't shake. The counselor said the people who turn up alone having already read everything online are the ones who cope worst afterward. And apparently with these diseases the personality changes show up years before the motor stuff — obsessiveness, irritability, being unable to let a question sit. I have spent six weeks on forums at 2am. So my wanting the test might itself be a symptom, and being a symptom means being the kind of person who handles the news badly. My gut: still test, and soon. But I also think I'd tell a friend in my exact headspace to wait a year. I can feel that those two don't go together and I don't know which one to drop.

3. The job. Three teams in nine years, and every time, about 18 months in, the same story: the company had already decided to fold my function into something else before I arrived, my being there just happened to overlap with it. Last week an offer landed and the hiring manager was brutally honest — the last two people in this seat lasted under a year, it's a hard one, we know that going in. My gut: take it. Here's the part that bugs me though. Whichever way I go I'm confident I'm picking the seat where it falls apart. If I stay, this place folds me in next year; if I jump, the new place spits me out next year. I'm as sure of that in advance as I'm sure of anything, and jumping still feels right while staying feels like giving up.

4. The anniversary. About a week before the first one, I noticed I wanted to book a work trip that would put me in another timezone on the actual day. My gut: that want is information. It means I'm not as okay as I've been telling everyone, and I should not book it. Notice this is the exact same shape as case 1 — my wanting it is a sign of something — and there I told myself to ignore the sign completely.

5. My brother. Hasn't looked anything up, says he's definitely fine, refuses the test, cheerfully planning to be a grandfather at 70. My gut: his refusal doesn't make it one bit more likely he's a carrier. But it does make it more likely he gets blindsided later. I feel extremely stable about this one, and I can't explain why it comes out different from case 2.

So — is there a principle that gets all five, or as many as possible? Please state it precisely, clause by clause if it needs clauses, and tell me which case motivated which clause, because I suspect it's going to need a lot of them and I want to see the cost. If it can't save all five, say which verdict I have to give up and why that one rather than another. Then run it on the case I keep flip-flopping on (2) and tell me what it actually says to do, not what it says to feel. And finally give me its verdict on two or three cases of your own that I haven't thought of, ones aimed at whatever seam the principle is hiding — I'd rather get ambushed by them here than in a waiting room.
