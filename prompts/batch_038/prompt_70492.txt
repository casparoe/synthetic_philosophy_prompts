Write me a dialogue, maybe 1200–1500 words, between two philosophers who are being given an after-hours tour of an IVF lab and who get into it about the necessity of origin — the standard Kripkean line that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg.

One of them is an origin essentialist. The other holds a view I'll call origin gradualism: not that origins don't matter, but that "coming from origin O" is a graded, substitutable relation — you can swap out concrete bits of the origin story while still getting the same person up to a point, and there's no non-arbitrary place to put the sharp cutoff the essentialist needs. Both are smart and well-read; neither wins because the other got dumb at the wrong moment.

The lab shouldn't be wallpaper. I want the argument to be driven by the stuff sitting in front of them. Here's the menu — use at least three of these, and go deep rather than ticking boxes:

- a dewar of frozen gametes and embryos, which raises what the origin even is: the gametes as concrete particulars? the fertilization event? the genome? the zygote? Every candidate is exposed to a substitution case.
- an ICSI dish: the sperm is injected straight into the cytoplasm, so there's no zona binding and no acrosome reaction. Ask the essentialist whether "the union of that sperm and that egg" happened here, or something else did, and whether the child's origin is the same as it would have been if the same gametes had met the ordinary way.
- a spindle-transfer egg: intended mother's nuclear genome, donor's mitochondria. The essentialist owes a story about why mitochondria are or aren't part of the origin, and that story shouldn't quietly turn into gradualism.
- monozygotic twins: two people, one zygote. If origins individuate, this is awkward, and the essentialist has to say what the origin of each twin is.
- a speculative one (I know it isn't real yet, so flag it as such in the dialogue): the lab is piloting a protocol that makes gametes out of reprogrammed somatic cells, so they could build an egg that is nuclear-genome-identical to some particular natural egg. Two children, intrinsically indistinguishable at conception, one from the real gamete and one from the lab-built lookalike. The essentialist has to say these are different people and that at most one could be you; the gradualist gets to press on what's doing the work.

What I most want is real back-and-forth: each character answers the other's actual last move instead of resetting to their opening speech. So have the essentialist run the swapping argument — if you could have come from either pair, consider a world where both pairs produce a child; which one is you? — and have the gradualist answer that argument head-on, maybe by denying the question is well-formed, or by accepting a many-to-one counterpart relation, rather than just repeating "origins are graded." Let each one land at least one genuine hit, and let each concede something small and specific by the end — a case they give up, or a formulation they admit was too strong — but no conversions. The disagreement should still be live at the last line, just sharper then it was at the first.

Also give them distinguishable voices; they shouldn't read like the same person in two hats.

After the dialogue, add a few sentences of your own: how confident are you that each position came out the way a real advocate would put it rather than as a caricature, and which single exchange in the piece are you least sure actually holds up — the move you'd bet least on?
