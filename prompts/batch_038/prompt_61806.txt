I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on the epistemology of markets. The room will be roughly a third market-microstructure economists who work in sequential-trade and auction models, a third philosophers of economics — several of them broadly sympathetic to the Hayekian claim that price systems aggregate dispersed knowledge better than any planner could — and a handful of behavioral finance researchers who study retail order flow empirically.

The paper's thesis, stated plainly: Milgrom and Stokey's no-trade theorem and the Grossman–Stiglitz result on the impossibility of informationally efficient markets are ordinarily taught as two unrelated curiosities, but they are one structural point seen from opposite ends. Under a common prior and common knowledge of rationality, private information cannot simultaneously be the motive for trade and the product of trade; and the more completely prices already reflect what is known, the less anyone can earn by finding out more. It follows that actual markets, which sustain enormous volume and yet still produce informative prices, cannot be explained by putting more information into the model. They require a standing supply of participants who trade for reasons unconnected to what they know. The claim I expect to be attacked is the normative one: the better market participants become as inquirers, the less informative prices are, so whatever epistemic credit we assign to markets is underwritten by a calibrated quantity of non-inquiring trade — and any political philosophy that treats markets as knowledge-generating institutions inherits that dependence as a stability constraint rather than as an incidental empirical footnote.

Please prepare my question-period sheet. I would like eight to ten objections, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument rather than by how likely each is to arise, and each phrased in the idiom of the sub-audience that would actually raise it — a microstructure theorist will not put the falsifiability worry the way a philosopher of economics will. For every objection, specify: whether it has real force or only the appearance of force; what I should concede in my first sentence; a reply that fits in about ninety seconds of spoken time; and an explicit note on where that reply is soft, or where it depends on a premise I have not defended anywhere in the paper. At least one objection must come from each of the three sub-audiences, and at least one must target the inference from the formal results to the political-philosophical conclusion rather than the formal results themselves.

Then, separately, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it lands, I cannot answer without giving up the normative conclusion — together with the reason it is fatal and the evasive answer I will probably reach for instead.

Finally: identify the single objection on which the paper survives or dies, and tell me what I should say in the first fifteen minutes of the talk to make that objection harder to raise later.
