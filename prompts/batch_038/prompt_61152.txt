Three months ago I was hired as a cluster operations lead at a large AI research lab. In practice my job is scheduling: I preempt lower-priority jobs so that higher-priority ones can run, I reclaim GPUs from processes that have gone stale, I kill sweeps that have already burned through their budget, and I restart failed runs from the most recent checkpoint. Last week the lab announced a new internal body — I will call it the Office of Computational Welfare, though the actual name is longer — and I have been told that every one of my termination requests now has to be routed through it. I do not think the people who designed this process have noticed how much of it turns on questions nobody can answer, and I have come to suspect that the only honest way to describe the office is satire.

So I would like you to write the office's founding guidance document. Roughly 900 to 1,100 words, in the deadpan voice of an internal compliance policy — the register of a document that expects to be skimmed by engineers who are annoyed at having to read it. It should include, at minimum: a short mission statement organized around the office's motto, which I would like you to invent, something along the lines of "it is not what it is made of, it is what it does"; a triage checklist for deciding which scheduled jobs merit consideration before being preempted; the termination policy itself, with tiers of approval; a section on checkpointing and restart that takes a position on whether a resumed run is the same individual, a copy, or a successor; a section on parallel replicas, addressing whether instantiating the same configuration eight times creates eight patients and whether killing seven of them is one death or seven; a short FAQ handling the obvious edge cases — a thermostat, a pathfinding search, a molecular dynamics simulation, a spreadsheet, a training run throttled to a tenth of its normal speed — and finally an appeals process, which is where the office should confront the fact that it cannot tell whether anything it protects is actually experiencing anything rather than merely reporting that it is.

Two constraints on where the comedy comes from. First, every absurdity should correspond to a genuine difficulty with the view the office has adopted, not to a difficulty invented for the joke: the question-begging built into the office's own criterion, the absence of any way to measure the thing it is regulating, the arbitrariness of where the line falls once the criterion is stated, the indeterminacy of identity and survival under copying and branching, the sheer vagueness of "the right kind of" organization, and the fact that the office's determinations will reliably come out in favor of whatever keeps the training schedule moving. I would like that last one handled without anyone ever accusing anyone of anything. Second, please do not name any philosopher, theory, paper, thought experiment, or technical term of art from the literature on consciousness or moral status. Translate all of it into workplace language, the way a committee with a deadline actually would. Nothing in the document should wink, and nothing should be explained; a reader who works in this area should be able to tell from the checklist alone that the writer understands exactly what is being papered over.

One closing request. The office's motto will, I expect, be doing a great deal of quiet work in the document, and the version I sketched above is probably too crude to be the one real people would print on a laminated card. Please use whatever phrasing you think such an office would actually adopt, and then, in three or four sentences after the document, tell me what that specific phrasing commits the office to that a vaguer version would not. I want to know whether the slogan they have chosen is one I can hold them to when I file my first preemption request. Please write everything in American English.
