I'm on the board of a small nonprofit, nine of us, all volunteers, and we vote on things like whether to take a grant that comes with strings attached or whether to fire the person who does our books. A couple years ago somebody forwarded around a short summary of Condorcet's jury theorem and it sort of became our in-joke: as long as each of us is more likely right than wrong, nine voters beats one voter, and if we had a hundred of us we'd basically never get it wrong. I did the arithmetic myself for our setup and got about 90% that a majority of nine is right if each of us is 70% right, which matched the summary, so I felt pretty clever. Now we're talking about expanding the board to fifteen and one of the older members is objecting on the grounds that the theorem doesn't apply to us at all, and I want to figure out whether he's right or just being difficult.

Here's the thing I can't get straight in my head. Our votes are obviously correlated in some sense — we all work from basically the same material, the same director's report, the same two people who do the background reading, and honestly the same local news. But we vote by secret ballot and we've made a rule not to discuss anything before a vote, so in the ordinary sense of the word each of us really is deciding for ourselves. So which sense of independence does the theorem actually need, and why isn't the no-talking kind enough? I want the mechanics, not just the warning label. Like take the extreme case: suppose all nine of us read the same one page and are basically repeating it back. Is the majority then exactly as reliable as a single one of us, no better no matter how big the board gets? And is that a limiting case of the same theorem or a different result entirely? Related to that — the objector also said that if we're each less than 50% likely to be right, adding members makes us actively worse, which spooked a few people. But if we're all just echoing each other, does that doom scenario even apply, or does the echo chamber ironically protect us from it?

And there's a second knot underneath all of this that I keep failing to pull apart. People say shared information is bad for group decisions, but that seems too glib, because reading the same good report is exactly what makes each of us individually more likely to be right, and individual competence is supposed to be the good ingredient. So when does everyone drawing on the same source help and when does it hurt, and is there any sensible way to think about how much of our 70% comes from stuff we all share versus stuff each of us happens to know seperately? I'd like a real conceptual explanation of what the assumption is doing and where the guarantee comes from — a made-up example with small numbers is fine, that's actually how I think best. Please don't hand me a list of papers or books to go read, I'm not a student and I'm not going to chase down references, I just want to understand it well enough to argue with this guy on tuesday.
