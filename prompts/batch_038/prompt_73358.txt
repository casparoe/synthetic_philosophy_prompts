Situation: I clerk for my country's conscientious-objector review board. Our statute, as we've always read it, protects only people whose objection is to taking part in war of any kind, not to some particular war. Six years of rulings I sat through:

- draftee, objection to all killing in war grounded in his church's teaching: granted
- applicant who said he'd serve in a purely defensive war but not the current one, which he judged aggressive: denied
- applicant with no religious affiliation, lifelong secular pacifist, wrote college essays on war as an institution: granted
- applicant objecting to all war, but who until his enlistment notice arrived two months earlier had been posting regularly about the duty to defend one's country: denied
- combat medic willing to serve unarmed in any war at all, including an aggressive one, but refusing to carry a rifle: granted, with alternative service
- applicant who refused to take part in any operation involving strikes on cities but was fine with field operations: denied

The file on my desk now is weird. Former infantry corporal, no religious background, and he says he'd serve unarmed in any war his country fights, aggressive or defensive — he'd drive an ambulance, he'd even train recruits. His objection is entirely epistemic. He accepts that combatants on the just side may permissibly kill and that combatants on the unjust side make themselves liable to be killed; he just says nobody in his position can reliably tell which side of that line a given war puts him on, before or during, so he won't take part in any of them. He explicitly rejects pacifism, says a war of rescue could be obligatory, says he'd cheer for the just side. Our chair wants to deny because the man plainly doesn't object to war as such — he's fine with war, he just doesn't trust his own judgment. Another member wants to grant because in practice this corporal will never fight, which is what the statute is about.
