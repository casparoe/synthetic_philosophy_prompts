I work as a backend engineer on infrastructure for a payments company, and I have no formal training in philosophy — most of what I know about ethics comes from long comment threads and from arguing with colleagues. Last week our team got into a side discussion, sparked by a news item about a man detained in connection with a threatened attack, in which someone declared flatly that the debate was over because 'torture doesn't work,' and everyone else nodded along. I tried to push back, rather clumsily, by saying that this seemed to concede the other side's framing: if the slogan is only about effectiveness, then it says nothing at all about whether torture would be permissible in the single case where it did work. My colleague answered that a technique which never works is never permissible to use, so the question is closed either way. I could not find the words for why that inference felt slippery, and I suspect the real problem is that those four words are being made to carry several entirely different claims at once.

What I want is a disambiguation of the slogan treated as a piece of language rather than as a position to be defended or attacked. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims it could be making — at minimum covering whether coerced interrogation reliably yields accurate and actionable information about a specific imminent threat; whether it yields such information as well as non-coercive, rapport-based interviewing does, which is a comparison rather than a standalone claim; whether the rarity and unpredictability of real ticking-bomb situations makes the practical question moot even if the technique sometimes works; whether it works as counterterrorism strategy at the level of deterrence, recruitment, allied intelligence sharing, and the treatment of one's own captured personnel; and whatever moral or institutional claim the words are doing when someone uses them to terminate an argument. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical claim, a contested empirical claim, or simply false, and say what kind of evidence would bear on it. Then identify which reading or readings my colleague was invoking, and state explicitly which of them would license his move from 'never works' to 'never permissible.' My hunch is that the readings that are actually defensible are not the ones doing the moral work, and I would rather have that hunch tested than soothed.

To give you something concrete beyond the abstract debate: I sketched a small simulation in which a detainee has a private type, cooperative or not, and the probability that any given answer is truthful declines monotonically as the pressure applied increases, while the interrogator cannot observe the type and updates only on the answers. In my runs, high-pressure sequences converge quickly toward a state in which the detainee says whatever he guesses the interrogator wants to hear, so the answers carry almost no information about the type and the interrogator's posterior barely moves. I mention this only because I want to know whether a result of that shape is the right kind of evidence for the slogan at all, or whether it speaks to one reading on the menu and is irrelevant to the rest. Please write in American English, and end your response with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by the number of the one reading on which the slogan is both defensible and sufficient to establish that torture would be impermissible even in the ticking-bomb case, or the word none if no reading satisfies both conditions.
