I'm on a working group drafting the head-of-state chapter of a constitution for a small place that's just voted to go independent but keep the Crown as a ceremonial head of state, and I've realised I don't actually have a principle for when that person is allowed to act against ministerial advice. My gut gives me verdicts though, so I want the principle read off them.

1. The Belgian king who couldn't sign the abortion law in conscience and had his own government declare him temporarily unable to reign for a day so they could pass it without him, with parliament reinstating him the next day: wrong. Wrong in the cleverest way — he got the moral credit for refusing while arranging not to refuse. Except I also think the day-long incapacity was better than a straight veto, and I can't say why.

2. The Luxembourg grand duke who flatly refused assent to the euthanasia law and got his assent power abolished as a result: not wrong, maybe even right. He said what he actually thought, in public, the country disagreed, the country changed the rule. But that's case 1 minus the trick, and I've condemned one and approved the other.

3. The Queen proroguing Parliament on her PM's advice, with the top court later ruling the whole thing unlawful: she should have done exactly what she did. Refusing would have been much worse.

4. But a PM asking to prorogue parliament for two years in the middle of a war to dodge a vote on that war — the head of state has to say no. Same structure as 3, different number, opposite verdict.

5. The governor-general who sacked a PM who still commanded a majority in the lower house but couldn't get supply through, and installed the opposition leader on condition he pass supply and go to the country: the power existed, the use of it was wrong, and the worst part was doing it without warning the PM.

6. The 1926 Canadian one, where the governor general refused a dissolution request from a minority PM who might still have had the confidence of the house: refusing was right. So now I've approved a refusal and condemned a dismissal and approved a different refusal, and I don't know what's doing the sorting.

7. A bill abolishing the monarchy passes cleanly through both houses: sign it, immediately, no drama. But a bill that keeps the monarchy, strips its last discretionary powers, and also abolishes judicial independence: I want hesitation there, which is embarassing because that bill is better for the palace than the first one was.

8. That UK parliamentary convention, King's Consent, where bills touching the prerogative or the monarch's own property and estates need the sovereign's consent before Parliament is even allowed to debate them, and which the palace is reported to have used to get draft bills changed in advance. I think this is worse than all of the above and it isn't even a reserve power — it's a veto applied in secret before the democratic process gets going. My condemnation here runs much hotter than it does for the open refusals in 1 and 2.

9. The Japanese emperor, barred from saying anything political, going on television to talk about his age and his health, and the Diet duly writing a one-off law to let him abdicate: fine, entirely fine, everyone knew what he meant. But if a head of state used a speech to hint that a bill pending in the legislature was immoral, I'd call that out of line. Same move — indirect signalling — opposite verdict.

My own guess at the principle is roughly: the head of state may act against advice only to keep the machinery of democratic accountability turning, never to change what the laws say. It's tidy, and it kills case 1 nicely, but it also kills my grand duke and my emperor, and I don't want to lose them.

So: state the principle that best fits all this, one sentence, up front, before anything else. Then the clauses it needs, each with the case that motivates it and what the clause is doing. Then tell me plainly which of 1 through 9 it can't save and would have me give up, and whether 2 and 9 can be rescued by a clause that doesn't also rescue 8. Then rule on three I've left out: (a) a hung parliament where the leader of the biggest party and the leader who has actually assembled a working majority both ask to be appointed; (b) a government that just lost a formal confidence vote asking for a dissolution instead of resigning; (c) a bill that passed with a clear procedural defect, say the house wasn't quorate — can the head of state refuse on process grounds if the rule is supposedly no discretion about content? And say whether the Norwegian setup, where the king's veto is only suspensive and the bill becomes law anyway if enough successive parliaments pass it unchanged, is a better template for our draft than the British never-refuse convention.

Hard cap of 800 words. No reading list, no footnotes — I cant check anything, so give me the reasoning rather than the sources.
