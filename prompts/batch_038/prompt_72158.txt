ok so i'm in a con law seminar where roughly every third week somebody rediscovers that a constitution is a self-binding device and the entire room acts like nobody's ever noticed before. i want to write something that pokes at that without being cheap about it — not just "boo, dead hand of the past." i want the piece to actually make the precommitment case and then follow it off a cliff. i'm going to read it out loud at our end-of-term thing.

can you write me a parody of a recorded subdivision declaration — covenents, conditions and restrictions — for a planned community? somewhere around 750 words, laid out the way the real ones are: a recording header block up top, a preamble built out of WHEREAS clauses, numbered articles with section numbers, then the declarant's signature block with a notary acknowledgment underneath.

the setup is that the developer has drafted the whole community as a Ulysses contract aimed at people who haven't bought there yet. the operative text should never say Ulysses or Odysseus or sirens or anything like that — no winking, no authorial asides, it stays in deadpan legalese start to finish and the analogy has to be carried entirely by the covenant logic. somebody who knows the entrenchment literature should be able to map it article by article onto the familiar moves: an amendment threshold that ratchets upward every time it gets used; a section on amending the amendment clause; votes counted against the original number of platted lots so the unbuilt ones still count; a variance process that exists on paper but is decided by a board whose members the declarant appointed in perpituity; a provision where current members acknowledge in advance that their future preferences will diverge from their present ones and agree to discount them accordingly; an enforcement article where architectural review is the last word and is expressly not reviewable by the membership; a sunset clause that renews itself unless somebody terminates it; and assessments that fund nothing except the machinery of enforcement. also work in one alternative strategy — a mandatory orientation designed so residents won't want the thing in the first place, the wax-in-the-ears approach rather than the rope-and-the-mast approach.

the hard part is that i don't want a strawman. the preamble has to argue FOR binding the future residents about as well as a good constitutional theorist could: nobody can be trusted at two in the morning, the point of the community is precisely the stuff they'll want to protect from themselves, and the declarant should be candid that it trusts its own successors no more than it trusts itself. the humor should come from the argument being basically right and then getting followed all the way down, so that by the last signature block a reader is laughing and also a little sick about having nodded along with the recitals. don't state any of the objections anywhere. don't editorialize. let the fine print do all the work.

keep the legal furniture generic — no invented case names or statute cites, just the standard declarant/member/assessment vocabulary.
