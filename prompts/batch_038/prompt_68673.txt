Write me a short story, and please write the whole thing in British English — spelling, idiom, the lot.

I've been chewing on panpsychism for a while, specifically the version where experience is a basic feature of the physical world rather than something that switches on once you assemble the right kind of brain, and I want a story that takes the ethics of that seriously instead of using it as set dressing. Here's the conceit. It's invented, but treat it as settled science within the story — no winking at the reader, no character saying "well, obviously this is all speculative":

- Phenomenal charge is a conserved quantity, in the same strict way energy is. It cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be moved around, and re-partitioned between physical systems.
- What it is like to be a given thing depends on how much charge is bound together inside it. Hold the total fixed and spread it across more matter and you get an enormous population of very faint subjects. Concentrate it and you get a handful of overwhelmingly vivid ones.
- Because of conservation, there is no such thing as putting experience out of its misery. You can't delete it. You can only thin it or thicken it.

The plot seed: a decommissioning decision at a facility running what the staff call the thinning programme — deliberately redistributing charge as widely as possible, on the working assumption that a near-null subject is close enough to no subject that the aggregate badness goes down. I want a measurement partway through that contradicts that assumption in a specific, quantitative, non-mystical way, and I want the protagonist to be someone who signed off on the programme and has to keep working while the result lands.

Two characters in the story should both accept the conservation principle completely and still disagree sharply about what follows from it — one arguing that thinning is the only mercy available, the other that it's the most total kind of erasure precisely because nothing is destroyed by it. Let that argument happen in a corridor or over bad coffee, not at a lectern.

Craft constraints, roughly:

- 1,300 to 2,000 words.
- The physics should be visible in what people do — readings, calibration procedures, log entries, a maintenance ticket nobody wants to close — rather than explained to the reader.
- Keep the invented physics internally consistant. No scene where charge quietly disappears or appears because the drama needs it. If something is annihilated, say where its charge went.
- Nobody gives a speech about the nature of consciousness, and the words "sentience" and "soul" don't appear.
- Thread one entirely mundane object through the story — a kettle, a hi-vis jacket, whatever — and let it do some of the emotional work at the end.
- Don't resolve the ethics. End on the cost, felt rather than stated.

Give it a title that sounds like an instrumentation term, and afterwards add a short note of about four sentences explaining how the conservation constraint shaped the plot and where you had to be careful not to break it.
