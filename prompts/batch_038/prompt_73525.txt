I'm putting together some hard exercises for my graduate epistemology seminar and I want one on the dogmatism paradox — you know the reasoning: I know that P; if P is true then any evidence against P is misleading; so I know that whatever evidence turns up against P will be misleading; so I'm entitled to refuse to look at it, or to discount it in advance. Everyone in the room says the conclusion is absurd, and my suspicion is that they say it much too fast, because nobody has ever shown them a case where the dogmatist comes out right.

So build me that case. I want a realistic scenario — a hospital lab, an engineering firm, a newsroom, a food-safety inspectorate, a courtroom, whatever, just not a trolley and not a brain in a vat — in which an agent knows some ordinary empirical proposition P, counter-evidence against P duly arrives, and her refusal to be moved by it is not merely excusable but correct, and correct for the reason the dogmatist gives rather then for some other reason lurking in the story.

The constraints are the whole point, so please hold to them:

- The scenario itself is one paragraph. Whole answer under about 900 words, discussion included.
- P must be known by an unexotic route: perception, memory, a test she ran herself, testimony from a reliable witness. No clairvoyance, no a priori knowledge, no infallible records.
- The counter-evidence has to be genuinely misleading — holding on to P must turn out right. But she may not know in advance which piece of evidence is bogus or why. If she knows the specific report is a fake, there's no paradox left, just ordinary knowledge, and I'll bounce it.
- No pragmatic escape hatches. Looking at the evidence must be free, quick, non-destructive and non-self-undermining: the observation doesn't disturb what's observed, nobody is harmed because she spent five minutes checking, no institutional pressure, no cost of inquiry, no blame either way. The reason for dismissing E has to be purely epistemic or the case is cheating.
- No devices. No demons, no perfect predictors, no neurosurgeons, no time travel.

Then show me the machinery actually working:

1. State P, the route by which she knows it, and the counter-evidence E; spell out the dogmatist's inference step by step, so it's visible that E is being dismissed by that inference and not by something else you slipped into the setup.
2. Run the standard escapes and say why each loses its grip here. The four I have in mind: (a) bite the bullet, knowledge just is dogmatic, so there's no paradox — fine, but then you owe me an account of why the bullet is biteable in your case and not generally; (b) rationality answers to your evidence, not to the truth of your evidence, so knowing that E is misleading doesn't license ignoring E; (c) misleading evidence versus defeating evidence — E can be false and still be a defeater, since what matters is what E tells her about the reliability of her own earlier competence; (d) the closure step is the weak link, because knowing P and knowing that P entails that E is misleading doesn't put her in a position to know the second-order claim, or at any rate doesn't entitle her to act on it before she's seen E. (b) and (c) are the live ones. Break them, don't wave at them.
3. If it helps, drop in a short exchange with a sensible colleague who tells her to read the report anyway, and show why that advice fails on these particular facts.
4. Finish honestly: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what it collapses into if I delete that feature, and how the smartest defender of (b) or (c) would attack what you've built — including whether this is really a counterexample or a disguised case of an agent who does know the evidence is bogus. If you think the specs can't all be met at once, say so and tell me which one you had to bend; a confessed failure is worth more to me here than a fudge.

No bibliography, and don't cite anyone — I want the reasoning, not the references. If you need a name for a position, coin one.
