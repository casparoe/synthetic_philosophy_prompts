I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of action — fourteen students, one eighty-minute session — and after two weeks on weakness of will I want to give up the third session to an exercise rather than to discussion of the readings. The aim is specific. I want each student to run into their own dynamic inconsistency before anyone utters the phrase "hyperbolic discounting," and I want the exercise to press on the philosophical question of whether time-inconsistent choice is a case of acting against one's better judgment at all.

What I need from you:

1. A payoff structure using stakes my students actually care about and that I can honestly honor. I can offer modest extra credit — call it up to two points on a hundred-point final grade — or snacks, but no cash, and every choice must be resolved during the session or within a few days of it.

2. The exercise must produce within-person preference reversal, not merely between-person variation in patience. Specify how the same student is put to the structurally identical choice at two different moments, how the option sets are arranged so that reversal is the predicted outcome rather than a lucky accident, and what counts, in the room, as evidence that a reversal has occurred.

3. Here is the confound I cannot get past on my own: a student who changes her answer between the two moments may have revised a belief — about how long a wait actually feels, about whether I will follow through, about what the reward is worth to her — rather than discounted differently. Build in whatever controls, filler elicitations, or ordering procedures you think bear on this, and tell me which residual confounds a single session simply cannot remove.

4. A precommitment stage, in which some students can bind their later selves and others cannot. The wrinkle I want, and have not seen anyone attempt: the binding should be administered by another student rather than by me, so that enforcement of an earlier decision rests in a second person's hands. Explain what this does to the interpretation of the results, since it is not obvious to me that the enforced student and the unenforced one are being tested on the same capacity.

5. A minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, the exact wording of the choice problems as I should read them aloud, the scoring and payout rules, and separate instructions for the student holding someone else's commitment.

6. A debrief that maps moments of the exercise onto the formal structure — stationarity, the difference between exponential and hyperbolic discounting, the naive/sophisticated distinction — and onto the dispute between treating the time-inconsistent agent as one agent whose evaluative judgments shift and treating her as a succession of agents in which the later self is at the mercy of the earlier one. I want the debrief to close by confronting the requirement that the incontinent agent acts against an all-things-considered judgment she still holds at the moment of acting, and to ask the class whether what they just did supports or undermines that requirement.

7. Candor about what the exercise cannot reproduce. The delays are minutes rather than months, the stakes are trivial, and the phenomenology of temptation in an ordinary akratic episode bears little resemblance to choosing between two envelopes. Tell me which limitation is fatal to which conclusion, and what I will have to supply in speech to compensate.

8. What predictably goes wrong the first time this is run, and what I do about it — including one student who will not play sincerely and one who will try to arbitrage the payout rules.
