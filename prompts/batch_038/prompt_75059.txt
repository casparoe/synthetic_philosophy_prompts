The paper allowed three hours and four questions, of which candidates answered one. The question this candidate took was:

> "Loschmidt's reversibility objection to Boltzmann's H-theorem is sometimes said to have been answered twice over: once by Boltzmann's retreat from certainty to probability, and once again by the postulation of a special low-entropy initial condition for the universe as a whole. Set out the objection. Explain what each of these two replies achieves, and whether either, alone or in combination, disposes of it. You should conclude by saying what you take the second law of thermodynamics to be — a law of nature, a theorem of statistical mechanics, or something else again — and should defend that characterisation."

The script is reproduced in full, exactly as submitted:

> Loschmidt's objection is that the H-theorem purports to derive a time-asymmetric conclusion from time-symmetric premises. The microdynamics of a dilute gas — Hamilton's equations with a time-reversal-invariant interaction — are unchanged by the transformation t → −t, p → −p. If a microstate beginning in a low-entropy macrostate reaches equilibrium after an interval T, then the state obtained by reversing every momentum at that moment retraces the history and returns the gas to its initial low-entropy macrostate at 2T. For every entropy-increasing trajectory there is therefore an entropy-decreasing one, and monotonic increase cannot be a theorem of the dynamics. The objection is sound as far as it goes, and Boltzmann's first statement of the theorem did overreach, because the derivation assumes molecular chaos before each collision, an assumption which the collisions themselves destroy.
>
> Boltzmann's substantive reply is statistical rather than deductive. The second law does not assert that entropy cannot fall; it asserts that a fall is overwhelmingly improbable. On the Boltzmann conception, the entropy of a macrostate measures the phase-space volume it occupies, and the equilibrium macrostate exceeds any non-equilibrium macrostate in volume by an astronomical factor — of the order of exp(10²³) for a mole of gas confined to half its container. Given that the dynamics preserve volume, a system placed at random within a low-entropy macrostate will thereafter be found in successively larger macrostates with probability indistinguishable from one. This is enough to dispose of Loschmidt: the objection shows only that decrease is possible, and the second law, properly stated, never denied that it was. Poincaré recurrence is sometimes pressed into service on the same side, but the recurrence time for a macroscopic system outruns any interval over which the question could meaningfully be asked, and a recurrence theorem does not in any case contradict a statistical regularity.
>
> Cosmology adds a refinement. The statistical argument requires that the system be in a low-entropy macrostate at some time, and it is the business of the dynamics to say what happens from there, not how it came to be there. The Past Hypothesis is the proposal that the universe began in a very special, very low-entropy macrostate; together with the microdynamics and a statistical postulate over the microstates within that macrostate, it yields entropy increase away from the boundary. Two difficulties are commonly noted. It is not easy to specify which macrostate the hypothesis selects with the precision that a law would seem to require. And the rival account — that our region is a rare fluctuation within an ambient equilibrium — fares worse, because it predicts that we should overwhelmingly be momentary observers with spurious memories rather than observers situated in a long, coherent, entropy-increasing history.
>
> What, then, is the second law? Two characterisations are available. It may be treated as a theorem: given the microdynamics, the Past Hypothesis and the statistical postulate, entropy increase follows; and a proposition which follows from premises does not seem to be a law of nature in the same sense as those premises are. Or it may be treated as an artefact of coarse-graining. Liouville's theorem guarantees that the fine-grained Gibbs entropy of a distribution is invariant under Hamiltonian evolution, so nothing in the microscopic description ever increases; increase appears only when the distribution is smeared across macrostates, and the smearing is something we perform. Entropy, on this view, is a property of a description rather than of a system. I take this second characterisation to be the right one. The first leaves the second law with no content of its own, whereas the second explains why the law has proved so resistant to derivation. It has a consequence worth stating plainly. If entropy increase depends upon a choice of description which is ours to make, then the second law is not a fact about the world but a fact about our representation of it, and the direction of time, in so far as it rests on the second law, is perspectival: nothing in nature distinguishes the two temporal directions, and the asymmetry we experience is imported by the coarse-graining.
