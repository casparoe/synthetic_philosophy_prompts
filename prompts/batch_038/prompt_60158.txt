ok so I just spent three hours arguing with my friend about why promises bind and my brain is completely fried, and I want an opinion that isnt just going to tell me I'm right.

My view, roughly: I don't think promissory obligation reduces to a duty not to disappoint expectations or to wreck someone's reliance-based plans. Same complaint I have about the Hume-flavored conventionalist story where promising is a handy human device and the "obligation" is really just rule-following with the promisee sitting there as the beneficiary. What I think happens is that promising is the exercise of a normative power — by giving an assurance that gets taken up, I put the other person in a position where they hold a standing, second-personal claim to demand performance and to resent me if I don't come through. The wrong I do by breaking is owed *to them*, not just bad in some impersonal way. And it's self-assumed: I create it, it isn't a background duty of fidelity that merely gets switched on by my utterance.

The part that's more distinctively mine — my friend thinks it's either crazy or trivially true, take your pick — is that I don't think the obligation is binary. I think its *strength* is graded by uptake: by how determinately the promisee has actually folded the assurance into their planning and how much of their future is now organized around it. A promise that was received but that they havent staked anything on yet creates a real but weak claim. The same promise six weeks on, after they've booked the flights and told their boss, creates a strong one. So the directed duty exists from the moment of uptake, but its weight tracks how much they've built on top of it. I think this is what explains why breaking a promise to a stranger you'll never see again feels less bad than breaking one to your sister, without me having to deny the stranger was genuinely wronged.

So: what would count against this? Not a tour of objections to every theory of promising in existence — mine specifically. I want the cases, intuitions, and arguments that should actually move me, ordered by how much they'd hurt, worst first. And be honest about which ones a committed defender of my view would just concede as damage and which ones they'd try to explain away, and which of my commitments would have to be surrendered to handle each.

The stuff I'm already braced for but want done properly rather than waved at: promises to yourself, where there's no second party to hold the claim, so where does the standing come from; promises to someone who's died; promises pulled out of me under duress or manipulation; gratuitous promises where the promisee genuinely doesn't care and would release me in a heartbeat; and cases where they've clearly waived the claim but I still feel bound — which cuts against the whole "the claim is theirs to hold" picture.

The one that actually scared me, so don't go easy on it: my friend says the graded-by-uptake thing is just the reliance/expectation account I claimed to reject, wearing a trench coat. If the strength of my obligation is set by how much they've built on the assurance, hasn't everything normatively interesting come from their side rather than from any power I exercised?
