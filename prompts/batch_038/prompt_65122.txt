I run the monthly in-service for our health system's ethics consultation service. About a dozen people in the room: mostly clinicians who do consults on top of a full-time job, two chaplains, one lawyer, and me — I'm the only actual philosopher, though I do consults too. Ninety minutes, conference room, whiteboard, no handouts and nobody reads anything beforehand. Next month's topic is the philosophical problem with moral testimony: the idea that getting a moral belief secondhand from someone more reliable than you is somehow worse than getting a belief about load-bearing walls or vancomycin dosing secondhand from the same kind of person. I know the puzzle and roughly where the fault lines are, so please dont write me a refresher. I want the plan for how I should run the explanation.

Things I specifically want sorted out:

What has to be in place in their heads before the puzzle is a puzzle at all, and in what order. My worry is that they'll immediately hear it as "are there really moral experts?", and if I let that framing sit for even ten minutes the whole session turns into a relativism argument I can't win in 90 minutes and that was never the point. Tell me what I have to establish first so that question doesn't come up, or if it's better to let it come up and burn it off early.

Which single case carries the hour. My instinct is one of our own: a family that accepts a recommendation from us and, when you ask them why it's the right thing, can only say the committee said so — plus the nurse who reads the recommendation at the bedside and is in exactly the same position. I want to know whether that case can bear the weight or whether it's too close to home in the wrong direction, i.e. they'll spend the hour defending their livelihood instead of following the argument. If it's the wrong vehicle, tell me what the case has to have and what it must not have, and give me something that still lands for people whose whole job is being deferred to.

There's a wrinkle I care about and I don't know whether it's a real one or just my day job talking. Every version of this problem I can think of is one person telling another person a moral claim. What we actually produce is a written recommendation from a group, which the family then hears at second hand from someone who wasn't in the room. Is that just testimony with extra steps, or does the group-and-document structure change what the problem is — and if it changes it, where in the sequence do I introduce that, before or after the basic puzzle has landed?

Then the misconception map, in the order they'll actually show up rather than the order they're interesting in. I'm already predicting: the defensive reading that I'm telling them their consults are worthless and families should ignore us; the "well, there's no such thing as moral expertise" version; the "so it's just an education problem, teach them to explain themselves" version; and the slide from "deferring is deficient" straight to "so be your own moral authority, that's obviously fine." Tell me which of these shows up in the first five minutes and which ones I can safely let wait until hour two, and exactly what I say at each point that heads it off without me looking like I'm dodging their objection.

Roughly how the ninety minutes divides up, what I should cut and say out loud that I'm cutting — I'd rather announce a simplification than smuggle it — and which of the two flavors of the complaint (the belief isn't properly yours versus the belief is fine but acting on it isn't creditable) I can drop without turning the whole thing into a strawman. And one question I can put to the room at the end that would tell me whether they've actually got the puzzle, as opposed to just thinking philosophers are being precious — something where a wrong answer and a right answer are both possible, because I don't want a question they can answer by parroting my framing back at me.

No bibliography and no "for further reading" — it would be guessing on your part and useless on mine.
