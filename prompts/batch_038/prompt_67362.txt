Just came off about a three hour arguement with my housemate and neither of us budged, so I figure the useful thing is the document we should have written down instead of talking over each other. Topic: whether there can be a rationale that nobody ever had. He says yes, constantly — a four-chambered heart has a rationale (keeps the oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart, makes sustained high metabolism possible), and that rationale was doing its work for hundreds of millions of years before anything articulated it, and would still be the rationale if we'd never evolved. Same story with a medieval shipwright who shapes a hull that minimizes wave-making resistance and cant say why: the hydrodynamicist who spells it out five centuries later is finding something, not inventing a description. I say the word is smuggling in a subject — "rationale" is an honorific we paste on after the fact, once some constraint structure plus selection or trial-and-error has produced something we can explain, and the explaining is always done by somebody, so reasons without reasoners is a category error wearing realism's clothes. We both conceded the term in play is something like a free-floating rationale (I think that's Dennett's, from the frame problem paper, but don't take my word for it), and we kept sliding sideways into Popper's world-3 objective-knowledge stuff, which is probably a different debate and I don't want it swallowing this one.

So: draft the joint statement the two of us could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims both camps accept — about convergent solutions across unrelated lineages and unrelated workshops, about design explanation being genuinely explanatory rather than decorative, about the ordinary subject-bound use of "reason" — and then the residual disagreements stated precisely, each one worded the way its own side would word its own view, no strawmen and no "actually you both agree" cheap shots. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling it would genuinely move at least one of us, with a case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction written down in advance so nobody can squirm later. Some candidates I want you to judge, keep, cut, or replace: (a) whether explanatory indispensability buys ontological commitment here — we keep reaching for convergence, insect wings and bird wings and aircraft wings, termite mound ventilation showing up independently in species that never met, and the question is whether the shared solution is a thing or just the same physics filtering the same variation twice; (b) whether his view generalizes too far — if rationales float free, why not intentions, or mistakes, is there such a thing as a free-floating error, and if not, why is rationale the one intentional-ish item that survives being detached from the agent; (c) authority — does an unowned rationale count as a reason for anyone, and if so whose, including for a creature that has never considered it; (d) the uninstantiated case — an optimal hull form for a fluid and a speed range that never occurs anywhere: is there a rationale there, and when I say that's just a point in a describable space with no normativity attached, do I win or am I begging the question.
