I am in the fourth year of a philosophy doctorate, and I have been invited to contribute a chapter of roughly six thousand words to an edited volume on whether contemporary language models understand what they process. The editors have asked for a defended position rather than a survey, and mine is that the underlying metaphysical question was settled long ago, and that the controversy only looks live because one side of it is still reasoning in a pre-Darwinian idiom. My spine is Dennett's notion of a strange inversion of reasoning — a phrase he lifts from one of Darwin's hostile nineteenth-century critics, whose complaint was that the theory installs absolute ignorance as the artificer, so that, in his words, "to make a perfect and beautiful machine, it is not requisite to know how to make it." Dennett's observation, in *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* and again in his 2009 essay setting the two inversions side by side, is that Turing performed a sibling inversion: "to be a perfect and beautiful computing machine, it is not requisite to know what arithmetic is." Read together, the two deliver competence without comprehension not as a paradox to be dissolved but as the ordinary route by which every non-miraculous achievement in the biosphere and in engineering is built — the bubble-up alternative to what Dennett calls the trickle-down theory of creation, on which whatever displays the competence must already have possessed the comprehension.

On my reading, that is fatal to the Chinese Room, and fatal in a way that is not merely the systems reply. Searle's crucial premise, as I reconstruct it, is that a process which shuffles symbols without grasping what they mean cannot thereby come to mean anything by them; and that premise just is the un-inverted intuition, the assumption that comprehension must be present wherever competence is on display. Once the inversions are accepted, the comprehension was never required at the level where the work is actually done, the man in the room is simply the wrong place to look, and there is no residual fact about whether the system understands over and above the pattern of discriminations, predictions, and self-corrections it reliably produces. It also follows, I think, that the dispute has gone verbal: both sides agree on every mechanistic and behavioural fact, the inversions tell us there is nothing further for understanding to consist in, and so the question whether a model really understands is a question about which vocabulary we find convenient rather than about how the world is arranged.

Since I do not want to spend my word budget re-arguing the inversions themselves, I would like you to take them as granted and work downstream from them, on three tasks. First, the chapter needs a diagnosis: an account of why the thought experiment kept striking careful readers as compelling for four decades, richer than the observation that they were crypto-dualists, and one that lets me present the surviving intuition as an atavism to be explained away rather than as a datum to be accommodated. Second, I need the cleanest formulation of my key premise, worded so that it cannot be collapsed back into the systems reply — my claim is meant to be that there is no understanding anywhere in the setup waiting to be redistributed, not that it belongs to the whole system rather than to the man. Third, I want to pre-empt the objection I was handed at a departmental colloquium, namely that the inversions carry no weight at this point in the argument because Searle never denied that a machine could behave competently, and to show why that objection already assumes what it needs to establish. Please give me the diagnosis, the premise as you would phrase it, and the reply to the colloquium objection, in that order.
