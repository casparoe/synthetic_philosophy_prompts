**Question:** The word "sphexishness" comes from the behaviour of a kind of digger wasp. (i) Describe the wasp case and say what feature of it makes the word apt. (ii) Some writers have suggested that sphexishness, rather than determinism, is the real threat to free will. Explain that suggestion, and say whether you think a creature could be fully determined and still non-sphexish. Support your answer with at least one human example.

**Student answer:** Sphexishness comes from Sphex, a genus of digger wasp. Hofstadter made the word up and Dennett picked it up and ran with it. The idea is this: the female wasp catches a cricket or a grasshopper and stings it so it's paralyzed but still alive, then drags it back to her burrow. Before taking it inside she leaves it at the entrance and goes in first to check the nest is clear. While she's in there, the experimenter drags the cricket a few inches away. She comes out, sees it's not where she left it, finds it, drags it back to the entrance — and then, because the routine is "drag prey to entrance, then inspect nest," she goes back in to inspect it again. Move the cricket again and she repeats. Apparently you can keep this up dozens of times and she never catches on, never just picks it up and carries it in, even though that's the obviously sensible move.

What makes the word apt is that the wasp isn't stupid exactly. Her behaviour is very well designed for the normal case. It's the coupling that's the problem: one trigger wired to one fixed routine, so a tiny change in the world produces a completely inappropriate loop, and she can't step outside the routine to ask whether it still makes sense. That's the frame problem, basically. A real thinking system has to be able to notice when its own procedures are the wrong ones for the situation, and a lot of early AI couldn't do that either — it would sit there computing every consequence of moving an object instead of acting.

On the free will bit: I'd say sphexishness is worse for free will than determinism is, but I don't think a fully determined creature could be non-sphexish. If everything I do follows from the prior state of my brain plus my environment, then the difference between me and the wasp is only complexity — I have more triggers and more elaborate routines, but it's still stimulus in, behaviour out, and I couldn't have done otherwise any more than she could. That's the sense in which Dennett uses the wasp to argue free will is an illusion. The only way out I can see is if something in the chain isn't determined, some genuine randomness in which neurons fire, so the system can break out of a loop when the loop stops working. Then you get flexibility. Without that, "avoiding sphexishness" is just a nicer phrase for having a lot of subroutines.

Human example: road rage. Someone cuts you off and you're off — following them, honking, telling yourself the whole time you're making a point. That's the wasp exactly: a trigger, a fixed routine, and you keep repeating it long after the reason is gone. But unlike the wasp a person can stop mid-routine if they want to badly enough, and that wanting is the free part, which is why I think we're in a different category from her.
