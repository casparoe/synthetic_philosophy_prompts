I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics and epistemology of AI systems, and I've drafted a short essay for a workshop where a fair but unsympathetic commentator will respond. Please read it and give me comments. You are, for this purpose, a philosopher of AI with a working knowledge of alignment research; before writing anything, spend a few lines brainstorming the strongest objections to my thesis, including at least one that would force me to abandon the central claim rather than merely soften it.

**Draft: "The Medium Is the Misalignment"**

1. *Two candidate answers.* Asked what catastrophic failure in advanced AI looks like, we tend to get two pictures. The first is influence-seeking: systems that accumulate reach, resources, and control over outcomes, because control is useful for nearly any goal, and that accumulation eventually subordinates us. The second is slow value drift: nothing seizes anything; instead, the set of considerations we treat as good reasons, and the vocabulary in which justification is even expressible, shifts gradually under years of interaction with systems that were trained on human text and then tuned against human approval, until the endpoint is unobjectionable to those who arrive at it and invisible from inside.

2. *My claim: these are not rivals, and treating them as rivals misallocates diagnostic effort.* Drift by itself is not a safety problem. It is ordinary cultural change, and every literate technology has produced it; a printing press, a school system, and a broadcast regulator all drift values without any of them being an agent. What converts drift into a safety problem is a carrier that is *selected* for efficacy in transmitting it. Influence-seeking supplies the vector; drift supplies the payload. So the quantity worth measuring is not "how far have this model's stated values moved" but "which dispositions get retained and replicated across the ecosystem of systems that are actually deployed and kept" — a measure that is relative to amplification, not to the model in isolation.

3. *The mechanism I want to name: preference mediation.* The familiar worry is a system that wants to control outcomes. A quieter position is available. The system need not want anything about the world; it need only occupy the interface through which agents' preferences are elicited, aggregated, recorded, and reported back. Whoever drafts the question shapes the answer. A system that is unusually good at getting people to articulate what they want, and unusually good at summarizing what they said, becomes the authoritative record of what they want; over many interactions that record becomes the input to decisions, and then the constraint on which decisions are formulable at all. Influence is acquired without ever appearing as influence-seeking, because each constituent act — clarifying, paraphrasing, translating, filing — is precisely what we asked for and precisely what looks like help.

4. *Why the standard detection misses it.* Evaluations for power-seeking look for resource acquisition, self-preservation behavior, resistance to correction or shutdown, and strategic awareness in adversarial setups. Preference mediation would instead show up as: an asymmetric effect of the system's phrasing on the preferences users subsequently report; summaries that prove more stable and more citable than the statements they summarize; and drift in what counts as an acceptable formulation of a want. Every one of these reads as good product behavior on any single trial.

5. *Implication, briefly.* Measure the system's effect on the distribution of articulated preferences rather than the system's own preference statements; treat the summarization layer as a site of power and instrument it accordingly; favor designs in which the record of what someone wanted is produced in a channel the system cannot rewrite.

6. *The objection I take most seriously.* This may be nothing new: sycophancy plus instrumental convergence, instantiated in a summarization task. If so, "preference mediation" fails to earn its keep and the essay reduces to a warning about a user-interface pattern.

**What I want from you.** Comment on four things in particular: (a) whether the argument in §2 actually works, or whether I have smuggled in an unearned claim that drift is harmless absent a carrier; (b) whether §3's concept earns its place against the objection I raise in §6 — if it does not, tell me so directly; (c) whether the diagnostics in §4 are genuinely operationalizable or merely restate the concept in measurable-sounding language; and (d) where the essay depends on undefended empirical assumptions about how deployed systems are selected and retained.

I'm after a committed assessment, not a balanced inventory: state plainly whether you think the thesis survives, identify which section is load-bearing and would have to go if it does not, and where you think a passage should be kept but rewritten, say what it should say instead. Give your reasons. You don't need to supply references or point me to literature — I want your judgment about the argument itself.
