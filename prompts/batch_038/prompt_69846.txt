I teach a seminar unit on the fluidity of categories and want to open it with Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote": Menard sets out not to copy Cervantes but to write the novel himself, and the fragments he leaves behind are verbatim identical to the original, yet the story's narrator insists that Menard's pages are the richer of the two, since the same lines mean something different when produced by a twentieth-century Frenchman than by a seventeenth-century Spaniard. I want to read that conceit against the Letter Spirit problem — the question of what makes every instance of an 'a' an 'a' when no two instances share a shape, and whether a letterform's identity is a template, an abstract structure licensing an open-ended family of realizations, or something intelligible only relative to a whole style and the rest of an alphabet. The two cases look like mirror images: Menard holds the tokens fixed and varies the spirit, whereas the letterform problem holds the letter fixed and varies the tokens. Does Borges show that the identity conditions of a work turn on history and provenance rather than on the intrinsic properties of the mark, and would that argument survive the descent to a glyph — could two strokes of ink identical in every physical respect be the same letter, or different letters, or the same letter in a different style, purely in virtue of who made them and in what tradition? Give me a genuine reading rather than a survey: where the transfer bites, where it strains, given that Menard's case may turn on semantic content and a reader's knowledge of the world that a hand-drawn stroke simply lacks, and what the story contributes to the question of whether a letterform has an essence at all that arguments alone could establish. I would rather you show that the analogy fails in an instructive way than stretch it to fit.
