I am a commercial litigator, and for the past eighteen months I have been reading Indian epistemology on my own time — currently a chapter on the pramāṇas. In the course of that reading I have become convinced that a move I make constantly in my day job is being discussed there, but I cannot tell which term covers it, or whether one term covers it at all. I would like to identify the operation precisely, and I care far more about the boundaries than about the label.

The move, described without any vocabulary from either field: I come to hold a definite new belief, and I hold it with the confidence of something known rather than conjectured, yet I cannot produce a rule that generated it. What I can produce is a pair of things I was already quite sure of which cannot both stand exactly as they stand — one of them has to be supplemented by something that neither of them mentions. The supplement is what I end up believing. Three instances, in increasing order of difficulty:

1. A counterparty's audited accounts show revenue rising steadily over three years and headcount falling by roughly a third over the same period. Both figures are reliable, and neither says anything about the other. I nonetheless find myself settled — not suspicious, settled — on the view that a material part of the revenue comes from an activity the accounts are not broken out to disclose. If cross-examined I can manufacture a generalisation ("firms that shed staff while growing revenue usually…"), but the generalisation feels like something I produced afterwards to dress the belief up, not the thing that produced it.

2. A witness whom I have independent reason to believe is alive and in the city does not answer at the only address anyone has for him; the flat is dark, the neighbours have not seen him in a week, his post is uncollected. I leave believing he is staying somewhere else in the city. Here I suspect two operations have occurred and I cannot tell whether they are one or two: the flat first told me he is not there, and then that, combined with his being alive and in town, told me he must be somewhere else. Is the second step a further move of its own kind, or a trivial consequence of the first?

3. Two provisions of the same agreement. Clause 4 gives the buyer an unqualified right to inspect the seller's premises at any time; clause 11 obliges the seller to keep certain process details confidential from every third party absent the seller's own consent. Each is unambiguous on its face, and I end up holding a belief that belongs to neither: that inspection under clause 4 cannot extend to carrying off the process documentation, or equivalently that clause 11 carries an unstated exception for the buyer. This belief is not about the world at all but about what one of the words must mean, and it arose only because the two provisions could not otherwise stand together.

My questions:

- Is there a term of art for the operation common to these cases, either in Western epistemology or in the Indian literature I am reading? For orientation: the chapter I am in lists perception, inference, comparison, and testimony as the means of knowledge accepted by its school, and remarks in passing that other schools extend the list, without saying with what. I am fairly sure my case is not among the four listed, but I cannot rule out that it is a species of inference that I am misdescribing.
- How much of my description does that term actually cover? Instance 3 looks like an interpretive manoeuvre rather than a way of acquiring knowledge about the world, and instance 2 may be two moves rather than one. I want the category's edges drawn explicitly, together with a statement of which of my three cases fall outside and why.
- The feature I care most about is the missing rule. My reading suggests the school whose list I quoted above holds that anything of this shape is really inference, with a suppressed universal doing the work. Set out the strongest form of that reduction as applied to my three instances, and then the strongest form of the objection that something is lost — what exactly is lost, and in which instance it is lost most clearly. I am not asking you to referee the dispute; I am asking for both sides at full strength.
- Is this the same thing as what people call inference to the best explanation? The two are often compared. In my experience the felt shapes differ: with the best explanation I have rival candidates and I weigh them, whereas here I have no rivals and the posit arrives as forced, with any alternative needing special pleading of its own. Is that difference philosophically real, or an artefact of easy cases — given that in the genuinely hard cases I do generate rivals after the fact?
- Finally, if you judge that no single established category covers all three instances, say so plainly and propose a term of your own, with a definition tight enough to exclude at least one of the three and to exclude ordinary inference and ordinary conjecture.

Two notes on what I am not after. Please do not assemble a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I will find the sources myself and a guess at them would be worse than nothing. And please do not settle for a name: what I want is the carving — the definition, the contrasts with the nearest neighbours, and the boundary cases, including a clear account of what this operation is not.
