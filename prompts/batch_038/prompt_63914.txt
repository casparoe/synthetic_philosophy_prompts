I have no academic training in philosophy. I read on my own, mostly late in the evening, and for the past year I have been circling a single question: whether there is something it is like to taste a cup of coffee over and above everything that could be said about how I behave and what I am disposed to report. My instinct is a firm yes. Recently I read a summary of Daniel Dennett's case against qualia — it appeared in the closing chapter of a popular book on consciousness — and I copied the passage down before returning the book. It runs as follows:

"Dennett's argument proceeds by way of a homely example. He asks you to imagine two coffee drinkers, Chase and Sanborn, who once adored the same cheap brand and who now both announce that they cannot stand it. Their explanations differ: one insists that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and that only his own palate has changed, while the other insists that his palate is the same and that it is the coffee which now tastes different to him. Dennett's point is that there is no way to settle, from the inside, which of them is right, and so their experiences must in fact be the same; the dispute is only about how each man describes them. That is the death blow. The private, ineffable, intrinsic properties philosophers call qualia turn out to do no explanatory work at all, and since no report or experiment could ever reveal them, they make no difference to anything that happens. What is left is a set of dispositions: to grimace, to reach for the sugar, to say that the coffee has gone off. Dennett is not denying that drinking coffee involves rich inner experience; he is denying that this experience comes with the special extras philosophers built into the word. The upshot is that the hard problem dissolves — once every functional and behavioral fact is on the table, nothing remains to be explained, and the conviction that something does remain is itself an illusion."

I suspect this is not quite fair to the argument, but I cannot say where it goes wrong, and I am aware that my suspicion may just be motivated reasoning on behalf of a view I want to keep. I would like an accuracy audit rather than an introduction to the debate, and not a list of references or further reading, which I could not chase down anyway. Specifically:

1. Go through the passage sentence by sentence and sort each claim into three columns: what a careful reader of the actual argument would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a misstatement.
2. Reconstruct the coffee-drinkers case properly. What are the premises, what is it meant to establish, and how many different descriptions of what changed does the argument turn on? Is the step from "there is no way to settle which man is right" to "their experiences must be the same" a step the argument actually makes, or one the popularizer inserted? And is the intended conclusion metaphysical — no such properties exist — or something more modest about our authority to describe our own experience?
3. The clause saying qualia "make no difference to anything that happens" sounds to me like a claim about causation. Is that what is being argued here, or has a different and much more familiar objection been folded into an argument that does not need it?
4. Can the paragraph hold both "what is left is dispositions" and "rich inner experience is retained"? Which of those two is Dennett's, and does the popular version slide between them at a point where the distinction matters?
5. Does the hard problem really dissolve on the strength of this example, or is that upshot imported from a larger and separate project? If the framing is anachronistic, say so plainly and tell me what the argument can carry on its own.
6. Is "illusion" the right word for the residual conviction? It worries me that if something seems to be left over, there is still a seeming — and that this may quietly restore what the argument was supposed to remove.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each of your main verdicts, and single out the one point you are least sure of — in particular anywhere you are reconstructing the argument from memory of the paper rather than from its text, since I have no way of checking that myself. And if some part of the passage survives correction, tell me what is left for someone like me to answer.
