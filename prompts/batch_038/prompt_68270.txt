I work two days a week in a conservation lab — medieval window glass mostly, plus one nightmare crizzled beaker that weeps whenever the RH climbs — and this term I'm also in an MA seminar on powers and dispositions, which has turned out to be a bad combination because now I can't stop doing the lab work philosophically. The studio just started trialing two new products from a supplier. One is a laminated panel whose interlayer stiffens and dissipates locally the instant it takes an impact, so the pane keeps every property it had but never gets to shatter. The other is a doped glass where the start of a microcrack triggers a local annealing response that erases the flaw before it runs — the property that would have made it break is gone by the time the blow lands. Sitting there, it struck me that one is a mask you can order from a catalog and the other is basically an electro-fink built into the material itself, and I wrote down an argument on the back of a condition report that I have not been able to get out of since.

Here it is. (1) For a thing to be fragile is for it to have some property P such that, if it were struck sharply, P would make it break. (2) Whether striking produces breaking depends on the whole situation and not just on P: something can soak up or shield against the blow while P stays put, something can remove P the moment the strike begins, and something can produce the breaking even though P was never there at all. (3) For any object and any candidate P, at least one of those three situations is describable — and in two cases I now handle at work, it's actual, engineered, and on the shelf. (4) So no property of the object, taken by itself, makes it the case that the object would break if struck. (5) But that is precisely what fragility was supposed to be: a property of the object that makes the counterfactual come out true. (6) So nothing is fragile in virtue of itself; fragility is a feature of object-plus-surroundings and never a feature of the object. (7) The same route works for soluble, corrosive, conductive, flammable, and — I checked, unfortunately — for brave and generous. (8) So it is literally true, however much it sounds like a paradox, that our thirteenth-century pane lying bare on the bench and a modern toughened panel inside its shock laminate are exactly as fragile as one another: neither is fragile simpliciter, both are fragile-relative-to-a-situation, and the situations are what differ. (9) Which means the extra foam we wrap the medieval pane in isn't tracking anything in the pane.

I can't live with (8) and (9) — the conservator who trained me has better hands than theory, and she pads that pane because the pane is worse, not because of how the room is arranged — and (7) also seems to wreck half of physics, since charge and everything like it would stop being a property of the electron. But here's what makes me suspicious of my own suspicion: the (7) case run on character traits is the situationist argument, and that one I find genuinely tempting, which suggests my glass argument is proving too much rather than proving something. My instinct is that (3) is doing something shady with 'describable', but I don't trust that instinct, partly because if (3) is the problem I'd expect the argument to be trivially fixable and it doesn't feel fixable. So: which step do I cut first, and why did it look completely innocent when I wrote it down? Tell me whether this is an argument with a false premise or an argument that just isn't valid, and don't settle for saying it equivocates somewhere — say what the two readings are. Then price the exits for me: Lewis-style proviso about the object retaining its basis, a ceteris paribus or normal-conditions gloss on the counterfactual, taking dispositions as irreducible and dropping the conditional analysis altogether, conceding that they're extrinsic, and the move away from single stimulus-manifestation pairs. I'd rather keep (1) and (3) than anything else in there, so tell me what each of those repairs costs me on the bench, and which one, if any, lets me say the medieval pane really is more fragile than the laminated one.
