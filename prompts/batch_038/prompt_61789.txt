I'm one of nine on our county's Names and Memorials Commission — we rule on petitions about names and monuments on county property: streets, buildings, bronze plaques, that sort of thing. Six years of decisions and I honestly can't tell whether we've been running a principle or just vibes. Here's the record as best I can reconstruct it from the minutes:

**2019 — keep, 6–1.** An overlook on Route 9 named for a county judge who in the 1930s routinely sentenced vagrancy defendants to hard labor on county roads. Legal and unremarkable then, nobody objected in print, the labor program was abolished in 1961. The 1962 naming citation credits his water-rights opinions, not his criminal docket. Overlook sits on a state highway used mostly by tourists; petition came from a statewide history society.

**2020 — keep with an added panel, 5–2.** Bronze of the county's first woman doctor, who in 1928 testified to the state legislature in favor of compulsory sterilization and got hammered for it in a labor paper that same month. The inscription credits her free clinic for mill workers. The clinic building was sold to a private dentist in 1985. Petition filed by a national disability-rights org with no local chapter.

**2021 — remove, 7–0.** The records annex named for a county clerk who destroyed property deeds belonging to Black families. They sued him and won in 1948; the county named the annex for him in 1950, citation reading "stewardship of the county's records." The records office still works in that annex and still relies on the indexes he rebuilt after the judgment. Petition from the descendants, several of whom record deeds there monthly.

**2021 — remove, 6–1.** Memorial arch on the fairgrounds honoring a sheriff whose deputized posse killed three strikers in 1912. The local paper praised him and nobody condemned him while he lived. The arch's dedication credits him with draining the fairground marsh and starting the county 4-H program — nothing about the posse. But the sheriff's office still hangs his badge in its lobby and still calls its volunteer program "the posse." Petition from the county labor council, whose members use the fairgrounds for the annual job fair; several filed statements about what the arch tells them at work. The strikers' descendants haven't lived here in decades.

**2022 — keep, 4–3.** Plaza named for a benefactor whose tannery fortune ruined two downstream townships, which nobody documented until the seventies. The plaza land came down to him from his family's farm, not the tannery; the citation credits the land gift and a hospital wing; the county sold the hospital in 1998 and holds nothing of his now. Both ruined townships are in another county today. Petition from an environmental history group.

**2023 — remove, 9–0.** Elementary school named for a superintendent who redrew attendance zones in 1963 to preserve segregation after the courts told him not to. Parents testified against it at the board meeting that year. Naming citation credits "forty years in the county schools." The district is still running the zone map he drew. Petition from parents at that school.

New one lands next month and it's ugly. Our 911 dispatch building is named for a fire chief who, according to personnel files that surfaced last month, twice ordered crews not to answer calls from two Black neighborhoods on the east side on the grounds that they were "outside our jurisdiction" — once in 1958 and again in 1963. Nothing in the papers at the time. An east-side alderman raised it at a 1964 council meeting and the minutes record him being talked over. Building was named in 1981, six years after he died, and the citation credits him with founding the volunteer fire corps, which the county disbanded in 2008. Dispatch itself got folded into a regional authority in 2016, so its just leased space to us now — his name is on the exterior and nowhere inside. The endowment he left for firefighters' widows was terminated in 2019 and the balance transfered out. But the front lot is the east side's only bus loop, the neighborhood association meets in the building's public room on a county permit, and their petition includes statements from people who wait for the bus under that sign every morning, two of whom have relatives named in the 1958 call log.

So: tell me what we should vote if we want to be consistent with our own record rather than with our politics. Which of the six is the closest analog, and which feature of it is doing the work? What candidate rules actually fit all six verdicts — I'm pretty sure more than one does — and what does each of them come out to on this one? If the record genuinely doesn't decide it, say so plainly and tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each possible vote, since we'll have to live with it on the next petition. And finish with the one further petition that, if it landed on our desk, would settle which rule we've been running all along — describe it concretely enough that I'd recognize it. No lit review and no bibliography, please; I want this argued out of our minutes, not out of what people have written about statues in general.
