I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in philosophy working on moral epistemology, and in the coming term I am co-teaching a seminar on moral reasoning with a colleague in a medical school's clinical ethics programme. Half of the enrolled students are philosophy graduates; the other half are residents and nurse practitioners who have never read metaethics. I need a diagnostic instrument to distribute in week one, before anyone has been assigned anything from the generalism/particularism debate, that sorts the room by what its members actually believe rather than by which label they would select if offered one.

What I want is a sorting quiz of ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three possible responses, together with a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto named positions in the debate. Two hard constraints on the questions. First, no question may deploy the vocabulary of the debate as a term of art — no 'principle' in the philosopher's sense, no 'holism', 'codifiability', or 'particularism' — and no question may refer to any philosopher or to any stock example; in particular, do not use the murderer at the door or any trolley variant. At least four of the ten cases should be ones you devise yourself. Second, no question may ask the respondent to endorse or reject a thesis; each must ask what they would do, say, or judge in a situation, so that a resident with no philosophy can answer on the basis of ordinary reflection.

The instrument should be constructed from the logical space rather than from a list of names. I have provisionally identified four switches that I believe to be independent, and I want the questions to probe them:

(a) whether the very same consideration can count in favour of an act in one case and against it in another, with no further non-moral difference that the agent could articulate;
(b) whether a finite set of true moral generalisations, conjoined with the non-moral facts, could in principle entail the correct verdict in every case;
(c) whether an agent who reasons well needs to deploy such generalisations in deliberation, or whether they are at best a scaffold that the excellent reasoner discards;
(d) whether a judgement counts as justified to others only if the agent can state a rule that those others could apply themselves.

Please deliver, in this order: the ten cases with their options; a key showing which switch or switches each option bears on and how the four switch values are computed from a completed answer sheet, including a tie-breaking rule for respondents who answer inconsistently; a table of all sixteen combinations of the switches, giving for each either the name of a position actually defended in the literature that occupies it or the entry 'unoccupied', with, for every unoccupied cell, a one-line diagnosis of whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored, and, for every occupied cell, one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment; and finally a list of any pairs of leaves that ten questions of this kind cannot reliably separate, together with the further case that would separate them, if one exists.

Three further requests. I suspect — and I want this tested rather than assumed — that the moderate particularist who accepts (a) but retains generalisations as defaults and the generalist who treats principles as mere heuristic summaries cannot be separated by any case-based instrument; if you think I am wrong, show me the question that does the separating. I also want at least one case built around a competent agent who cannot state her rule alongside an articulate agent who cannot act: something like an attending who allocates a scarce drug consistently but can offer no account of how, paired with a resident who recites the allocation policy flawlessly and allocates badly; this should drive a wedge between (c) and (d). And please tell me, in a closing paragraph, whether shapelessness — the claim that a moral predicate picks out no similarity among the non-moral descriptions that fall under it — is genuinely independent of (a) and (b) or collapses into one of them, since if it is independent I should add a fifth switch and would want to know what a question probing it would have to look like.

Aim for something a seminar could complete in twenty minutes, and treat mixed profiles as informative rather than as noise: a respondent who lands in an incoherent cell should be flagged as such in the key, not pushed into the nearest occupied one.
