For the better part of three decades the debate over the norm of assertion has cycled through the same candidates — knowledge, truth, justified or reasonable belief, and various weakenings of these — and the same body of evidence: lottery assertions, assertions that come out true by luck, experts asserting within their expertise against a known peer's dissent, and the apparent absurdity of asserting what one does not believe. What strikes me is that every candidate survives every case, because each side has a distinction ready to hand: violating the norm versus being excused for violating it, or the norm itself versus the conditions under which a speaker can reasonably be expected to comply. Once a theorist can assign the contested intuitions to either layer, no individual case seems able to discriminate between the candidates. Is that the right diagnosis of the impasse — that the two-layer machinery renders the theories underdetermined by all possible case data? Or does the fault line lie elsewhere, for instance in a prior disagreement about what sort of thing a norm of assertion is (a constitutive rule of a practice, an epistemic requirement on the speaker, a regularity in how we hold one another accountable), such that the knowledge theorist and the truth theorist are not really answering the same question? Argue for your diagnosis using what each side actually says about the contested cases, and then tell me what would count as progress: a kind of evidence the layer-shifting cannot absorb, a principle fixing which layer our intuitions track, a reformulation of the question, or a reasoned case that none of these is available.
