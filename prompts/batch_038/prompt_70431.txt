The student's answer, which I typed up from a scan (so any odd spacing is mine, not theirs):

> Dennett's "Cartesian theater" is the picture of consciousness we inherit from Descartes: the idea that somewhere in the brain there is a stage on which the contents of experience get assembled into one finished show and presented to an inner audience. Descartes thought the stage was the pineal gland. Nobody thinks that now, but Dennett's point is that the structure survived the anatomy. What he calls Cartesian materialism is the modern version of it — the view that consciousness just is what happens in some particular part of the brain, so that once you are a materialist you have left the theater behind. Dennett thinks that is too optimistic, because materialists keep smuggling the theater back in through the back door, but they at least have the right metaphysics, and for them the theater is a leftover habit of imagery rather than a real commitment. And since no single region has ever been found where all the processing converges into one stream, the theater model is empirically refuted, and the Multiple Drafts Model is what fills the gap.
>
> On the Model itself: the brain runs many parallel streams of editing at once, in different places at different times, and none of them ever gets stamped official. A content becomes conscious not by arriving somewhere but by leaving traces — by getting written down in memory and becoming available to guide behaviour and to support a report. So there is no moment at which processing crosses a finish line into experience. This is not a denial that consciousness exists, and it is perfectly compatible with there being neural correlates of conscious content; it only denies that the correlates converge on one place and one time. It also handles the Libet-style timing puzzles, where what a subject says about when they felt something does not line up with when the relevant neural activity was going on: on this model the report is just one more draft, not a readout of a privileged instant.
>
> Color phi shows why. Two lights of different colors, a few degrees apart, are flashed in quick succession. Subjects report not two flashes but a single spot moving smoothly from the first position to the second, changing color somewhere in the middle of the path — even though nothing at the middle of the path was ever lit in either color. Something has to account for that mid-path change, and Dennett says there are only two options. Either the system first gives the subject the honest experience of a spot of one color and then rewrites the record once the second light arrives, which he calls the Orwellian option after the altering of documents in Nineteen Eighty-Four. Or it reconciles the two flashes before anything is experienced at all and presents the whole moving, color-changing trajectory as a finished product from the start, which he calls the Stalinesque option after the show trials where the verdict is settled before the proceedings begin. The second is what actually happens: the visual system takes its time, and by the time the content is available for report it already contains the mid-path change, so there is no earlier honest version sitting around to be overwritten. That is why the Model is not a skeptical or deflationary theory. It says experience is constructed, but it is genuinely had.
>
> So the central claim is an empirical hypothesis about the brain. It is testable at least in principle: it predicts that you will never find a neural correlate of a finished frame, and nobody has. Dennett concedes that you cannot test it directly, but that only means the evidence is indirect. The conceptual part is secondary — it is about how we should describe the results once we have them.
