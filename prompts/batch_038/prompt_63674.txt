ok so I've got an itch I can't scratch and I'm hoping you can write the thing I can't write myself, which is an argument between two people who disagree about the word "if".

How I got here, and I promise I have no philosophy training, I fix things: our kitchen lights went out a couple of weeks back. I stood in the dark with a torch and said to myself "if the fuse didn't blow, the switch is dead." Totally sensible thing to think. Then I opened the box, the fuse had blown, and afterwards I caught myself thinking "if the fuse hadn't blown, the switch would have been dead" and immediately went no, that's rubbish, if the fuse hadn't blown the lights would just work. Nearly the same words, one of them was reasonable and one of them was a lie. That bugged me for days. Then I started reading and it turns out people fight about this, and there's roughly two camps, and I want to hear them fight in my kitchen.

- Camp one, who I think of as the one-if people: there's a single kind of if-sentence and it does state something that's true or false. The difference between my two sentences isn't a difference in the word, it's a difference in what the speaker already knows — which facts they're holding fixed, which ones they're letting go of. I believe this is roughly the Lewis and Stalnaker neighbourhood but I might have it back to front, so correct me if I do, use whatever the real positions are, I care about the argument not the labels.
- Camp two, the suppositional people: the if I used while standing in the dark isn't in the truth-stating business at all. It's more like a bet you place under a supposition — you suppose the first bit for the sake of argument and see whether the second bit follows — and it earns the right to be said when your odds for the second given the first are high. On this view only the had/would-have one is a genuine claim about how things would have gone. Ramsey started it, Adams and Edgington are the ones I keep running into.

Write it as a dialogue between one committed person from each camp. Put them in the kitchen on the night the lights went out, fuse box open, torch in someone's teeth, and let it start from my two sentences and widen from there. They're both adults who've read the same books — not a professor explaining things to a layperson — and both are allowed to be funny and a bit irritable.

Give them my own case file to fight over rather than the famous examples, and make them actually work through it:

1. The fuse pair above. Camp two says it proves there are two seperate animals in the language. Camp one has to show it's one animal in two different situations, and I want them pressed hard on exactly what changed when I learned the fuse had blown.
2. "If that noise upstairs is a burglar, I'll eat my hat." I'll say that cheerfully while being pretty sure it isn't a burglar. My understanding is that if you make if-sentences ordinary true/false claims this comes out fine, but if you make saying-it track the odds of the second bit given the first, it comes out wrong, because my odds of eating my hat given a burglar are roughly nil. So this datum looks like it cuts the other way and I want camp two to sweat over it properly.
3. "The fuse blew. If the fuse blew then the wiring's shot. So the wiring's shot." That's obviously decent reasoning. But also: what happens to an if-sentence when you stick a "not" in front of it, or tuck it inside "maybe", or report that someone else said it? If it isn't the kind of thing that's true or false, what is the "not" grabbing onto?
4. The one that started me off: when somebody tells me an if-sentence, what have I actually been told?

Ground rules for the writing. Neither of them is there to loose. Every time one speaks they have to answer the specific thing the other just said, not step around it and restart their speech — if they dodge, the other one should call the dodge. Each side should land at least one hit the other can't fully parry. Nobody wins by name-checking a famous argument as though the name settles it: if a mathematical result comes up (I gathered there's one saying you can't have both truth conditions for if-sentences and the rule that their probability equals the conditional probability, except in a degenerate language — which sounds like it hurts camp one more than camp two, but tell me if I've got that backwards) then somebody has to say out loud what the result does and who it actually hurts. Keep the words ordinary; if a technical term is unavoidable, have one of them translate it for the other in the room.

Somewhere between 1300 and 1800 words. Don't resolve it and don't let them agree to disagree in a cosy way — have the last few exchanges be each of them naming the thing they still owe and why they think they can pay it.
