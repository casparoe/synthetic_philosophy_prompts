I am a novelist — four books published, twenty-odd years of daily revision behind me — and the architecture of my current manuscript depends, rather more than I originally admitted to myself, on a philosophical argument I first encountered second-hand and have never managed to dislodge. It is the one usually credited to Rietdijk and Putnam. Two people pass each other in the street, walking in opposite directions; because they are in relative motion, their planes of simultaneity differ, and on the slice belonging to one of them some event a million light-years away has already been settled, while on the other's slice it is still genuinely open. If what is real for me is what is co-present with me, and if that relation must be symmetric and transitive, then reality accumulates: everything in the four-dimensional manifold is equally real, and the passage of time is an artefact of a perspective rather than a feature of the world. I know the shape of the resistance. I know that critics have urged that the argument mistakes a convention about distant simultaneity for a metaphysical discovery; that Stein and Savitt have pressed the point that becoming, on a relativistic picture, is properly a local notion which cannot simply be stretched into a global hyperplane; that in cosmology there are natural slicings a presentist might shelter behind. But I have absorbed all of this from summaries written for readers like me, and I am increasingly uneasy that I have been nodding along to formulations rather than following arguments.

So I would like you to do something that is, I think, harder than explaining it to me again. Translate the argument, and the resistance to it, into the vocabulary I actually work in — the one I use when I am cutting a draft: scene and summary, the chapter break as cut, cross-cutting between two strands, focalisation and whose present a sequence is anchored to, the distinction between the order of telling and the order of events, retroactive continuity, the trick by which a reader comes to feel that something has already happened before the page that reports it, the difference between a story that is fixed and one that merely appears fixed from within. Go through it systematically rather than in the round. For each philosophical claim on both sides, say whether it maps cleanly onto a structural device I already use, whether it maps only if I bend the craft term until it means something noticeably different, or whether it has no counterpart in that vocabulary at all. Let me give you two concrete pressure points to begin with. Does the move from local becoming to a global hyperplane amount to insisting that a cross-cut sequence must hang together for a single implied reader, and is that insistence a legitimate constraint or a smuggled one? And does a privileged foliation amount to nominating one focaliser for the entire book and refusing ever to cross-cut away from her — or is that a distortion, because a foliation is supposed to be something the world has rather than something a teller chooses?

Then I want the residue report, and I want it treated as the interesting part rather than apologised for: which pieces of the debate simply refuse to appear in my craft terms, and what that refusal suggests. Above all, give me a verdict, and let it be a committed one. Tell me plainly whether the residue shows that my vocabulary is missing something real that the metaphysicians are tracking — in which case my novel is borrowing its structure from a distinction I cannot actually express, and I should worry about it — or whether it shows that a good deal of this debate was never as substantive as its terminology implied, and my suspicion that the argument is at bottom a claim about how to tell a story has genuine force. Do not set out both readings with their respective merits and leave me to choose between them; take a position, give me your reasons, and flag wherever you are relying on a contested construal of the argument rather than on something the physicists and philosophers would all accept. Please write in British English.
