I'm 72, retired five years ago after 35 years doing stock assessments on groundfish — population models, harvest rates, mostly staring at spreadsheets of how fast a thing grows when you take a cut out of it every season. Since retiring I've fallen into philosophy, specifically decision theory and probability, and I've talked myself into giving a 40-minute paper at a university philosophy department's Friday seminar, with about 25 minutes of questions after. They were generous enough to invite an outsider, which I now suspect was a mistake.

Here's what I want to argue. Expected-value reasoning quietly substitutes an average across a crowd of parallel copies of you for an average across your own single future, and those two things only line up under conditions nobody bothers to check. So the right objective for a creature that lives once, forwards, and can't be restarted is the long-run growth rate of the thing it's compounding — call it the geometric rather than the arithmetic mean of outcomes. From that I want to claim three things: (1) you don't need a concave utility function to explain why people avoid gambles with positive expected value, because something like the concavity drops out of the arithmetic of repeated exposure; (2) the St. Petersberg paradox isn't a puzzle about human psychology, it's a badly posed average; (3) and — this is the part I think is actually philosophy — the whole thing rests on a claim about what a person is. A person is a temporally extended process, not a moment, so the unit you assess is the trajectory. I want to say that's a metaphysical premise wearing a mathematical disguise, and that once you see it you can argue about it like a philosopher instead of pretending it's just arithmetic.

The room: two decision theorists who are loyal to the Savage/von Neumann-Morgenstern framework and will treat my talk as an attack on representation theorems; a formal epistemologist who cares about accuracy and scoring rules and will want to know why I'm talking about money at all; one historian of probability; a visiting econ PhD who has actually read the Kelly and Thorp literature and knows the old Samuelson line that growth-rate maximization is only asymptotically optimal; and two grad students, one of whom works on infinite and improper utilities and Pascal's-wager-style problems. Nobody in the room has done biology. Nobody will have read much of the ergodicity-economics stuff.

What I need from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of me. Give me the eight or nine objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage times how likely, each one phrased the way that person would phrase it — including the jargon — plus a one-line plain-English gloss on each so I don't get lost mid-exchange. For each: a reply I can actually deliver in about ninety seconds, a note on exactly where the reply is soft, and whether I should concede it up front in the talk to take the sting out. Then tell me which single reply has to land or the paper dies, and finally the question I'm praying nobody asks, with what I'd say if it comes anyway.

Two of my own soft spots, so you know where to aim: I want to claim the growth-rate view governs one-shot non-repeatable decisions too, like taking a lump sum versus an annuity, and I don't know how to defend that without begging the question. And my intuition comes from density-dependent, non-stationary fish populations, which is exactly the setting where a time average stops being well behaved.

Please don't send me off to read anything — I want the arguments, not a bibliography. And I can't work equations on a board in front of these people, so wherever a reply really needs math, give me the sentence I can say in words instead.
