I retired four years ago after thirty-five years in industrial process control — designing and debugging control loops for refineries and water treatment plants — and I've since fallen down a rabbit hole of philosophy and decision theory, which my wife regards as a phase. I sit in on a Tuesday reading group with a mix of other retirees and a couple of grad students, and last month we did the absent-minded driver thing: the fellow on the highway who can't tell one exit ramp from another and can't remember whether he's already passed one, so that the sensible plan (exit at each ramp with some probability around two-thirds, as I recall) comes apart from what looks like the best move once he's actually sitting at a ramp. I think the setup comes from a game theory paper in the late nineties, but don't worry about references, my co-author handles those.

What got me going is that I spent decades building real absent-minded agents. A controller that reboots on a watchdog timer and comes up not knowing how many cycles it has run, a dosing pump whose counter gets zeroed by a brownout, a redundant pair of units that can't tell whether it's the first or the second to act — these are the driver's situation exactly, except nobody ever gave us a decision theory for it, we just bolted on extra state registers and hoped. So here's my half-formed idea: absentmindedness isn't a switch, it's a dial. Suppose the agent's memory of "have I already been here?" is only partly reliable — right sixty percent of the time, ninety-five percent, whatever — and suppose also that its own action at a node leaks information back to it about which node it's at. Then I want to know whether the famous gap between the plan-time answer and the at-the-ramp answer closes smoothly as you turn the dial up, or whether it collapses all at once past some threshold of memory reliability; and at the low-memory end, whether the right response is to buy memory (pay for a state register, i.e. precommit) or to live with the plan-time answer and swallow the inconsistency.

Could you write this up as a two-page project proposal for my old university's small bridging fund, which pays for a part-time year of work by an outsider like me plus one graduate student? I'm not asking for an essay that answers the question, I'm asking for the plan of the work. I need: the question stated sharply enough that it has a determinate answer and someone could tell me I'm wrong; a short account of why the existing camps — the ones who say plan-time reasoning wins, the ones who say the driver should just do what looks best at the ramp, and the ones who say the whole puzzle evaporates once you're careful about what the agent's evidence actually is — all leave my dial question open rather than settled; the method, month by month, for someone who's handy with simulation and badly rusty at proofs, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternative of just grinding through the decision theory; what a successful result looks like, including something a practicing controls engineer could actually use; what would count as the project failing, and I mean honestly failing rather than "left for future work"; and a candid line about where this is probably too big for one part-time year and what I should cut first. No bibliography and no literature review with citations — but do tell me, by description, which existing arguments and objections I'd have to engage with to be taken seriously.
