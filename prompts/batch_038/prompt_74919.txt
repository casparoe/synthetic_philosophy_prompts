I have no academic training in philosophy, law, or public health — I supervise a warehouse and have been reading about drug policy on my own for several years now, mostly the arguments over decriminalization and cannabis legalization. I keep encountering one particular argumentative move that I have never seen named, and I would like to know whether it has a name, or whether the reason I cannot find one is that I have mis-described it.

The move goes like this. Someone identifies a harm that the law itself is producing: people die of overdose because they cannot know the strength or contents of what they buy; street markets settle disputes violently because their participants cannot go to a court; people with convictions cannot get housing or work, and so return to selling. The reply concedes the harm and then uses it as a reason to keep the law as it is — overdoses are already killing people, so introducing legal sales would be reckless; the market is already dangerous, so we cannot let it grow; people with convictions are already unstable, so we cannot risk adding more of them. The damage the present arrangement does is entered on the ledger as a cost of ending the present arrangement.

Two further instances of what I take to be the same shape. First, the claim that people who use drugs conceal their use and therefore present for help only at a late stage, followed by the inference from those late, severe presentations that use is intrinsically destructive and needs to be deterred more heavily rather than less. Second, in the years after a law actually changes, harms laid down by the old regime persist — suppliers already established, people already convicted, habits of concealment already formed — and that persistence is then offered as evidence that the reform was a failure, even though the persistence is what the reform was supposed to end rather than what it caused. I believe the same shape appeared in arguments about alcohol roughly a century ago, where the danger of unregulated liquor was cited as a reason not to regulate it, but I would rather you not take that from me as history.

I know several things it is not, and I want to be told where I have drawn the lines wrong. It is not begging the question: the person is not smuggling the conclusion into a premise, they are pointing at a fact that is true. It is not a self-fulfilling prophecy, since no prediction of theirs is what brings the harm about. It is close to the act and omission distinction and to omission bias, but I think it differs: there, the complaint is that doing harm is weighed more heavily than allowing it, whereas here the harm is genuinely caused by the doing — by keeping the law in force — and then credited to the allowing. It is close to status quo bias, but that names a preference, and what I am describing is offered as reasoning, which is why it is harder to dismiss. And it may be closest of all to the baseline problem in policy evaluation, the question of which counterfactual you measure against, though there the issue seems to be measurement rather than argument.

So: is there an established term or body of work for this? If there is, tell me which of the neighbours above it actually turns out to be, and correct my boundaries. If there is not, coin one and define it tightly enough that I could apply it without stretching it. I would need the definition to separate it from a genuine irreversibility argument, where a change really would make things worse and cannot be undone once attempted, and from ordinary special pleading, where the standard is simply applied unevenly. I would also want its edges marked: does it still apply when the law causes only part of the harm; does it apply to a harm the law caused that would outlast repeal by a decade; and does it run in both directions, so that a reformer commits it too by counting harms that reform itself would create as though they were independent of the reform?

Finally, and this matters most to me, I would like your view on whether what I have described is a fallacy at all. It may be that the person making the reply holds a substantive position about who carries the burden of proof when lives are at stake, and that this position merely looks like a fallacy from where I am standing. If so, say that plainly, and tell me what the position would have to be in order to be defensible rather than a way of never having to answer for the status quo.
