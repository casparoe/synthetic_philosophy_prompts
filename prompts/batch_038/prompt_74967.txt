I'm on the landmarks commission for a small county — seven of us, appointed, we meet once a month. I have to circulate something before next Tuesday's meeting and I want it to be an actual decision memo, not a discussion document.

The case: a timber-framed market hall, designated in 1988. Since then the owners have had six seperate applications approved, by us or our predecessors, every one of them classified as like-for-like repair in kind — roof timbers (1991), the south wall re-clad (2003), the window sashes (2009), roughly half the floor joists (2014), the stone plinth repointed and partly rebuilt (2019). Our ordinance says designation can be lifted when a structure no longer "substantially retains the materials and design that were the basis of its designation," and it gives no numbers whatsoever. Staff reckon something like 70% of the original fabric is now gone, but no single approved job accounted for more than about 12% of it.

The current owner bought the place in 2020 and did none of the earlier work. She's now asking to replace the remaining original frame members on the north side with steel and to take out the interior gallery, because her insurer won't renew on the structure as it stands. If we approve, we will have presided over the disappearance of basically the whole building, one consented-to piece at a time. If we refuse, we're telling a woman her building can't be made safe, and we're also implying that all six prior approvals were mistakes, since none of them looked different in kind from this one.

One of the newer commissioners called it a heap paradox at the last meeting. I think he's right, and I also think that doesn't help me vote.

So please draft the memo I'll send round. It needs a clear bottom line — approve, deny, or approve with conditions, and if conditions, say what they'd concretely be and by when, since "further study" is not going to fly with the chair. It should address how a body is supposed to apply a standard with no threshold when the entire difficulty is that there was never a step at which the threshold got crossed, and whether our past approvals commit us to anything here. Separately, there's a motion coming in the spring to add a bright-line trigger to the ordinance — something like "replacement of more than 40% of original fabric requires a full designation review" — so tell me whether we should adopt a number like that, or whether the number just relocates the problem, and if it relocates it, to where.

Give the owner's side its strongest version, not a strawman: she didnt cause any of this and she has an insurance deadline she can't move.

Length: something I'd actually read on a Sunday, say 700 to 1000 words. And as you go, flag which parts of what you're telling me are settled practice among people who do this for a living and which parts are genuinely contested — I don't want a debatable position handed to me dressed up as the consensus, least of all in the section about the 40% number.
