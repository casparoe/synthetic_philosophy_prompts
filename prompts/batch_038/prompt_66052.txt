Last month I sat through a restorative justice conference as the brother of the victim: my brother was attacked outside his workplace, and the offender — a young man with no prior record — agreed to meet us rather than serve a custodial sentence. I am an engineer, not a philosopher, but I want to be precise about the framework the facilitator kept leaning on, because it shaped nearly everything that was said in that room and I am no longer sure it can bear the weight placed on it.

The framework is the familiar one in which law-abiding is a cooperative scheme. Everyone pays the cost of restraining themselves; the offender enjoys the benefit of everyone else's restraint while declining to pay, and so acquires an unfair advantage that punishment — or, in our case, the conference — takes back from him. Crime is free-riding, and justice is the cancellation of a debt owed to the scheme's other participants.

Two things bothered me. First, in my brother's case I could not see what the advantage was, and the facilitator could not quantify one either; the free-rider picture seems to work best where the offence is a gain — tax evasion, say, or insider dealing — and to go quiet exactly where the harm is worst. Second, the facilitator said two different things in the same breath: that the offender had taken an unfair advantage over everyone who keeps the law, and that the conference would give back to my brother something that had been taken from him. Those do not sound like the same transaction. One casts me as the incidental beneficiary of a general redistribution; the other casts me as a creditor.

So please audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the scheme, the contributions, the fare evaded, the ticket inspector, the settlement — and then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the use it was put to from the ones that are merely inelegant. I am particularly interested in whether it smuggles in the assumption that participation in the scheme is voluntary, and that the benefits it confers are wanted by those who receive them. Then tell me which of the facilitator's two claims the free-rider picture can actually support, whether some revision of the same family of pictures can support the other one, and what framing would have been more honest in that room.

Lead with your verdict on the analogy in a single sentence before the table, and hold the whole response to 600 words.
