I'm TA-ing an upper-level ethics course and I've got one script left to mark before Friday. Here's the question:

**PHIL 340 final, Q3 (25 points):** "Negative utilitarianism is said to be refuted by the case of the benevolent world-exploder. Explain the objection and say whether you think it succeeds. Distinguish at least two versions of negative utilitarianism in your answer."

And here's the student's answer:

> Negative utilitarianism (NU) is the view that the right act is the one that minimizes suffering rather than the one that maximizes happiness. The label comes from R. B. Smart's 1958 reply to Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, where Popper had argued that public policy should aim at reducing avoidable misery instead of maximizing happiness, because the second aim is both more dangerous and less certain about what it requires. Smart's own objection is the world-exploder: if minimizing suffering is all that matters, then a ruler who could destroy humanity instantly and painlessly would be required to do it, and NU also implies that everyone ought to commit suicide.
>
> I think the objection fails, for four reasons.
>
> First, it attacks a straw version of the theory. NU is usually presented as treating happiness as having *negative* value — pleasure counts against an outcome, which is why the explosion comes out not just permitted but mandatory. But most people who hold the view actually accept the weaker claim that suffering has strict priority over happiness, so happiness still counts once suffering has been pushed far enough down. On that version the bomb is not recommended, since it wipes out all the happiness that would otherwise have been produced. Smart assumes the strong reading and then presents it as the only one available.
>
> Second, the asymmetry doing the work in NU is just Benatar's asymmetry: the absence of pain is good even if there is nobody around to be benefited by that good, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody who is deprived of it. Benatar's version is immune to the exploder, since it tells us to stop creating new people rather than destroy existing ones, and extinction harms no one. So even if Smart's point about the bomb stands, the underlying intuition comes through unscathed.
>
> Third, NU is a claim about which outcomes are better, not about which acts are permissible, so an objection of the form "you'd have to press the button" is a category mistake.
>
> Fourth, as a matter of fact, killing people causes suffering: dying is frightening and painful and the survivors grieve, so the painless bomb isn't on the menu in the actual world.
>
> So the exploder isn't a refutation. At most it shows that NU has to be stated carefully.

Could you grade it out of 25 and write up the comments I'd hand back with it? I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: which claims are solid, which are confused or flat-out wrong, and which of the four replies actually engage the objection as Smart stated it. Don't worry about guessing what I'd have said — I just want it judged on the merits. End with a two or three sentence summary comment I can paste at the bottom of the script.
