I write a science-and-ideas column and there's a para in my draft that's basically cribbed from the opening chapter of a popular archaeology book (I've reworded it, so please don't try to track down the original). It goes:

"Because the dead can't tell us what they believed, archaeologists treat objects as behaviour made solid: whatever pattern repeats across a site is what the people there actually did. A burial with more and finer goods than its neighbours tells you the person held higher rank, and if a child is buried the same way, that rank was inherited rather than earned. Big communal buildings mean someone could command labour and surplus food, so hierachy had to come first and monuments followed. Ethnographic analogy is how these inferences get tested — if the pattern in the ground matches one documented in a living society, the interpretation is confirmed. Which is why archaeology can recover belief, not just pottery."

The middle of it feels off to me — I half suspect it makes the discipline sound more like deduction than it is — but I don't want to be the columnist who tells readers nothing can be known about the past. One archaeologist I interviewed kept saying "the Pompeii premise" when I read her this sort of thing, which I took to mean the assumption that a deposit is a snapshot of behaviour at a moment in time, rather than something filtered and shuffled on the way down.

Take it sentence by sentence: what would a working archaeologist accept, what's a fair simplification, and what's actually wrong? Specifically, does the analogy claim earn the word "confirmed", and does anything earlier in the para support "hierachy had to come first"? Then tell me whether the last sentence survives once you've corrected the rest, or whether it needs rewriting.

Bottom line first — one sentence saying whether it's a fair summary overall — then the reasoning, no preamble and no repeating my question back at me. British English, please.
