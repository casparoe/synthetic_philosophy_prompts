I retired six years ago from a career in experimental physics, most of it spent in metrology, where the question of what makes one measurement better than its predecessor is a daily working question rather than a philosophical one. Philosophy has since become my occupation, and I have been reading the historiography of science with the growing suspicion that my own discipline has been writing its history badly for a century and calling the result a chronology.

In six weeks I run the autumn in-service session for the volunteer docents at the science centre where I now chair the corps. The education director has given me ninety minutes on Whig history — the temptation to write the past as a march of progress toward the present — and on what it implies for the way our volunteers talk about the historical panels in the galleries. The audience is about sixty people: retired engineers, schoolteachers, nurses, a handful of spouses who are better read than they let on, and a contingent of undergraduates who work Saturdays. They are intelligent, confident, and entirely self-taught in this subject. I have a lectern, a whiteboard, and the galleries ten metres down the corridor.

Please do not explain Whig history to me. Plan how I should explain it to them. In particular:

1. The order of the components, and what depends on what. My own guess is that nothing will make sense until they accept that past enquirers were not aiming at our results and did not know they were missing them; test that guess against the alternatives and tell me what must already be in place before the term itself can be introduced.

2. The example that should carry the hour, and what it must have and what it must lack. I had proposed our own handwashing panel, which tells the familiar story of the obstetrician who notices the difference in mortality between two wards, insists on washing, is driven out of the profession, and dies unrecognised, and which closes by inviting visitors to feel superior to the physicians who refused. The docents deliver it with real conviction. Should I build the session on it, or will that turn ninety minutes into the defence of a panel they love? I should add that I would rather not stake the hour on the panel's accuracy, which I have read is considered by professional historians to be considerably tidier than the record supports.

3. A distinction I have been turning over and would like pressure-tested before I commit to it: three separable habits that get lumped together under Whig — the shape of the narrative, with the present as destination; the standard of assessment, judging past actors by what we now know; and the selection, which episodes get told at all. My hunch is that the third is unavoidable for an institution of our size, since walls are finite and a visiting family has ninety minutes, and that retrospective selection is not a vice in itself; and that the second is where the damage actually lies. Tell me whether this threefold split is worth putting to non-historians in ninety minutes, where in the sequence it belongs, and what will happen in the room if my hunch about which parts are avoidable turns out to be wrong.

4. The misconceptions they will bring or form, and the point at which each should be headed off. I predict: that Whig simply means biased or partisan; that the remedy is to suspend all evaluation, which several will hear as an instruction to mislead visitors about whether a past theory was false; that anachronism and Whiggism are one sin rather than two; and that the whole business is a quarrel among academics with no bearing on a Tuesday tour. Above all, tell me how to handle the objection I expect from the engineers in the second row: that science does demonstrably get better at predicting and controlling things, that this is a result rather than an interpretation, and that the historiographers' complaint therefore does not touch our galleries. I do not wish to win that exchange by pretending the objection is false.

5. A check I can put to them in the final ten minutes that would show the idea has landed rather than that they are being courteous to the chair — not a quiz, and preferably something that uses the galleries, since I can walk them there.

6. What I should leave out at this level, and which of those omissions I should announce rather than simply pass over in silence. I assume Butterfield's own religious and political commitments, the subsequent argument that by the time he wrote his target had already lost its vitality, the older controversy about whether the historian's task is to establish how things actually were, and the later literary-theoretic critiques of historical writing. Tell me what else, and how much of it should be said aloud.

I am not asking for readings, for myself or for them; I will handle that separately. What I want is the architecture of the explanation and the reasoning behind the sequence, not a script.
