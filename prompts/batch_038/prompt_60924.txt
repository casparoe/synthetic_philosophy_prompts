I am drafting the justification chapter of a proposal for a sortitioned legislative chamber in a mid-sized parliamentary state: members drawn by lot from the electoral register, paid, serving a single non-renewable term of eighteen months, with binding authority over a defined class of legislation and a suspensive veto over the rest. Before I defend that design, I want the criteria it would have to satisfy — and, more generally, the criteria any theory of political sortition has to meet if it wants to justify the lot as a general rival to election rather than as a device for occasional consultative assemblies. Write those desiderata.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, then state whether you regard it as non-negotiable for a theory of this ambition, or as a demand made by one camp (pro-lottery, pro-election, or epistemic-democrat) that a good theory could defensibly refuse. Please cover at least the following, adding whatever I have missed:

- **Authority.** What entitles a body nobody voted for, and nobody can vote out, to bind the losers? The requirement cannot simply be that it be accepted; it must explain what sortition does that election does not, or concede that it does less.
- **The pool.** The fairness of a draw is entirely downstream of the roster it is drawn from. Athenian lotteries selected from a citizen body that excluded the majority of residents, and modern jury venires built on registration and licensing rolls systematically under-enumerate the poor, the mobile, and the young; summoned jurors also fail to appear at rates that are not random with respect to class. A theory must say whether correcting the roster is a matter of justice or merely of statistical quality, and what becomes of the claim to impartiality when it cannot be corrected.
- **Epistemic conditions.** State precisely under what conditions random selection tracks good decisions rather than merely averaging them, and what happens when those conditions fail: correlated rather than independent error, questions with more than two options, where majority cycling makes the outcome a function of who controls the agenda rather than of the underlying distribution of judgment, and domains where the relevant competence is genuinely specialist.
- **Representation.** In what sense does a random sample represent a population? The theory must distinguish mirroring the distribution of characteristics from mirroring the distribution of opinions, and must handle the fact that opinions are not fixed before deliberation, so the pre-deliberative mirror may be the wrong target — and that a draw of a hundred and fifty is a faithful mirror on some dimensions and a coin flip on most others.
- **Institutional complements.** Athens did not rely on the bare lot: entry to office was screened, office-holders were audited on leaving, service was paid, terms were short and largely non-renewable, there was a standing action against anyone who proposed a decree contrary to the laws, and election was retained precisely where expertise mattered most, in military command. Say which of these are load-bearing for the epistemic and legitimacy claims, and whether a modern chamber can supply equivalents without importing the careerism sortition is meant to eliminate.
- **Expertise after the careerist.** Once the professional legislator is removed, where does drafting skill, budget literacy, and institutional memory come from? A theory must answer this without relocating the power it displaced into permanent staff, seconded civil servants, and lobbyists — the recurring complaint against the legislative term limits adopted by several US states in the 1990s, and the reason a theory of sortition should also say how its justification differs from the merely anti-entrenchment justification for term limits.
- **Manipulation.** Whoever frames the question, sets the eligibility rules, and chooses the stratification controls the output. Impartiality may be a property of the draw while the design remains entirely partisan.

Then do two things I care about most. First, mark any subset of these requirements that you believe is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that descriptive mirroring of the population, independence from both organized interests and a permanent administrative apparatus, and competence adequate to complex long-horizon legislation cannot all be had at once, and that every existing proposal quietly sacrifices one of the three; tell me whether that is right, or whether the real impossibility lies elsewhere — and if the tension I have named dissolves under scrutiny, say so. Second, identify the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of candidate theories.

Close with a short scorecard, no more than a paragraph each, for the pure-lottery view, the hybrid view on which sortitioned bodies hold agenda-setting or veto power but not initiative, and the view that the lot should be confined to quasi-judicial single-shot decisions such as juries and discrete citizens' assemblies.

Be committed. I do not want a neutral survey of what advocates and critics have each said; I want your list, your own verdicts on which items are negotiable, a definite position on the impossibility question and on which desideratum does the most work, and a stated recommendation about which of the three requirements above my chamber should sacrifice. If one of the items I have stipulated turns out to be a prejudice of the pro-sortition camp rather than a genuine requirement, drop it and explain why.
