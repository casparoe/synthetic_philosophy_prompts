Bit of an odd one, and it's mostly for my own head rather than for print — though chunks of it might end up in a column.

I cover science and ideas now, but before that I spent years on the patent beat, mostly biotech, which means I've read more PTAB decisions than is healthy and I still think about credit and priority in patent-law terms: conception, reduction to practice, diligence, prior art, the person having ordinary skill in the art, obviousness, derivation, named inventorship, and the whole exclusion list of things you simply can't own — laws of nature, natural phenomena, abstract ideas, mathematical methods.

Here's what I want. Take the debate about simultaneous discovery — the Newton/Leibniz calculus fight including the Royal Society panel that found for Newton and effectively wrote its own report, Darwin and Wallace being read out together at the Linnean Society in 1858 because Lyell and Hooker arranged it, Merton's claim that multiples are the normal pattern in science and singletons the anomaly, and the other half of it, whether it matters who got there first given that priority is mainly a reputational institution (the Matthew effect, obliteration by incorporation, the Nobel rule capping a prize at three laureates) — and restate the whole argument in my patent-law vocabulary. Not as an analogy I can wave at readers. As a real translation, claim by claim, with the legal categories doing the load-bearing work.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

1. What translates cleanly? I suspect Merton's readiness-of-the-field claim is basically PHOSITA plus prior art, and a multiple is just what happens when something is obivous to the skilled person and two teams independently reduce it to practice. If that's right, spell out the awkward consequence: patent law's answer to an obvious thing is to give nobody any rights at all. Does the inevitability thesis, translated, become an argument for abolishing priority?

2. What only fits if you bend it? Try the historian's objection that Newton and Leibniz — or Scheele, Priestley and Lavoisier — never discovered the same thing, that there's no fixed object for two people to both hit. What in my vocabulary could carry that? Claims defining the invention, overlapping-but-not-identical scope, written description, enablement? Tell me exactly where you're fudging.

3. What has no counterpart at all? Run the paradigm cases through the machine honestly: calculus is a mathematical method and natural selection is a law of nature, so both are unpatentable subject matter and the framework can't admit them in the first place. Same question for truth — the law allocates a monopoly, science allocates credit, and neither tracks whether anything is true, but only one of them admits it. Is that gap a hole in the legal vocabulary, or evidence the does-it-matter-who debate is thinner than it sounds?

4. And the case where the two allocation systems collided head-on: CRISPR, where the Nobel went to Charpentier and Doudna and the interference went the other lab's way. Which rule gave the better answer about who discovered what — and what does it say that the US has since moved to first-inventor-to-file and killed off interference proceedings entirely?

Two rules. Don't smuggle the law's assumptions in and call it translation: every time you have to convert discovery into invention to make something fit, flag it in the margin. And no reading list or citations — I can't chase sources and don't want to. As long as it needs to be.
