I'm a staff writer covering machine learning and the behavioral sciences, and I need to get a dispute straight before I commit any of it to print. In the spring I sat in on a small closed workshop on learning human preferences from behavior; the organizers permitted me to attend but asked that participants not be named, so I've used pseudonyms and kept the description of the work at the level of detail I was allowed to record. The argument I want adjudicated broke out during the discussion period after a talk and resumed at dinner. I took notes at the time and have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can. Both speakers are competent, neither was posturing, and I don't think either was simply confused — which is why I need someone to go through it move by move rather than tell me which camp the field belongs to. I'm not looking for references, a literature survey, or a primer on the area; I need the argument assessed as it was actually made.

The setting, as far as I may describe it: the group whose work was under discussion had trained a model to predict a person's next choices from several years of logs from a grocery-delivery service — what users actually confirmed in the app, at what hour, after how much scrolling, how often they canceled or returned an order. The model was then used to generate recommended baskets. The headline result was a pilot in which the app inserted a mandatory confirmation screen — a delay of roughly two seconds plus a summary of the basket's nutritional profile — for one group of users, with a control group that confirmed as before. Behavior in the delay condition differed systematically: those users confirmed fewer high-sugar items, and the orders they did confirm were canceled or returned at far lower rates. The authors said the delay condition revealed the users' real preferences and that the model should be trained on that data, because the control-condition data reflected impulse rather than preference.

The exchange, close to my notes:

1. "Nadia" objected that this framing gets the inference backwards. Her claim: all you can ever recover from behavior is a reward function that, together with some model of the agent, rationalizes that behavior. Two seconds of friction plus a nutritional summary is a different decision problem, with different available actions and different attention costs, so the delay condition doesn't strip away a distortion — it installs one. There is no experiment in which we observe choice under no friction at all, so the frictionless preference is not a limit one can approach; it is a term without a referent. Her conclusion: the honest description of what the group built is that they chose the behavioral environment whose outputs they happened to like and then labeled the resulting reward function true.

2. "Owen" replied that this makes the whole enterprise vacuous, and that she knows it doesn't. If the delay condition reveals nothing, then no condition reveals anything; yet we say all the time, and not arbitrarily, that choices made under conditions that defeat an agent's own aims are not that agent's preferences. He pointed to the data: cancellation and return rates well above baseline in the control condition; users saying in exit surveys that they hadn't meant to buy what they bought; their later choices reversing the earlier ones. Revealed preference, he said, is not a snapshot but a pattern across time, and when a choice is followed by the agent's own repudiation of it, the repudiation is evidence about the earlier choice rather than just one more data point to be averaged in. He added that Nadia's position cannot say what is wrong with the control condition at all, and that you-can't-get-behind-behavior is a slogan, not an argument.

3. Nadia: grant the repudiation. But notice what you've now done — you've moved from inferring preferences from behavior to inferring preferences from behavior plus a theory about which behaviors are distorted, and the theory is doing the work. Present bias, attention costs, defaults, social desirability: each is a posited mechanism, and for any dataset you can find some mechanism whose removal makes the agents look more coherent. That's not a worry, it's a theorem-shaped fact. The same choice data is consistent with endlessly many pairs of reward function and agent model. Even in the clean case where the agent is exactly optimal, the reward isn't pinned down: adding a potential-based shaping term leaves the optimal policy untouched, so there is a whole family of reward functions consistent with the very same behavior. Once you let the agent model vary as well, the space of compatible stories explodes. So what makes your distortion theory non-arbitrary?

4. Owen: two things. First, mechanisms aren't free parameters; they're constrained by independent evidence — the shape of the intertemporal choice data, the dose-response curve as the delay lengthens, and above all the fact that a two-second screen carrying a nutritional summary and a two-second screen carrying a random image would not produce the same effect. That contrast is a real experiment, and it's the one the group should have run: if the effect tracks informational content rather than friction as such, you've located something. Second, uniqueness was never the goal. Nobody thinks the reward is one unique function; we act on equivalence classes and on the decisions that are invariant across them. What I object to in your framing is that it converts a hard, partly solved inference problem into a case for despair, and despair is the one response that does nothing for the people whose choices are being modeled.

5. Nadia: despair isn't my proposal; abstention under uncertainty is. Here's what I think the talk ducked. Suppose you accept your account — that the delay-condition data reveals the users' preferences better than the control data does. You then train on it and deploy a system that optimizes the recovered reward. You have built something that acts on the world well outside the training distribution: longer horizons, higher stakes, shifted populations. And the reward you recovered is accurate only in the sense that it rationalized a particular pattern of grocery orders. A model that is right about what people confirm on a Tuesday night is not thereby right about what they want, and the gap between those two claims is where these systems go wrong. Your own identifiability point cuts against you here: since the data underdetermines the reward, the deployed system fills in the underdetermined part with whatever the optimizer finds, which is exactly the part you had no evidence about. Calling the delay condition true preference licenses the extrapolation you should most distrust.

6. Owen: I agree with nearly all of that, which is why I don't think it answers me. Extrapolation risk is a reason to build conservatively and keep a human in the loop, not a reason to deny that some behavioral environments elicit choices that are more rather than less the agent's own. And on the concrete design question: if you had to ship either the delay-condition model or the control-condition model, and you believed what you just said about repudiation and cancellations, you'd ship the first. So the disagreement isn't about what to do — it's about whether you're permitted to describe it as preference-learning. My view is that you are, and that refusing the description makes the practice harder to police rather than easier, because it hides the real question, which is which distortions you are correcting and on whose authority.

7. Nadia's last word, at dinner: then we agree about the engineering and disagree about a label — and labels are precisely what gets printed. If you call it preference-learning, you inherit every claim anyone has ever attached to preferences: that they are the agent's own, that satisfying them is good for the agent, that the system's objective and the user's interest coincide. You haven't earned any of those. Two seconds and a pie chart earned them.
