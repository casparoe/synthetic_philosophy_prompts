I direct scientific programs at a private foundation. Each year we receive between forty and sixty letters of inquiry from investigators who state that their work amounts to a paradigm shift and who ask to be exempted from ordinary peer review on the grounds that referees inside the incumbent framework are constitutionally unable to see the merit of a rival to it. By our own retrospective accounting perhaps one of these per decade deserved the exemption; the remainder range from premature to deluded, and from inside the letter the two are hard to distinguish. The board wants a written triage procedure that a program officer — scientifically literate, untrained in philosophy of science — can run on a single letter in about ninety minutes and close with a recommendation. That recommendation goes to me; mine, with the file, goes to a three-person external panel. The panel decides money; the procedure only decides whether a file reaches the panel and in what form. The officer must also produce a one-page statement of reasons that the proposer will see, so whatever the procedure outputs has to be something one can say to a disappointed scientist without insult.

Build it out of what Kuhn actually supplies rather than out of the slogan his name has become. At minimum I want it to use: the difference between a genuine anomaly and the anomalies-in-waiting that every framework carries and normal science rightly declines to treat as urgent; the marks of crisis in a field — rival schools proliferating, the framework's boundaries blurring, practitioners reaching for metaphysical argument, a willingness to try anything — as distinct from mere dissatisfaction or from a hard technical problem; the point that a framework is surrendered only to another candidate, so that a critique with no positive apparatus is not yet a revolutionary proposal; the disciplinary matrix and its components (symbolic generalizations, models, shared values, exemplars), for determining which part of an incumbent framework a letter actually proposes to displace; incommensurability, specifically as it governs who is competent to evaluate the letter and why a panel's incomprehension is not by itself evidence of emptiness; and Kuhn's list of shared values — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness — as considerations that underdetermine choice rather than settle it.

State the questions in the order the officer should ask them, what an answer must contain to count as sufficient, the points at which the procedure terminates in a rejection, and the points at which it must stop and hand the matter to a person because what remains is a question of values, of risk appetite, or of whom to trust rather than of fact. Mark every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedural costume.

Confront one difficulty directly instead of smoothing it over. Kuhn's position appears to entail that the choice I am asking you to proceduralize cannot be proceduralized: the shared values yield no algorithm, and the alternatives cannot be compared on neutral ground. Tell me what portion of the decision a triage procedure can legitimately claim to handle — perhaps only whether the preconditions for revolutionary politics obtain, rather than which side is right — and what portion it cannot, and design the procedure accordingly.

Then run it on three files. First: a molecular biologist documents a persistent failure of a standard model, unexplained for fifteen years and absorbed by the field as a technicality; the letter offers a detailed diagnosis, no alternative framework, and no predictions that differ from the incumbent one. Second: a letter whose central terms cannot be mapped onto the panel's vocabulary — the proposer's uses of information and constraint do not line up with the field's — where two prior panels found the work unintelligible and a third found it compelling. Incommensurability says unintelligibility is not refutation, and we cannot write checks on faith; say what the procedure does here, with whom it escalates, on what evidence, and at what cost. Third, the answer I will not like: our flagship six-year program in neural coding, described internally as revolutionary since inception. When the officer runs the procedure on the founding documents, they disclose no anomaly, extend the incumbent framework's symbolic generalizations, and report successes of precisely the kind that framework already promised. The procedure returns something like normal science, and unusually good normal science, and I have to tell the board that its most celebrated program has been mislabeled for six years. If that is the output, show me exactly which features of the documents drive it, and say whether this is a verdict on the program or only on the vocabulary used to sell it.

Close with what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire systematically, whether it will pass almost nothing in fields whose crises are social rather than technical, whether it favors articulate proposers over inarticulate ones, whether it is designed to reject the genuine article early and at what rate, and which of these errors we can correct and which we should simply accept and monitor.
