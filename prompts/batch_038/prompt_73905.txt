I'm trying to pin down what I actually believe about when a thought experiment yields knowledge of the world rather than a vivid restatement of what one already assumed. My verdicts, case by case:

Galileo's two falling bodies, the faster and the slower tied together: this genuinely delivers a conclusion, since the speed-sorted theory contradicts itself and no experiment was needed. Einstein imagining riding alongside a light beam: same, it exposes a real tension between the electrodynamics of moving bodies and the relativity principle. Einstein's freely falling elevator: fully convincing, and I'd say it delivers the equivalence principle. Newton's bucket: not convinced, because the concavity of the water licenses a claim about space itself only if you have already excluded motion relative to the surrounding matter. Maxwell's demon: it delivered something real, that erasing a record costs work, but only once new physics arrived to cash it in a century later, so I doubt the imagining did any of the work. The Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen pair: yields nothing, since the question turned out to be decidable by experiment. The twin paradox: not a thought experiment at all, just algebra in a confusing costume. Schrödinger's cat: fails at its stated job, because running it leaves me thinking a macroscopic superposition would be strange, not that it is impossible.

Two things bother me. Galileo's case and EPR look structurally identical to me, each a reductio on premises the opponent already grants, yet my verdicts are opposite. And I find Mach's rotating globes persuasive against the bucket while granting Einstein's elevator, which is the same style of reasoning pointed the other way.

My tentative principle: a thought experiment yields knowledge exactly when it exposes an inconsistency among commitments one already holds and that inconsistency has only one coherent exit. Test it against all eight cases, saying in each which clause does the work or why neither does; repair or replace it where it fails; and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to retract. Then apply whatever you settle on to three cases I haven't listed: Newton's two globes joined by a cord, Wigner's friend, and a lamp switched on and off infinitely many times before some finite instant.

Finally, someone in my group says Galileo's argument is not forced at all, because the Aristotelian can simply deny that two bodies tied together make a single body with a determinate natural speed, so everything turns on a borrowed premise about how the speeds of composites are supposed to combine. If he is right, does the principle need a clause about which premises the imagining is permitted to borrow, and would that clause be principled or just a device for protecting the cases I already like?
