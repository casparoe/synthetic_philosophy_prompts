I cover science, medicine, and ideas for a weekly magazine, and over the past two years I have reported on three stories that have ended up pulling against one another in my head: a state legislature's fight over medical aid in dying, the official review of a hospital that lost power during a storm and had to decide who stayed on a ventilator, and a profile of people who give away most of what they earn. For an upcoming issue we are running a sidebar in which each writer on the beat states, under their own name, where they have actually landed — not a survey of the positions available, but a declaration of their own. My draft is below. I would like you to act as a professional moral philosopher who works specifically on the doctrine of doing and allowing and who is willing to be blunt with an intelligent non-specialist: I am far less interested in being reassured than in learning which parts of my view are load-bearing and which are rotten. Please think carefully and do not write your answer first. Begin with a working pass in which you set each numbered commitment beside each of the others and note, pair by pair, whether there is friction, apparent friction, or nothing at all; only then produce the audit.

Here is what I believe.

1. Whether a harm comes about through my doing it or through my allowing it is not, in itself, a morally relevant fact. I reject the claim that the act–omission line carries any fundamental weight. What matters is how much harm there is, how it is distributed, and what reasons the agent had.
2. Even so, killing is usually worse than letting die in the world as we find it: killers typically act on worse reasons, break more specific obligations, and destroy more that the victim had a stake in. So I expect the two to come apart in practice constantly, even though they do not differ in principle.
3. A case I keep returning to. A competent, terminally ill patient asks his doctor to end it. If she withdraws the ventilator, I think she does him no wrong. If she instead injects a lethal drug at the same request, with the same aim of ending his suffering and the same result, I think she does wrong him. And I want to insist that the difference is not about consent or about what is in her head, since I have held those fixed across the two versions. It is that in the first case his disease kills him and she steps aside, while in the second she introduces a new cause of death.
4. Relatedly, I do not think the doctrine of double effect can do any work for me. Appeals to what is intended rather than merely foreseen strike me as either empty or as a way of restating a conclusion in technical dress.
5. On method, I distrust thought experiments that are visibly engineered to produce a verdict — the elaborate switching-and-diverting kind — and I think a theory should not be selected on the strength of intuitions about scenarios no one will face. That said, the two cases I lean on hardest are the familiar one about a child drowning in a shallow pond whom a passerby could save at trivial cost to himself, and the one about a surgeon who kills one healthy patient to harvest organs for five. In the pond case the bystander acts wrongly; in the transplant case the surgeon acts wrongly; and I take those wrongs to be of roughly the same order of gravity, which is to say that letting the child die is on a par with killing the patient.
6. Yet I do not think morality is anywhere near as demanding as that parity seems to imply. Failing to give away a large share of my income to effective aid organizations is a failure of generosity, not a wrong. I also do not believe agents enjoy any special permission to look after their own projects and relationships — I am not moved by agent-relative prerogatives. My objection is simply that a theory which condemns ordinary comfortable people for leading ordinary comfortable lives has thereby disqualified itself.
7. As a matter of politics, however, I think the law should go on treating acts and omissions very differently. A criminal code that punished failure to rescue in general would be intrusive, unadministrable, and corrosive of ordinary liberty, and a bright line is worth what it costs in under-inclusiveness.

Now the deliverable. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which merely look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging, and for each one state precisely which commitment has to go and what giving it up would cost me in the verdicts I care about — I want the price of each repair, not just its description. Flag explicitly at least one pairing that I expect to be told is a problem but that you think dissolves on inspection, and explain why it dissolves. Then tell me whether any of these can be kept by re-describing rather than abandoned, and name the single revision that would repair the most damage at once. Close with a short portrait of the person I would become if I made that revision: I want to know whether the cheapest fix is one I would still recognize as mine. Please do not send me a reading list, citations, or a history of the distinction — I can find that myself. What I need is the audit.
