I'm a PhD candidate in medieval political thought, co-teaching a seminar session on Aquinas's treatment of tyranny. About half the class will arrive assuming that scholastic theology functioned as a casuistry for assassination, and I would rather disabuse them with a document than with a lecture. Please write a parody of an internal People Operations policy issued by a startup whose board is devoutly Thomist — "Removal of a Tyrannical Executive: Employee Guidance and Escalation Pathway" — of roughly 650 words, in numbered sections with a short FAQ at the end, sustained in the flat affectless register of real HR prose and never winking at the reader.

The humor has to come from the policy being a faithful restatement of Aquinas rather than from imagining a violent scholastic, so please include: a definitions section that separates a tyrannical executive, who directs the office to his own private advantage, from one who is merely severe, incompetent, or disliked; an eligibility test establishing who may initiate removal and why an individual contributor may not; a branching escalation turning on whether the executive has a superior to appeal to or none, with what follows in each case; a clause requiring a comparison of harms, so that no remedy is authorized where the resulting disturbance would leave staff worse off than the tyranny itself; and a terminal provision for cases where no human remedy is available at all, which should be rendered as a referral in exactly the same bureaucratic tone as everything else. Do not quote Aquinas, cite texts, or deploy Latin tags — every condition must survive translation into HR language. (For your own orientation: his discussion of tyranny in On Kingship and the question on sedition in the Secunda Secundae are the relevant material.)

Two targets should be legible at once: the procedural optimism of the genre, which assumes any harm can be routed to a form, and the popular picture of the scholastics as confessors to assassins. The internal consistency requirement is strict — every refusal the policy issues must be one that Aquinas's own reasoning actually supports — so that a medievalist reads it as accurate rather than as caricature. And it should end somewhere the class will find uncomfortable rather than reassuring: the document must make plain what the position commits one to when the conditions are not met, and it should not apologize for that.
