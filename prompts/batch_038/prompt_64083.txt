I have spent a good many years in rationalist-adjacent corners of the internet, so I know Dennett's three stances and the argument of "Real Patterns" reasonably well: roughly, that a pattern is real when it supports successful prediction and cannot be recovered from lower levels at feasible cost, and that the intentional stance earns its realism by being the only stance from which certain behavior is predictable at all. What I lack is a name for a wrinkle I keep encountering, and I have come to suspect the wrinkle is not a wrinkle but a distinct phenomenon.

Here it is. There are cases in which adopting a stance does not merely locate a pattern but helps produce one, so that the stance's subsequent predictive success is partly of the stance's own making. Three instances, in increasing order of how little they have to do with minds:

1. For years, macro commentary could not say what a major central bank intended, and its decisions looked close to noise when described in terms of reasons. Then a shorthand caught on among traders, according to which the institution had a standing intention to intervene once equities fell past some level. Nobody at the institution had announced anything of the kind. Within a couple of years that shorthand predicted its actual decisions better than any account assembled from committee members' stated views.

2. An employer rolls out an assessment instrument sorting the staff into a handful of types. I do not think the types tracked anything at the outset. Two years later, meetings run in that vocabulary — illustratively, someone says, let her finish, she needs closure — and colleagues now anticipate one another noticeably well, including people who report the disposition to themselves in the same words.

3. An analytics team segments a customer base in a way that, by its own later admission, did not carve reality at the joints. Pricing, packaging, and support queues are then organized around the segments. The segments become robustly predictive and — this is the part I care about — remain so years after the original tool and team are gone.

So: is there an established concept for the case where a level of description is made real, in the sense that matters, by the practice of describing things at it?

I know several things it is not, and a good answer should say so explicitly rather than let me conflate them. It is not the Thomas theorem or Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy, which concern beliefs bringing about the events they predict, not a descriptive level acquiring the standing of a genuine level. It is not Hacking's looping effects, or not obviously so, since only my second case concerns people; the first and third concern things nobody would call persons. It is not Searle's institutional facts, because nothing was declared or collectively accepted in cases 1 and 3 — an idiom simply started working. It is not the performativity thesis in economic sociology, which is about theory shaping the economy it describes rather than about the ontology of levels. And I have run across performative prediction and performative stability in the machine-learning literature, which appear to concern a predictor's output shifting the distribution it predicts; adjacent, but silent on whether the resulting pattern deserves to be called real. Tell me which of these is closest, and name any others I have missed — there may be something in the philosophy of the special sciences about levels being recruited by their users that I simply have not encountered.

Then the payoff I actually want. Does this threaten Dennett's argument? If part of the intentional stance's predictive success is caused by the stance's adoption, then the evidence licensing realism about the pattern is partly generated by the practice of collecting it. I have a hazy recollection that Dennett himself suggests we become proper intentional systems in part by being treated as such, which may already accommodate all of this; if so, say whether that is the same claim or a different one. And please draw the line I need, between patterns that are stance-dependent in origin but stance-independent in maintenance, and patterns that decay once the stance is withdrawn. Which of my three cases falls on which side, and does case 3 make the distinction collapse?

I am not after a label and a wave of the hand. Give me the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. If there is no established term, say so plainly and coin one — with a definition, boundary cases, and an explicit statement of what the term must exclude so that it does not collapse back into self-fulfilling prophecy. Distinguish the neighboring concepts in the prose, but do not turn this into a reading list. Please answer in American English.
