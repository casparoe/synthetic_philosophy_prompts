I'm a staff engineer on a trust-and-safety / evals team, and once a quarter I run a 6-week internal rotation for new hires. Week 3 is "aggregating judgments," and I want to build a take-home exercise around the Condorcet jury theorem, because our design docs keep waving at it to justify scaling up LLM-judge panels and majority-vote / self-consistency stuff, and I'm pretty sure half the team is cargo-culting it. I have no philosophy background — I know the rough shape of the theorem (independent voters, each better than a coin flip, majority accuracy goes to 1 as the group grows) and basically nothing else, so please don't assume I can fill in gaps myself.

Deliverable: a take-home that a candidate should be able to finish in about 90 minutes, plus a model answer and a marking scheme.

Constraints:

- Candidates are engineers, not philosophers. The handout has to be self-contained and must not require them to have read anything or to name any papers or books. Explicitly no bibliography or reading list — I don't want answers that are just guessed-at citations.
- The difficulty should come from the crux, not from vocabulary. I want it to be impossible to pass by restating the theorem and listing its assumptions.
- Keep my numbers, and make them actually do arithmetic with them. That part I can grade myself.

Here's the scenario I want to use — feel free to tighten the wording, don't rewrite the setup:

Our release-risk panel is 9 engineers. From two years of logged decisions, an individual engineer voting alone, before seeing anyone else's call, correctly predicts whether a candidate release will cause a user-visible incident 68% of the time. Leadership wants to grow the panel to 400 people across the org because "the theorem says the error rate goes to basically zero." The catch: every panelist reads the same auto-generated risk summary (one model, one prompt) before voting. An audit says the summary is right 70% of the time; when the summary is right an individual panelist is right 90% of the time; when the summary is wrong an individual panelist is right only 15% of the time.

Part A: what does the theorem predict for a 400-person panel given the 68% figure, what does this process actually converge to, and why do the two answers come apart? I want them to reconsile the 68% with the 90/15 split — i.e. notice that the marginal number is consistant with the audit numbers, which is exactly what makes the naive appeal look legitimate — and then compute the real ceiling. Also worth having them say what the 9-person panel's accuracy is under the audit model, since if it turns out we're already at the ceiling today, I'd much rather know that than have a part of the exercise that's secretly trivial.

Part B: change the audit numbers. Now the summary is right only 45% of the time, individual accuracy conditional on a right summary is 90%, conditional on a wrong summary 25%. Have them work out the marginal competence, the large-panel limit, and what that does to the usual "just add more voters" advice. I suspect this one lands somewhere genuinely nasty — please confirm the arithmetic explicitly in the model answer and spell out the implication in plain English, because that's the thing I want my team to walk away with.

Part C: a design question. Give them our actual situation and ask for a concrete change to the process that would let us honestly appeal to the theorem — or an alternative aggregation rule if they think the theorem simply doesn't apply here — plus what they'd measure from data we already log to check whether the change worked. I especially want a strong answer to say how you'd test the independence assumption empirically from logged votes rather than just assert that it's violated.

Then hand me:

1. The exercise handout exactly as a candidate would see it, parts A–C, with the numbers inline.
2. A model answer, roughly 800–1000 words, showing the arithmetic rather than just reporting it.
3. A marking scheme out of 30, broken down by part, that names the specific moves an excellent answer must make — not "discusses independence," but the actual step, e.g. "recomputes marginal competence from the conditional figures and notes it clears 0.5" style entries — and a concrete list of mistakes that should cost marks, specific enough that I can apply it without exercising philosophical judgment, e.g. "plugs 0.68 into the limit and reports ~1.0 without checking the independence condition." For each part, tell me roughly what a competent-but-shallow answer scores versus a top one.
4. Since I can't referee the philosophy myself: a short note flagging anywhere a strong candidate could reasonably disagree with your model answer without deserving marks off — e.g. if there's a defensible line on which kinds of correlation are harmless, or a good argument that "probability the majority is correct" is the wrong target for a release decision anyway.

Last thing: if any of my scenario numbers are internally inconsistent, or if they make one of the parts collapse into something obvious, tell me and propose a fix — don't quietly work around it.
