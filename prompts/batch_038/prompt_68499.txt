Write a satirical internal memo of roughly 750–900 words, issued by the Managing Editor of a mid-sized regional daily, announcing the immediate dissolution of the newspaper's Verification Department on the grounds that the newsroom has formally adopted a deflationary theory of truth. The register should be recognizably corporate — restructuring language, effective dates, reporting lines, procurement vocabulary, an FAQ for staff — and the humor must arise entirely from the philosophy being applied correctly rather than from caricature of it. A reader who has never encountered deflationism should still find the memo funny; a reader who works on truth should recognize that the author understands the view and its standard objections, and should judge the satire fair rather than cheap. The target is a position and a style of administrative discourse, not any real person: all staff, readers, and outside consultancies are invented.

The memo must include the following:

1. The founding argument, stated as new editorial policy. Because asserting a sentence and asserting that the sentence is true are, on the adopted view, one speech act performed twice, a department whose job was to determine whether claims are true has been discovered to have been determining nothing over and above the claims themselves. Include at least one clause set out as a literal instance of the truth schema — the sentence on the left in quotation marks, the bare sentence on the right — and have management deploy it as though it were a formula in the employee handbook.

2. A treatment of blind generalizations. The masthead will now carry a standing line endorsing everything printed in yesterday's corrections column as true. Explain, in-house and without condescension, why management holds that this endorsement adds no content: the truth-predicate is doing anaphoric work, standing in for sentences too numerous to repeat one by one. Include one staffer's reply — invented — that reads the endorsement as a substantive guarantee, and a management response that is technically impeccable and humanly useless.

3. A letters-page incident. A reader has submitted a sentence that says of itself that it is not true. The memo must not pretend this is a non-problem, and must not resolve it by abandoning deflationism. Show the compliance response — quarantine of the submission, a reminder that schema instances come with availability conditions, the awkward silence about what is left to assert — and let the reader see that the view inherits the paradox rather than dissolving it.

4. The normativity objection, voiced by a departing Verification editor in a quoted interoffice note. Her charge should be the real one: if truth is nothing over and above disquotation, then accuracy has ceased to be an aim with any content, and the paper has no account of why anyone should pursue it or of what its old motto was pointing at. The memo's reply must be one a deflationist could sincerely make — that aiming at truth just is aiming, for each subject matter, to assert what is the case, which the schema already delivers; or that the paper now maintains a family of local truth-predicates, one per desk, none of them needing a shared nature. If you have management consider a rival bid from a consultancy offering truth as a property that varies by domain, with ethics supplied one and the sports desk another, let it be rejected as inflationary scope creep, in the language of vendor selection.

5. A hard consistency constraint. Nowhere in the document may anyone concede that truth has a nature, an essence, or an underlying property, or that a statement is made true by a fact or a state of affairs. Wherever the corporate register pushes toward such a phrase — a formulation beginning with what makes this true — the memo must swerve, and the swerve itself should be a source of comedy at least twice.

6. A rewritten corrections policy. An erratum may no longer report that a prior statement was false, since falsity is handled in the same deflationary way; work out what the new correction language literally says, and let its emptiness be felt by at least one aggrieved subject of a past error.

7. A close consisting of the revised masthead motto, a change of reporting lines in which former Verification staff are reassigned to a desk whose name reflects the new aim, and a signature block.

Format the piece with a memo header (To, From, Date, Re), numbered sections, a boxed sidebar, and an FAQ block containing at least five questions drawn from real staff confusion. Keep it deadpan throughout: no winking asides, no stage directions, no explanatory footnotes, no naming of living people. Do not append a bibliography, a list of sources, or a pointer to further reading.
