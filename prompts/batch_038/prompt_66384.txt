I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm deep in a piece about whether the historical-Jesus business can ever actually converge, or whether each generation just builds a Jesus in its own image and then dresses it up in method. Ive been reading the standard history-of-the-quest material — Schweitzer's demolition of the nineteenth-century liberals, the postwar restart, E.P. Sanders and the people arguing after him — and I've got maybe a dozen hours of interviews with people on both sides. My editor wants an actual number in the story rather than a "scholars disagree" shrug, and I've realised I want one for myself too, because my gut figure keeps sliding around depending on what I read last.

So here's the proposition, as tight as I can make it: by 2060, a clear majority of scholars who publish on the historical Jesus in the main academic guilds will accept as settled at least one substantive claim about what Jesus himself thought he was doing — something with real content beyond the skeleton everyone already grants (Jewish teacher in Galilee, baptised by John, drew crowds, executed under Pilate). By "settled" I mean it's in the reference works and textbooks as a finding rather than as one school's view, and the people who deny it have to argue against it instead of just ignoring it. I want your credence on that, and I want it decomposed, with a rough probability for each term and then the combination.

Some terms I'd expect you to price: whether the independence assumption behind multiple attestation gives any real evidential lift once you take seriously that the sources may share oral tradition underneath them; whether the embarrassment criterion has positive force or merely filters out material the early movement happened to find awkward for unrelated reasons; whether dissimilarity can be repaired rather than abandoned, given that a Jesus who looks like nothing in first-century Judaism and nothing in the early church is a Jesus who caused neither; whether the source poverty is simply binding, so that no method however clever could reach self-understanding claims; and whether guild consensus would even be a reliable indicator of truth here rather than a measure of which demographics currently run the field. But here's a term none of my interviewees put in and I specifically want you to price: the base rate from comparable cases. We have other ancient figures with an arguably no-worse paper trail — Socrates, where we get three mutually irreconcilable portraits and not one from a neutral observer; John the Baptist himself, where we have a court historian writing after the fact plus the gospels; a couple of rabbinic figures whose sayings were collected centuries later. If the honest answer for Socrates is that we can't recover what he thought he was doing, how far should that drag the Jesus number down, given the source situation is worse in every obvious respect? Treat that as a seperate multiplicative term if you like, but don't fold it quietly into the "source poverty" one.

Then two things. Which term is your final number most sensitive to, and what single development — a discovery, an argument, a change in how the field is organised — would move it most? And: my two main sources sit at roughly 0.5 and roughly 0.15 on my proposition, they're both smart, neither is a crank, and neither of them will say anything unkind about the other on the record. Tell me which term in your decomposition they most likely disagree about, because I want to write that disagreement accurately instead of as a vague "they differ on method."
