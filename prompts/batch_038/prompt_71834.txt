I have no formal training in economics or mathematics. I left school at sixteen, spent twenty-odd years working in logistics, and for the last four years I have been reading game theory on my own, slowly and out of order, because I run a monthly strategy night at which a dozen of us play poker variants and German board games and then argue about what we should have done. One of the group writes software for a trading firm and reads a good deal more widely than the rest of us; last week he circulated a short write-up so that we would have something to discuss on Thursday. The relevant passage runs as follows.

"There is a way to solve many games with no machinery beyond common sense. Call one of your options dominated when some other option of yours does better against everything your opponents might do. Nobody rational plays a dominated option, so cross it out. Once it is gone, further options become dominated, because your opponents now have fewer sensible choices available to them; cross those out too, and keep going until nothing more can go. Three features make this attractive. First, it does not matter what order you do the crossings in — you always land on the same leftover set. Second, whatever survives is what a rational player will play, so in small games the procedure finishes the job and you never need to reach for an equilibrium concept. Third, you only ever have to compare one option against one option: a mixed strategy is just a lottery over your pure options, so if no single pure option beats a given option, no combination of them can either. In practice you can apply a cheaper test that comes to the same thing — an option gets crossed out exactly when it could never be your best reply to anything the opponents might do — and in most games you can also drop any option that is never better than another and is worse in at least one case, since dropping those cannot hurt."

Two things in that passage make me uneasy, and I cannot tell whether the unease is my own confusion or a corner he has cut. First, our poker games usually have four or five players rather than two, and I have a dim recollection of my textbook warning that arguments of this kind get delicate once there is more than one opponent. Does his identification of "dominated" with "never a best reply" survive that setting, or is it a two-player fact? Second, when I try his third point on a small game of my own, I get a verdict I do not know how to read. I made a three-row, two-column game in which my first row pays me 1 against one column and 0 against the other, my second row does the reverse, and my third row pays 1/4 whichever column the other player picks. Comparing rows one at a time as he instructs, I keep the third row, since neither of the others beats it in both columns. Yet I cannot believe a sensible player would ever choose a row that pays 1/4 for certain in a game where the other two rows pay 1 half the time; and since I cannot name any single row that beats it, I cannot see how his own procedure would ever get rid of it either.

So: go through his passage sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a working game theorist would sign without qualification, which are loose but harmless for a group like ours, and which are wrong in a way that would actually mislead us if we ran with them. Where a claim is wrong, give me the correct version and the smallest concrete example that exposes the error — small enough that I can put it on a whiteboard in two minutes and let the group find it themselves. Pay particular attention to the second feature, that whatever survives is what a rational player will play, since that is the sentence I expect the group to quote back at each other for years, and tell me what it should have said instead. I am not after references or a reading list; I will chase anything down myself if I need to.

Because I have no way of checking any of this, please be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict you give, and then single out the one claim in your own answer that you are least sure of and say what would settle it.
