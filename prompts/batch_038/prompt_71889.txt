I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on minds and computation, and I want a diagnostic sorting instrument to hand out before the students have read anything: a decision tree that carries a student from a shared starting point to a named position in the debate over the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument, the family of arguments running from Lucas through Penrose. Its purpose is to form debate groups, so it must sort students by what they actually believe about cases and claims, not by which label they would choose for themselves.

**Starting point and audience.** Assume the student has completed one term of first-order logic; knows what a formal system, a proof, and a Gödel sentence are; and can follow the schematic shape of the argument — if a formal system F is rich enough to capture a mathematician's arithmetical reasoning and is sound (or merely consistent, depending on the version), then that mathematician, on being handed a specification of F, can see that F's own Gödel sentence is true, so her reasoning outstrips F. Assume she has never read the primary or secondary literature and will not recognize the customary names for the resulting positions.

**Size and form.** Twelve to sixteen question nodes and ten to fourteen terminal leaves, rendered as an indented text tree: each node states the question, each branch states the answer, each leaf states its label. Every node must be answerable by someone who does not know which leaf her answer leads to, and no node may name a philosopher, a school, or a leaf. Paths may converge on the same leaf, but no leaf may be reachable by a path that contradicts an answer already given. Where one node must precede another because the later question presupposes the earlier answer, mark that dependency in a single line.

**The angle I want.** Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of views: the trunk should sort students by which premise of the argument they are prepared to abandon, or whether they keep every premise and attack the inference or the vocabulary instead. To that end, the node set must include, at minimum:

1. At least five nodes phrased as concrete hypotheticals rather than as doctrinal claims. Two of these are mandatory. First, a case in which an engineer hands the student a complete, inspectable specification of a system said to reproduce her arithmetical reasoning in full, and asks what she is then entitled to conclude about herself. Second, a case involving a device that consults a genuine halting-problem oracle, so that its behaviour is not Turing-computable; ask whether a mind containing such a component counts as not-a-machine in the sense that matters to her.
2. At least one node that separates two senses of mechanism that the literature tends to run together: being simulable by a Turing-computable procedure, versus being fully governed by deterministic physical law. A student who accepts the second and rejects the first must not be routed to the same leaf as one who accepts both.
3. At least one node turning on consistency versus soundness — whether the human reasoner may be assumed only to avoid contradiction, or to issue nothing but truths.
4. At least one node turning on idealization: whether the conclusion concerns what a reasoner with unbounded time, memory, and reflective access to her own workings could establish, or what actual people do establish in practice.
5. At least one node asking whether the argument's conclusion, if granted, would buy anything worth having — any consequence for consciousness, understanding, or freedom — as distinct from a technical point about formal systems.

**Requirements for the leaves.** Each leaf carries: (a) a descriptive position name of your own devising; (b) one sentence on what the position accepts; (c) one sentence on its most awkward commitment; (d) a tag — LIVE, if you expect at least one student in a typical seminar to land there; RARE, if the view is defensible but unlikely to be volunteered; or VACANT. The leaf set must include at least: a computationalist leaf; a leaf on which human arithmetical reasoning is itself inconsistent, so that neither a consistency nor a soundness premise is available to be exploited; a leaf that concedes the inference and locates non-computability in the physics of the brain, in both an exotic and a mundane version (the mundane version invoking continuous real-valued dynamics rather than any appeal to quantum gravity); a leaf that accepts the argument throughout but denies that the notion of mechanism thereby refuted was ever the interesting one; and a leaf on which the argument equivocates between proving and knowing, or between what the reasoner could establish in principle and what she could establish given her actual epistemic access to her own operations.

**The grid.** After the tree, list every combination of answers that your node set makes logically possible but that corresponds to no leaf. For each, say in one or two sentences whether the gap exists because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply bothered to occupy it. Three to six such entries is about right; substantially more suggests your node set is over-parameterized, in which case prune it.

**Separability audit.** Identify at least two pairs of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate — where the answer patterns leading to them are indistinguishable in a first-session questionnaire — and for each pair state the further question that would distinguish them, together with your judgement of whether a student could answer that question honestly rather than by guessing which camp she would prefer to join. Do not paper over these pairs by inventing extra nodes.

**Mis-sorting notes.** For three nodes of your choosing, one line each on how a careless, overconfident, or strategically motivated answer would place the student somewhere she does not belong.

**Prohibitions.** No reading list, no citations, and no attributions to named philosophers anywhere in the deliverable — position names must be purely descriptive, and a leaf must be fully intelligible to someone who has never encountered its proponents. Do not preface the tree with a tutorial on the incompleteness theorems; the audience already has what it needs. Keep the whole thing under roughly 1,500 words and prefer compact formulation to completeness of coverage.
