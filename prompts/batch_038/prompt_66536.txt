I TA a section on the socialist calculation debate for a history of economic thought course — this week it's Mises' 1920 article in translation, Lange's two-part 1936–37 reply in the Review of Economic Studies, and a chunk of Hayek's 1945 piece on knowledge — and after a student asked, half-joking, whether a company the size of Walmart is basically a planned economy with a stock ticker, I went home and tried to build a proper reductio out of the question. The idea was to have something spicy to argue against from the socialist side in discussion. Instead I convinced myself of it, and now I can't tell whether it's a good argument or a piece of sophistry I've dressed up nicely, so I'd like a real diagnosis before I put it in front of twenty undergrads.

Here it is. (1) An agent can choose rationally among alternative uses of productive resources only if it can rank those uses on a single scale, and in a complex economy the only such scale available is money price, because a price is what carries the information about what those resources would fetch in their next-best use. (2) Inside a firm, productive resources get reallocated by managerial direction rather than by exchange: the tooling, the warehouse space, and the engineers' hours go to whichever division someone with authority says they go to, and none of those things are bought and sold against one another. (3) So the condition (1) says is necessary simply doesn't hold within the firm's boundaries. (4) Therefore internal allocation isn't rational calculation in Mises' sense — it's informed guessing, i.e. a pocket of central planning. (5) The bigger the firm relative to the economy, the bigger the pocket; that's just the picture Coase opens his 1937 paper with, the firm as an island of conscious direction sitting in a sea of market coordination, except Coase treats it as a puzzle to be explained and I'm treating it as a problem. (6) But firms the size of small national economies demonstrably exist, and they're continuously profit-tested: if their internal guessing were as blind as (4) implies they'd be eaten alive by smaller rivals, and in the aggregate they arent. (7) So either (1) is false or (6) is. (8) If (1) is false, then the impossibility claim at the heart of the debate collapses into a claim about degree — Hayek's local-knowledge point plus the incentive problem — and that's exactly the ground Lange thought he'd already covered by having a planning board imitate Walrasian tatonnement and set accounting prices by trial and error. Conclusion: if Mises' argument works it refutes the firm; the firm is not refuted; so the argument doesn't work.

Tell me where it goes wrong, assuming it does. My own suspision, which I can't articulate, is that (2) and (3) are doing something sneaky with the word price — something about the difference between a resource having no price at all and a resource merely not being traded internally — but I might equally be wrong in the other direction, and (6) is just an empirical dodge, or (4)→(5) slides from "the firm contains a pocket of planning" to "the firm is a planned economy," which don't sound like the same magnitude of thing. For what it's worth I'd much rather cut almost anything else than (6), because I've seen the step-8 move deployed by market socialists and I don't want to hand them the entire debate on a technicality. So: which single step would you cut, why did that step look innocent when I wrote it, what does cutting it cost the anti-socialist side of the debate, and is what's left enough to answer Lange or does Lange genuinely win the residue? And if you think the argument survives every cut you can make and I should just reject (1), say so plainly — but then explain what Mises was actually doing with the firm, since he obviously thought firms could calculate and I'd like to know why he wasn't troubled by this.

End your answer with a single last line beginning "Verdict:" that gives the number of the step you'd reject and a short phrase naming the cost of rejecting it.
