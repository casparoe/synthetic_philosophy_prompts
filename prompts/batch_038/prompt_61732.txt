I have served for three years as one of the resident members of my city's panel on historic wrongs — a civic body, not a court, which advises the council on what it should formally acknowledge and what it should fund. Our record to date runs as follows.

1. Where the Victorian corporation itself voted to clear a parish and move its residents onto marshland without compensation, we accepted that the city was at fault, apologised, and funded a memorial.
2. Where an almshouse had been founded on a merchant's slave-trading profits and still operated under his name and coat of arms, we declined the word 'guilt', spoke instead of a benefit that had never been made good, and required the charity to contribute to a fund.
3. Where we were asked about a 1930s rally the council had permitted in a municipal park while voting against endorsing the movement that held it, we refused any statement of the city's fault, though we supported a permanent exhibition.
4. Where a municipally owned bus company had refused to employ non-white staff until the late 1960s, we accepted the city's fault outright, and accepted in addition that residents who were not alive at the time are implicated in it.
5. Where a factory owned by an out-of-town company polluted our river and the council had lobbied to keep it open for the jobs, we refused to call the city guilty but agreed that it owed repair.

Next month we take a case of a different shape. An annual civic ceremony, run and paid for by the council, honours a regiment raised in this city that burned villages during a colonial campaign. The regiment was disbanded decades ago; the council neither raised it nor authorised the campaign; and we can trace no property or revenue the city holds from what it did. Last year, however, the council voted to retain the ceremony, against a petition asking it to stop.

Predict how the panel will rule on whether the city can properly be called guilty here, and how it ought to rule if it wants to remain consistent with its own precedents. I want the candidate rules that our five decisions are jointly consistent with — I am fairly sure there is more than one — what each of them implies about the ceremony, which precedent is the closest analogue, and, if the record genuinely does not decide the case, what principle we would be committing ourselves to under each option we could choose. Someone once told me that a German philosopher writing just after the war sorted guilt into four distinct kinds, and I keep wondering whether this panel has been sliding between them without noticing; if you can reconstruct that scheme, tell me which of our five decisions belongs to which kind. Finally, on a point I cannot settle myself: is continuing to honour a deed a way of inheriting guilt for it, or a fresh wrong of our own making? Does the distinction change the ruling? Please say how confident you are in the verdict you predict, and single out the point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
