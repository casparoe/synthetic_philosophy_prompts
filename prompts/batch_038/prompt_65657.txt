so i'm running a little reading group at my uni on constructive math and it's week three and i'm kind of regretting it. half the people there have decided they're "intuitionists" but when you press them the only thing they can say is that they don't buy excluded middle, which is like defining vegetarianism as not liking the color of meat. one guy insists he'd accept a classical existence proof but only "provisionally," another thinks every function on the reals is continuous and cannot be talked out of it, and a third says the whole fight is really about what a statement means rather than about proofs. i want to make a handout for next week that's basically a sorting instrument: a decision tree that starts from a single concrete case and walks someone to the position they actually hold, without them getting to pick a name off a menu first.

the entry case is that old two-case proof — either √2^√2 is rational and you're done, or it's irrational (gelfond–schneider settles that) and then raising it to the √2 gives you exactly 2 — so you've got an existence claim for irrational a, b with a^b rational and no idea which pair is the witness. start there, and make the first question the one about whether that proof actually delivered anything or just showed the alternative was closed off. from there i want maybe six or seven branch nodes, each phrased as a case or a commitment someone could answer honestly without knowing where it leads: does proving a disjunction commit you to saying which disjunct; does proving something exists commit you to an algorithm that digs the witness out of the proof, or only to the fact that digging would succeed; do you accept that a computable search which provably cannot fail is a search you can carry out; do you think there are choice sequences that aren't fixed in advance by any rule, and if yes what does an idealized mathematician's free choices over time rule out about functions on the continuum; is the logic itself up for revision or fixed once and for all; is a real number a completed object or a potentially endless production; should the whole edifice be rebuilt from scratch or just re-read. then five to seven leaves, each with its name attached, and one or two sentences on the commitment that leaf finds hardest to swallow — i'd expect something covering brouwer with the creating subject, the recursive/russian line that accepts both church's thesis and the markov principle, bishop-style constructive math that wants neither, the meaning-theoretic anti-realist who says it's all about assertibility conditions, and the pluralist for whom the question is which universe you're standing in so that excluded middle can be perfectly fine inside some of them.

the part i care about most is the negative space. after the tree, list the answer-combinations that no node leads anywhere with, and say why each one is empty — whether it's flat-out inconsistent (i'm pretty sure one of these pairs blows up on the spot, and i want the tree to say so rather than quietly steer around it), or incoherent in some subtler way, or just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored and waiting for someone to occupy it. also flag any two leaves your own tree can't really tell apart — i suspect at least one pair is only separable by questions nobody could answer from the armchair, and if that's true i'd rather the handout admit it. keep the whole thing to something i can print on two sides, plain text with indented branches is fine, and please don't make it a ladder where each step down is "more skeptical about classical math than the last," because that's exactly the misreading i'm trying to kill in week four.
