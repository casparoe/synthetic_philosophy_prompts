I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I am giving the closing address at an interdisciplinary data-governance forum: forty minutes plus questions, an audience of about two hundred, mostly practitioners and academics rather than my usual lay readers. What I need is a question-period prep sheet, not a further defense of my argument. The argument is written; I need to know where it will be hit.

The thesis I am defending is that data protection law is a legibility machine. Compliance does not shrink what the state can know about its citizens; it converts diffuse, deniable, perishable observation into indexed, attestable, retention-bound records, and in doing so improves the state's ability to reconstruct a person's life after the fact. The quantity that matters, on my account, is not how much is known but how reconstructible it is: whether an authority, at time t, can reassemble a verifiable account of what a given individual did at time t minus n. It follows that the familiar three-way ranking of regimes — privacy-protective Germany descended from the 1983 census decision and its doctrine of informational self-determination, laissez-faire America, omniscient China — is ranking the wrong variable, and that on reconstructibility the compliance-heavy European regime and the Chinese one sit closer together than either side's self-image allows, with the United States as the outlier.

The four moves I will make, so that the objections have something concrete to target:

1. Every consent architecture is simultaneously a documentation architecture. Legal bases, purpose limitation, records of processing, impact assessments, breach notification, and the standardized categories the law itself supplies all require someone to write down what exists about whom and under what justification. The privacy-protective state ends up holding an auditable map of the very flows it set out to restrict.
2. The right of access cuts both ways. The individual's power to demand a copy of the file is inseparable from the authority's power to demand the same copy in the same schema; the dossier is legible to both readers, and only one of them is compelled to keep it.
3. The American regime scores high on volume and low on reconstructibility. Much of what is known about Americans is inferred rather than recorded, contractual rather than statutory, scattered across firms with no common categories, no duty to retain, and no duty to explain. The aggregate is enormous; the authenticated account of any one life is hard to reassemble and decays quickly.
4. The Chinese system is reconstructibility by design: identity-anchored, consolidated, durably attached to the person, and enforced through joint punishment. It is not that China knows more; it is that what it knows stays assembled.

The room, as precisely as I can describe it: roughly a third are European data protection practitioners — regulators, data protection officers, in-house counsel — who regard accountability and record-keeping as the enabling condition of rights, not their price, and who will resent being told their life's work is an instrument of state legibility. About a quarter are American constitutional and information-law scholars, fluent in third-party doctrine, in aggregation and mosaic arguments, and in First Amendment protection for the publication of lawfully obtained truthful information; several will think I have rediscovered their own critique of privacy-by-consent and simply drawn the wrong comparison from it. A smaller group are empirical scholars of Chinese internet governance who stress fragmentation, local variation, the court-enforcement origins of the dishonesty lists, and who are allergic to any comparative frame that treats social credit as a single omniscient apparatus. A scattering of political theorists and historians of bureaucracy will recognize an argument about state legibility under a new name and will want to know what I have added. And the moderator, from a technology-policy outlet, will spend the last five minutes trying to extract a policy implication.

Please give me eight to ten objections ordered by the damage each would do in this particular room — not by how often it has occurred to me, and not by how easy it is to answer. For each one: whose mouth it comes from, phrased the way that sub-audience would actually phrase it and in its own vocabulary; a severity verdict of fatal, damaging but survivable, or absorbable in a sentence; a reply I can deliver aloud in about ninety seconds; an honest note on where that reply is softest, meaning the spot where I would be in trouble if the questioner followed up once more; and whether I should concede something early in the talk to disarm it.

Beyond that, three things. First, identify the single question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes the tension I have not resolved, most likely about what my thesis is supposed to imply for policy, since a measure that makes compliance look legibility-enhancing seems to counsel weakening the very instruments I would want to defend — and give me what I would say if it comes anyway. Second, give me the one reformulation of the thesis that survives the strongest objection, in a sentence or two I could actually utter from the podium. Third, list the empirical claims in my sketch that carry the most weight and that I must be careful not to overstate, since I cannot verify anything in the moment.

Two constraints. Do not cite anything: no bibliography, no named studies, no article numbers, no invented findings. If an objection turns on a fact I would have to look up, tell me that a fact is needed there rather than supplying one. And do not soften the thesis into something unobjectionable in order to make the objections easier to answer; the whole point of the exercise is that the thesis as I have stated it has exposed flanks, and I would rather see them. Aim for something I can absorb on a train ride, around fifteen hundred words.
