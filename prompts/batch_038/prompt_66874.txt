I am preparing a short essay for a workshop on the philosophy of biology, and I would be grateful for detailed critical comments on the draft below. It is complete enough to be argued with, but I know where its weaknesses are and I would rather you found others. The paragraphs are numbered so that you can refer to them directly.

**Draft: The Same Stratagem Twice — Popper, Situational Analysis, and His Retreat on Natural Selection**

1. There are two well-known moments in Popper's engagement with Darwin. In the mid-1970s, in his replies to his critics and again in his intellectual autobiography, he maintains that natural selection, as it is usually formulated, is close to tautologous, and that Darwinism functions as a programme of metaphysical research rather than as a testable theory. In 1978, in a paper in Dialectica on natural selection and the emergence of mind, he announces that he has changed his mind about the testability and the logical status of the theory. The standard reading of this pair is edifying and, I think, wrong: the philosopher is corrected by the science and concedes gracefully. My claim is that the 1978 move is not a concession to biology at all, but the application to biology of a template Popper had already built for the social sciences — situational analysis — and that the template brings with it a cost Popper would elsewhere have called fatal.

2. The tautology charge should first be stated precisely, because its imprecise versions are easy to dismiss. Take Darwin's phrase about the survival of the fittest and define fitness as actual reproductive success. The theory then says that those organisms which in fact leave more offspring leave more offspring. Popper's objection is not merely verbal: a claim that no distribution of reproductive success could contradict is, on his criterion, a claim that says nothing about the world. Note carefully what the charge targets. It targets a formulation, not the research programme, and it leaves untouched the question whether some better formulation exists.

3. Popper's own way out, as I read him, is to deny that fitness must be defined by its effects. Treat it instead as a dispositional property an organism has in virtue of its traits relative to an independently describable environment — a propensity to leave a certain number of descendants, on the model of the propensity interpretation of probability he had been developing since the end of the 1950s. The theory then makes a claim that can be false: given this environment and this standing variation, these variants will do better. If they do not, we were wrong.

4. Here is the structural point I want the essay to turn on. That is exactly the shape of a situational model. For the social sciences Popper built explanations out of a description of the agent's situation plus a principle of rational action, and he held the rationality principle to be all but empty and not empirically testable in isolation. His advice when such a model's prediction fails is not to abandon the principle of rationality but to revise the description of the situation. The empirical content, and therefore the risk of error, lives in the situational description; the principle is the schematism that converts that description into a prediction. Fitness-as-propensity occupies the place of the rationality principle. The specification of the environment occupies the place of the situational description. On this reading the recantation is not a change of philosophy of science but its extension into a new domain.

5. This makes sense of something the standard story finds awkward: how Popper could insist, in the same period and sometimes in the same passage, that Darwinism is a metaphysical research programme and that it is testable. On the situational reading there is no contradiction to explain. A metaphysical principle can be the organising core of a testable model, provided the testable content is supplied from elsewhere — from independently specified situations. Popper says as much about rationality. He does not, so far as I know, say it about natural selection, and filling that silence is what the essay proposes to do.

6. Now the cost. Popper's whole critical apparatus is aimed at theories that cannot fail: historicism, psychoanalysis, and in politics the utopian social engineer who explains every shortfall by appeal to conditions he did not predict. His test for whether an auxiliary move is legitimate is whether it adds testable content or merely rescues the theory by reinterpreting its terms — an immunizing stratagem. Consider, then, what happens when a situational model in biology fails. The moth population did not darken; the resistant strain did not spread; the predicted life-history trade-off did not appear. The Popperian who has adopted the template does not conclude that natural selection has been refuted. He concludes that the situation was mis-specified: an unrecorded predator, an episode of gene flow, a frequency-dependent effect, a maternal effect. Each of these is a real possibility and in principle checkable — but checkable only after the failure, and only by supplying further situational detail.

7. So the question that decides whether the 1978 escape genuinely de-tautologizes the theory or merely rescues it is temporal rather than logical: can the environment be specified independently, in advance of knowing which variants win? Where it can — a novel pesticide applied to a population with standing heritable variation, where we can say beforehand which phenotypes the poison will favour — the theory is genuinely risk-taking and the tautology charge is simply mistaken. Where the environment can be reconstructed only afterwards, from the fact that some variant spread, the situational model degrades into a narrative of the actual course of events. At that point Popper has rebuilt inside biology the thing he spent his life attacking: an account of history that cannot be contradicted, because every outcome is explained by the situation as it turns out to have been.

8. This is why the interesting Popper is not the recanter but the diagnostician. His much earlier insistence that evolution describes a unique and unrepeatable historical sequence rather than a law-like regularity is usually filed away as a confusion. On my reading it is the surviving core of his worry, and the 1978 paper concedes too fast. What he ought to have said is neither that natural selection is untestable nor that it is testable, but that its testability varies with the ex ante availability of the situational description — and that in the historical parts of the discipline, where the situations are phylogenies, extinctions and the origins of novel structures, it degrades towards narrative for reasons that have nothing to do with tautology.

9. Two difficulties I have not solved. First, the propensity analogy may bear more weight than it can carry: a propensity to survive is arguably unlike a single-case probability, because it is unclear what the reference class is, and if the analogy fails then paragraph 3 loses its engine. Second, I have no worked case that discriminates between the ex ante and the ex post readings. I also expect the objection that the situational analogy is decorative — the rationality principle concerns the reasons of agents, and there is nothing in a moth's environment analogous to a reason.

**Comments I would like**

2. Paragraph 4. Tell me whether the analogy with situational analysis is load-bearing or decorative. 3. Paragraph 7. Is ex ante specifiability of the environment a real criterion, or does it collapse into the difficulty Popper himself acknowledged, that of telling a legitimate auxiliary from an immunizing stratagem? If it collapses, say what would have to be added to it to make it non-trivial. If it does not collapse, tell me what a defender of the situational template would say about the historical cases in paragraph 8, where I claim it does degrade.

4. Suggest two concrete cases from evolutionary biology that would discriminate between the ex ante and ex post readings — one in which I could argue the situational description was genuinely available beforehand, and one in which it plainly was not. 5. Structure. Which paragraph should be cut or compressed? Where will a reader who does not work on Popper lose the thread? Does paragraph 8's claim that the earlier Popper is the interesting one undermine the framing offered in paragraph 1, and if so, how should the opening be revised?

6. Overall judgement, stated bluntly. Is the thesis one that a philosopher of biology would find worth arguing with, or does it reduce to the familiar complaint that fitness cannot be measured independently of reproductive success?
