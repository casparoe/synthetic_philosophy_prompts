ok so I've spent way to many years reading the same argument about "Western values" in comment sections, and somewhere along the line I started noticing a shape in it that I can't find a word for. Not the shape of any one claim in the argument — the shape of how the list behaves over time.

Here's what I mean. Two people are arguing about whether the West has a distinctive set of values. One posts a list: free speech, gender equality, church/state separation, tolerance of criticism, democracy. The other posts a different list: rule of law, private property, individual conscience, the scientific method, plus something about the Greeks and the Reformation. Both insist they're describing the same inherited thing, and both think the other guy has simply gotten it wrong. Fine so far, that's just a dispute.

But go pull the same argument out of earlier decades and the lists barely overlap. The version you'd get in the 1930s and 40s cheerfully includes stuff like a specifically Christian character, or a ranking of civilizations, that the present-day version would treat as disqualifying. And nobody ever says "I'm revising the list." Everyone says these have always been our values, the other side is the confused one. So the extension churns almost completely over a century while everyone using the term experiences it as pointing at one fixed object.

And there's a two-directional immunity thing that I think is the actually intresting part:

- when someone points out that the West also did slavery, colonial expropriation, religious wars, torture, the reply is that those are betrayals of Western values rather than instances of them — the tradition only gets scored on its wins;
- when someone points out that plenty of non-Western societies also had public argument, markets, legal limits on rulers, and in places something like gender equality, the reply is "sure, but not natively / not consistently / not as the organizing principle" — so uniqueness survives whatever the record shows.

Both kinds of disconfirming evidence get absorbed, and what's actually doing the work of fixing the list is the foil. The list is whatever we don't share with whoever we're currently contrasting ourselves against, and since the contrast partner keeps changing, the list keeps changing, but it's always talked about as an inheritance that was handed down.

I know the boring answers. It's not quite no-true-Scotsman, because that's a single claim getting patched, and this is a whole category whose membership gets re-derived every generation without anyone noticing they're doing it. It's not motte-and-bailey either, because there's no stable defensible core to retreat to — the motte moves too. And people wave at "self-sealing definition," which captures the immunity but not the fact that the content is contrast-determined and historically unstable at the same time. Those feel like three separate near-misses and none of them is the thing.

So: is there an established concept for this exact configuration — a category whose extension is fixed by contrast with a foil, so that it churns silently while its users experience it as fixed? I have a vague sense there's a literature somewhere in political theory or rhetoric that's got something adjacent, but I don't know it and I won't be able to look anything up, so please don't hand me a reading list — no bibliographies, no suggested sources, just the analysis. What I want is the carving. What is this thing precisely? Which near-miss concepts would you reach for first, and how does it differ from each of them, one at a time? Where are the edges — what would a case look like that isn't this but looks like it from a distance? And if it genuinely has no name, coin me one I could drop into a comment thread without it sounding like jargon, with a definition and boundary cases.

Two bonus questions if you have room: is the two-directional immunity move one phenomenon or two that just happen to co-occur, because I can't tell? And does it matter whether the foil is real and current (an actual rival power) versus imaginary or historical — I suspect the churn rate is much faster in the second case, and I don't know if that's a real difference or me pattern-matching.
