Eight months ago I was appointed deputy director of a humanitarian organization created by the merger of two bodies with incompatible operating philosophies: a disaster-relief agency that allocates everything through a published cost-effectiveness methodology, and a faith-based community-development organization whose principle for sixty years has been unconditional, long-term presence in particular places. I came up through the evaluation unit. I wrote much of the methodology their side has been contesting, and for several years I argued in internal memos that their preferences were a betrayal of the people we exist to serve. I now hold the pen on allocation, and if the merger fails it will be attributed to me.

The institutional structure constrains what is even tradeable. The board has nine members, four appointed by each founding body plus an independent chair; a resolution requires six votes including at least three from each founding group, so neither side can carry a round alone, and deadlock freezes the previous year's allocation. Roughly $4.1M is allocated in a two-day quarterly round; a separate rule permits reallocation of up to $600,000 within seventy-two hours in an emergency, on my signature and the chair's. My staff can produce numbers, their program staff cannot and will not, so claims about how much something matters reach me as testimony rather than data. One major funder holds a standing veto over any partnership with a sanctioned government body. Every compromise must appear in the published quarterly report, so there are no private side deals: what we trade is visible and becomes precedent for the next round. Their four are not a bloc — one has been quietly sympathetic to our methodology for years. Amending the methodology itself takes two quarters and a supermajority, and two of our grants are conditional on not publicly contradicting it.

We deadlocked for the second time last week and lost a matching window. My instinct is to concede everything that costs us little and treat everything that costs us a great deal as a matter of principle, and I have come to think that instinct is destroying value on both sides.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run on any proposed trade, quarterly or emergency, in under an hour, usable by a program officer with no training in bargaining theory. Keep the procedure itself to about a page of numbered steps: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what the procedure does with that answer, where it terminates in a refusal to trade at all, and where it stops and hands the matter to me or to the board because the question is one of values rather than fact.

It needs to do the following. Tell me how to distinguish a trade that genuinely creates surplus for both sides from one that merely transfers value. Show how the deadlock default and each side's outside option jointly fix the range of acceptable trades, including how to reason about their capacity to act alone with restricted funds and our lack of any equivalent. Say what can discipline a claim of intensity when the claim is cheap to make and the claimant knows exactly what my numbers would have to show. Identify when a compromise position is worse for both parties than either original proposal, so the correct output is no trade at any split. Include a step that separates trades over means, priority, and timing — which I have assumed are always available — from trades that commit the organization to a public judgment about what is right, and be explicit that I may be wrong that the first category is costless. Handle the case where accepting a trade makes us a participant in something we have publicly condemned, and tell me whether that functions in your procedure as a veto, a price, or a referral. Handle the publicity constraint: which trades exist only if they are never written down, and whether one can be structured to survive publication. Handle the sympathetic member of their four, and when dealing with individuals rather than the bloc is sound and when it is self-defeating. And include at least one branch whose output is to improve our outside option and accept the deadlock this round, with the conditions under which that is correct, since I do not think I have ever seriously considered it.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. First: a national distribution program run through a government ministry documented to divert supplies away from one region inhabited by a minority our partners have stood with for thirty years; their proposal is that we fund it, ring-fence that region, and accept a twenty-two percent higher cost per unit delivered, which my analysis puts at roughly a sixth of the round's measured benefit. Second: they will withdraw a standing objection to our emergency triage protocol that has cost us two years if we fund a small clinic network in a district where they have deep ties and where our metrics detect no effect at all; the concession looks large to me and cheap to them, and I want the procedure to test that asymmetry rather than accept it. Third, under the seventy-two-hour rule: famine onset in a region where they have thirty years of infrastructure and we have none, where they can and say they will act alone with restricted money and be publicly credited either way, and where the same $600,000 would do considerably more good this quarter in a different country in which no one will notice if we walk away. If the procedure's answer in the third case is that I should concede the round, say so plainly and price the precedent; do not soften it into a compromise the procedure does not actually support.

Finally, mark every step that is a judgment call dressed as procedure, and close with a short section on failure: how a competent and unscrupulous counterpart could exploit this instrument, and the three things it cannot tell me at all. I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of sources — I want something I can hand to a colleague on the morning of a round.
