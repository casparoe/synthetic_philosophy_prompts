I am a senior at a public high school, and this year I am doing an independent study on the eighteenth-century quarrel about taste. For the past two weeks I have been reading David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" in an online edition that reproduces the text as it appears in his Four Dissertations, with the original spelling intact. I would like help with two passages from it. My teacher has warned me that I tend to read old texts for their conclusions rather than for their arguments, so I am trying to slow down and check whether the steps actually work. Please reason only from what I quote below. I have no access to commentaries or journal articles, and I would rather you think with me than hand me a bibliography.

Passage A, from the second paragraph of the essay:

"As this variety of taste is obvious to the most careless enquirer; so will it be found, on examination, to be still greater in reality than in appearance. The sentiments of men often differ with regard to beauty and deformity of all kinds, even while their general discourse is the same. There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliant: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions. In all matters of opinion and science, the case is opposite: The difference among men is there oftner found to lie in generals than in particulars; and to be less in reality than in appearance. An explication of the terms commonly ends the controversy; and the disputants are surprized to find, that they had been quarrelling, while at bottom they agreed in their judgment."

Passage B, from the paragraph in which Hume turns to morals, and from a later paragraph on the value of general precepts:

"This great unanimity is usually ascribed to the influence of plain reason; which, in all these cases, maintains similar sentiments in all men, and prevents those controversies, to which the abstract sciences are so much exposed." And: "The merit of delivering true general precepts in ethics is indeed very small. Whoever recommends any moral virtues, really does no more than is implied in the terms themselves." And: "Of all expressions, those, which, together with their other meaning, imply a degree either of blame or approbation, are the least liable to be perverted or mistaken."

Here is what I cannot resolve.

1. Please give me a sentence-by-sentence paraphrase of Passage A in ordinary modern English, keeping the argumentative structure visible rather than smoothing it into a summary. The syntax is the main thing defeating me, especially "so will it be found, on examination, to be still greater in reality than in appearance."

2. The asymmetry. In matters of science, disagreement lies in generals, and "an explication of the terms commonly ends the controversy." In matters of taste, disagreement lies in particulars, and explication instead reveals that the critics "had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions." But in both cases the diagnosis sounds identical: the words meant different things to different people. So why does clarifying the words cure the scientific dispute and aggravate the critical one? Is there a principled difference available to Hume here, or is he simply describing two kinds of case and then asserting which one is which?

3. A tension between the passages. Passage A treats the general terms of praise as the place where confusion hides. Passage B says that terms carrying blame or approbation are "the least liable to be perverted or mistaken." My tentative reconciliation is that in morals such a term has nothing in it beyond its evaluative charge, so it cannot mislead, whereas in criticism the term fixes the valence — praise or blame — while leaving the standard being applied undetermined, so two critics can each be using "elegance" faultlessly and still be talking past one another. Does that reading survive the text, or am I rationalizing on Hume's behalf? If it fails, what would a better reconciliation look like?

4. What exactly does Hume mean by "particulars," and by "general discourse"? I cannot tell whether particulars are whole works, passages within works, or properties of works, and whether "general discourse" means a shared vocabulary or a shared set of doctrines. This matters because my answer to question 2 changes depending on which reading I choose.

5. The switch in vocabulary. In the science case Hume ends by saying the disputants "agreed in their judgment"; in the taste case he speaks throughout of "sentiments." Is that shift load-bearing, or is it stylistic? If it is load-bearing, what is the difference he is relying on?

6. Testimony. Slightly later in the essay Hume says we naturally seek "a rule, by which the various sentiments of men may be reconciled." Given Passage A, what could a critic's testimony actually transmit to me? I have been working with a distinction of my own between descriptive testimony — a general claim I can take on someone else's authority, such as that a novel is elegantly written — and demonstrative testimony, in which I am pointed at particular passages so that I can form the response for myself. Passage A seems to me to imply that only the second kind can do any work in criticism, since the general terms carry no agreed content. Does Hume's argument support that? And if it does, does it have the consequence that I can never learn from a critic that something is beautiful unless I am already able to see it — which would make the standard he is hunting for unable to settle a genuine dispute between people who really do differ?

7. Finally, how skeptical is this passage? Hume opens the essay by observing: "We are apt to call barbarous whatever departs widely from our own taste and apprehension: But soon find the epithet of reproach retorted on us." And: "And the highest arrogance and self conceit is at last startled, on observing an equal assurance on all sides, and scruples, amidst such a contest of sentiments, to pronounce positively in its own favour." Is that skepticism functioning as a premise in Passage A's argument, or only as a frame around it? Does Passage A, read strictly, commit Hume to there being no fact of the matter about whether a given work is elegant?
