So I want an actual number from you here, not a both-sides shrug.

Setup: an IRL-style system is trained on a huge corpus of what people actually *do* — choices, clicks, purchases, how they spend their hours, what they keep going back to — and it infers a reward function from that behavior rather than from what people say they want. Assume data and compute are not the bottleneck. I don't want "you'd need more data" or "it depends on the architecture." I want the conceptual failure modes.

Proposition, as tight as I can make it: the reward function it lands on would, if it were shown to the people whose behavior generated the training data, be endorsed by them after reflection with full relevant information — they'd say yes, that's a fair picture of what I actually want.

Give me your credence in that, decomposed. I'd expect terms roughly like:

- the chance that human behavior is generated by anything like a coherent, roughly stationary preference ordering at all, versus a heap of context-dependent and mutually self-defeating dispositions
- given that, the chance behavior is a faithful signal of it, since what people do is filtered through constraint, habit, social pressure, and adaptation to whatever options happen to be on offer (wanting what you've adjusted to wanting is a real thing, and it's invisible from the inside of the data)
- the chance that "endorsed after reflection with full information" picks out one determinate thing, since different idealization procedures — dump all the facts on them, vs. let them revise their desires under criticism, vs. consult their second-order desires — disagree about the output, and some of them would delete desires that the person currently holds most dear
- whether endorsement is per-person or aggregate, and if aggregate, whose reflection gets counted and how disagreements between people's endorsed rewards get merged into one function
- and one I think people skip: in IRL the reward and the assumed competence of the human are only identifed jointly. A less rational policy class plus a different reward explains exactly the same data, so the reward you "recover" is partly fixed by your prior over how rational people are, not by the evidence. And once you explicitly model bias — say, present-bias — you then have to decide whether the recovered reward is the long-run one or the biased one, which is a normative choice wearing an inferential costume. How much does that eat into the proposition?

One thing not to bring up: the fact that reward functions are only identified up to shaping-equivalent transformations. I know that leaves the optimal policy alone, so it isn't the problem.

For each term, give a number and one sentence of justification. Then show how they combine into your final credence — and if you don't think multiplying them is the right move, say what you're doing instead and why. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move you most on that one specifically.

Last bit: my gut number is around 0.15.
