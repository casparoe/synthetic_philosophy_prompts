I retired six years ago from a career in experimental physics — neutron scattering, chiefly — and have since been reading moral philosophy with something like a convert's appetite. A local reading group is working through Dante's *Divine Comedy* in translation and has asked me to speak to them; I would rather give them a problem than a summary, and I want your help making sure the philosophy in it is sound.

Consider the arithmetic of Dante's cosmos. Hell's population increases monotonically and its inmates are held for unbounded time, so on any additive welfarism the disvalue of Hell is unboundedly negative. Purgatory, by contrast, is built to empty. Heaven holds an unbounded number of the blessed in unending joy, so its value is unboundedly positive. The net is then a difference of two infinities, which my own field taught me to distrust: rearrange the terms of a conditionally convergent series and you can make it sum to anything at all. What axiology would have to be true for Dante's overall verdict on his universe — unambiguously affirmative — to be well defined rather than merely triumphant? Is he a lexicographic thinker, holding that beatitude and torment are incommensurable orders of magnitude, so that no quantity of the second offsets any of the first? Or is the value of his cosmos non-additive, residing in the fittingness of the arrangement rather than in the sum of the welfare within it?

I would like these hypotheses tested against particular episodes rather than in the abstract. Two candidates: the neutrals in the vestibule, refused by both Heaven and deep Hell, who seem to contribute nothing to any total — a marginal contribution of exactly zero, the case that most unsettles aggregative ethics; and Beatrice's descent to secure the rescue of one living poet, an act with likewise no effect on any infinite total, which the poem treats as decisive. And does *contrapasso* function as a proportionality principle at all, when it licenses unbounded punishment for bounded wrongdoing — or does Dante's apparent reliance on the fixity of the soul's orientation at death supply the missing premise?

Be concrete about scenes and structure. I do not want references or further reading; I have no patience for a bibliography. Paraphrase the poem rather than quoting it, since translations differ and I want the sense. Where the lens of infinite ethics distorts the poem, say so plainly — I would sooner concede its resistance to my framework than steamroller it — and tell me what the poem contributes to these debates that argument alone does not. Finally, indicate how confident you are in each claim you make about what Dante is committed to, and single out the one you are least sure of and why.
