I am marking scripts for a final-year philosophy of mind paper and would like an independent assessment of one of them before I enter a mark. The question set for the paper was as follows:

**Question 3 (compulsory).** Nagel's 1971 paper on brain bisection and the unity of consciousness asks how many subjects of experience a patient whose corpus callosum has been severed contains. (a) Describe the experimental arrangements that make the question arise. (b) State Nagel's answer, and explain why he holds that it does not oblige us to say that each such patient is two people. (c) Is his position stable, or does the evidence force a choice between one subject and two? Argue for your view. You may also draw on Parfit's treatment of the bisection case in *Reasons and Persons*.

A candidate submitted the following script:

> Nagel's question arises because of a surgical treatment for epilepsy. Where drugs fail to control seizures, the corpus callosum — the main bundle of fibres connecting the two hemispheres — is cut, so that seizure activity cannot spread from one side to the other. After the operation the patient appears entirely normal, and it is only in the laboratory that anything philosophically interesting emerges.
>
> The standard technique is lateralised presentation. The subject fixates a central point and a picture is flashed for a fraction of a second to the left of it. Because the visual pathways cross, the image is registered only by the right hemisphere. Asked what he saw, the patient says he saw nothing, or offers a guess; yet his left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, can select the matching object from a set he cannot see, or point to it. Flash the same image to the right visual field and the patient names it at once. Parallel dissociations appear in touch: an object explored out of sight in the left hand cannot be named, though it can be retrieved on command.
>
> Nagel concludes that the split-brain patient contains two subjects of experience rather than one. With the callosum gone the hemispheres have no means whatever of exchanging information, so what it is like to be the right hemisphere and what it is like to be the left hemisphere are entirely disjoint: there is no single stream in which both sets of experiences occur, and therefore no single subject of both. That the patient seems normal counts for nothing, since his ordinary reports issue from the left hemisphere, which has no access to the other side and invents a plausible story to fill the gap. Gazzaniga's confabulation cases demonstrate precisely this: shown a chicken's claw and a snowfield and asked to pick out associated objects, the patient selects a shovel and then explains, when asked why, that it is for clearing out the chicken coop — the coop, that is, being what his right hemisphere had seen. Here is a subject confabulating about the springs of his own behaviour, which is direct experimental evidence of two centres of experience.
>
> Nagel thinks this need not commit him to saying the patient is two people, on the ground that the two streams share a body, a name and a broadly continuous memory. The move looks unstable. If two subjects have their own experiences, their own learning and their own reasons for acting, then the fact that they are packaged inside one body is a fact about packaging. Once that is conceded, Nagel is already committed to the conclusion Parfit draws from these cases: that identity is not what matters, and that what matters is the holding of psychological continuity and connectedness, which admits of degree. Bisection is just the case in which continuity is divided rather than broken, so the honest verdict is that these patients are two people sharing a body — and Nagel's reluctance to say so is a bow to ordinary language rather than to the evidence.
