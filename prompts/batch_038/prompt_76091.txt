I'm setting the final for a graduate seminar in social epistemology and information economics. The students have worked through the standard sequential-decision model of informational cascades (Banerjee; Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch) and can already do the two-step likelihood calculation, so I don't want that tested for its own sake. Write one question, answerable in fifty minutes and worth twenty-five marks, whose crux is how much epistemic weight apparent consensus carries.

The question should present two students' verdicts on the model and require candidates to adjudicate between them: one holds that because a cascade can settle on the wrong action while looking unanimous, consensus is worthless as evidence of anything; the other holds that wrong cascades are less likely than right ones, so consensus still carries information — just much less than naive aggregation of independent signals would suggest. Add a second part asking what an outside observer who sees only the public action sequence, and none of the private signals, can legitimately infer from it, and whether letting the number of agents grow without bound ever restores the Condorcet-style guarantee that the truth wins out in the limit.

Give me a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that takes a definite side in the dispute and argues for it, rather than splitting the difference between the two students, together with a marking scheme that allocates all twenty-five marks across named moves and says, for each move, the specific error that should cost marks — at a minimum, conflating the fragility of a cascade with the irrationality of the agents inside it, and treating herding driven by payoff externalities as though it were informational. Finish with a brief note identifying where your model answer depends on idealizations of the setup that a strong candidate could reasonably challenge, and whether challenging them would change your verdict.
