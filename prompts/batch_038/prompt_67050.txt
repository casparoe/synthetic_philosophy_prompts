I am assembling a set of notes on the philosophy of psychiatric classification, and I have come across a recurring pattern for which I cannot locate a settled label. I would rather not settle for a plausible-sounding synonym, so let me describe the pattern at some length before saying what I want from you.

The pattern arises when a diagnostic category is welded to an entitlement or an obligation that has nothing intrinsically to do with the category's clinical content: a school district that will fund reading support only under a particular code, an insurer that will pay only for a named condition, a benefits agency that requires certification of incapacity in a specified vocabulary, a court that recognises one formulation as grounds for exemption and not another. Clinicians working under such arrangements are typically not dishonest. They simply find that the person in front of them will get help only if the record says something specific, so the code drifts toward whatever the relevant form will accept. Two consequences follow, and the second is the one I find interesting.

The first is that recorded prevalence rises steeply while the underlying difficulties in the population are, so far as anyone can tell, unchanged; the older label does not so much vanish as get displaced, so that a case which would have been recorded one way in 1995 is recorded another way now, and long-run series become incomparable in a manner that is hard to quantify. The second is that the clinical picture itself begins to move. Because the people now arriving under the category are arriving by a different route and with a different profile from those who arrived under it before, clinicians' sense of the typical presentation changes; teaching cases change; descriptions in successive reference manuals are revised to accommodate who is actually turning up; and researchers comparing cohorts thirty years apart conclude that the disorder's phenotype has drifted, when what drifted may have been the door through which patients entered.

The obvious place to look for this is Hacking, and the obvious thing to call it is a looping effect: a classification of people changes the people classified, who in turn change the classification. I am not satisfied that the fit is right, for two reasons, and I would like to be told plainly if both are mistakes. On one side, in the cases I have in mind the person carrying the label often neither knows it nor endorses it. A young child with a code in a school file has no conception of what it says; an adult whose claim was certified under a category she would repudiate has not acquired a new way of understanding herself; in some instances the classified actively resist the label, or would prefer a different one, and the label sticks anyway because it is the one that pays. If the mechanism is supposed to run through the self-understanding of the classified, it does not seem to run through anything here except paperwork, incentives, and the conduct of third parties. On the other side, unlike a tax bracket or a zoning designation, this category is meant to name something real about the person, and it goes on being used that way in clinical reasoning even while its extension is being fixed from outside by what unlocks a service. So the result looks like a kind that behaves as though it were interactive with nobody in the loop identifying with it, and whose content is rewritten from the administrative end inward.

What I want from you is the carving, not a name to put on a slide.

First: is there an established concept — in Hacking's own work or elsewhere in the philosophy of psychiatry, of medicine, or of social measurement — that covers this configuration as such? If there is, say what it commits me to, since I would rather learn that my two objections are already handled by the theory than discover a new gap in it.

Second: sort this out against the neighbours that occur to me, and for each say what the pattern described above has that the neighbour lacks, and what it lacks that makes the neighbour the wrong thing to reach for. The neighbours I have in mind are: a mere inflation of recorded rates, with the concept untouched; a change in the breadth of what a term is taken to cover, driven by ordinary shifts in usage rather than by an incentive structure; the general tendency of any measure to be distorted once it is used to allocate rewards, of which the diagnostic case would be one instance; and the classification of people as an interactive kind in the full sense, where the classified take up, resist, or reshape the category and thereby make it something new. I suspect the pattern sits between the third and the fourth, but I cannot articulate the boundary, and I want the boundary articulated.

Third: can a kind be interactive in the sense that matters when the uptake is institutional rather than personal — that is, when the feedback loop is closed by the behaviour of assessors, agencies, and manuals rather than by the self-conception of the classified? This is the question on which everything else turns, so please treat it directly rather than deferring to how the phrase is usually glossed, and tell me whether the answer differs if the classified are unaware, indifferent, or hostile.

Fourth: is there a signature that would let an observer distinguish this from a genuine change in the condition, whether in prevalence or in presentation? I am thinking of something like the shape of the time series at the moment an entitlement rule changes, the behaviour of neighbouring categories that lose cases, or the difference between cohorts assembled by clinicians and cohorts assembled by records. Say what evidence would actually be diagnostic and what evidence merely feels diagnostic.

Finally, if no established term fits, propose one. Define it so that it excludes its neighbours by construction, and give me at least two boundary cases: one that looks like the thing but is not, and one that does not look like the thing but is.
