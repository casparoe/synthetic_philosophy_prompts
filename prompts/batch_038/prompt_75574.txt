My co-author and I are drafting a piece for a philosophy-of-mind venue, and I want the central dialectic stress-tested before we commit to it. Please write a single continuous dialogue between two philosophers — equally sharp, equally well-read, neither a strawman — about the paradox of phenomenal judgment.

The scene: the two are sitting in front of a transcript generated by a stipulated device we can call the Duplicator. When attached to a subject, the Duplicator produces that subject's exact verbal behavior, including the subject's reports of what it is like for them, their hesitations, their introspective confidence, and their philosophical pronouncements, while the subject's own activity is stipulated to be wholly non-experiential: the reports come out of a model of the subject's functional and neurophysiological dynamics, with no phenomenal states anywhere in the loop. One philosopher is a committed epiphenomenalist who cheerfully accepts that the Duplicator is possible. The other is an opponent who thinks that concession makes epiphenomenalism impossible to assert, let alone to know.

Please build in the following:

- The epiphenomenalist should not hide behind mystery. Have them develop the natural move — that reports are caused by neural states that carry information about experience, so a report can be accurate about experience even though experience causes nothing — and then make them defend the account of content that move requires when the opponent asks why the content is about experience rather than about the neural activity it actually covaries with.
- The opponent should press two charges and keep them clearly distinct: first, that on epiphenomenalism the theorist's own beliefs about experience, including the belief in epiphenomenalism, are insensitive to how things stand with experience, so accepting the theory removes any ground for holding it; second, that the Duplicator transcript is word-for-word indistinguishable from the epiphenomenalist's own transcript, so the epiphenomenalist has no first-person procedure for telling which case they are in.
- The epiphenomenalist should have real answers rather than concessions — for instance, that requiring knowledge to be caused by its object is a demand we decline to make in mathematics or modality, and that the grounds for epiphenomenalism (the conceivability of the Duplicator itself, together with exclusion-style reasoning) do not depend on the trustworthiness of introspective report.
- At some point they must confront one specific question directly: whether the epiphenomenalist can say anything true about the Duplicator's subject without special pleading, including the sentence that the Duplicator's subject has no experience.

Format: hard cap of 900 words, no turn longer than five sentences, open in the middle of the scene with no narrator and no more than one italicized line of staging. Do not engineer a reconciliation; if they fail to converge, end with a ledger of at most six lines, written in their shared voice, recording what each side now concedes, where exactly the dispute remains live, and what evidence or argument would settle it.
