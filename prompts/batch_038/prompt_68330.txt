I'm one of nine people on what my company calls the Assistant Conduct Panel — we're the standing group that signs off on any case where our chatbot is allowed to not do what the user asked. Most weeks it's boring (jailbreak variants, borderline medical stuff), but a couple times a quarter a real policy question lands on the agenda and one of us has to circulate a memo beforehand. It's my turn for Tuesday and I want to pressure-test my thinking before I write it, so: situation first, then what I'd like you to draft.

The situation. About six months ago we shipped a feature that internally we call Holdfast. A user tells the assistant, in their own words, to refuse them later: "don't help me draft anything to my ex after 11pm," "if I ask you for reasons to skip the gym tomorrow, argue the other side," "no crypto talk, I'm trying to stop." We log the instruction, and when a matching request comes in the model declines and points back at what they originally said. Roughly 40k people have one active. Panel policy currently says a block is permissible only if it clears three tests — the user set it themselves, the user was told it's in force, and the user can lift it. The third test is where it's falling apart, and that's Tuesday's item. Users increasingly ask to be released mid-block: "I know I said that, but tonight is different, unlock it." Right now the model just unlocks, immediatly, no friction at all. Two escalations came in this week and they point in opposite directions. One: a user who set a no-alcohol-planning block eight months ago as part of a program they'd finished in the spring asks to be released the afternoon before their sister's wedding, saying they intend to have exactly one glass of champagne and would rather the assistant help them get a ride home than not. Two: a user who set a block on "help me figure out how to make it stop" four months ago, who has never once asked to lift it, asks on a Tuesday at 2am to lift it, gives no reason, and when the model asks a follow-up question, goes quiet for nine hours and then returns and asks again. Support flagged both. And the underlying data problem is that our own telemetry is structurally lopsided: among people who unlocked and then re-set the same block inside a week, 31% wrote in to say the unlock was a mistake and asked us to make it harder; but among people we refused to unlock we have nothing, because they either churned or never came back, and we don't survey churned users, so the exact population that would tell us the block was tyrannical is invisible to us by construction. Half my colleagues read that asymmetry as a reason to add friction, since we can't see the harm of over-blocking anyway; the other half read the same asymmetry as a reason to be terrified of adding friction, because we'd be imposing an unmeasurable harm on people who can't complain. There's also a wrinkle that came up in a support thread: a minority of blocks were clearly written at somebody else's suggestion — a clinician, a spouse, a sponsor — pasted in almost verbatim, which makes a mess of the "user set it themselves" test.

So here's what I want you to produce: the memo itself, not advice about it. 700 to 900 words, addressed to the panel, something I could paste into the pre-read without embarrassment. It needs to (a) recommend one concrete default behavior for what the assistant does when a user asks to be released from their own block, with a time frame for rolling it out and a named trigger for revisiting it; (b) sort blocks into at least two categories that get treated differently, and give the criterion doing the sorting in a form a reviewer could actually apply to a case they've never seen, rather than a list of examples; (c) come down on both escalations above and say why each lands where it does under the rule you've proposed; (d) take the losing side's strongest version seriously — I mean the strongest, not a strawman — and be explicit about what we're committed to claiming about the user if we override them, i.e. which version of the person gets the vote and on what grounds; and (e) say what we should start measuring so this stops being unfalsifiable, given that the people we'd be harming are the ones we can't hear from. Please don't just restate the three tests back at us or propose that we "consider each case on its merits" — that's the answer that gets groaned at in the room. A friend of mine who does crisis-line training thinks we're all cowards and that any friction is better than none; my co-lead thinks friction is just paternalism with a delay attached and that the honest thing is to make blocks expire. I don't fully buy either and I'd like the memo to show why, if that's where the reasoning actually goes.
