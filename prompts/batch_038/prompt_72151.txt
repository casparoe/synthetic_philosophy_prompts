The topic is the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until the first heads on toss n, and the payoff is 2^n, so the expectation diverges. Every response I have run into seems to work by installing a bound somewhere. Bound the utility function — and then face the reply that any unbounded utility function can be paired with a payoff schedule that grows fast enough to regenerate the divergence. Bound the bankroll — and the expectation collapses to roughly the base-2 log of the maximum payoutable amount, plus one. Or bound the number of plays and rank the game by long-run growth rate rather than by expectation. (I'm aware there is also a family of variants in which the expectation series converges only conditionally, so reordering the outcomes changes its value; that is not what I'm after, but the proposal should say why my question is different from it.)

My idea is that the bound everyone is looking for is not in the payoffs at all but in the information. No agent is handed the payoff distribution for free. A real decision-maker has to establish what the game is — how large the counterparty's bankroll actually is, whether the cap is real, whether the coin is fair — and establishing that costs time, money, and trust, with the cost growing as the parameters that make the game attractive grow. Concretely: suppose the casino can pay at most 2^L, and suppose confirming each doubling of that cap costs c units of due diligence, so c·L in total. The net value of playing becomes (1 − c)L + 1, which turns negative once c exceeds 1. The more interesting consequence is that as L grows, the game looks better on paper and simultaneously gets more expensive to know that it looks better — the evaluation cost scales with the very quantity that makes the expectation large.

A second, related hunch comes from simulation. If you Monte Carlo the uncapped game, the sample mean never settles; it drifts upward as you add draws, which is what a distribution with no finite mean looks like in practice. In that situation an engineer switches to a trimmed mean or a median, and those do converge. So: is there a principled normative analogue, a way of ranking such games by a tail-robust statistic instead of an expectation? And would that be a rival to expected-utility theory or a limiting case of it, given that for bounded utilities the trimmed statistics and the expectation eventually agree? I suspect the answer turns on whether the game is played once or repeatedly, which is where growth-rate reasoning re-enters.

The question I want sharpened: is there a version of the St. Petersburg problem that survives all the standard bounding moves because the cost sits in specifying and verifying the game rather than in paying it — and if so, does resolving it require amending expected-utility theory, or only supplying it with an account of costly information that it has always assumed away?
