I'm running the first meeting of a graduate reading group on iterated knowledge and want a decision tree that sorts participants by what they believe about the KK principle rather than by the camp they announce. Two anchors are printed at the top of the sheet: KK says that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p, which is to say that the condition of knowing p is luminous; and Williamson's anti-luminosity argument gets its result by pairing closure under known entailment with a margin-for-error premise that safety licenses — if you know that C obtains at one point in a series of closely similar cases, C still obtains at the next.

Build the interior of the tree from which commitment a person restricts or drops: the safety-grounded margin premise, closure, the assumption that someone who believes C in one case would still believe it in the next, or the sorites shape of the series itself. Then sort by whether the failure they admit is universal or confined to inexact, empirical conditions. Six to eight nodes, each a concrete case answerable without knowing where it leads — the idealized mathematician reflecting on her own proof, the person in agonizing pain, the knower with no route to discovering that she knows, one ascription issued at two conversational standards. Eight to twelve leaves, each tagged with its answer-combination and, where one exists, the position's standard name, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Four leaves are mandatory: KK restricted to an idealized, strong concept of knowledge; KK false while some phenomenal conditions stay luminous; KK false only because the two occurrences of knows are read at different standards; and a leaf where iterated knowledge survives even though KK as stated fails. Close by listing the combinations the tree rules out, saying for each whether the exclusion is logical or merely unpopulated, and by flagging any two leaves your questions cannot actually separate.
