Background, since it matters for what I'm asking: I spent 35 years at a state ag experiment station doing insect ecology and IPM trials, retired four years ago, and have fallen into philosophy since — mostly library books on ethics, mostly feeling like I'm reading a language I half know. In the spring I've been asked to run a session for our county master gardener crowd: about a dozen retired people with vegetable plots, plus one very confident fellow from the mosquito abatement district. I want to hand them a decision tree so that by the end each of them knows which position on pest-control ethics they actually hold — not what they'd call themselves, but what their own answers commit them to. Two of them will announce they're utilitarians and one keeps talking about character and virtue, and I suspect all three will land on leaves that don't match those labels, which is half the fun.

So: please play a professional philosopher who works in animal ethics or practical ethics and has also bothered to learn how pest control actually gets done in a field or a marsh. You'll need both halves. Think carefully before you start writing — this is harder than it looks.

Here's the angle I want the tree built around, and it comes from my end of things rather than the philosophy end. In practice there are at least three completely different levers, and nearly everything I've read treats only the first one as a real question:

1. Killing insects that are already here — pyrethroid knockdown, Bti dumped in the wetland, chitin-synthesis inhibitors that get the larvae at their next molt.
2. Stopping insects from ever existing — sterile male releases, the Wolbachia trick where the eggs just never hatch, pheromone mating disruption, draining the standing water so there's nowhere to lay. Nobody gets killed by these, because there's never anybody there to kill.
3. Making a place unusable without killing anyone in it — row covers, repellents, kaolin clay films, a sacrificial trap crop that pulls them off the good plants.

Lever 2 looks to me like it raises a genuinely different question from lever 1 (is a life that never starts a loss to anybody?), and lever 3 raises a third (is being shut out of a host plant a harm to the one shut out?). I want the tree to make people answer those seperately instead of collapsing everything into "are you for or against spraying."

Beyond that I'd like nodes on: how much confidence in insect sentience is enough, and what happens when you multiply a small probability by enormous numbers; whether the target pest and the bycatch are the same kind of question (the swallows' caterpillars, the midges in the ditch); whether it matters how much life the individual had left (an adult moth with five days versus a larva with three weeks); whether value sits in individuals or in the community; and whether refusing to treat here just moves the deaths to somebody else's field somewhere else. Add whatever I've missed and drop whatever you think is decorative.

What I want back:

- Start with brainstorming, not the tree. List a dozen or so candidate branch points, say which you're keeping and why, and which you cut and why. I specifically want to see the cut ones.
- Six to eight nodes, ten to fourteen leaves.
- Every node phrased as a question a gardener could answer without knowing any philosophy jargon or any entomology jargon. No node may contain the name of a position or end in "-ism." If answering a node requires a fact, put the fact in the question in one clause.
- Every leaf gets a name if there's a recognizable one and "unnamed" if there isn't, plus one sentence on the most awkward commitment the person standing there has to live with.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate — where two different answer patterns land in the same place, or one pattern could honestly go either way. Don't paper over it.
- Note the answer-combinations that are logically available but that no leaf occupies, and say in a line each whether that's because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has gotten around to it. I'd bet lever 2 combined with strong sentience and huge numbers has something interesting sitting in it.
- Tell me straight if one of my three levers isn't a distinction anyone should care about, or if I've described a mechanism wrong. I'd rather be corrected than flattered.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I can't chase them down and I don't want invented ones. Names of positions are fine, and welcome.
