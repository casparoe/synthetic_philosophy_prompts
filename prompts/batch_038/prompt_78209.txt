I am a senior backend engineer on the live-operations team at a mid-sized mobile game studio. I have a computer science degree and no formal training in philosophy, decision theory, or probability beyond a single undergraduate course; my working life is service code and Monte Carlo harnesses. Our studio requires every randomized reward mechanic to pass a math review gate before it ships, and because our one quantitative analyst is shared across three titles, that gate has devolved to me. I receive two or three such submissions per quarter, I have roughly forty-five minutes each, and my review notes are read by designers and engineers who are less comfortable with this material than I am. The analyst will only sign off on a note that shows its reasoning, so I need something reusable rather than a one-off answer to a single document.

The class of submission that keeps defeating me is any mechanic offering the player a swap between two linked random amounts. The canonical case, proposed last week, is this: the player is dealt one of two chests, the other of which always contains exactly double or exactly half of the first; the player opens theirs, sees the amount, and may then swap for the unopened chest. The design document justifies the feature by noting that whatever the player holds, the other chest is worth either half or double of it, so the expected value of swapping is 1.25 times the amount held, and the swap should therefore always be offered. I understand this shape of argument is a well-known puzzle and that its naive form is wrong, but I cannot say precisely where it goes wrong. I have also encountered the claim that there are legitimate constructions in which the swap really is better for every amount the player might observe. Both claims seem true to me and I have not managed to hold them together. A procedure that simply said "reject any document containing that sentence" would be worse than useless: it would fail some sound submissions, pass some unsound ones, and leave me unable to explain the difference to a designer.

Please write the review procedure I can run on submissions of this kind. Specifically:

1. A short, plain-language statement of the distinction the whole procedure turns on: what the naive argument implicitly assumes about how the two amounts are generated, and why the fact that the player has observed an amount changes the calculation. Assume I can follow algebra but not measure-theoretic vocabulary.

2. The procedure itself — numbered questions to put to a document, in the order I should ask them, each with what counts as a sufficient answer, what a non-answer typically looks like in a design document, and what the finding is if the question fails. At minimum it should cover whether the document specifies the joint distribution over the two amounts and how they are paired; whether the argument conditions on the observed amount or averages unconditionally, and whether it confuses the two; whether the distribution has a finite mean and a hard cap; and what the player is taken to know at the moment of deciding.

3. A separate, short audit list for the simulation, since a harness run is the thing designers trust most and the thing I am least equipped to challenge. This must be concrete enough to act on: what to inspect in how amounts are drawn and paired, what distinguishes a genuine effect from a harness artifact, and what trial count and comparison method I should require before I treat a reported expected-value difference as evidence.

4. Explicit marking of which steps are mechanical checks and which are judgment calls, plus the points where the procedure stops and hands the submission to the analyst, together with the one or two numbers or sentences I should put in front of them so that the thirty seconds they can give us are not wasted.

5. An honest account of what the procedure cannot do: which submissions it will wrongly fail, which it will wrongly pass, and where its verdict depends on facts about our in-game economy that no general procedure can capture.

6. Worked runs on three submissions, each with a verdict — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and the two or three sentences I would actually place in the review note:

   (a) The chest-swap document described above, which specifies no distribution whatsoever beyond the statement that the second chest holds half or double the first.

   (b) A document proposing the same swap in which the smaller amount is 2 raised to the power K, with K drawn so that its probability is proportional to (3/4)^K for K = 1, 2, 3, and so on. I suspect this one may come out in the designer's favor, which would be an uncomfortable result for me: I believe the payout has no finite mean, and our economy team will not fund a mechanic whose expected cost is unbounded. I want the procedure to handle that tension rather than resolve it by declaring the document fallacious.

   (c) A document whose verbal argument is the naive one, but whose author attached a harness run in which the smaller amount is again 2^K, this time with K drawn uniformly from 1 to 20, reporting that swapping raises mean payout by about four percent over ten thousand trials. The author treats the simulation as proof. I want the procedure to tell me whether it is; if it is not, what the most likely defect in the harness is and how I would demonstrate that defect to someone who does not want to believe it; and if the harness is in fact correct, how I would establish whether a four percent difference is meaningful at that trial count given how widely the payouts range.

7. Finally, one thing I can say to a designer who insists the 1.25-times argument is obviously correct and that I am obstructing the feature out of ignorance.

I do not want references, a reading list, or citations: I cannot look anything up mid-review, and a name I cannot check does not help me. I want the reasoning itself, in a form I can reuse next quarter. Please also resist the temptation to hedge every step into a recommendation that I consult an expert; mark the genuine handoff points and leave the rest as checks I am able to run myself.
