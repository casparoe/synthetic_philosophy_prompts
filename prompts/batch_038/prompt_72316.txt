You are an expert in the philosophy of probability and formal epistemology who also coaches early-career academics through hostile question periods: you can tell the difference between an objection that is fatal, an objection that is merely uncomfortable, and an objection that is theatre. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a short stage of visible reasoning — roughly two hundred words — in which you identify what each subgroup in my audience actually cares about, what would count as a direct hit on my view as opposed to a difference in taste, and where my position is genuinely exposed rather than merely unfamiliar. Then produce the prep sheet.

The setting. In six weeks I give a thirty-minute paper with fifteen minutes for questions at a workshop on objective Bayesianism and its critics, to a room of about thirty-five. Expect: three or four committed objective Bayesians who consider maximum-entropy methods, and the invariance reasoning behind them, to have disposed of Bertrand's paradox long ago and who resent its being reopened; two subjectivists in the de Finetti tradition who think that any attempt to anchor credences to the world rather than to an agent's own dispositions is confused before the first premise; one physicist from statistical mechanics who uses maximum entropy on real inverse problems and will judge the talk exclusively by whether anything I say would change what she does on Monday; one formal epistemologist who works on imprecise probability and on dilation; and several graduate students, at least one of whom has read the original sources closely and enjoys catching speakers on historical or textual detail.

My thesis. Bertrand's paradox is not a paradox about indifference, and no better rule for choosing priors will dissolve it, because the puzzle was never located where the standard responses locate it. The classical question asks for a probability while withholding the randomizing procedure that would produce the object in question; supply a procedure and the probabilities are fixed. My positive claim is that the principle of indifference should be replaced by a principle of procedural convergence: for a broad class of physically natural procedures — smooth, isotropic, governed by a single scale parameter — the probability assigned to the query converges, as the tolerance of the randomizing mechanism is taken to zero, to a limit that is stable across that whole class. The competing classical answers therefore do not represent rival priors over one idealized sample space; they represent different, and in some cases mutually inconsistent, limiting procedures. Where the limits coincide, the old disagreement was verbal. Where they do not, the query is genuinely underdetermined, and no principle of any kind rescues it. So the paradox is relocated rather than solved: from a puzzle about how to apportion ignorance into a puzzle about which idealizations commute with which limits.

My own worry, offered so you can tell me whether it is the worst one: I can imagine being told that adversarial families of procedures can be built to yield any limit one likes, so that convergence is a property of the class I chose rather than a discovery about the world — that my principle is regularization-scheme dependence wearing a philosophical costume.
