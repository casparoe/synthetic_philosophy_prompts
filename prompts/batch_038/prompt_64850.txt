Here's the chain. It's my reconstruction of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem plus some steps I added myself, and the conclusion is something I definately don't believe, so something is wrong — but every step looks fine to me when I stare at it.

1. Each person's preferences over uncertain prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each person's preferences can be represented by a utility function that's unique up to a positive affine transformation — you can rescale it and shift it, and nothing else.
2. Society also has preferences over prospects that satisfy the same axioms, so society's preferences get represented by a utility function too.
3. Pareto indifference: whenever every person is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent between them as well.
4. From 1–3 (the theorem): society's utility is a weighted sum of the individual utilities, W = a + w₁u₁ + w₂u₂ + …, where the a and the weights are just fixed numbers.
5. Now the veil step: take an impartial observer who doesn't know whose life they'll live and has an equal chance of living each of them, and who satisfies the same axioms. Their ranking is then the weighted sum with all the weights equal. So you get total/average utilitarianism out of nothing but rationality plus unanimity — no moral premise anywhere.
6. (mine) Nothing in 1–5 depends on how many people there are. A person who doesn't exist just doesn't show up in the sum, which is the same thing as contributing zero to it.
7. (mine) So bringing someone into existence whose utility is above zero raises the sum, and by 5 that's better. Keep going and you get: lots of people with barely-worth-living lives beats a smaller number with wonderful lives, whenever the total is bigger.
8. (mine) And by exactly the same arithmetic, removing one person and spreading what they had across five others is better whenever the five gains outweigh the one loss. So the chain licenses taking organs from one healthy person to save five, it licenses the billions-of-miserable-ish-people world, and it says a world where three people each have 10 units is precisely as good as a world where one has 28 and two have 1 each, since both sum to 30.
