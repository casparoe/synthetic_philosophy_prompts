I am the sole instructor for a fourth-year interdisciplinary seminar jointly listed between our philosophy and anthropology departments, informally titled "The Neolithic and the Good Life." There are twenty-two students with uneven preparation: roughly half have had a course in formal logic and some normative ethics, half have had archaeological method but almost no philosophy, and exactly two have read any population ethics. The reading list is the standard terrain — Sahlins on foraging societies as affluent in the sense of limited wants rather than abundant means, Woodburn's contrast between immediate-return and deferred-return economies, the palaeopathological literature on falling stature, dental disease and infectious load in early farming villages, Diamond's widely anthologized essay arguing that agriculture was the worst mistake in human history, and the more recent political-archaeological work on grain, legibility, taxation and the earliest states. Last year's final posed a broad comparative question, and I marked twenty-two essays that were almost pure recitation: fluent summaries of the two camps, no analysis, and a ritual gesture at "it's complicated." This year I want a question that cannot be answered that way. To force the issue I have written an argument of my own, which will be printed as a handout on the front of the exam paper; it appears nowhere in the literature, so no student can have met it before, and no amount of memorised archaeology will dispose of it. My co-examiner worries this is unfair, but I think the unfamiliarity is the entire point: I want to see whether they can read an argument rather than recognise a topic.

Here is the handout argument. I will call it the Generous Neolithic argument, and I want it presented to the students exactly as it stands, numbered as follows.

1. Any verdict that a large-scale change in how a people lived was a mistake must be comparative: the change was a mistake for those affected only if they were worse off than they would have been had the change not occurred.
2. Those affected by the agricultural transition are the inhabitants of the earliest farming villages and everyone descended from them — which is to say, all of us.
3. Had the transition not occurred, none of those people would have existed, since the population sizes that made each of them possible depended on a food supply that foraging could not have produced in those places.
4. For a person whose life is worth living, existing is better than never having existed.
5. So the people affected by the agricultural transition were better off than they would have been had it not occurred.
6. The transition also multiplied by orders of magnitude the total number of lives worth living, and no other feature of a change — not health, not stature, not leisure, not equality, not freedom from taxation or from war — can outweigh the addition of that many worthwhile lives.
7. Therefore the agricultural transition was not a mistake. It was among the best things that happened to our species.
8. And the reasoning generalises: any change that raises carrying capacity and thereby multiplies the number of worthwhile lives is an improvement, whatever it does to average welfare or to the distribution of that welfare.

I am confident that (8) is unacceptable, and I suspect the argument cannot survive its removal intact, but I do not want the question to hand students any of that. The deliverable I need from you is a complete, usable examination item, and I would like you to approach it as an experienced examiner who is equally at home in population ethics and in the archaeological record. Before you draft anything, think carefully and set out, in a few hundred words of working notes, the full range of things a strong candidate could do with this argument: whether it is valid as written or whether some step fails to follow; which premises are contested by whom; where the person-affecting and impersonal frameworks are each being invoked and whether the argument is entitled to use both; what the empirical record does and does not settle about the counterfactual in (3), including the possibility that population pressure preceded rather than followed intensification, that the transition happened repeatedly and independently, that it was reversible for any given community, and that its costs were borne very unevenly within villages rather than evenly across them; and which of these many possible objections is the single crux on which the question should turn. Say explicitly which crux you have chosen and why it is the one that best separates students who understand the argument from students who merely dislike its conclusion.

Then produce the following, in this order. First, the exam question as it will actually appear on the paper: the instructions students see, the marks available (25), the time allowed (75 minutes, in an unseen three-hour paper on which they answer two questions), and whatever framing sentence or two is needed to tell them what is being asked without telling them where the flaw is. Second, a model answer of approximately 1,200 words, written at the standard of the best answer achievable by an excellent undergraduate under those time constraints — not a publishable paper. It must be self-contained: students may not consult sources during the exam, so the answer should cite nothing, quote nothing, and rely on no specific fact a candidate could not have got from the course list. It should engage seriously with the strongest version of the argument rather than knocking over a straw man, and it should end by saying what is left of the claim that agriculture was a mistake once the diagnosis is in. Third, a marking scheme built around six named must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth summing to 25, and for each move a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it — for instance by objecting to the conclusion instead of the inference, or by treating the archaeological evidence as though it could settle a question about counterfactual comparison, or by invoking the repugnant conclusion as a label without saying why it bites here. Fourth, a short set of grade-boundary descriptions saying concretely what a 22/25 answer contains that a 16/25 answer lacks, what a 10/25 answer looks like, and a standing instruction to markers on what earns no marks at all — in particular, that rehearsing the pro and con literature about foraging affluence and Neolithic health, however accurately, should score nothing on its own.
