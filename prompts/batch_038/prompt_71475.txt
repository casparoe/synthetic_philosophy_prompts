I am a palliative care nurse with a long-standing philosophy habit, and for the past year a small reading group of mine has been working through the question of whether death is bad for the person who dies. We keep colliding with the Epicurean move: harm requires a subject, and by the time death arrives there is no subject left to be harmed — a line that seems to be reinforced by the symmetry with the time before we were born, which nobody counts as a misfortune. I am not convinced, but I cannot extract a clean principle from my own reactions, and our last meeting ended with me asserting things I could not defend. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on the metaphysics and ethics of death and who is willing to take an amateur's untidy intuitions seriously: think the cases through carefully before you reply, but keep that working to yourself and give me only what I request at the end. I do not want references, a reading list, or the names of papers or philosophers — I want principles I can argue with.

Here are my verdicts. A healthy twenty-four-year-old killed instantly in her sleep by an aneurysm: her death is a grave harm to her, even though nothing feels bad to her and she never knows it happened. A ninety-year-old who dies in her sleep having outlived everyone she loved and finished everything she cared about: not a harm, or at most a negligible one. The stretch of time before I was born, during which I likewise did not exist: not a harm to me at all, and I feel the difference from the twenty-four-year-old's case very sharply even though the two look like mirror images. A cellist who loses her hearing and, through the same injury, loses all interest in music, so that she minds nothing at all: her life is genuinely worse, and her not minding does not repair it. A dead man whose enemies stage an elaborate hoax that makes him a celebrity for the very politics he spent his life despising — a hoax he would have been devastated to learn of, and which no one in his family ever corrects: I want to say his life is not made worse by this, which I recognize sits badly with the cellist. Lies told about me after my death that ruin my reputation: bad for me. The same lies told about me while I am alive but sealed off so that they never reach my ears: also bad for me, though somehow a shade less bad. My ashes scattered somewhere I would mildly have preferred to avoid, in a way that hurts nobody involved: not bad for me. And the one I keep flip-flopping on: whether the eventual sterilization of the earth as the sun brightens, which erases every trace and every memory of my life, is a harm to me — it feels like the final undoing of everything I did and also like something far too remote to touch me.

My own attempt at a principle, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is this: call it the Presence Condition, on which an event is bad for a person only if there is some time at which the person exists and is in a worse condition than she would otherwise have been, except that I am willing to count the worse condition as a property of the person's life taken as a whole rather than of any moment within it, so that things can go badly for a life even after the person is gone, provided they land on something that is still there to be landed on. Tell me whether this condition, or some repaired version of it, generates every verdict above; where exactly it fails; and in particular how it is meant to break the symmetry between the twenty-four-year-old and the time before I was born, and how it is meant to separate the cellist from the hoax victim. Now reply with exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no restatement of my cases. Items one through three should each be one candidate principle (mine as repaired, or a rival), each naming the case that motivates it and giving its verdicts on the aneurysm/prenatal pair and on the cellist/hoax pair. Item four should give the verdicts of the principle you favor on two further cases of your own choosing, cases that discriminate between the three candidates. Item five should state plainly which of my verdicts that principle cannot save and why I ought to give it up. Each item should run to no more than three sentences.
