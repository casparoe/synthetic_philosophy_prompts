ok so this has been rattling around in my head for like six months and I want to see if someone can make something of it. I run a small computer repair shop and my brother in law is one of those people who brings up Gödel every single Thanksgiving to prove that machines will never have minds — the whole "if it was a formal system it couldn't see the truth of its own Gödel sentence, and we can see ours, so we're not machines" speech. I went down the wikipedia rabbit hole to be able to argue back and honestly I ended up more interested in how badly the theorem gets used than in winning the argument. What I want you to write is a parody of an official government application form — the kind with section headers, checkboxes, and tiny-print footnotes — for a machine applying to be legally recognised as a non-mechanical mind. The premise is that some office somewhere actually tried to turn that line of thinking into paperwork. Around 800 words. Deadpan bureaucratic voice all the way through, no winking at the reader, and it should never state the philosophical objections out loud — they should just be built into the structure of the form, so that the form turns out to be impossible for anything to complete.

Things I'd love in there if you can make them work (don't force all of them, but the technical bits have to be right, because the point is that someone who knows the theorems reads it and goes "yeah, that's exactly it"):

- Eligibility requires the applicant to be effectively axiomatized / recursively enumerable AND to contain enough arithmetic to encode statements about its own syntax — and the trap is that meeting both is precisely what makes the first theorem apply.
- A self-certification step where the applicant attests to its own consistency, with fine print about why a strong consistent system can't do that, followed by an alternative subsection for having a qualified outside reviewer certify it instead, which then needs its own certification, all the way up.
- The centerpiece demonstration: the applicant must output a sentence that is true but unprovable for itself, and then answer a form question justifying its use of the word "true" there, given that truth-rather-than-mere-unprovability seems to require already assuming its own consistency.
- A section for applicants who tried to patch themselves by adding their own Gödel sentence as a new axiom, with a blank field for "number of iterations completed" and a note about what happens when that number doesn't stop.
- A "frequently denied applications" sidebar: things too weak to do any arithmetic, things that are inconsistent or paraconsistent, and things that aren't recursively enumerable and therefore get routed to a seperate office entirely.
- A fee schedule and an appeals process in which the appeals board is subject to exactly the same eligibility rules as the applicant.

No citations, no real papers, no real people — invent the office, the form number, the enabling statute, all of it. I want it to work on the surface for someone who's never taken a logic course (bureaucracy, catch-22s, nobody reads the fine print) and to land harder for someone who has. And if there's one checkbox on the whole form that no applicant can tick without making itself ineligible, that's the box I want people to remember.
