I am a junior at a public high school in central Ohio, and I run our school's history podcast, which in practice means two friends and I recording in a library study room during third period. Our district's humanities lecture series is bringing in a professor next Thursday evening, and my faculty adviser has asked me to record a twenty-minute interview with her that will run as the episode the following week. She is an economic historian who works self-consciously in the tradition of the Annales school and of Braudel's long durée: on her account, the only things genuinely worth explaining are the slow structures, and the wars, treaties, and celebrated individuals that make up almost everything my classmates think history is about belong to a layer of surface noise. What makes her position worth interviewing rather than merely summarizing is a twist I would like the questions to press on directly. She argues that the operative long-run structures are not physical geography as such but inherited regimes of taxation and land tenure, and that those regimes persist not because they are optimal or because anyone wills them but because renegotiating them is prohibitively expensive for everyone involved. Climate then enters her story in an unusual way: not as a cause of events, but as a limit on how much slack a society has for renegotiation, with harvest yields and grain prices setting the terms on which the fiscal and property order can be reopened. She has also written that individuals matter, but only inside a structure that has already settled what kinds of individuals can matter.

Please draft eight to ten questions for that interview, keyed to the content of her position rather than to her career; I do not want anything of the "what first drew you to this field" variety, and I would rather not ask her to explain the Annales school to listeners, since the episode will open with my own three-sentence introduction. At minimum, I want one question on each of the following, and you may add others that you think belong: whether her claim about the long durée is a claim about causation, about explanation, or about periodization, and how she keeps those three apart; the worry that slow structures get defined negatively, as whatever is left standing once events have been subtracted, which would make them impossible to identify independently of the events they are supposed to explain; how she answers the objection that every structure she describes was itself produced by some earlier happening — a plague, a conquest, a run of failed harvests — so that events look prior in the order of becoming even if structures are prior in the order of explanation; where a single catastrophic harvest season, or twenty cold summers in a row, sits in her scheme, since it seems to be both a shock and a structure; the charge that this style of explanation drifts toward geographic or climatic determinism and has historically been enlisted to treat regional inequality as a fact of nature rather than a result anyone is responsible for; and a closing question about what evidence she would accept as showing that a particular event really did move the structural level instead of merely exposing what was already there.

For every question, give me the answer she is most likely to give and then one follow-up that only makes sense in light of that answer. I expect her to reach more than once for the remark that individuals matter only within a structure that has already decided which individuals can matter, so please order the questions such that that reply becomes progressively less useful to her as the interview goes on, and make the final question one she cannot answer with it at all. Tell me also which two questions to cut if she talks long and we run out of time, and flag anywhere that I have misstated her view or built a question on a false premise — I have read only one of her essays and a handout my teacher prepared on Braudel's three time-scales in his study of the Mediterranean under Philip II, so I would much rather be corrected now than on the recording.
