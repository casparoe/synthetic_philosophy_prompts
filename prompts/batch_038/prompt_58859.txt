I chair an independent panel that has spent seven years choosing a site for a new secondary school in a rural English county. On Thursday I open the public meeting at which we announce our recommendation, and I want you to write those opening remarks for me: five minutes, 700 to 800 words, in British English.

The facts the speech must respect:

- Our technical appraisal scored the two shortlisted sites — the former goods yard on Vale Road and the Halloway Fields site — level on the weighted matrix, four hundredths of a point apart out of a hundred. Our own surveyor has confirmed that this is well inside the uncertainty of the underlying estimates. He will be sitting in the second row with the matrix in front of him, next to the losing parish's consultant.
- We nevertheless recommend one site, and it is Vale Road.
- The room holds parents from both catchments, a reporter from the county paper, the surveyor, and one independent member: a retired professor of economics who argued at the last meeting that if an appraisal cannot separate two options, the honest procedure is a public lottery and anything else is post-hoc rationalisation dressed up as a reason.

What the speech must not do: it must not claim a consideration the matrix does not contain; it must not describe the decision as arbitrary, random, or a coin toss; it must not say that in the end it makes no difference which site is chosen, because seven years of other people's evenings went into proving that it does; it must not name a single philosopher, book, or paper aloud — the ideas have to arrive without their labels. It must contain no quotations from anyone, living or dead: I am not going to quote at a planning meeting, and I have no means of checking wording on my feet.

Background you may draw on, at most twice, briefly and accurately, and never as decoration:

- Aristotle raised the figure of a man placed exactly between food and drink, as hungry as he is thirsty, in an argument about why the earth stays where it is. It began as a puzzle about balanced physical forces, not about people.
- Al-Ghazali put the matter in terms of two similar dates in front of a hungry man who cannot take both, and held that the will carries in it something whose nature is to differentiate between two similar things. Averroes, commenting on him, took the other side.
- Leibniz denied that a perfect equipoise is ever genuinely instantiated, appealing to minute perceptions below the threshold of consciousness.
- Spinoza was prepared to concede that a man so placed would die of hunger and thirst, and then asked whether such a man should be counted an ass rather than a man.
- The ass is named for Buridan, though it is disputed whether Buridan ever set the case out himself. The view the story satirises was his: where two courses are judged equal the will cannot break the deadlock and can only suspend judgement until circumstances change and the right course becomes clear. That last point matters more to me than any of the others, because suspension is precisely what this panel has been forbidden — the capital funding window closes at the end of March and the land must be secured before then.
- For your information only, and not for the room: the distinction I have in mind at crux three below is the one Ullmann-Margalit and Morgenbesser drew between picking and choosing in a 1977 paper.

The cruxes I want worked through rather than written around:

1. There are at least three different things our tie might be, and the speech must not slide between them: the world is symmetric between the two sites; or our instrument cannot resolve a difference that is genuinely there; or the difference, though real, makes no difference to anyone's reasons. Commit to one. Expect the economist to say you are claiming the second while writing as though you had the first.
2. The symmetry is an artefact of our own scoring matrix, whose weights the cabinet fixed by vote four years ago; in a sense the decision was taken then and Thursday's announcement is arithmetic. You may concede this — but you must then face the obvious reply, that this merely relocates the deadlock one level up, and you must stop that regress somewhere honest instead of implying it has dissolved.
3. There is a difference between choosing on the basis of a preference and picking where preference runs out, and this panel picked. Say so plainly. Then do not let picking sound like a shrug: say what governs a pick that has no preference behind it. I suspect the honest answer concerns how the pick was made, whether it can be stated in public without embarrassment, and whether it can be reopened — but argue it, do not assert it, and keep it out of lecture register.
4. Answer the lottery proposal in the room, in one or two sentences a fourteen-year-old could follow. You may concede that a draw would be more candid about the tie. You may not concede that our recommendation is therefore indistinguishable from a draw.

The part I care about most: somewhere in the last third, the speech must turn to the Halloway Fields families directly. It cannot offer them the consolation that their case was weaker, because it was not, and they know it. Whatever it offers instead must be true, must be something a person can actually hold on to the following morning, and must not be a promise about the future that the council has no power to keep.

Tone and form: plain spoken English, short sentences, no rhetorical questions in the final minute, no applause lines, no invocation of community spirit, one moment of dry self-deprecating humour early about how long we have taken. Announce the recommendation within the first ninety seconds — these people have waited seven years, and burying it would read as shame. Everything after that must resist the pull to justify it. Continuous prose, no headings, no stage directions, no bracketed notes beyond at most two pauses marked in square brackets.

After the speech, add no more than a hundred and twenty words telling me which of the three kinds of tie you have the panel claiming, and what your two sentences to the economist actually do, so that I can tell whether you have engaged with the problem or composed carefully around it.
