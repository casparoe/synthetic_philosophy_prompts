I'm working up the claim that the metaphysics of dispositions ought to be answerable to materials engineering, and before I argue for it I want the target spec written down. So: what does an account of dispositional properties actually have to deliver? Give me the requirements list — the cases it must handle (finks, masks, the mimic cases, the glass that's fragile but never gets dropped), the distinctions it cant collapse (disposition vs. its categorical base, multi-track vs. single-track, disposition vs. mere capacity), with the consideration behind each one, and mark which requirements are load-bearing and which are just one camp's prejudice (intrinsicness? the demand for a conditional analysis?). Say who the theory is supposed to be adequate *for*, since I want it usable by someone doing failure analysis as well as by a metaphysician.

The part I care about most is the stuff that usually gets skipped. Engineers ascribe properties to batches as distributions — Charpy impact energy at a given temperature comes with a scatter band, and a coupon that lands below spec is still the same alloy — and they deliberately build interruptions in: a shear pin or a fuse is a designed fink on the assembly's disposition to carry load or current, a coating is a standing mask on the metal's disposition to corrode. Does an adequate theory have to make sense of population-level ascription, and of components whose whole job is to be finked or masked? Tell me which single requirement rules out the most candidates, and if some subset of the list can't be met all at once, say which and why — I'd rather find that out now.
