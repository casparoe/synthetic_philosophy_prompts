I am drafting a paper for a workshop on Victorian moral philosophy and want comments on a condensed version of the argument before I expand it. Section numbers are mine; the whole thing is currently about 2,500 words and what follows is the skeleton.

**Title: The Missing Evidence: Sidgwick's Psychical Research as Unfinished Business in *The Methods of Ethics***

1. Sidgwick is normally split in two. There is the philosopher of *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), the cool reconstructer of utilitarian and intuitionist reasoning, and there is the co-founder and first president of the Society for Psychical Research (February 1882), an office he held longer than anyone else. Biographical treatments tend to file the second under temperament: a melancholy man looking for consolation after losing orthodox belief. The philosophy and the séances are quarantined from one another, and the quarantine is rarely argued for.

2. My claim is that the quarantine is untenable, and that the hinge is the deepest structural problem in the *Methods*. Sidgwick finds that egoistic and universalist practical reason each issue demands he cannot dismiss, and he cannot construct an argument internal to ethics that settles the conflict; he calls this a dualism of the practical reason. He also suggests that the contradiction can be avoided only by supposing a moral governance of the universe that guarantees a coincidence, at least in the long run, between doing one's duty and one's own happiness — a supposition he treats as a hypothesis he is unable to establish. But for an empiricist of Sidgwick's stripe, inability to establish a hypothesis a priori is not evidence that it is false. It marks the hypothesis as one on which evidence, if any were available, would be decisive.

3. And what evidence would be decisive? Evidence that personal agency survives bodily death, and that it retains moral character and interest in the living. That is exactly what Sidgwick said he hoped psychical research might supply. So the SPR programme is on my reading not a diversion from his ethics but its empirical wing: the dualism generates an evidential demand, and the Society is the institutional attempt to meet it by the only means Sidgwick considered legitimate — collected testimony, controlled experiment, statistical compilation, and the remorseless elimination of fraud. This gives his methodological obsessiveness a philosophical explanation. Consider the thought-transference experiments with the Creery sisters, among the Society's first projects, which collapsed in 1888 when two of the girls were found to be signalling to one another: that is not a debunker's triumph but the epistemics of a man who needed the evidence to be good, because a bad result left him with an unresolved dualism.

4. The counter-reading, stated as strongly as I can. Sidgwick's published position as a psychical researcher is deflationary: he came to accept telepathy but would not treat mediumistic phenomena as proof of survival, and he died in 1900 without claiming the evidence he wanted. Perhaps, then, his ethics never needed the SPR. Later utilitarians dissolve the dualism from inside ethics, by denying that prudence has the authority Sidgwick grants it; and even if the dualism stands, it may be a systematic ideal rather than a premise of moral practice, so that ordinary agents can act without resolving it. On this reading the Society is biographical residue — anxiety management kept in a separate ledger from philosophy.

5. What I think distinguishes my thesis from the familiar remark that Sidgwick wanted a God: it explains a methodological choice rather than a mood. A private religious need could have been met by theology or by reflection. It could not explain decades spent founding committees, designing controls, and presiding over fraud trials.

My requests: (a) accuracy — is my characterization of the dualism and of what Sidgwick says about it fair, and are the SPR details right as stated? Flag anything overstated or misdated. (b) interest — does section 5 really separate my thesis from the standard Sidgwick-needs-a-postulate line, or is the distinction cosmetic? (c) does section 4 give the quarantine reading its best case, and what have I left out of it? (d) what is the strongest objection to section 3 that I have not answered? (e) structurally, is this better argued as a paper about the dualism with the SPR as evidence, or as a case study in the history of evidence with the *Methods* as backdrop?

Please separate explicitly what is settled among Sidgwick scholars and historians of psychical research from what is genuinely contested, and where my sections 2 and 5 state an interpretive reconstruction rather than a position Sidgwick himself asserts, say so plainly rather than letting it pass as reportage. I would also rather you mark your confidence: if you are sure one of my historical claims is wrong, say it is wrong; if the interpretive question has no agreed answer, say that instead of presenting one reading as the consensus.
