I have spent something like fifteen years reading arguments about whether voting is rational, mostly on blogs and forums of the heavily quantified, expected-value-obsessed variety, and I have come to think the standard exchange has ossified. One side observes that the probability of a single vote being decisive in a national election is somewhere between negligible and imaginary, and concludes that voting is theatre performed for the benefit of one's own self-image; the other replies with an appeal to duty, or to the expressive content of the act, or to some universalising move about what would happen if everybody stayed at home. Both sides know their lines. Neither ever concedes anything. I should like to see the argument run somewhere less worn, and I have a specific stretch of ground in mind.

**The request.** Write a dialogue, of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, between two voters standing in the queue outside a polling station in a safe English constituency on the day of a general election. Both are in their fifties; both have voted for the same centre-left party at every election for twenty-odd years. One — call her the Loyalist — intends to do so again today. The other — the Striker — has decided that this time she will not: she will either vote for a small party that cannot conceivably take the seat, or spoil her ballot paper, and she has done so because over three successive cycles the party has moved steadily towards the position of its main rival on a policy that both women regard as a serious and ongoing wrong. Nothing about the seat is in doubt. The Loyalist's candidate will win by nine thousand votes.

**The Striker's case**, which I want developed rather than merely asserted, is one I have never seen argued properly, so I will sketch it and leave you to build it out. Call it the turnout ratchet. The Striker's claim is not that her vote is decisive — she agrees it is not, and thinks the Loyalist would be foolish to pretend otherwise. Her claim is that the *unconditionality* of her support is itself the party's most valuable asset. A party that can count on a certain block of voters turning out for it regardless of what it does can spend that certainty: it can drift towards the rival's position knowing the drift costs it nothing, because the alternative for its supporters is the rival. Every time the Loyalist holds her nose and votes against the worse option, she lowers the price the party pays for becoming worse. The ratchet turns one way only, and it turns because people like the Loyalist keep feeding it. What she calls loyalty, the Striker calls an unconditional transfer of bargaining power, and she thinks the transfer — not the drift — is what makes the drift possible.

**The Loyalist's reply** should be at least as strong, and I want it made on two levels rather than one.

Empirically: she should press the Striker on the mechanism. Abstention is invisible and unattributable; a party that sheds four per cent in the safe seats cannot tell whether it has been disciplined by its own loyalists or has simply had an off day in the weather, and a signal nobody can decode changes nobody's behaviour. She should force the Striker to choose between abstaining and voting for the small party, since only the second produces a countable number, and then argue that the countable number is itself absorbed: it is reported as a protest, folded into a narrative about disaffected voters the leadership must win back, and spent by the party on its own terms rather than the striker's. She should also point out that the seat is safe only on the current boundaries and the current national picture, and that a strategy whose safety depends on the seat staying safe is a bet, not a principle.

Morally: she should object to who pays for the strike. The costs of three or four more cycles of drift fall on the people the policy harms, not on the striker, who keeps her hands clean and her conscience intact at somebody else's expense. A strategy whose burdens are borne by third parties and whose benefits are deferred and speculative needs a far heavier justification than one whose costs you carry yourself. Here I want the duty-and-expression strand to do real work rather than being waved at: she should say plainly that she does not believe her vote is decisive, and does not need it to be, because she does not think the value of voting is exhausted by its causal contribution — that registering opposition to the worse option is part of what it is to stand in the right relation to it, and that the Striker's fastidiousness is itself an expressive act, performed for an audience of one.

**Craft requirements.** Neither character should be a strawman, and neither should win by being better informed. They should respond to what the other has actually just said — I want interruptions, concessions, a moment where one of them notices that an objection she has made applies with equal force to her own position, since I think that is exactly where this argument gets interesting. The Striker in particular should have to face the symmetry that a strike only disciplines a party if enough people strike, and that each striker reasons her own contribution is negligible, which is precisely the reasoning she condemns in the Loyalist. Do not resolve the dialogue: end it with the queue moving and the two of them going in to vote, each having shifted position on at most one point. Keep the turns short enough to sound like two people talking in the cold rather than two pamphlets taking turns. If either character leans on a philosopher, let it be by idea and not by name. Please write in British English, using British electoral vocabulary throughout.

**One last thing, and I would ask you to take it seriously.** After the dialogue, add a short note, out of character, of perhaps two hundred words, covering: which of the two cases you found harder to write against, and why; the single point at which each character is most likely to be mistaken; what finding or argument — empirical or philosophical — would change your own verdict on whether the turnout ratchet can ever make withholding one's vote obligatory rather than merely permissible; and where the dialogue has taken liberties, in particular any empirical claim about how parties respond to lost turnout that you have assumed for the sake of the scene but which is genuinely contestable, or any reading of the duty-and-expression strand that a defender of it might reject as too tidy.
