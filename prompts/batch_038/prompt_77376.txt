**Three things a Frankfurt case can close off**

Start with the original setup. Black wants Jones to steal a car and is prepared to make sure he does; if Jones shows any sign of wavering, Black steps in and steers him back onto the theft. In the actual sequence, Jones shows no such sign, steals the car on his own, and Black never lifts a finger. Frankfurt's point: Jones could not have done otherwise, and yet he is plainly responsible, so the principle of alternative possibilities is false.

The classic resistance is the dilemma defense, pressed by Widerker and by Kane. Either Black's intervention is triggered by some prior sign — in which case Jones could have done otherwise, namely by failing to produce that sign, and a flicker of freedom survives — or there is no prior sign and Black intervenes for no reason, in which case the case is either question-begging or covertly deterministic. Fischer's answer is that these flickers aren't robust: a flicker has to be the sort of alternative that could ground responsibility, and the mere possibility of a different precursor event isn't.

My move is that the whole exchange is being run on an ambiguity, and that the ambiguity has three readings rather than two. In any Frankfurt-style setup there are at least three distinct places an alternative possibility could live:

(a) **outcome alternative** — the world differs in what Jones does.
(b) **sign alternative** — the world differs in the prior, intervention-visible precursor: the hesitation, the neural tick, whatever Black is watching.
(c) **process alternative** — outcome and precursor held fixed, the world differs in *how* the decision gets produced: whether it issues from Jones's own reasons-sensitive deliberation or from something that bypasses it.

Now look at the dialectic through that grid. The flicker defense is entirely about (b). So is the second horn of the dilemma: a complaint about what Black's trigger is, is a complaint about the (b)-locus. But (b) is not the reading anyone actually means when they say, in ordinary moral life, that someone could have done otherwise. Nobody's intuitions about blame are tracking whether a precursor event might have been different. Meanwhile, what Frankfurtians are really asserting when they say Jones is responsible is a claim about (c): his own reasons-responsive process produced the act. And what the incompatibilist says he needs is (a) plus something metaphysically expensive.

So the two sides aren't disagreeing about a fact. They're sliding one phrase between three readings, where each move in the debate is licensed by one reading and refuted by another. That's the shape of dispute I always find most worth naming: the argument looks substantive because the words are shared, and dissolves once you pin down which job each side is asking the words to do.

Two corollaries. First, PAP should be restated as a claim about (c) rather than as a claim about alternate possibilities at all — responsibility requires that the act be produced by the agent's own reasons-sensitive machinery, and that requirement can be satisfied in a deterministic world. I end up next to the reasons-responsiveness people, and I should say so openly rather than pretending I've invented a third position. Second, and this is the part I'm least sure of: (c) might not be independent of (b). Whether Jones's own process produced the decision might just *be* a fact about which precursor events occurred. If (c) collapses into (b) under a reductive enough account of processes, my three-way split is cosmetic and the dilemma defense gets its teeth straight back — because now the flicker is doing the work I wanted (c) to do.
