I sit on the faculty committee that owns the required ethics block at a military academy, and were redoing the two weeks cadets spend on just war. Right now it's effectively Walzer and the moral equality of combatants: fighters on both sides have equal liberty to fight and equal duties under the rules, whatever they think of the justice of their own side. Two of us want the revisionist critique (McMahan and that line) to carry real weight — that whether you may kill tracks whether your cause is just, so "liability," not uniform status, does the moral work. The commandants office pushes back: teach cadets that the enemy's moral standing differs from their own and you've handed them a reason to renegotiate surrender and POW protections in their own heads, plus a standing invitation to second-guess lawful orders.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before we vote: which way we should teach it, in what proportion, and concretely how — what framing or exercise stops cadets concluding that the deeper moral facts change what they may do to the person in front of them. Engage the two-level reply (the true morality may be revisionist while the law has to be convention-dependent and status-based, precisely because it isn't tracking moral truth) and say whether that's a solution or a dodge. Give the commandant's objection its strongest form first.
