Analyze the Lisbon earthquake of 1 November 1755 as a test case for Leibniz's thesis that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. The facts you will need, briefly: the shock struck mid-morning on All Saints' Day, so the churches were crowded and the houses lit with candles; it was followed by a tsunami that caught the survivors who had fled to the open quays of the Tagus, and by fires that burned for days; tens of thousands died and most of the city was destroyed. The event became the most discussed catastrophe of its decade in European letters. Voltaire's poem on the disaster, published the following year, attacks the optimists' maxim that all is well, and names Pope and Leibniz together as its authors. Rousseau's long reply of August 1756 defends the optimism Voltaire attacks, but by an argument that relocates the calamity: nature did not construct twenty thousand six- and seven-story houses, he observes, and had the inhabitants been more evenly dispersed and more lightly lodged the damage might have come to nothing; most of our physical ills are our own work, moral evil derives from free man, and providence is to be trusted on the evidence of sentiment rather than demonstrated by argument. In the years that followed, the rebuilt Baixa — a grid of wide streets, its buildings framed to flex rather than fracture — together with the systematic collection of reports from across the affected region, often described as the beginning of seismology, constituted a practical answer to the same event.

Against Leibniz's own framework, as set out in the Theodicy (1710) and the Monadology (1714), I want a disciplined mapping rather than a survey. Specifically:

1. What exactly does the thesis commit its holder to saying about this event? Work through the machinery: God's choice among whole composable worlds rather than among events, the principle of sufficient reason, the difference between permitting and willing, antecedent and consequent will, and the insistence that no part can be evaluated apart from the whole series.

2. What observation could, on Leibniz's own commitments, count against the thesis? If none can, is the reply that we cannot see the whole a legitimate concession to the epistemic position of a finite knower, or a way of making the thesis unlosable — and can you distinguish those two readings on the basis of the texts rather than assuming which one applies?

3. Here is a distinction I keep needing and cannot find. Call global optimism the claim that the actual world is unsurpassable among possible worlds taken as wholes; call local optimism the claim that no part of the actual world is such that some possible world improves on that part at no cost to the rest. Is local optimism entailed by global optimism? Does Voltaire's complaint in fact target local optimism while Leibniz's resources defend only global optimism? And does Rousseau's relocation of the disaster to human building practices concede local optimism — in which case, given that for Leibniz God chooses the entire series including those free choices, what remains of the defense? Press on whether the distinction holds or collapses, and say which.

4. Treat Pombal's reconstruction as evidence bearing on the thesis: an apparently local improvement that seems not to have required any alteration elsewhere in the universe. Does the demonstrated improvability of the arrangement weigh against local optimism, and if so, does that pressure transmit to global optimism?

5. End with a verdict rather than a balance sheet: did the episode refute Leibniz's thesis, immunize it, or bypass it — and does the fact that Voltaire's tutor-optimist in Candide (1759), rather than the argument itself, became the public memory of Leibnizianism tell us something about the thesis's epistemic status? Where the theory is simply silent about Lisbon, say that it is silent instead of making it speak.
