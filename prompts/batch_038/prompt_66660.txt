I serve on the seven-member board of the Cordero Basin Groundwater Authority, a joint-powers agency covering four municipal water districts and a county irrigation board in a semi-arid high-desert basin. The Authority was created in the aftermath of a punishing drought, and Article VI of its founding compact is frankly a self-binding device: pumping allocations for each member are fixed in ten-year cycles, may be revised only at a scheduled revision, only on the record of a hydrologic assessment completed within the preceding eighteen months, and only by a five-sevenths vote, and the article adds that no mid-cycle revision shall be entertained "for any cause, however urgent." The recitals say the point was to stop members from capitulating, cycle after cycle, to short-run pressure to over-pump. The same compact, however, gives any member the right to withdraw on eighteen months' written notice, after which its wells revert to state general law, which is considerably laxer. Three years into the present cycle, our smallest member — a town of roughly eleven thousand — has petitioned for a mid-cycle reallocation. It has commissioned an isotope-tracer study, reviewed by the state, indicating that natural recharge in the basin is about forty percent below the figure on which the current allocations were modeled, and that two of its production wells will lose pressure within four to six years at current pumping. Staff have laid out three options: deny the petition as barred by Article VI; find that a correction of allocations resting on a demonstrated factual error in the underlying model is not a "revision" within the meaning of the article, and reallocate now; or leave the allocations untouched and instead authorize a temporary bridge transfer from the Authority's banked strategic reserve — a fixed annual volume for three years, adopted as an ordinary annual operating rule, automatically sunsetting — while directing staff to redo the hydrologic assessment so that the next scheduled revision proceeds on corrected numbers. The board is split: the two directors whose districts would surrender water under any reallocation want denial, one director wants the correction reading, and the vice-chair favors the bridge transfer but has said privately that it looks to her like denial with better public relations. Counsel's warning is that the correction reading, once adopted, invites every future member to characterize its grievance as a factual error, which is precisely what the article was drafted to foreclose. We meet in eleven days, and staff need my memo circulated three business days before that.

Please draft that memo — call it twelve hundred words, addressed to the board from me as a director rather than as an outside adviser. It should have an actionable bottom line: which option I am recommending, the vote I should seek, the conditions and sunset language that ought to attach to it, the sequence and deadlines between now and the next scheduled revision, and what I want entered in the minutes so that whatever we do is legible to the board that inherits it. It should give reasons directors could actually answer in the room, including the two who are hostile to the petition, and it should identify honestly what the strongest consideration on the losing side is and why it is not a strawman — I would rather the memo state that case better than its author could than wave it away.

I also want the memo to confront the conceptual knot in our facts rather than hide behind the compact's text, though it should not turn into a seminar. Specifically: whether a commitment device that yields whenever a member can show genuine urgency is still a commitment, and whether what is before us is a question about the meaning of Article VI or a question about whether this Authority still wants to be the kind of body Article VI was written to create; whether the article can bind directors who never voted for it and whose constituents are paying costs its drafters did not anticipate, and whether the respectable responses to that are interpretation, amendment, or exit; whether the bridge transfer genuinely preserves the device or quietly repeals it, given that everyone expects it to be renewed — that is, whether a measure that is temporary in form and permanent in expectation is meaningfully different from a revision; and what the withdrawal clause does to the whole arrangement, since if any member can cut its own ropes on eighteen months' notice, it is not obvious that the Authority ever tied itself to anything, and I would like the memo to say what our decision is therefore actually accomplishing. Do not include a bibliography or citations and do not summarize the literature — I have read the standard treatments of Ulysses-style self-binding, the objection that each generation should be free to rewrite the rules it inherits, and the debate over whether entrenchment can bind a body that retains the power to unbind itself, and I want that material applied to our facts. I want a definite recommendation and defended reasoning, not a balanced survey of the three options.
