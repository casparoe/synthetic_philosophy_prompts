The question, as it appeared on the exam:

> **Q5 (20 marks).** Red and Blue take turns placing a stone of their own colour on an empty cell of a rhombus-shaped Hex board with n cells per side, n ≥ 2; Red moves first. Stones are never moved, captured or removed. Red wins if her stones contain a connected chain joining the left edge of the board to the right edge; Blue wins if his stones contain a connected chain joining the top edge to the bottom edge. You may take as given the theorem that a full board contains a chain for exactly one of the two players, so Hex is never drawn.
> (a) [7] Give the strategy-stealing argument for the conclusion that Red has a winning strategy. State explicitly what is being supposed at the start, and state explicitly what Red does on a turn where the strategy she is following tells her to play on a cell that is already occupied.
> (b) [5] List the properties of Hex the argument needs. For each, say what would go wrong in a game that lacked it.
> (c) [4] What does the argument fail to tell you, even in principle?
> (d) [4] Does the same reasoning show that the first player wins Connect Four (7 columns, 6 rows, a disc falls to the lowest free cell of the column you choose, four in a line wins)? Justify.

And here's the student's answer, transcribed from the scan with their spelling and their own paragraph labels:

> (a) Suppose for a contradiction that Blue, playing second, has a winning strategy; call it S. Red makes an arbitrary first move, anywhere she likes, and from then on plays as though she were Blue: she imagines the colours swapped and follows S, which she is allowed to do because the Hex board is symmetric under a reflection that trades the left-right pair of sides for the top-bottom pair, so a strategy for connecting top-bottom reads as a strategy for connecting left-right. If S ever calls for a cell that is already occupied, either by Blue or by her own throwaway first stone, she just plays on some other empty cell instead, it doesn't matter which. This is legitimate in Hex because an extra stone of your own colour can never be a liability — stones are never removed, so adding one can only create adjacencies, never destroy a chain. So Red ends up with everything S would have built for Blue plus maybe one spare stone, hence she gets a left-right chain, hence Blue loses. But S was supposed to be winning for Blue, and by the theorem quoted in the question both players cannot win on a full board. Contradiction, so Blue has no winning strategy. Hex is finite, deterministic, perfect information, no chance and no draws, so by determinacy one of the two players has a winning strategy; it isn't Blue, so it is Red.
> Notice also that Red's first move was completely arbitrary, which means the argument shows something stronger than people usually claim: any opening move Red picks is a winning one. She can start wherever she likes.
> (b) Properties used. (i) Finite, deterministic, perfect information, two players, zero-sum — needed for the determinacy step at the end; with chance or hidden information you couldn't conclude that somebody has a winning strategy at all. (ii) No draws — if the game could be drawn, the argument would only show that Red can force at least a draw rather than a win. (iii) Monotonicity, i.e. an extra stone of your own is never a disadvantage — this is what licenses the "play somewhere else instead" step, and it's the property that fails in games where you can be zugzwanged, like chess, where having the move can be fatal. (iv) Enough symmetry that Blue's task can be re-read as Red's task, in Hex by reflecting the board.
> (c) It doesn't tell you how to play. It's a pure existence proof by contradiction: you learn that a first-player win is out there but you get no opening move, no algorithm, no idea what the strategy looks like, and except on boards small enough to search exhaustively nobody knows it. That's the price of a reductio.
> (d) Yes, more or less. Connect Four is also finite, deterministic, perfect information, zero-sum, and both players have the same objective, so (i) and (iv) carry over unchanged. It can be drawn, if the grid fills up with no four in a row, so strictly speaking the argument gives you that the first player cannot lose rather than that he wins, but draws are rare so that's basically the same conclusion. Monotonicity should hold too: an extra disc of your own colour can only help you make a line, it can't break a line you already have. I'm pretty sure the first player wins Connect Four under perfect play if he starts in the middle column, and this argument is why.
