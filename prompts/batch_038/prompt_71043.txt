ok so, no academic background here — I fix boat engines, and over the last year I've gotten way too deep into how farmed shrimp and fish are actually raised, mostly because I eat a lot of seafood and I can't decide whether that's fine or awful. It's turned into a side project: I'm building a one-page scoring sheet that rates a farmed product on animal welfare, and I want to get the framework right instead of just adding another column every time I read something new.

So before I score anything, I want the spec. Write out what a welfare measure for farmed fish and crustaceans has to be able to do to count as worth using at all. The cases it has to handle, the distinctions it's not allowed to blur, the things about these animals' biology it can't be flatly inconsistent with, and the consideration behind each requirement. Then tell me which ones are dealbreakers and which ones are just preferences of one camp or another.

Some of what I keep running into, so you know where my head is at:

- I've been thinking in terms of what I call bad-hours — total time an animal spends in a state it would get out of if it could. I know that's probably naive, and I want to know what has to be true for something like it to work, or what kills it.
- A lot of the animals die before harvest anyway, and the killing itself seems to be the single worst event for most of them. So a score that rewards a fast stun could end up endorsing a miserable life, and a score that only looks at life quality basically ignores death. Does a measure have to combine those, and how is it supposed to do that without making it up?
- Shrimp and salmon aren't the same animal. Any measure has to work across species where we can't even agree what counts as a sign of distress — a lot of these animals don't show much when they're doing badly, and doing nothing can mean fine or can mean shut down. Missing data has to have a rule, and I suspect that rule ends up driving most of the final numbers.
- It has to survive a farmer trying to game it. If I count mortality, people harvest early or cull quietly. If I count density, they build smaller tanks.
- And it has to be fillable by one person who shows up once a year with a clipboard. I suspect this one and the "actually capture what the animal's life is like" one are fighting each other — say so if they are.
- Also: if farmed scores badly, does the measure push people toward wild-caught, where nothing gets measured and the fish basically suffocate? I don't want my sheet rewarding the unmeasurable.

Flag any pair of these requirements you think can't both be satisfied — if the list itself rules out the whole project, I'd rather know now. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most of the approaches people actually use.

Then, at the end and keep it short — a couple lines each, no more — how a stress-physiology score, a behaviour-and-choice score, a mortality-and-outcomes score, and a pure inputs score (density, water quality, stunning) do against the list, and which is least bad for my purposes. No reading lists or papers to go look up, I want the reasoning, not the sources.
