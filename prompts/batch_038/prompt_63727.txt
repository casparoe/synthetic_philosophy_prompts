I have been collecting my own verdicts about which inferences a heritability estimate does and does not license, and I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. The cases, with my gut reactions:

1. Narrow-sense heritability of 0.8 for a trait in a well-nourished population; someone infers that 80% of the gap between that population and a worse-fed one is genetic. Invalid, flatly.

2. The same 0.8; someone infers that a universal improvement in nutrition could shift the mean by at most the remaining fifth. Also invalid — but I cannot say why without invoking some distinction between variance and level, and I suspect that distinction is the whole of the answer rather than part of it.

3. A genetically varied seed lot split between a rich field and a poor one: heritability close to 1 within each field, the between-field difference wholly environmental. Not a paradox and not a misuse of the statistic; this is exactly what the statistic means.

4. A condition inherited in a straightforward Mendelian fashion whose damaging phenotype is prevented outright by a dietary change applied to everyone — phenylketonuria is the usual example. No heritability estimate is embarrassed by this.

5. Twin estimates for personality and attitude traits: I discount part of the monozygotic correlation as artefact, since raters treat two children who look interchangeable as though they were, and the monochorionic situation has no dizygotic counterpart. Yet genomic estimates from unrelated individuals, which typically come out lower, I treat as a floor that corroborates the twin design against the sceptic who says it is all shared family environment — on the grounds that they capture only additive effects tagged by common variants. I cannot hold both. The discrepancy is either evidence against the twin estimate or it is not.

6. Heritability of 0.5 in each of two populations whose environments are matched on everything I can measure: I want to say the residual mean difference is probably genetic, while conceding that "everything I can measure" is carrying the entire load.

7. Heritability of a cognitive trait rising from roughly 0.4 in childhood to 0.8 in adulthood as shared-environment influence falls: I want to read this both as evidence that families matter less than assumed and as evidence that people increasingly select their own situations, and I do not see how variance components alone could tell the readings apart.

8. High narrow-sense heritability licenses a short-run response-to-selection prediction within that population; broad-sense heritability does not license even that.

My tentative rule is that an estimate licenses claims only about the apportionment of variance within the population and range of environments actually sampled, and nothing about levels, means, other populations, or other environments. Cases 5, 6 and 7 are where I think it either breaks or needs a clause I cannot articulate. Please state the principle, or a small set of rival candidates, precisely and clause by clause, giving the case that motivates each clause; say what it delivers for cases 1–8 and for at least two further cases I have not listed; and state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, distinguishing those I should refine from those I should abandon. Where specialists themselves disagree — how much of the twin–genomic gap is artefact, whether matched-environment arguments can ever be discharged — separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Please write in British English.
