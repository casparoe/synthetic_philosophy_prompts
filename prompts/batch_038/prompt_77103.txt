My ethics reading group has been going in circles about whether you can get morality from other people's say-so, and last week I tried to write the anti-deferral argument down cleanly to see if it actually works. Now I think it proves way too much and I can't find the leak. Here's the version I gave them:

(1) Testimonial knowledge isn't subject-specific: if testimony can give you knowledge anywhere at all, then whenever a speaker knows that p and you have no reason to doubt them, you can come to know p on their word, whatever p happens to be about.
(2) Plenty of people know that factory farming is wrong. (Assume realism for the sake of the argument, that's not where I want the fight.)
(3) So they can tell you so, and you can come to know that factory farming is wrong purely on their word.
(4) If you know X is wrong and nothing more pressing is competing, you shouldn't do X.
(5) So you should stop buying that meat, on the basis of their word alone.
(6) But look at the agent you've just described. They have no grip on why it's wrong. Ask them to weigh the reasons and they can't produce any; they can't tell when the authority is overreaching or when the case in front of them isn't the case the authority ruled on; and they'd have complied just as readily if the authority had said something else. Their compliance isn't attributable to them in the way moral agency seems to require.
(7) If (6) describes a genuine defect, then believing-on-testimony is not an adequate basis for moral action.
(8) Now apply (6) anywhere. You have no grip on why your antibiotics work, or why the bridge holds, or why the treaty you read about was signed when it was. You could not produce the reasons if asked, and you'd follow the expert just as readily if they were wrong. So by (6) testimony is never an adequate basis for acting — which is obviously false, since practically everything we do rests on it.
(9) Therefore (6) is not a genuine defect, and there's nothing special or objectionable about taking morality on trust.

The group's response was that the argument is fine and the pessimists about moral testimony just have to eat (8). I don't buy that, but the best I can do is a feeling that something slides between (6) and (8), and that (9) is a bigger claim than (8) licenses. If I have to be attached to one premise it's (1), because dropping it looks like dropping testimony in general — but tell me if that's the wrong thing to be protective of.

So: which step or steps do you reject, why did that step look innocent when you first read it, and what does the rejecter pay? And separately, is my (6) even a fair statement of the pessimist's worry, or have I written up the most damaging version of a much weaker complaint? There's one case that keeps nagging at me and I want it handled explicitly: someone raised inside a genuinely corrupt moral community, whose own judgment is reliably bad and for whom deferring to an outside voice looks like precisely the right move. Does that case destroy whatever asymmetry someone would need to block (8), or can it survive it — and if it survives, what does the survivor have to say about the ordinary case in (2)?

Be straight with me about how confident you are in the diagnosis. If you think the argument is valid and the only real question is which premise is cheapest to give up, say that rather than hunting up a fallacy to please me. And if you think I've stacked the deck in (1), or mis-stated the other side so badly that the whole exercise isn't worth doing, say that plainly instead of playing along — I'd rather hear it now than in the seminar.
