Context: I teach a second-year methods seminar, "How Historians Argue." 18 students, one 100-minute session, big room with movable tables, printer access, zero budget, no screens (I ban phones). Its a mix of history and politics majors. They have read nothing by Braudel and nothing on the longue durée — deliberately. The point of the session is that they should feel the difference between the three timescales of history (events; conjunctures, i.e. cycles of decades; and the near-immobile layer of geography, climate, soil, and tides), and in particular feel why Braudel thinks event-history is foam riding on the surface rather than the current underneath. They have to run into that through what they do, not through me explaining it. And they must not hear the words longue durée or Braudel until the debrief.

Here's the material I've made up — treat it as ground truth, don't swap in real places or real facts. A fictional coast: the port of Carraig Point at the mouth of the river Treth, and the inland market town of Aldergate 30 miles up-valley, 1750–2000.

Longue-durée layer: the Treth's mouth has a shifting sandbar that only admits deep-draft ships on spring tides; prevailing westerlies mean inbound sailing ships must beat upwind into Carraig's approach while the rival harbour of Kell Down, 25 miles along the coast, is a downwind run; the hinterland is thin acid soil on granite, good for oats and sheep, with no coal measures under it, but coal seams lie 40 miles inland on the far side of a ridge; the estuary has a large tidal range, so cargo can't be worked at low water unless you build wet docks.

Conjoncture layer: a navy-provisioning boom from 1793 to 1815 that triples the oat and salt-beef trade; a herring fishery peaking in the 1840s and gone by 1880; a railway route decision in 1861 sending the main line through Aldergate instead of Carraig Point; consolidation of the coasting trade into a handful of steamship firms in the 1920s; a ferry-and-lorry shift in the 1970s that makes road distance to the industrial north matter more than sea access.

Event layer: a storm in 1839 that wrecks nineteen vessels in the roads; a combative harbour commissioner in the 1850s who spends five years fighting the railway route and loses; a harbour board embezzlement scandal exposed in 1911; one shipping firm moving its registry away in 1927; a charismatic dockers' leader during the 1970s strike.

Outcome that everybody in the room is told up front and nobody disputes: by 2000 Carraig Point is a small tourism-and-retirement town, Aldergate is a city of 200,000, and Kell Down is a middling but functioning port.

What I need:

- Teams receive only one timescale's documents and each has to write a short account of why the region turned out the way it did. Then a cross-examination and card-trading phase. Build the mechanism for that into the rules, including what a team can demand and what it must give up.
- One deliberately cross-cutting card: deforestation of the hinterland that is partly caused by datable shipbuilding contracts and partly by centuries of soil exhaustion, so teams fight over which file it belongs in.
- A moment where a team is forced to put a specific year on something that has no year — the silting of the bar — and then has to notice what it cost them to do so. That's definately the crux moment for me; it has to fall out of the rules, not be a debrief question I ask afterwards.
- One event card that's a genuine counterfactual hook: the 1861 route went by a handful of votes and could plausibly have gone the other way, while the ridge-and-coal geography made the inland line cheaper no matter who sat on the board.
- The actual cards, word for word, not descriptions of cards. Plus group sizes, a minute-by-minute timeline, whatever incentive or scoring structure you use (no real money), the debrief questions that map particular moments in the game onto particular timescales, and what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it in the room — including if the event team feels cheated and the structure team gets smug and deterministic.
- End with an honest note on which features of Braudel's view this activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. I expect at least one is that the game hands students the timescales pre-sorted, when part of his point is that the historian has to choose a scale of analysis and defend the choice.
