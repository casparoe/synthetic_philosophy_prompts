Ok so I'm putting together a handout for a thought-experiment seminar and I want a dialogue, no framing needed, just the piece itself.

Take the setup as given rather than arguing with it: a mid-size country has just approved a one-off somatic treatment, call it the Mod, that permanently improves how well a body converts money and food into actual functioning. Treated adults need roughly 40 percent less income to stay warm, fed, healthy and mobile than untreated ones do. Doses are scarce — enough for one in five adults this decade. Two wrinkles. First, a lot of the payoff turns out to be positional: in the trial regions where many people in the same local labour market got treated, employers started designing jobs around the treated as the default and real wages adjusted, so the untreated ended up worse off than they were before. Second, a sizeable minority refuses the Mod on religious and identity grounds, refuses cash compensation in its place too, and insists nothing is being taken from them.

Write a dialogue between a resourcist — Dworkin flavored, thinks in auctions, hypothetical insurance markets, and the line between brute luck and chosen ambition — and a capability theorist, Sen flavored, who thinks the target is what people are actually able to do and be. Both sit on the panel that has to decide who gets the Mod and whether recieving one should be clawed back out of the recipient's share of everything else. Each is trying to talk the other out of their metric, and they should answer each other's last point instead of restating their own pitch.

I want both sides sharp, no strawmen. The resourcist needs a real answer to adaptive preferences and to the fact that the Mod shifts conversion rates rather then the stock of resources anyone holds. The capability theorist needs a real answer to the charge that their metric hands a committee the job of adjudicating every individual's functionings, and that on the positional externality they can only either ban the treatment or shrug at the harm done to the untreated.

Format: exactly five numbered exchanges, one turn apiece from both characters in each, a few sentences per turn. Start cold at exchange 1, no scene-setting, and no wrap-up after exchange 5. The one exception at the end: two sentences telling me which of the two you think came out ahead, how confident you are about that, and which single point in the whole thing you're least sure of — including whether you may have gotten either view's commitments slightly wrong.
