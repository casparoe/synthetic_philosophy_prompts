So, four years into a novel about a company that sells thinking. Not AI — actual people. About sixty employees, a "root" who takes the client's question and breaks it into subquestions, each subquestion escalated to whoever has just enough context to answer it, and the answers get composed on the way back up. You know the picture: the claim is that this *decomposes* cognition rather than scaling it, and that a group like that can settle things no member could settle alone, without anyone in the building being a genius. I need one scene for the middle of the book and I want it engineered, not improvised.

Build me the case where the decomposition works flawlessly and still goes wrong. Specs:

- One scene, under about 500 words of actual fiction — a room, people talking, not a flowchart in prose. Analysis comes after, separately.
- Realistic near-present. No AI, no savants, no exotic tech. Nobody in the firm is dumb, corrupt, tired, or captured. That's the whole point: the failure has to come out of the structure.
- The client's question has to be one a single sharp generalist could have answered alone in about a week. So the firm's advantage is genuinely decomposition, not scale or proprietary data.
- Every escalation step must be answerable by the person it goes to, given only what they were handed. I want to be able to point at each rung and say yes, that was a fair ask of that person.
- The final deliverable is wrong — or confidently beside the point — and no single employee ever held the information needed to see that. Not "nobody bothered to check": nobody *could* have, from where they sat.
- The failure has to live in the carve-up, not in any leaf. Specifically: I want the way the question was decomposed to have quietly decided the answer before anyone thought about it, with every downstream person doing thier job correctly given that framing.

To be clear about what counts as cheating, since this is partly a test of whether the "decomposition just relocates the hard cognitive work" objection has any teeth at all: if the wrongness comes from somebody flubbing their step, or from a vague brief, or from a political fight inside the firm, the case fails — I have five of those already. And don't stipulate that the answer is wrong; make it wrong in a way a reader can see for themselves by following the tree.

Then, after the scene:

- Walk the escalation tree step by step. What was asked, who got it, what they could see, what came back, what got composed. Mark exactly where the carve-up happened.
- Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if I remove it.
- Give me the strongest available reply from someone who thinks factored cognition really is a good idealization of deliberation — something like "of course it fails, that's just what framing a question badly means, and solo deliberation fails the same way." I want the version that actually hurts, not a strawman, and then my character's best answer to it.
- Finally: what would I have to change to turn this from a cautionary tale about one badly run firm into a genuine problem for the view? For instance, does adding a reconciliation role at the top — someone whose job is to check the carve-up against the client's actual question — fix it, or does that just smuggle the generalist back in through the door?

Start straight in with the scene. No preface, no restating what I asked for, no throat-clearing about how you read the specs.

At the end, give me a straight read on how confident you are that the case actually meets every spec — I'd much rather be told where it's cheating than have it look airtight and find out in draft three — and name the one point in your analysis you're least sure about. If you think one of my constraints is secretly unsatisfiable with the others, say so definitly and say which one, rather than quietly bending it.
