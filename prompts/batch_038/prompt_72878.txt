Could you write me a short story — say 1,500 words — that dramatises the socialist calculation debate, but with the usual informational objection taken off the table?

Setting: a fully automated planned economy. The Central Planning Board has actually solved what Mises and Hayek insisted couldn't be solved. It has live data on every household's preferences, every machine's condition, every tonne of inputs, and it runs Lange's tatonnement in seconds — announce prices, observe the surpluses and shortages, revise, converge. No carbon-copy bureaucracy, no queues, no black market. For all the standard purposes, the plan works.

The story should be about what still goes wrong, and I want the subtler failure rather than the obvious one: the board can price and allocate anything that already exists, but nothing tells it about what nobody has thought of yet. On the Hayekian view, competition wasn't mainly aggregating given knowledge, it was generating it. So what does a planner do when the better crop rotation, the new process, the product people would want if only they'd seen it, isn't in the data because it hasn't been tried?

Turn it on a concrete small case rather than a speech: an engineer or regional manager who stumbles into something better and can't get it into the plan, or a board member who notices that every 'trial' has to be authorised in advance, which is the very thing that suffocates it. Ground it in the mechanics — shadow prices, the iterative revisions, the way managers recieving rewards for hitting targets have every reason to sit on an improvement. Keep the politics ambiguous and the ending uncomfortable for both sides.
