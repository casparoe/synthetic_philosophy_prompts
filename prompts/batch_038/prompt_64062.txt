So last Thursday a few of us ended up in the pub after work and it turned into an argument that I still cant settle in my own head, and I'm wondering if you can referee it for me. I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training at all, so some of my paraphrasing below is probably clumsy — treat it as the gist rather than the wording.

It kicked off because Rosie had been watching something about the colour phi effect: you flash a red dot in one spot and then, a fraction of a second later, a green dot a bit to the side, and people reliably report seeing a single dot travel across and change colour somewhere along the way, even though there was nothing in the middle of the path to change colour. Dev, who's read a bit about consciousness, said this shows the brain hangs around until it has enough information and then edits the sequence before it becomes an experience — so what you get is a constructed but perfectly genuine experience of movement-plus-colour-change. Rosie said that's exactly backwards: there's no point at which anything becomes an experience for anybody, and the report you give afterwards is just another round of editing rather than the output of a finished one.

Then it went like this.

(1) Dev said Rosie's picture can't be right, because reports happen at times and a report has to be caused by something. If there's no moment at which the content gets settled, then the report is being caused by nothing in particular, which is nonsense. So there has to be a finish line, even if nobody knows where it sits.

(2) Rosie said he was sliding from "the report is produced at a time" to "the experience was settled at that time", and those are different claims. The report is itself a process with a duration and its own editing going on, so asking when the experience was settled is like asking what time a build finished — you can name a moment, but only by picking a convention about what counts as done.

(3) Dev said fine, then pick the convention: the moment of the spoken report. And he said Rosie's view has a much worse consequence anyway. On her story there's no fact about whether the brain revised the sequence before he experienced it or rewrote his memory of it afterwards. Two accounts that can't be told apart, ever, aren't two accounts. So either she concedes a finish line or she concedes the whole thing is empty talk.

(4) Rosie said that was the point and not a bug: the two accounts are indistinguishable becuase there's no vantage point outside the system from which you could compare the memory against what was presented, since nothing ever gets presented to anybody. Every check you run is just another draft, including the check.

(5) Dev said that's plainly false in systems he actually works on. "The log said X but the state was really Y" is a meaningful and testable distinction, and a system can be instrumented so you can tell whether a record was written before or after the event it records. If you can do it there and not in a brain, he said, that's a fact about brains, not a fact about the concept of a record — and he offered a concrete test: make the subject answer slowly, with a forced delay before they speak, and see whether what they report changes with the length of the delay.

(6) Rosie said a log is only a log if something reads it, and if the reader is also writing into it, then he's rebuilt the theatre with two rooms and a corridor, and the question has just moved down the corridor.

(7) Dev's last move was the Libet timing results — the finding that a touch takes several hundred milliseconds of brain activity before it becomes a conscious experience, plus the further claim that the brain then refers the experience backwards in time so it feels simultaneous with the stimulus. He said you can't even describe that result without a clock and a moment somewhere, so whatever Rosie says about drafts, some version of the theatre has to come back to account for it.
