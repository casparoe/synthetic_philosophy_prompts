I spent thirty-odd years in a low-temperature lab doing solid state physics, and since retiring I've been trying to make up for never having read any philosophy past school. Lately I've been working through the stuff on hard choices and incommensurable values — Chang, Parfit, some Raz — and it's making my head spin in a way I didn't expect, because I thought I already knew what "incommensurable" meant. In my line of work it's a perfectly precise idea: two quantities are incommensurable when there's no common unit, so a ratio between them isn't a hard number to get, it's a category mistake. Nobody agonises over whether 4 kelvin is better than 3 kilograms. So when the philosophers say two options are incommensurable, I assumed they meant the same thing: the comparison isn't difficult, it's undefined, there's simply no fact of the matter which is better.

But that reading leads somewhere I don't much like, and I can't tell whether these people are actually endorsing it or whether I'm smuggling in the physics sense. If there's genuinely no fact about which option is better, isn't the choice just a coin flip, and isn't whatever I pick equally un-criticisable? Why would anyone spend two months in agony over a decison that, on this picture, is no more reason-guided than picking a restaurant at random? And then there's the business of the options being "on a par," which I gather is meant to be a fourth positive relation alongside better-than, worse-than, and equally good. If it isn't equally good and it isn't better or worse, what's left over? To me "on a par" just sounds like a polite way of saying they tie — and if they tie on some scale, then there IS a common measure after all, which seems to contradict the incommesurability claim outright. There's also that small-improvement argument I keep running into, where you add a slightly nicer office to one of the options, and I can follow the logic step by step but I don't see why the conclusion is supposed to be interesting rather than a symptom of our having been vague all along. In the lab, if two readings come out so close you can't tell them apart, you say your error bars are too wide and go build a better instrument. Is that instinct transferable here at all, or is it exactly the mistake the whole literature is a warning against?

I'm not after a survey or a bibliography — I've got the books, what I need is someone to point at the spot where my reasoning goes wrong. So please be blunt about whether I've collapsed two distinct things into one, walk me through the parity-versus-equal-goodness distinction as concretely as you can with a worked example, and tell me straight whether the "no fact of the matter, so it's arbitrary" conclusion is something these authors actually accept or something they're at pains to avoid. And since I have no way of checking you: tell me how confident you are in the account you give, and single out the one point in it that you're least sure of.
