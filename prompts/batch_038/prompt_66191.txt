I spent thirty-eight years as a plant physiologist, mostly measuring things, and took up philosophy only after retiring. My reading has lately been in the philosophy of biology, and last week I attended a public lecture by a synthetic biologist whose refrain throughout the evening was that DNA is a code. He made the slogan do a great deal of work. It licensed him to say that biology is now an information science on a footing with computing; it licensed the claim that a sufficiently complete reading of a genome would in principle yield the organism, since one can run a program once one knows the code; and when someone in the audience objected that the same sequence behaves differently in different cells, tissues, and environments, he waved it aside as a complication of the reading apparatus, the code itself being unchanged.

I have worked with the codon table for decades and I do not dispute it for a moment. But I suspect the sentence is doing several distinct jobs at once, some of which it cannot do, and I should like to see the jobs separated.

Could you set out, as a numbered menu, the different precise claims that DNA is a code might be making, arranged from the merely trivial to the most radical? For each, say whether it is a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive and defensible claim, a substantive but contested claim, or simply false. I should particularly like the menu to engage with:

- the narrow technical sense — an arbitrary, convention-like mapping from triplets to amino acids, mediated by the adaptor molecules and the synthetases — and whether that mapping is arbitrary enough to earn the word code at all, given that stereochemical and error-minimising accounts of the code's origin would make the assignments non-arbitrary, and given the frozen-accident picture Crick proposed, on which the arbitrariness is a historical contingency rather than anything like a design feature;
- the claim that DNA carries information in a quantitative, communication-theoretic sense, and whether that measure is doing any explanatory work in a cell or merely supplies a convenient unit;
- the semantic step, from a mapping to the claim that a sequence specifies something about the phenotype, and what would have to be true for that step to be legitimate rather than a slide;
- the program or blueprint sense, in which the genome is a set of instructions the cell executes, and what that assumes about where the message ends and the reading machinery begins — especially in a cell whose machinery is itself partly a product of what it reads;
- whether one and the same sequence being read differently in different contexts is compatible with code-hood, or whether it defeats the description.

Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually support the three things he used the slogan for — the parity with computing, the predictability of the organism from sequence, and the dismissal of the questioner — and which of them a careful philosopher of biology would advise me to stop using the sentence for.

Please close with a single line beginning Verdict: naming the one reading I should keep — the strongest claim the sentence can bear without collapsing into either a truism or a falsehood — followed by a one-word tag (platitude, defensible, contested, or false).
