I sit on the council of a city of about 60,000. One of our paving contractors — call him Ray, his dad laid a lot of the roads around here — has offered to pay for the new playground at the east side park. About $180k, which is the whole thing. That neighborhood has been asking for a park since before my kids were born.

Here's what makes it awkward. Ray isn't asking for anything. No naming rights — he specifically said no plaque, no press release with him in it. He's never given to a campaign, mine or anybody's, and says he doesn't plan to start. And I don't vote on who gets the paving work anyway: staff award it by lowest responsive bid under a formula the council set eight years ago. The only votes I actually have that touch his business are the size of the annual paving budget and, coming up in the spring, whether we hire an outside firm to audit our unit prices, which run high for the state. He hasn't mentioned the audit to me once.

My colleagues split down the middle. One says refusing is pure vanity — free money for kids who've waited fifteen years, no conditions attached, and if we say no the money goes to the next town over and Ray still paves our roads. The other says the minute you take it you've got a creditor, and it doesn't matter that Ray never asks for anything, because that kind of pull doesn't work by asking.

I keep thinking I could split the difference: run it through the general donations fund we already use for things like eagle scout benches, so it's pooled in with other gifts and nobody on council, me included, can see who gave what when we vote. But I'm not sure that changes anything real, just what I know. And separately, there's maybe two firms in the whole state that can do work at our scale, so we can't really do without his company already, gift or no gift. I honestly can't tell if that makes the gift worse or makes the whole worry beside the point.

Which of my colleagues is closer to right? Is there a version of taking the money I could defend out loud if it came out later, and does the pooled-fund idea actually fix the thing that's bothering me or just hide it from me? And how much weight should those fifteen years carry against my reluctance — I don't want the answer to be that I get to feel clean at somebody else's expense.

Plain continuous prose please, like an email back to me. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — I want to read it in one go, not scan it.
