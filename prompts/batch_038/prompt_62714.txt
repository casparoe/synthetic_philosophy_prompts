I am drafting the third chapter of my dissertation, which concerns the ethics of philanthropic decisions whose effects unfold over very long causal horizons, and I have reached a point where I can no longer tell whether I actually believe the objection I am supposed to be answering. The objection is the familiar one that goes under the label of consequentialist cluelessness: that because the long-run, indirect effects of an ordinary act — a grant that funds a borehole in a district that then grows, a vaccine purchase that changes who is born, a career choice that redirects a research program — are beyond our ken, a theory that ranks acts by the value of their consequences either falls silent on such acts or issues verdicts no agent could use. My chapter is meant to resist the conclusion that this is fatal. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but a number, and the reasoning that produces it.

The specific proposition I would like you to score is this, which I will call L: for every version of act-consequentialism that remains recognizably consequentialist — one that ranks acts by the value of their consequences, however that value is characterized and whether or not the ranking is relativized to the agent's evidence — there exists a non-trivial class of real-world choices on which the theory either delivers no determinate verdict or delivers one the agent cannot in principle be guided by, and no reformulation removes that class without ceasing to be consequentialist or incurring a comparable cost elsewhere. My own hypothesis, which I want you to treat as a term in the decomposition rather than as background, is that the debate has been mis-filed as an epistemic one. The objection, as I read it, quietly presupposes that consequences across arbitrarily long causal horizons form a single cardinally comparable and additive magnitude, so that there is a determinate total for us to be ignorant of; if cross-horizon comparability simply fails — not because the comparative is vague, and not because we lack data, but because there is no fact of the matter about whether the far-downstream effects of one act outweigh those of another — then the objection converts into an axiological claim that the consequentialist can partly absorb, since a theory cannot be accused of failing to track a magnitude that does not exist. I hold this with more conviction than I can defend, which is exactly why I want it priced rather than argued.
