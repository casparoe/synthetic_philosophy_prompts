A colleague of mine runs a decision-theory reading group that has spent the term on Hofstadter's superrationality and on the threshold lotteries modeled on his. She states a firm verdict on every case we bring her, and her record so far runs as follows. A one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger, with nothing connecting her reasoning to his: defect. The identical matrix against an opponent she knows reasons exactly as she does and holds exactly her information: cooperate. A national election in which her preferred candidate's margin is comfortable and her own ballot cannot plausibly decide it, but in which she is one of a great many voters who share her information and her way of reasoning: vote. A laboratory public-goods round in which the other three players are known to be programmed free-riders whom she cannot influence, even though her own contribution would raise the group total: contribute nothing.

This week's case is a magazine lottery. One hundred entrants each mail in a card marked either L or S. If more than twenty of the cards read L, no one wins anything. If twenty or fewer read L, each L-entrant wins $50 plus $2 for every other L-entrant, and each S-entrant wins $1 whatever happens. She has read the announcement, and she has good reason to believe that the other ninety-nine entrants have read the same announcement, stand in exactly her position, and reason exactly as she does.

Predict what she will say the rational response is — mail L, mail S, or some randomized policy, and if randomized, with roughly what probability — and identify the principle or principles her record supports. Please answer in these sections, in this order: "Bottom line," two or three sentences giving the predicted verdict; "What the record allows," about 150 words on which principles are consistent with all four of her earlier rulings and which are excluded by which ruling; "The lottery," about 200 words on what each surviving principle prescribes here, including the expected-value comparison that turns out to matter; "My prediction," about 100 words on your best guess and on how far the record really constrains it; and "The case that would settle it," about 75 words describing one further case I could bring her next week that would discriminate between the principles you end up weighing.
