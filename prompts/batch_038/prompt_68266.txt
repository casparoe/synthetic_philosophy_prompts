I am drafting a chapter of my master's thesis on the welfare economics of a proposed tax on sugar-sweetened beverages, and my supervisor has now written the proverb "there's no disputing about tastes" in the margin three separate times, each time to close down an objection rather than to answer it. He ties it explicitly to revealed preference: on his account, utility in the economist's sense just means getting what a person chose, so any attempt to stand behind the choice and ask whether it really served the chooser is a confusion about what the theory is even claiming. He also invoked the phrase as the title of Stigler and Becker's 1977 American Economic Review paper, which he says settles the matter by making stable tastes a standing assumption of the analysis. I suspect he is sliding between a folk proverb and a methodological convention, but I cannot yet articulate exactly where the slide happens, and I want to be able to say it in the body of the thesis rather than bury it in a footnote.

The two objections he waved off are these. First, my chapter relies partly on a contingent-valuation survey in which respondents state what they would pay for various outcomes; he objects that such surveys measure nothing at all, because stated willingness to pay is infected by framing, hypothetical bias, and the embedding effect — and the proverb is then brought in to block the natural reply that those stated figures are still evidence of something about how the respondents evaluate their options. Second, I argue that preferences formed under decades of beverage marketing, under long habit, and in some cases under dependence should not enter the benefit calculation at face value, and that a measure of experienced well-being might do better work than preference satisfaction; his response was again the proverb, this time to say that there is no standpoint from which anyone's tastes can be assessed as better or worse informed, so my proposal is merely one person's taste attempting to legislate over everyone else's.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that this single sentence could be making, as a numbered menu running from the platitudinous to the radical. For each reading give me (i) a one-sentence formulation specific enough that a person could accept it while rejecting its immediate neighbors, (ii) a verdict — trivially true, substantive and defensible, dubious, or simply false — with the reason for that verdict, (iii) what would have to hold in the world for it to be true, and (iv) whether it is a claim about taste in the aesthetic sense, about preference in the technical sense of a complete and transitive ranking, about the epistemic access people have to their own good, or about the authority their judgments carry in policy. Then tell me which reading, if any, actually disposes of each of my two objections, and be explicit about the readings that are true but irrelevant to either one and about the readings that would do the work only if they were considerably stronger than they are. I am especially interested in two things: whether a reading on which taste judgments are not truth-apt at all is consistent with my supervisor's own conclusion that getting what a person chose is what makes that person better off, since that conclusion looks like the sort of claim that could itself be true or false; and whether the methodological reading he attributes to Stigler and Becker — tastes treated as stable and identical across people so that differences in behavior are explained by prices, incomes, and constraints — is even in tension with there being perfectly good disputes about tastes going on. Please flag any place where the same sentence would have to carry different meanings in his two uses of it, and any place where a reading that sounds modest in the seminar turns out to be doing something much more aggressive in the argument. Write the response in American English. I do not need references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I need the disambiguation and the verdicts.
