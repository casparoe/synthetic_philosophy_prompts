For six years my town has been arguing about a monument, and on the morning of the fourteenth the argument will end in the way arguments like this end: with a crane. I have been asked to give the remarks before the stone is lifted off its plinth. I would like you to write them.

The particulars. A subscribers' committee put the monument up almost a century ago to eleven workers killed in a fire at the Carrow mill during the strike that preceded it. The relief on its face gives the mill owner's account: an orderly crowd, an accident, a grief that asks nothing of anyone. Two of the eleven were seventeen. The trouble has never been the deaths. It is that the town's annual remembrance service was held at the base of the stone, which meant that for decades we conducted our grief in front of a version of events the dead had no part in writing. The council has now voted to move it to the county archive, where it will be accessioned and kept in a climate-controlled room that members of the public may enter by appointment. A museum has been proposed that may or may not be built within the decade. The plinth will stand empty through the winter.

There will be about a hundred and fifty people: three descendants of the eleven, one of whom is ninety-one and opposed to the move; the historical society, which petitioned for it; families of former mill workers who consider both sides to have missed the point entirely; the council chair; a class of sixteen-year-olds who have been told this is a lesson; and the retired county archivist, the only person in town who has actually read the coroner's file, who will notice at once if I say anything careless about the difference between forgiving and forgetting.

Now the part I cannot do myself. My thinking about this has been shaped by reading Margalit on the duty to remember and Rieff on the case for forgetting — the insistence, on the one hand, that some memory is owed to particular persons rather than to propositions, and the suspicion, on the other, that remembering is not automatically a virtue and that some forgetting is a condition of a livable life. Out of that I have arrived at a distinction I believe the town has been blind to for six years, and I want it to be the spine of the speech. Call it the difference between custody and commemoration. A stone kept in a dark room at constant humidity, catalogued and never seen, is being remembered in one sense and forgotten in another, and the sharp question is whether there is any difference at all, for anyone who will ever encounter it, between the archive's basement and the skip. I do not want the speech to answer that glibly in either direction. I want it to hold the question open in front of a hundred and fifty people, and then to say what I think we are actually doing on the fourteenth: neither remembering nor forgetting, but changing who is responsible for the remembering, and on what terms.

The constraints. No philosopher's name, no book title, and no term of art anywhere in the text — the distinction has to be made in ordinary language, in no more than three sentences, by someone standing on grass in front of a hole. I am not permitted to use the words closure, healing, moving on, or erasing history, and I would not use them if I were. The speech must separate three things the crowd has been conflating for six years — remembering, commemorating, and preserving — by showing the difference rather than listing it. It must end by handing over to the reading of the eleven names, which the school class will do, and must say something true about the empty plinth that survives that reading.

It must also be willing to make somebody in the room uncomfortable. Choose deliberately who, and tell me in one sentence outside the speech why that person rather than another. Equally, it must not collapse into an argument for the council's decision — I refuse to deliver politics over a hole in the ground — nor into an even-handedness so scrupulous that it says nothing at all.

Around eight hundred words, six minutes, sentences a non-actor can say aloud without running out of breath, and no more than two places where the audience is asked to think hard. Do not invent facts about the fire beyond what I have given you here; where you need a detail you do not have, leave me a bracket.
