I have covered consciousness research for a general-audience science magazine for several years, and a pattern in the copy I edit has begun to bother me. Articles routinely announce that there is no inner observer, no little person watching a screen at the back of the skull — and then, two paragraphs later, describe a moment at which information "arrives," a single settled content that the subject "has," or a region where things "come together." I have been calling this, for my own purposes, **theater residue**: the leftover structure of the inner-theater picture surviving in prose that officially rejects it. I am building a sidebar — a diagnostic readers can run on any claim they meet in a press release, a talk, or my colleagues' copy — and I want you to design the instrument.

**First, pressure-test my decomposition.** I suspect the theater picture is not one commitment but three separable ones, and that residue consists in keeping one or two while disclaiming the third:

1. **Finish line** — the claim implies a determinate time at which an experience's content is fixed, so that the question "what was it like a moment before that?" has one right answer.
2. **Single content** — the claim implies one determinate thing is experienced at a time, rather than several partially formed, probe-relative versions of it, none privileged.
3. **Spectator** — the claim requires something further that receives the content, even when that something is called "the self," "global availability," "a higher-order representation," or "the organism."

Tell me whether this is the right cut. Revise it, add a fourth feature, or collapse two into one if you think that is more faithful — but justify the change, and do not simply relabel my three.

**Then build the space.** Enumerate the combinations your final feature set permits. For each, say whether any named position in the literature occupies it, and for the empty ones say why they are empty: incoherent, unattractive but coherent, or merely unexplored. I am especially interested in combinations that are occupied only by positions nobody actually defends, and in combinations that a working scientist would insist they occupy but cannot coherently describe.

**Then convert it into a decision tree my readers can actually use.** Each node is a single question about the claim as stated, phrased so that a reader with no training in philosophy or neuroscience can answer it from the sentence in front of them — no node may ask "is this view dualist?" or "does this author believe in qualia?" Give each node a one-line gloss of what answering yes commits you to, and explain your ordering: which questions must come first because later ones presuppose their answers. Every leaf carries the name of a position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The following must appear as leaves, or be explicitly explained away as not real positions: a naive inner-theater view; a "relocated homunculus" view that disclaims a spectator while retaining both other features; the multiple drafts model; global workspace theory in its psychological, theater-without-a-private-audience form; its neuroscientific broadcasting-and-ignition descendant; integrated information theory; a higher-order representation theory; illusionism about the theater itself; and at least one leaf reading "indeterminate — this claim cannot be sorted," with the reason. Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say whether any further question would separate them or whether they are the same position under two names.

**Then demonstrate it.** Trace the path, node by node, for each of these six claims, all of which are close paraphrases of things I have genuinely encountered in print:

1. Visual processing takes some tens of milliseconds, so you are always living slightly in the past.
2. The stimulus was processed without awareness; it became conscious only when the information was broadcast to the rest of the brain.
3. When two differently colored lights are flashed in alternation, people report a single object moving and changing color partway along — yet no such intermediate state ever occurred in the world, so the brain must have composed the sequence before delivering it to awareness.
4. There is no single moment at which the subject decides; the decision is spread across competing processes, and the feeling of deciding at a particular instant is a story told afterward.
5. The seat of consciousness has been located: a patch of posterior cortex whose activity, and whose activity alone, tracks what patients report experiencing.
6. Information that has not reached consciousness cannot be used to make a decision; unconscious processing cannot drive choice.

For each, state which features the claim retains and whether the residue is **cosmetic or load-bearing** — that is, whether the claim can be rephrased to shed the residue with no loss of empirical content. If it can, show me the rephrasing. If it cannot, say what the residue is buying the author.

Two housekeeping notes. I do not want a bibliography, a reference list, or paper titles; where you attach a name to a leaf, flag any attribution you are not confident of rather than guessing at a date or a publication. And finish with three or four sentences of your own: which leaf you think most science journalism actually occupies, which leaf the scientists I would quote for such a piece would say they occupy, and whether the gap between those two is a confusion that better copy would fix or a real philosophical disagreement that the copy cannot resolve.
