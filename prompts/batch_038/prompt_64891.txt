'Samuelson's point was that economists had been fooling themselves for a century: utility names something nobody has ever observed, so consumer theory had to be rebuilt on the only thing that is actually on the record, which is choice. When a shopper takes basket A while basket B sits within reach of the same budget, they have shown that A is preferred to B — and since every purchase is a showing of this kind, preferences turn out to be nothing over and above choices. That is what makes the theory scientific: it is refutable. Observe someone take B while A is affordable, and then take A while B is affordable, and the consistency axioms have been broken, so the whole construction comes down. Better still, the standard results fall out for free: choices satisfying the axioms generate demand curves that slope downward, which is why the Giffen good — a thing people buy more of as it gets dearer — had to be treated as a curiosity. Samuelson thereby showed that the entire theory of demand follows from behaviour alone, with no residue of psychology, and Robbins, who had insisted that economics rested on introspective assumptions no observation could check, was simply wrong about the foundations. To be sure, none of this tells you why anyone buys anything. The theory declines on principle to say what goes on inside; utility now means nothing more than whatever ordering the choices happen to reveal, so the old quarrel about whether happiness can be measured was dissolved rather than won.'
