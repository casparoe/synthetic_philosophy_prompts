I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship in philosophy and I need a two-page proposal together by the end of the month. Here's the half-formed thing I want made rigorous.

The Taurek debate about whether the numbers count has been stuck for decades, and I think part of the reason is that "the numbers count" is actually three separate claims that people slide between without noticing:

1. an axiological one — that losses to different people literally add up, so five deaths are five times as bad as one, and it's the additivity that does the normative work;
2. a claim about reasons — that each of the five has a claim on me just as strong as the one person's, so what aggregates is the number of reasons rather than the badness, which means 2 can be true even if 1 is false (roughly the contractualist line, if I've got it right);
3. a procedural one — that even granting nothing sums and no reasons aggregate, giving each person an equal chance is the only way to show them equal concern, so the numbers get in through how the chance procedure is designed.

My hunch: Taurek denies 1 and everyone reads him as thereby denying all three, while his opponents spend their energy defending 2 and quietly presuppose 1, and the coin-flip literature is really arguing about 3 with 1 and 2 assumed. If the three come apart there are cells in the table nobody occupies — deny 1, accept 2, deny 3, say, which would give you a view on which you save the five, but not because anything sums and not because of fairness to chances.

Turn that into the proposal. I want: the question stated so precisely that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions leave it open rather than merely disagreeing; a month-by-month method saying what I'd actually be doing — building cases, testing seperability, checking whether a coherent view can occupy each cell; at least one concrete case where the three claims genuinely come apart (e.g. five people whose individual claims are each weaker than the single person's, or a case where a chance procedure is impossible so you just have to pick); what would count as the project failing — my own guess is that it fails if the claims turn out to be non-separable, or if every unoccupied cell gets closed by an argument that collapses back into a familiar view; and an honest line on scope, i.e. which of the three claims I should drop if twelve months isn't enough.
