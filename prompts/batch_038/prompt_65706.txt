Bit of an odd one. I'm a philosopher who's been reading complexity theory on the side for a couple of years, and I keep running into a shape of argument I can't find a name for — and I suspect the name I want isn't in the complexity literature at all.

Two instances, from memory so the details may be wobbly:

First, there's a result to the effect that a whole class of proof techniques can't possibly settle P versus NP — the class being techniques that would go through unchanged if you gave the machines free access to some arbitrary extra subroutine. The reason offered is that there are subroutines relative to which the two classes come out equal and others relative to which they come out different, so a technique that's blind to which subroutine you picked can't tell the cases apart. Notice what's happening there: nobody has shown P = NP, and nobody has shown the conjecture false. They've shown that a *way of arguing* is unavailable.

Second, and similarly, there's an argument that the obvious strategy for proving circuit lower bounds — find some simple property that lots of easy functions have and that one particular hard function lacks — is doomed, *provided one-way functions exist*, because a property simple and common enough to be usable would also let you tell pseudorandom strings from genuinely random ones, which is the very thing you're not supposed to be able to do. What strikes me as philosophically wierd is that the barrier is itself conditional on an unproved cryptographic conjecture, so the impossibility of the method is about as shaky as the thing it was meant to help with.

Here's the bit I actually want help with. In my own field we've got a perfectly familiar move where you object to an argument by saying it proves too much: if your principle worked it would also deliver a conclusion in some unrelated area that nobody accepts, so the principle is out. But that move targets a premise or a conclusion. What I'm seeing in these two cases targets methods — it says a family of reasoning steps is off the table regardless of what you'd conclude with them, and it establishes this by a theorem rather than by an intuition about overreach. And there's a third thing I keep confusing with the other two: arguments along the lines of, if NP had polynomial-size circuits then the polynomial hierarchy would collapse to its second level, that would be absurd, so NP probably doesn't have polynomial-size circuits. There the conclusion is about the claim rather than the method, and it's offered as evidence rather than proof, which feels like a different animal entirely — maybe something to do with how we justify maths conjectures we can't actually prove.

So: is there an established concept, in complexity or in philosophy, that covers the first two as a single kind? What are the nearest existing notions, how does each fall short of what I've described, and does the third case belong in the same category or in a separate one? If there's no general term, I'd like you to coin one and define it with boundary cases, because I'm after the carving rather than the label. Be explicit about what counts as an instance and what doesn't — is a no-go theorem in physics the same thing? Is an incompleteness theorem? Is "this argument only works if you're already a constructivist" a case of it, or something else?
