I TA a grad seminar that's half philosophy dept, half econ, and I'm writing the take-home. We just did three weeks on Harsanyi and I've now heard four different students say some version of "so utilitarianism is basically a theorem of rationality, you just have to grant the axioms." That is, word for word, the take I read on rationalist blogs and EA forums for a decade, so I have feelings, and I want the exam to make them eat the claim rather than recite it.

Here's what I need: one two-part question, about 90 minutes of work, 25 marks total, that a student who has memorized the statement of the theorem cannot pass but a student who actually understands it can.

Part A, the formal part, ~10 marks. I want the stem to hand them the full setup so nobody has to go looking anything up: a fixed finite population, lotteries over a finite set of basic prospects, each individual and the social observer having a preference ordering over lotteries that satisfies the expected utility axioms, hence each represented by a von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function unique up to positive affine transformation. Then ask them to state Pareto indifference and anonymity precisely and derive the conclusion — positive weighted sum, equal weights under anonymity, unique up to a common positive affine transformation — and to say explicitly what anonymity is contributing and what survives if you drop it. And I want a sub-part on the ex ante versus ex post Pareto routes: what extra structure each one needs, why the two approaches can come apart, and which route if either gets you the linear form without already assuming the observer is expected-utility-rational over lotteries. Hammond's point about what it takes for the ex ante and ex post approaches to be consistent is the thing I'm fishing for here.

Part B, the diagnosis, ~15 marks. The core question: this is a representation theorem, so where exactly does the normative work enter, and what would you have to add to turn it into an argument for a substantive moral view? I want them to locate premises, not summarize a debate. Concretely, I'd like the question to force them onto at least four of these, and I want you to tell me if you think any of them isn't really a crux:

- that the social observer's ranking is the moral ranking at all, as opposed to one more person's ranking
- that the quantity getting summed is well-being rather than something like an individual's extended preference about being in your situation, which can be driven by their attitudes toward you rather than by your welfare, and the further question of whether aggregating extended preferences across people is even coherent
- that equal consideration is coming from anonymity, not from rationality
- that expected-utility linearity in probabilities is doing the aggregating work, and that if you require the observer's preferences over lotteries to be coherent while also respecting each individual's own beliefs about the lottery, you seem to need probability agreement across people, which is a much less innocent premise than it looked
- that the population is fixed, so the theorem is silent on everything the internet actually fights about

Marking scheme: allocate the 25 across named moves, and then give me the list of wrong turns that should cost marks. The ones I care most about catching — saying the theorem derives utilitarianism from rationality alone; treating the social aggregation theorem and the veil-of-ignorance / impartial observer argument as though they were one argument instead of two with different premises; asserting either that the theorem refutes prioritarianism or that prioritarianism is untouched by it, without engaging the question of whether the summed quantity is the person's good; invoking utility monsters or the repugnant conclusion as if they were counterexamples to a fixed-population representation theorem; and the reverse-error of saying the theorem assumes interpersonal comparability and so begs the question, without distinguishing the version where comparability is assumed from the version where it's supposed to be earned out of extended preferences plus equiprobability. Then tell me in a few lines what seperates an A from a B — I want that stated as moves present or absent, not as vibes.

Model answer should be around 900 words and should actually do the derivation in Part A, not gesture at it. Two house rules: don't have the question ask students for citations or a bibliography, and don't build anything that depends on my students having read a particular paper — the stem has to be self-contained. And if you think I've got the crux wrong, say so in the notes after the marking scheme; I'd rather be corrected than have a tidy exam that tests the wrong thing.
