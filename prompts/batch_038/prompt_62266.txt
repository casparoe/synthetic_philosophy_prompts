Our philosophy-and-lit reading group is pairing A Christmas Carol with Bernard Williams on internal and external reasons next week, and I'm supposed to open the discussion. The Williams claim I care about: an agent has a reason to do something only if doing it could be reached by sound deliberation from what he actually wants and cares about now — so announcing to Scrooge that he ought to be generous gets no grip unless something already in him could take him there. The externalist says the reason was there whether or not Scrooge could see it.

My hunch, tell me if it survives contact with the book: the three spirits never hand Scrooge a single new reason. His sister, Fezziwig, Belle, Tiny Tim, the cold schoolroom — it's all material he already had, just made vivid. They're manufacuring the deliberative route, not discovering anything. That would make the story a parable for internalism, and at a price: moral conversion looks like therapy rather than finding out what you were always required to do.

Give me:

- The setup (~150 words): both positions stated in a form the novella can actually test.
- What the spirits do (~400 words): stave by stave, and whether the mechanism is information, imagination, or something else. Fold in the Spirit throwing Scrooge's own surplus-population line back at him over Ignorance and Want, and Scrooge at the gravestone asking whether what he's shown is what will happen or only what may happen.
- The price (~250 words): what internalism has to give up, especially whether it can call the pre-visitation Scrooge blameworthy at all.
- Where it breaks (~250 words): the passages that resist — Bob toasting "the Founder of the Feast," the charwoman and the undertaker's man selling his stolen bedding to Old Joe, the debtor couple relieved by his death. Those look like reasons that don't wait on anybody's motivations.
- What Dickens adds (~150 words): anything the debate misses that the story catches.

Don't quote the book at length, just point at scenes — nobody's going to have the text open.
