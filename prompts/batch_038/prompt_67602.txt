For the past eight years I have designed public information for a municipal service whose users are almost entirely recent arrivals: notices on water safety, vaccination, tenancy rights, fire evacuation, school enrolment. In the spring I became the person formally accountable for that material, and in July one of our own charts — a repeating-symbol graphic on how long water must be boiled — was read by a family in one of our reception facilities as guidance about acceptable shower temperature. Nobody was seriously hurt, and the incident was arguably a failure of my layout rather than of the medium. But it has made urgent for me an old intellectual interest: nearly everyone who advises our unit now tells me that what we need is a genuine picture language, and the ancestor of that claim is Otto Neurath's Isotype, developed with Marie Reidemeister and Gerd Arntz in interwar Vienna and later renamed and exported.

I am applying for a one-year fellowship that pairs a design studio with a philosophy department, and I want the two-page proposal built around a question whose shape I can see but cannot yet state precisely. My half-formed idea is that the argument about whether Isotype counts as a language runs together at least three claims that ought to be kept apart: that a pictorial notation can be acquired far faster than a verbal one; that pictures are systematically less determinate in content than sentences; and that a visual convention can be international in a way no verbal convention can be. Neurath himself seems to have balanced these carefully — treating the system as an auxiliary that never replaces words, insisting on the trained transformation step between the specialist's data and the artist's drawing, and preferring repeated identical symbols to enlarged ones precisely because he doubted what a picture can settle. What I want to know is which of those three claims his project actually needed, and what happens to the rest of it once they are separated. I should also note that my prospective supervisor thinks the philosophically interesting question is whether Neurath's general epistemology — nothing foundational, every statement revisable within a system — supports the picture-language ambition or quietly undercuts it.

Please turn this into a proposal. I need a precise statement of the question, an account of why the existing answers leave it open (the design-historical celebration of Isotype, the semiotic rejection of pictorial universality, and the philosophy-of-science readings of Neurath each seem to miss something the others have), a month-by-month method that makes honest use of what I actually possess — a studio, eight years of archived commissions, and direct access to the people who read this material — a description of what a result would look like, and a statement of what would count as the project failing. Finish with a candid assessment of one worry I cannot resolve: whether a philosophical question survives being answered partly by comprehension testing, or whether that reduces it to market research. Do not include a bibliography or a reference list; I will supply the sources myself.
