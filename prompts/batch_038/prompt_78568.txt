I am writing a paper for my school's philosophy seminar comparing religious moral systems with secular moral theories, and I have run into a framing difficulty I would like help resolving.

My working assumption has been that Buddhism is straightforwardly consequentialist. The doctrine of karma seems to settle it: the moral quality of an act is fixed by what it produces, since skillful acts ripen into well-being and unskillful ones into suffering, so on this reading the right act is simply the one with the best results. If that is correct, though, Buddhism looks like an early version of utilitarianism, and I cannot see what a Buddhist could appeal to in order to resist the standard objection — the man at the door who asks where my friend is hiding, where the lie saves a life and therefore, by the calculus, is required. Utilitarianism bites that bullet, and I assumed Buddhism would have to bite it as well.

So my question is this: given that karma already supplies the consequentialist machinery, what resources does Buddhist ethics have that utilitarianism lacks, and is there any reason to treat it as a distinct metaethical position rather than a precursor of the same view?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning behind it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
