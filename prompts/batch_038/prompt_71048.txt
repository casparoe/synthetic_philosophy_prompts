I'm drafting a chapter of my dissertation on ambiguity aversion, and I've built an argument whose conclusion I can't accept, though I've gone over each step several times and can't isolate the bad one. It is meant to show that ambiguity aversion is a framing effect rather than a rational response to evidence.

Setup: Ellsberg's three-color urn — ninety balls, thirty red, sixty that are black or yellow in a proportion the agent isn't told. Consider the familiar pattern: bet on red rather than black, but bet on black-or-yellow rather than red-or-yellow.

(1) Rational preference between acts is fixed entirely by the agent's beliefs about states and her utilities over outcomes; no other feature of a choice problem is rationally relevant.
(2) Suppose the agent has no information favoring any particular composition of the urn. Then no assignment of probabilities over the three colors is supported by her evidence except one-third each.
(3) Given (2), the two bets in the first pair have equal expected utility, and likewise the two bets in the second pair.
(4) The ambiguity-averse agent nevertheless strictly prefers red to black, and black-or-yellow to red-or-yellow.
(5) So by (1) and (3), her preferences are not fixed by her beliefs.
(6) By (1) again, nothing besides beliefs and utilities fixes rational preference; therefore what does fix these preferences is something rationally irrelevant — the wording of the acts, or the source of the uncertainty behind them.
(7) Preferences fixed by features of that kind are framing effects.
(8) Hence ambiguity aversion is a framing effect.
(9) And the same route applies to any preference pattern that no single probability function can represent.

Step (9) is what I can't live with. Since almost no real decision comes with a precisely specified probability function, the argument would classify nearly all actual deliberation — including ordinary caution about model uncertainty — as a framing effect. I would much rather keep (1), which looks like no more than the requirement that rational preference not turn on how a problem is described; but I recognize that is a preference, not a defense of it.

Which step should I reject? Say why it looked sound when I wrote it, and then price the alternatives: what does the maxmin expected utility theorist pay for giving up (1), what does a defender of imprecise or multiply-representable credence pay for giving up (2), and what does each owe on Savage's sure-thing principle and on dynamic consistency once the choice is embedded in a sequence of decisions? Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis you settle on — which consideration would push you to a different step, and where you think your own reconstruction of my reasoning is most likely to be off.
