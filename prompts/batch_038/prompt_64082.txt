Last night I spent three hours in a friend's kitchen in Leeds arguing about whether a system that has only ever encountered text can be said to mean anything by its words, and we ended it no further apart than we began. She builds retrieval and translation systems for a living; I read philosophy of language for pleasure and badly. Her position, as she put it, was that the whole complaint about grounding is a philosopher's artefact: a lexicon is a web, and once the web is dense enough every node is pinned down by all the others, so there is nothing left over for ostension to supply — the corpus is already a record of the world, and a system fluent in that record is fluent in the world. Mine was that a definition is only ever a parcel of other words, so that chasing meanings round the circle yields a closed loop that has never touched a lemon, a cold floor, or a coerced signature. The argument began, incidentally, over a consent form her employer's system had rendered with a negation in the wrong place, which someone had signed. We agreed we could not settle it with more premises, and I have come to think it is better settled in fiction, where the disagreement can be made to happen to people. I would therefore like you to write a short story that stages it.

Here is the apparatus I would like you to build the story out of, though you should feel free to adjust proportions and to invent freely within it. A certification institute — give it a name and a bureaucratic texture — licenses machine interpreters for use in a post-conflict administration where a mistranslation has already cost someone their liberty or their limbs. Two candidate systems are before it. The first is purely textual: it has absorbed everything written in the relevant languages, including field manuals, interrogation transcripts, grain ledgers, school primers and private diaries, and it has never had a sensor. The second, which the institute's staff call the Warden, has been built to answer the objection: a small body with one camera, one manipulator, a thermometer and a microphone, confined to a bare room in which an attendant stages a fixed inventory of objects and situations. Both are to be taught the same closed vocabulary of two hundred words, and the story should name at least a dozen of them explicitly. Three of these must be words that cannot be pointed at under any circumstances — the sort of thing one might choose is a temporal adverb, a modal qualifier, or the name of a social relation — one must be a colour term, and at least two must pick out qualities for which the Warden has no organ at all, so that its embodiment turns out to be as much a limit as an asset: it can be shown ice and can be shown a blade, but nothing in its room is sweet and nothing in its room smells of smoke. The certification is supervised by the institute's chief lexicographer, who was born blind, who wrote the paraphrases used to teach the textual system, and who receives the entire ostension programme second-hand: the attendant points and speaks to the Warden, then files a written log, so that what reaches her as evidence of grounding is, once again, only text. Include a scene in which someone proposes the obvious compromise — grafting the Warden's sensorimotor categories onto the textual system's lexicon, so that a fraction of the symbols are grounded directly and the remainder inherit their grounding by description — and someone else objects that this merely relocates the problem rather than solving it. Include at least three test episodes drawn from the vocabulary: one in which the textual system succeeds and the Warden fails outright, one in which the reverse occurs and the failure is unmistakable, and one whose outcome is genuinely undecidable from the log.

Three constraints on execution. First, the thesis must never be stated: no character may deliver a paragraph of exposition explaining what grounding is or why it matters, and the argument has to be carried entirely by the tests, the logs, the vocabulary and what people are unable to do. Second, the ending must be one on which my friend and I would each claim victory — she should be able to read it as demonstrating that competence outran the need for bodies, and I should be able to read it as demonstrating that something remained permanently unbanked — and both readings must be supported by concrete details already present in the text rather than by ambiguity of phrasing. Third, keep it between 1,800 and 2,500 words, give it a title, and write it throughout in British English, in British spelling and British idiom; an interleaved structure of narration and the attendant's log entries would suit the material well, if you want it. Do not name any real philosopher, do not allude to any actual experiment, and do not cite or recommend any literature — the story should stand entirely on the machinery you have built inside it. After the story, and clearly separated from it, append a note of no more than 150 words identifying which specific moments and details a textualist would cite as evidence for her reading and which a grounding theorist would cite for his, without adjudicating between them.
