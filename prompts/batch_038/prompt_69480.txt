For three years I've directed a small research program attached to a state-level office that reviews algorithmic decision tools in two domains: pretrial release recommendations, and eligibility triage for a disability benefits program. The office's authorizing statute says such tools must be statistically valid and non-discriminatory, which everyone concedes is ambiguous, and our job is to tell the commissioners which particular mathematical criterion they can defensibly audit against. A funder has now offered a four-person, three-year expansion — two hires junior and quantitative, one a lawyer with essentially no measure theory, and I'd keep the fourth slot myself — and they want a prioritized research agenda before the money is committed. I'd like help thinking through what that agenda should be, and more importantly pressure-testing my instincts about which questions are actually load-bearing rather than merely fashionable.

The candidate lines of work I keep circling are these. (a) Whether the familiar impossibility results are really about decision rules at all, or are instead claims about a fixed score combined with differing base rates — that is, whether the impossibility is conceptual or merely empirical, and hence contingent on the population rather than on the criteria. (b) The finite-sample version: how much of the apparent trade-off is a product of scarce data in small demographic cells rather than of anything true in the idealized population, and whether that shifts with sample size, group size, or the granularity of the score. (c) Group-dependent thresholds. If separate calibration buys compatibility among criteria, then the residual conflict is no longer between statistical criteria but between those and something like individual-level comparability, and I want to know whether that residual is the conflict that actually matters legally. (d) Causal and counterfactual criteria: whether adopting them dissolves the impossibility or simply relocates the contestable choice upstream into the selection of a causal model, which in our two domains is at least as politically fraught as the choice of criterion. (e) Aggregation: treating the choice among criteria as itself a social-choice problem and asking whether there is an Arrow-flavored obstruction to any procedure that combines them, which would be a genuinely different species of impossibility from the ones currently on the table. (f) Measurability: whether whichever criterion the office picks is even estimable in deployment, given proxy labels, missing outcomes for people who were never released or never enrolled, and privacy constraints on collecting group membership at all. (g) The level-of-analysis question: whether criteria should be assessed on the score or on the downstream decision procedure — thresholds, triage ordering, caseload budgets, what happens to people the tool never reaches — since it is plausible that much of the existing debate is an artifact of fixing attention on scores.

Here is the wrinkle I want you to engage with rather than set aside. Suppose that in year one one of my junior hires proves a clean reduction: for any two criteria in the threshold-rule family, the set of jointly achievable outcomes is determined entirely by the score's ROC geometry and the groups' base rates, so that once you know those two things the choice among criteria collapses into a choice of loss weights and no further feasibility facts remain to be discovered. Treat that as granted. Does it gut the agenda or reorder it? My instinct is that it renders (a) and (b) near-trivial, leaves (c), (e), and (g) as the only live questions, and makes (d) more urgent rather than less — but I can't tell whether that instinct is correct or merely aesthetically satisfying.

What I want back is a defended agenda, not a survey: an explicit ordering with the top two or three lines of work identified, the reasoning for each placement, and an equally explicit account of what you would deprioritize or drop and why. State the criteria you are ranking by — how upstream a question is, whether three years and this particular mix of people can move it, whether an answer would change what the commissioners can actually do, and how much has already been written on it relative to its importance — and say where those criteria pull against one another, since I suspect the question that would most change practice is often the one nobody can settle. Tell me what evidence over the first eighteen months should move something up or down the list, and close by naming one thing I have wrong above, or one line of work that is missing entirely. Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or named papers; I want the argument made from the ideas themselves, and where you cannot defend a claim about what has or has not already been shown, flag it as a guess rather than dressing it up.
