I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am putting together a long feature on the many-worlds interpretation and the quantum immortality argument that is often said to follow from it. The piece will be accompanied by a reader-facing sidebar, and I would like you to write that sidebar: a decision tree that takes a reader from a small number of plain starting commitments to a named position on whether Everettian branching makes death impossible for them.

The audience matters for how the nodes must be phrased. My readers have absorbed the popular version of the story — the wave function never collapses, every outcome happens, we branch — but they know no formalism and they have never read the philosophy literature. Every branch point must therefore be a concrete case or a plain-language claim they can respond to without knowing which position their answer leads toward. No node may use the technical vocabulary of the debate; those terms belong at the leaves, where each may carry a gloss of twelve words or fewer.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First, state the independent questions you take to be at issue — roughly nine to eleven branch points, two or three answer options each — and indicate which combinations of answers they jointly permit. Only then locate the actual positions in that space. The cases the tree must deploy include: the idealised box run a thousand times over, and whether it means anything to the survivor that the surviving branches come to hold a vanishingly small share of the total amplitude; whether what the reader cares about is that they wake up or that someone qualitatively continuous with them wakes up, posed through a mundane analogue such as a backup copy or a pair of successors; whether there is any fact about how many successors there are at all, so that counting branches may be hopeless while counting amplitude is not; whether the person before the experiment should expect to find themselves in one outcome or to have experiences in all of them; whether ordinary dying is a switch or a dimmer, and what that does to the argument once the box is replaced by a stroke; whether very low-amplitude branches are stable enough for anyone to notice having survived in them; and whether one can be an Everettian about everything except observers' minds, so that each mind gets a single outcome.

The leaves must include, at minimum: the strong immortality thesis; the verdict that immortality is technically true but hollow, because what survives holds almost no amplitude; the conclusion that immortality is a reductio of Everett rather than a consequence of it; the escape route in which each individual mind has exactly one outcome and can therefore die; the view that immortality is true but gives no reason whatsoever to step into the box; the Parfitian view that branching makes survival a matter of degree; and the exit via collapse or hidden variables. Each leaf should carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — what the reader must swallow to live there.

Three further requirements. Identify at least three combinations of answers that the tree permits and that no one actually defends, and diagnose each: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Flag at least two pairs of leaves your instrument cannot genuinely separate for a lay reader — I suspect hollow immortality and the prudential rejection will be among them — and say what further question would separate them if the reader were willing to do the formalism. Finally, end with a path key: for each leaf, the answer string that reaches it.

Attach an author's name to a position only where you are confident the attribution is correct; otherwise describe the position and say explicitly that you are leaving it unnamed. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations of any kind.
