I work in quality engineering at a plant that makes injection-molded parts for automotive suppliers, so my whole professional vocabulary is measurement systems analysis, gauge R&R, control charts, capability studies (Cp/Cpk), sampling plans, OC curves, common cause vs special cause. I've been a black belt for about fifteen years and I run our annual MSA and gage calibration program, which is to say I think about statistics all day but almost entirely as "is my process stable, is my measurement system adequate, is my output within spec."

My sister is a postdoc in social psychology and over the holidays she tried to explain the replication crisis to me, plus the frequentist-versus-Bayesian fight that sits underneath it. I read a couple of the overview pieces she sent afterward — the one arguing that most published findings are probably false, and something about a big project that tried to redo a pile of published psychology experiments and got a much weaker picture the second time around. I could follow the individual complaints: people fiddling with their data until the result crosses the magic threshold, stopping the study early because it started looking good, running twenty tests and reporting the one that lit up, journals only publishing the hits and never the misses, studies too small to detect the effects they claim. But every time I tried to think about them in my own terms I ended up somewhere suspiciously comfortable, like "that's just a bad gauge study" or "they don't know whether their process is even in control." My sister said that flattens the actual disagreement, but she couldn't quite articulate why, and honestly I suspect she just thinks her framework is deeper.

So here's what I want. Take the replication-crisis argument, including the methodological dispute between the frequentist and Bayesian camps about what a p-value does and doesn't license, and restate it as faithfully as you can in the language I actually use — measurement systems and %GRR, stability and control limits, process capability, acceptance sampling and producer's/consumer's risk, special vs common cause. Not as cute metaphors; I want the real mapping, the way you'd write it if you were forced to express their claims in my notation. Then give me the residue report, and be blunt about it: which claims came across clean, which ones you had to bend or quietly re-describe to make them fit, and which ones have no counterpart in my toolkit whatsoever.

That last bucket is the part I actually care about, because I think it's where the interesting question lives. When the Bayesians complain that a p-value can't express evidence for a hypothesis and that the frequentists have no principled way to fold in what you already knew before the study ran, I genuinely don't know where to put that in my world — I don't talk about belief, I talk about process behavior and conformance to spec, and my priors basically live in the choice of control limits and the sampling plan rather than in any number I update. So I want you to tell me which way to read the gap. Is my vocabulary missing something real, something the QE framework simply has no room to represent? Or is their disagreement less substantive than it sounds, two dialects over one underlying decision problem that my plant-floor framing actually makes clearer? And while you're in there, call me out wherever the translation tempts you to import my assumptions and pass them off as theirs — the bias I'd expect is toward reducing everything to a measurement-system problem or a stability problem, and I'd rather you flag that tendency than let it slide.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
