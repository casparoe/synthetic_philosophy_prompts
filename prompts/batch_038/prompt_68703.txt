My social choice reading group spent an hour on Sen's liberal paradox and two people ended up in a fight I coudn't referee. Setup we were working with: one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, two people — call them Lewd and Prude. Lewd ranks Prude-reads-it > Lewd-reads-it > burned-unread; Prude ranks burned-unread > Prude-reads-it > Lewd-reads-it. Minimal liberty makes Lewd decisive over (Lewd reads, burned) and Prude decisive over (Prude reads, burned); Pareto says Prude-reads beats Lewd-reads because they agree on that one; transitivity through the two liberal verdicts says the opposite. Then it went:

1. A: so that's it, liberty and Pareto are incompatible, full stop — no social ranking can honor both, and welfare economics has to give one of them up.
2. B: massively overread. Look at what's doing the work: Pareto only bites because Lewd ranks Prude's reading above his own, i.e. a preference about somebody else's private business. Restrict everyone to ranking states by their own reading alone and there's no unanimity anywhere, so the cycle just goes away. The theorem is about a pathological domain, not about rights.
3. A: you can't ban those preferences. Caring about other people's conduct isn't a pathology, it's most of morality — Prude not wanting Lewd to enjoy the book is the same shape as me not wanting you to smoke in my car. And domain restriction is precisely the maneuver impossibility results are supposed to make you suspicious of, unless you have a principled line for which other-regarding preferences get to count.
4. B: then stop fixing it in the preference space and fix the definition of a right. A right is a game form: it says which actions are open to whom, and you evaluate what comes out of people exercising them. Sen's condition isn't "Lewd may choose," it's "the social ranking must track Lewd's preference over that pair no matter what anyone else thinks" — a much stronger and weirder thing to call a right. Build rights out of action sets and there's no contradiction left to explain.
5. A: that's changing the subject rather than answering it. A game-form right only guarantees you an option, never that you get what you want — and it leaves the real question standing, which is whether the social ranking can be both Paretian and liberty-respecting. Telling me not to ask about rankings isn't an answer about rankings.
