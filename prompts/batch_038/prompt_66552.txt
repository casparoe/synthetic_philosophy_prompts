A friend and I argued until two in the morning about Hacking's looping effects, and I should like a third party to adjudicate a narrow point rather than relitigate the whole debate. Her position is that what determines whether a looping effect threatens psychiatric realism is its loudness: a category whose prevalence explodes once the label circulates — patient communities, self-diagnosis, identity uptake — is on her account the safer one, because the feedback is visible and therefore eventually corrected, whereas a category that quietly shifts its extension through institutional gatekeeping, with no response at all from the people labelled, drifts unnoticed and is where a realist ought actually to worry. My reply was that visibility buys corrective pressure only where an independent handle on the pre-classificatory pattern survives, and that loud looping tends to destroy precisely that handle.

To test this, rank the following five kinds by how damaging their looping profile is to the claim that the category tracks something that was there before we classified it, most damaging first. All five are stipulated, so evaluate the profile rather than the plausibility of the description, and do not go looking for the real disorders behind them.

(a) A mood diagnosis whose label passed into ordinary speech, whose criteria were not revised at any point in the period in question, and whose survey-based prevalence rose roughly fivefold across two decades, alongside an organized patient community that recruits through the label.

(b) A childhood behavioural diagnosis whose criteria were broadened across two successive editions of the manual, producing a matching rise in prevalence, and whose broadening was met by organized protest from the people labelled and their families, resulting in a partial rollback.

(c) A dissociative condition recognized almost exclusively within the inpatient population of a single treatment centre, with no patient organization, no self-diagnosis and no uptake elsewhere, yet rising over fifteen years from zero to a substantial share of that centre's admissions.

(d) A syndrome whose name has been familiar from decades of media coverage but whose reported prevalence has been flat for forty years across jurisdictions with very different clinical cultures and reimbursement rules.

(e) A category applied by courts and insurers rather than by clinicians, encountered by the labelled only through the gatekeeping institution and never through a community of the labelled, and whose recorded incidence moves in step with changes in that institution's incentive structure.

Deliverable: exactly six numbered items and nothing else, with no introduction and no conclusion. The first five give, in your ranked order, each kind's letter and its position, with one or two sentences of justification; the sixth states in one or two sentences whether the loud/quiet distinction survives your ordering, and which of us it favours.

If you think the request rests on a confused premise — that these five profiles are not commensurable on any single dimension of damage to realism, or that loudness is not the sort of property that can be ordered this way — say so plainly in place of the ranking, and tell me what you would need to be shown instead.
