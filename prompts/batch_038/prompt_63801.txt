Third year in epistemology, dissertation on understanding and cognitive offloading. Next week our reading group has to present a case to my supervisor and predict where she'll land, and I'm the one presenting, so I want to get this right.

She's an odd interlocutor: she's completely fine with treating a person-plus-their-tools as one cognitive system in principle, so you get nowhere with her by pointing out that the thinking happened outside the skull. Her verdicts so far:

1. Delivery driver, same route daily for three years, turn-by-turn the whole way. Drives it flawlessly. Can't sketch the neighbourhood or say what he'd do if the bridge were out. Verdict: he doesn't understand the layout of the area.
2. Mathematician who runs every long derivation through a CAS and never checks by hand because there isn't time. Instantly knows when the output is garbage, can reconstruct any individual step if pushed, can tell you why the answer had to come out the way it did. Verdict: she understands the mathematics.
3. Clinician using a diagnostic ranking tool. Better hit rate than her colleagues. Can't say why the top candidate is favoured, and admits that in the one case where the tool flagged something she had independent reason to think was dangerous for that patient, she'd have gone with the tool. Verdict: doesn't understand the diagnosis.
4. Historian who writes with a tool that reads and synthesises the secondary literature for her. She hasn't personally read most of what it summarised and couldn't reproduce the synthesis unaided. But she's caught it inventing sources twice, and she can take apart each of the three rival readings of the events and say exactly where each one goes wrong. Verdict: she understands the debate.
5. Not a case, but two asides she's made. Once: you can hold every true belief about a subject and still not understand it, and the question to ask about any tool is what happens when the tool gets it wrong. Another time she described understanding as knowing what depends on what. She doesnt seem to notice those pull in different directions, and I can't tell which one does the work for her.

The case I have to present: a cohort member drafted a whole chapter with a model as his sparring partner — it threw objections at him, he answered, it revised, he revised, six weeks of that. In a mock viva he answered every why and what-if question correctly and in his own words, including which assumption is load-bearing and what breaks if you drop it, and what would decide between the two rival accounts. Two things though. Asked to build the argument cold with no tool in the room, he stalls completely. And a friend fed him a confident but bogus objection to one of his true claims as a test — instead of rejecting it he spent an hour weakening the true claim to accommodate it. He's never caught the model making a technical error and says he wouldn't know how to check one except by asking it again.

So what does she say about him? I want an actual prediction with a number on it, not a menu of readings — if you think it's a genuine coin flip for her, say that plainly and tell me what tips it. Tell me which rule or rules are consistent with all four case verdicts (I assume several fit, and that the historian case knocks some out), which of the survivors the new case forces apart, and whether you expect a split verdict — understands this but not that — and if so exactly where the split falls and on what grounds. Then give me one further case I could float at the workshop that would settle which rule she's actually running.
