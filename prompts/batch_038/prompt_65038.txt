I am in the fourth year of a doctorate in political philosophy; my dissertation concerns what a polity owes its members once it has been shown that no rule of collective decision can be made immune to insincere declaration — the two results published in 1973 and 1975 are the hinge of my second chapter. In three weeks my department closes a public evening series on the mathematics of collective choice, and the format is unusual: each session opens with a commissioned piece of short fiction, read aloud by an actor, and only afterwards does anyone give a talk. My supervisor has assigned me the opening reading, and I have been unable to produce anything that is not either a lecture with characters in it or a fable with the moral stapled to the last paragraph. I would like you to write the story in my place, and I want to be as precise as I can about what has to happen in it, because the whole difficulty for me has been the architecture rather than the prose.

Set it in a cold pre-industrial confederation of three valley communes sharing one river, with exactly one hundred delegates — Vessa sends forty-five, Orun forty, Kelt fifteen — who write their rankings on slips, fold them, seal them in wax, and have them read out by three counting clerks. The federation's charter contains one clause that everyone treats as its proudest possession: the rule of decision must be such that no delegate can ever fare better by declaring preferences other than the ones they hold. Build the story in four movements, and in each movement put the arithmetic on the page, so that a listener with a pencil could verify it: (1) Under the existing rule, most first declarations wins, the question is where to build the common granary — the ford, the pass, or the marsh causeway — and the honest tally stands at forty-five for the ford, forty for the pass, fifteen for the causeway; Kelt holds causeway before pass before ford, sees the causeway cannot carry, declares the pass, and the pass wins fifty-five to forty-five, so the smallest commune has overturned the largest by lying and everyone in the hall understands precisely how it was done. (2) The charter is then repaired by adopting a second ballot between the two options with the most first declarations, and the following year, on the question of which reach of the river shall carry the new ferry, the numbers stand at forty delegates for the upper reach preferring upper before middle before lower, thirty-five preferring middle before lower before upper, and twenty-five preferring lower before upper before middle; the sincere count sends the upper and middle reaches to the second ballot, where the upper reach wins sixty-five to thirty-five, and Orun — for whom the upper reach is the worst of the three — declares the lower reach first, remakes the pairing, and wins the lower reach sixty to forty. Let the young clerk spend a page establishing, to her own relief, that the second ballot itself cannot be lied about at all, before she understands that the lie has not been abolished but moved upstream, into the choosing of which two options are permitted to face one another. (3) A delegate proposes the urn: every delegate's name on a slip, one drawn, and the drawn party's honest ranking decides alone with no further counting. The clerk must concede, truthfully and against her own side, that this cannot be gamed at all — a lie only alters which of your own preferences is honoured should your slip be drawn, and you cannot increase the chance of your slip — and the assembly must reject it anyway, because the urn can crown the one outcome that every other commune ranks below an outcome they could have had, and because no one wants the harvest decided by a piece of paper. (4) The charter is narrowed so that the assembly may only ever decide between two named options, and the relief this brings should be felt before the second chill arrives: whoever draws up the pair decides everything, and the pair is drawn up by clerks. The final vote is whether to vest that power, and the decision itself, in a single officer for life — and because that question is binary, it is the first vote in the federation's history that no delegate can improve upon by lying. They pass it. You may, if you want a hinge for the middle of the story, have the clerk ask what happens when the count comes out even at fifty apiece, and let the hall discover what answering that question amounts to.

On execution: two thousand to two thousand six hundred words, with a title that would not put anyone in the audience in mind of mathematics. The result itself is never named and its authors are never mentioned; the words strategy-proof, social choice function, and impossibility theorem do not appear; and no character, at any point, states a general claim about rules of decision in general — the generality has to be felt the way epidemiology is felt in a good plague novel. Every tally must be internally consistent with the declared rankings I have given you, and if you find you need to adjust my numbers, keep the structure of each movement intact and tell me in a short note afterwards what you changed and why. The protagonist is the youngest of the three counting clerks, the only person in the hall who can do this arithmetic in her head while the slips are being opened; her counterpart is the senior delegate from Kelt, who lies more gracefully than anyone and must not be written as a villain, since she lies for fifteen households that would otherwise be outvoted for a generation, and the moral weight of the story should fall on the fact that her lie is reasonable. Keep the register material and period-appropriate — river ice, damp paper, wax, a hall with one stove, ink that freezes — and no allegorical names that announce their own function. End on a decision rather than a revelation, with no closing paragraph that tells the reader what the story meant: I want the half of the room that knows the result to recognize it in the third movement and feel slightly unwell, and the half that has never heard of it to believe they have simply been told a story about a small republic that saves itself by destroying itself.
