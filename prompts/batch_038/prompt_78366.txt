I write the practical certification exam for sterile-compounding technicians — the people who actually make IV admixtures in hospital pharmacies. About 2,000 candidates a year, roughly 120 scored stations and items that we rewrite on a rolling basis. Lately I've been stuck on the inversion idea that keeps coming up in Darwin and Turing, the one about getting design or intelligent-looking behavior out of a process that understands nothing at all, competence with no comprehension in the loop. I can't shake it because our exam is a machine for certifying competence, but half my item writers clearly believe we're certifying comprehension, and nobody has ever written down which claim we're actually making. It isn't academic for us. We've had incidents where a tech executed every step correctly right up to the moment something off-script happened — a garbled order, a hood alarm mid-batch, a vial that looked wrong — and then did something that was defensible-looking and catastrophic, and in at least one case they had scored in the top band on our exam two years earlier.

My current informal screen is bad and I know it's bad. When I want to know whether an item tests comprehension rather than rote, I ask three things: the perturbation test, where I change one detail that shouldn't matter and see if they still get it right; the explanation test, where I ask them to say why the step is done that way; and the teaching test, where they walk a new hire through it. Problem is the last two mostly seem to measure articulateness. Our most experienced candidate last cycle — 22 years on the floor, no documented errors, trains everyone's new hires in practice — could not answer a single why-item and failed. A six-month program grad who recites the textbook back sailed through, and I'd bet money she'd freeze in a real deviation. Also, if comprehension just is a richer, more flexible kind of competence, then my whole two-category distinction collapses into "harder items," and I want someone to tell me straight whether that's what's happening here and what to put in its place if so.

So: turn the inversion idea into a procedure my item-writing team can run without any philosophy background. I want it in stages — first a triage that decides, for each competency in the blueprint, whether comprehension is load-bearing at all or whether reliable rote is genuinely enough (I suspect it's enough for some things and I want that said out loud rather than assumed away), with the questions asked in order and what counts as a sufficient answer to each; then, for the competencies where it is load-bearing, a design and scoring rule for items, including how far to push a perturbation before you're testing something else entirely; then the places the procedure stops with a no and the places it hands the call to a human because it's a values question. Hard constraints: nine item writers, one three-day retreat a year plus monthly half-days, proctors score with pre-printed checklists and have no clinical judgment training, candidate testing time is fixed and cannot grow, and every failing score has to survive an appeal, because we cost people their jobs — so anything that requires a proctor to rate the quality of an explanation is legally exposed unless you can make it objective. Run it end to end on three of our competencies: the garbing and hand-hygiene sequence for cleanroom entry, the response to a pressure-differential alarm that goes off halfway through a batch, and reconciling a controlled-substance count that's one vial short at shift change. I expect at least one of those to come out in a way I don't want, and I'd rather you flag it than soften it. Finish with what the procedure can't do — which of our real failures it will still miss, and where it will confidently give us a number that's just fluency wearing a lab coat.
