For an independent study in my school's philosophy sequence I am comparing two arguments that look structurally identical but that people file under completely different headings, and I would like help deciding whether the resemblance is deep or only cosmetic.

The first is the surprise examination. A teacher announces that there will be an exam sometime during the coming week on a day the class cannot know in advance. One student reasons that the exam cannot fall on Friday, because by Thursday night only one day would remain and the class would know; Friday being ruled out, it cannot fall on Thursday either, for the same reason; and so the argument walks backward to Monday and concludes no such exam can be given at all. Then the exam arrives on Wednesday and genuinely startles everyone.

The second is the centipede game. In the version I have been working through, Alice and Bob alternate for a hundred turns, and a pot of money grows substantially each time it is passed. At any turn, the player to move can either end the game by taking the pot — collecting slightly more than they would have collected by passing and letting the opponent take on the very next turn — or pass, which is what makes the pot large enough for both of them to do well. Backward induction says Alice should take immediately at the first turn, and in the experimental sessions I have read about, subjects reliably do not.

Here is the comparison I want tested. In both cases the reasoning is a chain of steps each of which looks impeccable on its own, and in both cases the conclusion is flatly contradicted by what actually happens. A classmate of mine insists that the two failures are nonetheless of different kinds. In the examination case, he says, the trouble lies entirely in the reasoner's knowledge: the announcement is precisely the sort of thing the class cannot consistently treat as known, so the chain snaps at its first link, and the teacher's promise can still come out true. Nothing in the world goes wrong. In the centipede case, he says, nothing anyone asserted undermines itself; instead the chain rests on an assumption about the players, and that is what breaks — after Alice passes, Bob's evidence tells him she is not the kind of player the argument requires, so passing is his rational reply, and the argument that told him to take has destroyed its own premise.

Is that asymmetry real? Put differently: is "the reasoner cannot know the announcement" a genuinely different species of failure from "the players cannot both be commonly known to be rational," or are these two ways of describing a single retreat — backward induction collapsing the moment the reasoner must model how its own output will be received and acted on? Please tell me where the analogy genuinely breaks down, if it does, and which of the two puzzles you think has the more convincing resolution available to it. I am after the adjudication of his claim rather than a tour of every position that has ever been taken on either puzzle, and I would rather not have names and titles listed than have the argument itself.
