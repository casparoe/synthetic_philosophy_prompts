My housemate Nadia is halfway through a stats masters and my friend Eli trades options for a living, and last thursday they got into a genuinely bad argument over a coin game Eli invented to decide who had to clean the bathroom. The rules: flip a fair coin until it lands heads and count how many flips that took, call that number n. Heads on flip 1 pays you $2, flip 2 costs you $1.33, flip 3 pays $1.60, flip 4 costs about $2.29, and the general rule is you get (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n/(2n-1) dollars — so the stakes keep climbing, heads on the tenth flip is roughly +$54 and on the twentieth roughly +$26,887. Eli pointed out that each outcome's contribution to the average is just its payoff times 2^-n, i.e. ±1/(2n-1), so the whole game adds up to 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - 1/7 + ..., which is pi/4, about 79 cents. He said that's what the game is worth and he'd cheerfully charge 75 cents a play and still be giving you a deal.

Then it went sideways. Here's the back and forth as best I can reconstruct it, in order:

(1) Nadia said the game doesn't have an expected value at all. The series 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - ... only converges conditionally — strip the signs and 1 + 1/3 + 1/5 + ... blows up — so the sum isn't a property of the game, it's a property of the order you happen to add things in. She said she'd rearranged the terms on an envelop and got minus five dollars, and could have gotten a million if she'd bothered.

(2) Eli said you're not allowed to rearrange, because the order isn't up for grabs: the flips happen in time, one after another, and which flip heads shows up on is a physical fact about the process, not a bookkeeping convention. Fix that ordering and the answer is 79 cents, full stop.

(3) Nadia said fine, but it's moot, because nobody can actually play the game — you'd need an unlimited bankroll and unlimited patience, and the second you truncate it at some finite number of flips the value changes. So the 79 cents isn't telling you anything about any gamble a real person could ever face.

(4) Eli said that's a different and cheaper complaint: the same is true of the St Petersburg game and of pretty much any gamble with a fat enough tail, and those obviously have expected values, so "you can't play it" can't show the value fails to exist. He added that if you played his game over and over, your average winnings per round would settle down around 79 cents, and that's just what expected value means.

(5) Nadia said that last claim was flat wrong, that the averages don't settle down anywhere, and that this was actually the clean way to make her original point: if the long-run averages misbehave like that then the expectation provably doesn't exist, and anyway in real probability theory the expectation of a random variable is only defined when the average of the absolute values is finite, which here it obviously isn't.

(6) Eli admitted he wasn't sure about the averages but said it was beside the point, since he was only ever talking about one play of the game, and for a single play 79 cents is the fair price — and if Nadia thinks otherwise she should name her own number instead of hiding behind "undefined." She said "undefined" is the number, and that refusing to name a price isn't hiding, it's the whole point. Then he said her refusing to pay 75 cents for a game worth 79 cents was irrational, and she said agreeing to pay anything for a game with no value would be the irrational thing.

They both turned to me to referee and I honestly couldn't follow half of it. Can you go through moves 1 to 6 one at a time and rate each — clearly right, partly right, clearly wrong — and explain what's actually going on in each? I'm most curious about whether Eli's "the ordering is fixed by time" reply in move 2 really answers the rearrangement worry, whether his St Petersburg comparison in move 4 supports him or backfires, and whether move 5 is as decisive as Nadia thinks it is. And then tell me what neither of them said that they should have — including whether the 75-cent offer at the end is actually something Nadia should take.
