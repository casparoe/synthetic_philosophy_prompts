I write literary thrillers — three out with the same publisher, fourth contracted and due in about four months, 95k, currently sitting at 60k and going badly. Here's the problem. My books run on people talking other people round: a recruiter for a movement that starts off sincere and ends up horrible, a mentor, a source being kept talking by a journalist, a husband. Two things have gone wrong at once. My editor says the antagonist's big scene in chapter nine isn't chilling enough, that "he sounds like a nice man having a good chat". Separately, my best beta reader has told me the mentor — who I love, who is supposed to be the moral ballast of the whole series — is creeping her out, that he grooms the heroine into taking a posting he wants taken, and that I clearly don't realise I've written it that way. So one scene is too soft and one is accidentally sinister, and I have no way of telling which is which apart from vibes.

What I want is a procedure I can run on a scene. Not an essay about manipulation, not a defence of a theory. I did philosophy A-level thirty years ago and remember roughly nothing, and my editor has done none at all and needs to be able to use the same thing when she marks up my draft. So: the questions to ask, in the order to ask them; what counts as a proper answer to each; where the thing bottoms out in a verdict I can actually act on (cut, revise, keep as is, or this isn't a scene problem it's a structure problem); and — crucially — where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision back to me because its a question of what this book is for rather than a fact about the page. There are maybe forty scenes I'd flag across the draft and I can give each one ten or fifteen minutes with a notebook. It also has to work on a single scene in isolation, because I can't reread the whole novel every time I want to check one page.

Ground it in whatever the philosophy of this actually knows. I assume there's more to it than "manipulation is when you bypass someone's reason", but if that turns out to be the whole of the usable content, say so and don't pad it. Name names if it helps you organise the thing; no reading list, I won't read one.

What it has to cope with, because all of these are in this book:

- the persuader who never says anything false and never hides anything relevant, but has chosen the week, the room, and the eleven minutes of silence before he opens his mouth;
- manipulation spread across chapters, where no single scene fails any test but the arc adds up to something;
- the target who knows exactly what's being done to her and lets it happen anyway, and in one case enjoys it;
- and the layer I suspect everyone forgets: the reader. Half my problems aren't character-to-character, they're me-to-reader, and I want the procedure to tell me whether that's the same question or a different one.

Then run it on three scenes.

One. Chapter nine: Douglas, my recruiter, talking to Ffion, 22, whose brother drowned in February. He doesn't lie to her once. He tells her plainly what the group will ask of her — the money, the hours, two friends she'll have to drop — and he answers every question straight. But he has picked the week, he has picked the cold room off the back of a memorial service, and he lets her cry for what I've written as eleven minutes before he says anything at all. She walks out a member. My editor thinks it's too warm. I think it's the best thing in the book. I'm probably wrong about atleast one of those.

Two. Peter, 68, the mentor, in his kitchen, getting Nessa to take the posting she's already refused twice. Nothing he says is untrue. He's self-deprecating about his own career, he tells a long funny story about messing up in Beirut in the nineties, he makes the tea, and at the low point of the conversation he says 'I'd never ask you to do this if I didn't think you were better at it than me' — which is true and which he believes. She leaves having said yes.

Three. A narrator one. Close third on Nessa for a whole chapter, three weeks after she's decided not to tell her husband something. I report her errands, her thoughts about the weather, a row with a plumber. No sentence is false and the narration never asserts she's told him. I just don't mention it, and the reader comes out believing the husband knows. Is that manipulation, or is that simply what having a point of view means? And — this is the bit I actually need — how does the procedure tell that apart from the identical technique deployed to set up a cheap reveal on page 300? I'm capable of both and I want to know which one I've done.

One quick stress test while you're in there: Peter and his wife of forty years. He does the washing up while narrating his own martyrdom; she has known precisely what he's up to since 1987 and finds it funny. If your procedure calls that manipulation, I'll assume the procedure is broken.

Finally, be honest about what it can't do — where it will misfire, which distinctions you've had to fudge or drop to keep it runnable in fifteen minutes, whether per-scene verdicts can ever add up to a verdict on a book, and whether there's a real risk I end up sanding off exactly the quality that makes the good scenes good. Give me at least one place where the answer it produces is one I won't like.
