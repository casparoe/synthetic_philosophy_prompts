So I've been doing paralegal work in London for about a year and there's a cluster of habits in this system I can't get my head around. I want someone to tell me what picture of law they only make sense under.

Three things, all from the Human Rights Act 1998 and how it actually plays out. First: when a court thinks an Act of Parliament clashes with the Convention rights, it's supposed to strain the wording — read things in, read things down, bend the statute until it fits — for as far as that's possible. Second: when the bending can't honestly be done, the senior courts can issue a declaration of incompatibility, and that declaration by its own terms doesn't affect the statute's validity, doesn't stop it operating, doesn't stop it being enforced, and isn't even binding on the parties. The person who got the declaration usually still loses their actual case. Parliament might amend it later, sometimes a minister patches it by order, or nothing happens at all. Third: before a bill's second reading the minister in charge has to publish a written statement saying either that in his view the bill is compatible with the Convention rights, or that he is unable to say that but the government wants the House to proceed anyway.

I'm American and I grew up with courts just voiding statutes outright, so all three feel strange to me.

What does this apparatus presuppose? It seems committed to the idea that a rule can be fully valid law while carrying an official, published, on-the-record moral defect, and that noting the defect is the end of the court's job. Am I reading too much into it? And the two halves pull opposite ways — bending the text treats the moral problem as something to be interpreted away, whereas the declaration treats it as something to be logged and walked past. Can a single theory hold both, or is the practice papering over a disagreement it never resolved?

And what is that second kind of ministerial statement doing, exactly? Someone standing up saying I can't tell you this is rights-compatability compliant, please pass it. What has to be true about law, and about the relationship between the people making it and the people applying it, for that to be a legitimate sentence to say out loud in a legislature?

Tell me which rival views this whole setup quietly rules out, where it's genuinely underdetermined so that two opposed theories could each live with it comfortably, and what would have to change concretely in courtrooms and in drafting offices if you swapped in the view it's supposedly rejecting. My flatmate says there's some seventy-year-old argument this all comes down to but he won't spell it out for me.
