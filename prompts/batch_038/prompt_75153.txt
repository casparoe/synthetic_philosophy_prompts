I sit on the online conduct panel of a mid-sized learned society — call it the Association, about 4,000 members, mostly academic with some policy and industry types. There are three things we can do to people: remove or annotate a post, suspend a member's access to the forum and the members' mailing list for a fixed period, or bar someone from the annual conference and from society offices. We've been going about six years and we've accumulated a body of decisions we're supposed to be consistent with, but nobody's ever actually written down the principle, and the membership is split down the middle on whether we're too soft or too trigger-happy. Our new chair wants us to spend one whole meeting articulating what we've in fact been doing before we hear the next case, and I'm trying to get my head round it beforehand.

Our record so far, roughly:

- A senior fellow organised a coordinated campaign to mass-report a junior member's posts until the platform's automated systems suspended her account. We suspended him from the forum for six months.
- A member posted a confidently wrong claim about a clinical intervention that got shared far outside the forum. We removed it, put up a correction from the society's science comittee, and left it at that — no loss of access.
- A member spent two years posting abrasive but non-abusive criticism of the president and the executive, incompetence being the mildest of it. We declined to act, on the grounds that a members' forum is precisely where that sort of thing belongs.
- A member posted an anonymous-but-easily-identifiable account of a colleague's psychiatric leave, framed as concern for that colleague's students. Twelve-month suspension plus a required written apology.
- A non-member blogger published several members' home addresses. We couldn't touch his blog, but we banned him from all society events indefinitely.
- Some genuinely unpleasant jokes from a member's undergraduate years resurfaced, with nothing similar since. We ruled that pre-membership conduct which hasn't been repeated is out of scope.
- A member drafted and circulated a petition asking a professional regulator to strike a fellow member off over a theoretical position the man held. We issued a formal censure, published to the whole membership, but he kept his access.

Next month's case: a very prominent member — considerably more senior than anyone we've ever sanctioned, one of the Association's big names — filed a formal research-misconduct allegation against a rival's most-cited paper, through the rival's own university and entirely within that university's procedures. He then announced on the forum that he had done so, with a summary of his concerns. The allegation was dismissed as unfounded eighteen months later. In the meantime the rival, himself a professor at a reasonable institution, lost one grant and withdrew from a recruitment process. The complainant says he acted in good faith and that raising such concerns is a duty; several members say the timing and the publicity show he knew exactly what he was doing; we have no evidence either way beyond the public record.

So what should we decide, if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I'd like you to work out which rules genuinely fit all seven earlier verdicts. I can see a harm-to-identifiable-people rule, a power-asymmetry rule, something about whether the Association's own mechanisms were misused, and something about scope and timing — but I suspect at least one or two of those fails a precedent on inspection, and I'd bet there's a better candidate I haven't thought of. Then say what each surviving rule implies for the new case, which way you'd vote and why, and which of the old decisions is the closest analogue, with an honest note about where the analogy breaks down. Since I'll have to defend this in the room: if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, spell out what principle we'd be adopting by going each way, and describe the kind of future case that would make that choice look foolish. It'd also help to flag which function of sanction each candidate rule quietly assumes — payback, deterrence, expressing what the Association stands for, or just protecting members from one another — because the chair is a legal philosopher and will ask. Finally, propose one hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the surviving rules, so we can put it to the panel as a test.
