At a food-security seminar last month a speaker closed a discussion of Bengal in 1943 with the line that famines are always about distribution and never about production, and treated it as settling two questions at once: whether those deaths were caused by shortage, and whether any famine can properly be called a natural disaster. I know the Sen-inflected version of the point — that aggregate food availability in Bengal did not fall dramatically, that wartime inflation let some groups bid food away from others, and that certain occupational groups lost the purchasing power to command what was there; I also know this is why the literature contrasts availability decline with entitlement failure. But the Irish blight and the Great Leap Forward do not look like that description at all. So I want the slogan taken apart rather than defended. What distinct claims could it be making — about aggregate supply in a given year, about food being physically present versus exchangeable by the starving, about the level at which one measures (national, regional, household, class), about what would actually have prevented the deaths, and about where responsibility lies? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; which of them the Bengal case establishes; whether they can come apart in a single famine; and which, if any, would license the further move from distribution to political crime.
