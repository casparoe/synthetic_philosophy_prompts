I am four years into a novel set during the first eighteen months after the collapse of an authoritarian regime in a fictional central European country, and the chapter that will make or break the book is a courtroom scene. My editor keeps writing "drama!" in the margins, and my legal reader — a retired appellate judge who sends me long, unsolicited letters — keeps insisting that the interesting question is not what the defendant believed but what the clerk in the records office believed. Both of them have, in different ways, pushed me toward the old quarrel between legal positivism and natural law, and I have spent a year reading around it in the Hart–Fuller tradition, including the postwar informer cases that shadow this whole debate. What I have found is that the two camps largely talk past each other, and I am determined not to write a novel in which one character is a mouthpiece for the right answer. So I want the document they never produce.

Here are my invented facts, which I ask you to treat as fixed. Twenty-two years ago, the regime enacted Decree 41, requiring any hospital administrator who became aware that a physician had treated a patient without filing the state's registration forms to report that physician to the security authorities on pain of imprisonment. Decree 41 was passed by the procedure the regime's own constitution prescribed; it was published in the official gazette before it took effect; it applied prospectively; it was general, short, and unambiguous; it was never amended or secretly reinterpreted; and ordinary courts applied it in reasoned, published judgments for two decades. Dr. Lena Brandt, a hospital registrar, reported a surgeon who had been treating wounded demonstrators. The surgeon served eleven years and was released half-paralyzed. Brandt had, in addition, a grievance: he had twice blocked her promotion. The new government's transitional court must now decide her criminal liability, and the new constitution contains a dignity clause but no provision addressing pre-transition decrees.

Draft the joint statement that a scrupulous Hartian positivist and a scrupulous Fullerian natural lawyer — both of whom agree that Brandt's conduct was odious and that the transitional court ought not simply to execute Decree 41 — could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration over this case. I want: a numbered section of claims both can sign, including claims about what Decree 41 was and about what the new judges should do; a disagreements table with columns for the proposition at issue, the positivist's formulation in wording she would accept as her own, the natural lawyer's formulation in wording he would accept as his own, and a judgment whether the divergence is substantive or verbal; and three cruxes, each with the argument or matched pair of cases that both would accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction written down in advance. At least one crux must be whether the inner morality of law — generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, congruence between official action and declared rule — has any purchase at all against a measure that satisfies every one of those desiderata and is nonetheless monstrous; another must be whether the transitional judges' refusal to apply Decree 41 identifies what the law always was or instead makes new law with retrospective effect. Tell me plainly which parts of the quarrel are only about the word law, and whether a novelist should care.

Lead with a bottom line of no more than three sentences stating what you take the real dispute to be. No footnotes, no citations, no reading list — I have the books. The entire document must not exceed 900 words.
