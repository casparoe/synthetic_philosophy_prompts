I'm a novelist — third book out in the spring — and for the last two years I've basically been living inside the head of a character who works as a freight analyst at a container port, a woman who can tell you to the penny what it costs to land a reefer of frozen chicken and who has quietly stopped buying a great many things. Researching her dragged me into all the philosophy about whether one person's choices make any difference when the harm is collective, and somewhere along the line I stopped being able to tell which views where hers and which were mine, which I'm told is a familiar occupational hazard. The problem is that I've been asked onto a panel about the book at a festival in the Borders in October, and sitting next to me will be a friend of mine, Rosalind, who teaches moral philosophy and is the least merciful conversationalist I know. She's already warned me that she'll make me state my position properly instead of in the slippery way novelists do it, so I've written it down and I'd like it checked over before I humiliate myself. Please treat every bit of this as mine rather than hers.

The package, as best I can put it into flat statements. (1) Whether an act is wrong comes down entirely to how things go because of it — I don't believe in side-constraints or duties that hold regardless of consequences, and I don't think motives or intentions make an act right or wrong, though obviously they matter to how we judge the person. (2) Given that, I think the inefficacy worry has real teeth: I can't point to a single soul whose life is worse because I flew to Naples once, or bought a pack of chicken thighs, or put a cross on a ballot paper, so those acts aren't wrong in the plain sense. (3) All the same, I'm certain you ought to fly less, eat less meat and vote, and that "I'm only one person" is a rubbish excuse; the way I've been justifying this to myself is the probabilistic one — somewhere along the chain there's a restocking order, a shipment, a threshold, and there's some tiny chance my purchase is the one that tips it, and a small chance of a large harm is still a genuine reason. (4) On climate in particular I don't really believe in thresholds at all: emissions simply accumulate, each tonne does its bit continuously, and nothing magic gets crossed — I've been treating that as reinforcing (3) rather than competing with it. (5) I'm also drawn to the thought that taking part in a collective harm makes you answerable for it whether or not your part makes any difference, call it complicity, and I don't think that's consequentialism wearing a fancy hat. (6) I vote in everything and I know perfectly well my vote won't decide anything; my reasons are expressive, it's a statement of who I take myself to be, and I think that sits fine with (1). (7) In these collective situations I keep saying that the individual and the collective come apart, and that act-consequentialism is self-defeating — that it's the theory which tells each of us to do the very thing that, done by all of us, makes the shared outcome worse; that's a large part of why I lean on (5). (8) On cases I'm firm that buying the chicken is wrong and eating it is not: if my host has bought it, or if it came out of a skip behind the supermarket, I'll eat it and I don't think I've done anything whatsoever.

So: go through these and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and how badly, worst first, and for each of those what I'd have to give up or restate and what the restatement costs me. I'm just as interested in the ones that only look like problems — if (7) or (6) is a non-issue, say so plainly and say why, since I've lost sleep over both — and I'd rather you told me one thing is fine than flattered me by finding fault everywhere. Then tell me which single change would tidy up the most damage and roughly what the position looks like once I've made it. Last of all, give me what you reckon Rosalind's opening question will be, the one I need an answer ready for. Please no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter, even if it runs long. And I don't want a lecture about climate change or a tour of the debate; I've read enough of that. I want the audit.
