I've been reading the communitarian-vs-liberal debates for a couple years now, mostly becuase of work — I do policy stuff around communal land tenure — and there's a pattern I keep running into that I can't find a word for. I've started to suspect there isn't one. Here's the pattern. A liberal says the state isn't in the business of promoting any particular way of life; it just sets up a neutral framework — individual title, contracts, courts, school to sixteen, one official language for public business — and inside that framework communities can live however they like, so long as members are free to leave. That sounds fine, because no belief is being suppressed and nobody is told what to think. But the framework isn't neutral in the way advertised, because it doesn't merely tolerate ways of life, it requires them to have a particular shape in order to be usable by it at all. To hold land under the neutral title system you have to be able to name individual owners, which a community that holds land in common and re-allots it by kinship can't do without becoming a different kind of community. To be a party to a contract you need members who are legally individuated and can be sued. For exit to be real the state has to make sure leavers have credentials and market capacities, which means years of exactly the schooling the community's apprenticeship system exists to replace. To be heard in court you need the official language. In every one of those cases the demand isn't "stop believing X." It's "be capable of doing what a liberal individual does, even if you never actually do it" — and the community is free to refuse, and free to disappear.

The part I think makes this different from the usual complaint is that it survives every concession the liberal is willing to make. Grant that the framework is neutral in its justification, with no appeal to any conception of the good anywhere in it. Grant that the effects are unequal but unintended. Grant that consent is real and exit is genuinely available. The requirement about internal form is still sitting there, and it's still there because a framework that has to be operable by individuals one at a time imposes an individual-shaped structure on anything that wants to operate within it, whatever its designers intended or believed. So it isn't just the neutrality-of-justification vs neutrality-of-effect point, since I'm not complaining about unequal outcomes, I'm complaining about a standing requirement on organizational form that never appears in anyone's statement of the framework. And it isn't quite Sandel or MacIntyre's line that liberalism is a comprehensive doctrine wearing a procedural costume, because I don't think you need any particular view of the person or the good to notice it — you just need the machinery to work. And it isn't paternalism, since nobody's welfare is being consulted at any point, and it isn't oppression by a majority either, because the same demand would show up in a framework designed by saints.

So — does this have a name? I've come across "the myth of liberal neutrality," liberalism-as-perfectionism, the accommodation cases about facially neutral rules that fall much harder on some groups, Shachar's paradox of multicultural vulnerability, Kymlicka on exit and internal restrictions, and all of them circle it and none of them is it. If one of those actually is it, tell me which and where I'm misreading them. If nothing fits, coin something: a definition with the boundaries drawn, plus how you'd tell it apart from (a) plain majority domination dressed up as procedure, (b) a framework that's neutral in principle but merely unequal in effect, and (c) the banal point that every institution carries a culture. And please don't just hand me a label, I want the carving. What counts as a case and what doesn't? For instance: does a rule that public contracts must be in writing count, given that an oral-economy community can just decline and take the loss — at what point does "you may opt out at a cost" turn into "you must reorganize"? Does the phenomenon need the framework to be justified in neutral terms, or would it also apply to an openly perfectionist state that happens to require the same forms? Is it about the state specifically, or would a professional licensing body or a bank's know-your-customer rules be instances too? Where does the thing stop?
