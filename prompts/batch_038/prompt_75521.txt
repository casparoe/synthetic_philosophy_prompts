I teach a seminar on the philosophy of history, and I would like to open our session on civilizational "collapse" with a piece of satire rather than another round of secondary literature. Please write an obituary, in the deadpan house style of a broadsheet's obituary page, for the Late Bronze Age palatial system of the eastern Mediterranean — the interlocking palace economies of the Aegean, Anatolia, the Levant, Cyprus and Egypt that came apart around 1200 BCE. Roughly 550 words.

The conventions of the genre should carry the argument, so please include: a cause-of-death paragraph in which the quoted specialists (all invented; do not put words in any real scholar's mouth) stand for the rival explanatory schools — drought, seismic clusters, the raids commemorated on the walls of Ramesses III's mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, the interruption of the copper and tin trade, and the systems view on which the tightly coupled redistribution economies were themselves the vulnerability, so that no single stressor is the culprit; one dissenting voice who has gone back through the file and reports that the majority of the destructions recorded at the canonical sites were misdated, inferred on thin evidence, or never happened at all; and a "survived by" line that quietly dismantles the premise, listing the Luwian and Aramean principalities of southeastern Anatolia and northern Syria whose rulers go on inscribing themselves with Hittite titles and imagery in hieroglyphic Luwian for four more centuries, an attenuated but continuous Egypt and Assyria, the Phoenician ports that gained autonomy from both, Greek-speaking communities in Cyprus and at Lefkandi — set against what genuinely did not survive: the Linear B archive, after which the Aegean goes without writing for some four hundred years and resumes only with an alphabet borrowed from Phoenician and adapted to carry vowels.

The target is the collapse genre itself — its habit of naming a body, assigning a single cause, and closing with an edifying lesson for the present — not any particular living author. Stay deadpan; never state the objection editorially. The reader should be led by the structure alone to the question of whether a way of organizing people died or only its bookkeeping class, and whether "collapse" is even the right sort of predicate for that. Include the conventional in-lieu-of-flowers gesture and a paragraph of instruction to the living, both played straight enough that their absurdity is felt but never announced.

Since I want my students to argue with a choice rather than infer one, finish with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of FAILED, TRANSFORMED, or ILL-POSED — and write the piece so that, whichever you select, the reader can feel what the other two cost.
