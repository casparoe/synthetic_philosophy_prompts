I'm the TA for a fourth-year philosophy of science course and I've somehow ended up writing one of the two essay questions for the final. My section covered the interpretations of probability, and I really want to dodge the question every student has prepped for in advance — compare and contrast frequentism, subjectivism, and the propensity view — because they all recite the three slogans and then close with something wise-sounding about how each has strengths and weaknesses and the truth is probably somewhere in between. So I've been building a case that I think forces the issue instead of just inviting a survey, and I'd like you to turn it into a real exam question with a model answer and a marking scheme.

Here's the case. A small vineyard co-op owns a hand-built grape press from 1911; the workshop that made it closed in the 1930s and nothing else like it exists anywhere. There are 40 harvests of logbooks, roughly 200 cycles per harvest, so about 8,000 cycles in total, and it broke down mid-cycle 8 times — though 6 of those 8 came in a single five-year stretch after a botched repair, and the frame has been rebuilt twice since. This year a mechanic inspected it, found the main bearing badly worn, and told the co-op she'd put the chance of failure on the next cycle at about 30%, stressing that that's her judgment from thirty years of fixing comparable (but differently designed) machines. The co-op's insurer, meanwhile, applies an actuarial table for vintage agricultural machinery with a flagged main bearing and gets 6% per cycle, drawn from a pool of around 1,200 machines none of which share the press's design. A physics grad student has built a finite-element model of the worn bearing and insists the failure probability is a real single-case physical tendency fixed by the actual microstructure and load, and that the mechanic's and the insurer's numbers are just better or worse estimates of it. To complicate things, the co-op has decided to retire the press after this harvest — the next cycle is the last one it will ever run — and the board's compromise so far has been to average the mechanic and the grad student and plan around 18%.

What I need from you: the question wording itself, pitched at 45 minutes and about 800 words of student writing; a model answer of roughly 700 words that actually takes a position rather than admiring all three interpretations from a safe distance; and a marking scheme out of 25 broken into named moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line description of the standard bad version of each move. A couple of constraints. I don't want any credit to depend on name-dropping — a student who never mentions a single philosopher by name but nails the analysis should be able to get full marks, so please don't build the scheme around attributions or a reading list, and don't make the model answer quote anybody. I do want the scheme to catch a few specific confusions I've already seen in practice sections: students who treat the propensity view as just frequentism plus an explanation of why the frequencies come out the way they do, students who think the existence of a decent long-run record refutes subjectivism rather than merely bearing on it, and students who say the mechanic's 0.3 and the insurer's 0.06 are both correct "relative to their frameworks" without noticing that they're supposed to be answering the same question about the same event. The move I most want to reward is noticing that retiring the press means there is no actual long run left to appeal to, so the frequentist has to go hypothetical, and then saying what work the hypothetical sequence is actually doing and whether it can bear that weight.

One more thing to fold in. I was going back and fourth about this with a friend who does reliability engineering, and he thinks the whole question is a fake problem: on his view the three numbers are just different estimators with different variances and different sampling assumptions, and arguing about what probability "really is" is idle. I want the marking scheme to give real partial credit to a student who presses his objection, but not full credit, and I'd like the model answer to say why — what's left over after the engineering point is granted. Please write the whole thing in American English.
