I write about the mathematical foundations of the social sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on how much of modern welfare theory turns on a single result: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, which says that if social and individual preferences over risky prospects both satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and society is indifferent between two prospects whenever every individual is, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. I have put my own position on the record in the draft, and I want a consistency audit before it runs. I hold that:

(1) the theorem is decisive — social welfare has to be additive in individual well-being, and a rival view is only credible if it can say which premise it rejects;
(2) the weights are equal, fixed not by the theorem but by the sympathetic-observer argument, which is why I describe the upshot as utilitarian rather than merely additive;
(3) Rawls is right that parties behind a veil of ignorance should not be modeled as expected-utility maximizers who assume an equal chance of being anyone, since that is a principle of insufficient reason asked to do work it cannot do;
(4) interpersonal comparisons of utility are empirically dubious — I cannot see how we could ever know that one person's gain outweighs another's loss;
(5) I am a prioritarian: the same increment of well-being matters more the worse off the person who receives it;
(6) I also take seriously the objection that summing utilities cannot be meaningful at all, since each person's utility function is pinned down only up to a positive affine transformation.

Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each, say which commitment has to go and what it costs to surrender it, and where the conflict runs through the theorem rather than around it, name the premise actually being denied. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most coherence. Some of these may only look incompatible; if so, say so rather than flagging everything.
