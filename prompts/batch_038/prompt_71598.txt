I'm in the second year of a PhD, and rather than starting from a theory I've been trying to settle what I actually think about hard choices by writing down my verdicts on a batch of cases and seeing whether they add up to a principle. Two people are pushing on me from opposite sides. My supervisor's view is that what the incomparability literature describes is nothing over and above vagueness or imprecision in the better-than relation — the collapsing principle, in Broome's sense — and that positing anything else is a redescription of the datum. My partner, who works in economics, thinks that if I'm genuinely willing to take either of two options then they are equally good for me, so the phenomenon I keep reporting doesn't exist. And this isn't purely academic: I'm choosing between two jobs at the moment, which is why the list has the shape it does. Here are my verdicts.

1. The academic job I want — research in a narrow field I love, low pay, a department of four, a city where I know nobody — versus a policy research job in the city where my partner works, my mother is ageing, and my friends live: decent pay, less autonomy, work I'd find mildly interesting. Neither is better than the other, and they are not equally good either; I can't rank them. The datum I care about is this: when I imagine the academic job improved slightly — a modest raise, one extra week of teaching relief — it still does not come out better than the policy job. It is still a coin flip from the inside.

2. Vanilla or chocolate, on an ordinary day. Neither better. But when I imagine the chocolate with an extra scoop, chocolate is plainly better. So this pair behaves like a tie in a way the career pair does not, even though both are, on the surface, cases of neither better nor worse. I want an account that explains that difference rather than recording it.

3. My grandmother's ring. For any amount of money in the ordinary range, selling it is not worse so much as a question that fails to get a grip: I don't weigh it, I refuse the framing. But if the money would pay for my sister's surgery, selling is clearly better. Comparability seems to switch on somewhere along the money scale, and I want to keep that without being told either that the ring has a price or that filial piety is infinitely valuable. It has the shape of what Griffin calls discontinuity, though I'm not sure the label fits.

4. Betraying a friend's confidence for money: for no amount is it better, I think. Yet I also don't think the disvalue is infinite — a world in which I betray a friend for a fortune seems finitely worse than one in which I don't. So the trumping shape and the cardinal shape both misdescribe it.

5. Two swimmers, equally distant, strangers of the same age; I can save only one. I ought to save someone, and either is permitted. Afterwards I expect to feel something that is not mistaken, but I do not think I should have saved the other. Now compare: suppose I take the policy job and, five years on, think I should have taken the academic one. That regret strikes me as warranted rather than confused. Both cases are, by my lights, cases in which neither option was better. This is the pair I cannot reconcile — why does hindsight license the second and not the first?

6. Once I commit — sign the contract, settle on the project — I think the commitment generates reasons that were not there before: switching back would be a kind of betrayal of myself even if the two options are exactly as attractive as they were. I accept that, and I take it to be roughly the commitment element in hybrid voluntarism. But I also think the reason would be just as real had I settled the matter by flipping a coin, which looks like getting normative force out of nothing. Both verdicts cannot be obviously right.

7. A longer life with a manageable chronic condition versus a shorter life without it. I can't tell you which is better, but here I suspect there is a determinate answer and that I'm simply badly placed to find it. That is different from case 1, where I don't think my difficulty is ignorance. I'd like to know what marks that difference, if it isn't how the two situations feel from the inside.

Is there a single principle — a small number of clauses, stated precisely — that delivers all of these verdicts, or nearly all? For each clause, tell me which case motivates it and what it rules out. If the honest answer is that I need two accounts, one for the hard-choice cases and another for the wrong-question cases in 3 and 4, say so and draw the boundary in a way that isn't circular. Then tell me what the resulting view predicts about cases I haven't listed: what happens to the career pair if the academic job is improved not slightly but substantially, and whether there must be a threshold; whether the view licenses me to pay a small sum to move from the policy job to the academic one and then pay again to move back; and whether a long series of tiny improvements can carry me from incomparability to betterness without any single step doing it. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate and which one I should give up — I would rather lose a verdict than multiply value relations — and answer my supervisor directly: what is the test, if any, for telling a genuinely vague or imprecise better-than relation apart from a comparison that has no answer, and is a parity-style fourth relation doing explanatory work in case 1 or only renaming it?
