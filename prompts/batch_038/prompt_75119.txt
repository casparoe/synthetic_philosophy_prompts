I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social epistemology: sixteen students, one eighty-minute session, and a table supplied with index cards, two decks of playing cards, a whiteboard, a photocopier down the hall, and no laptops. My only budget is a pizza I am willing to put at risk.

The students have already encountered the Condorcet jury theorem, but exclusively in its popular one-line form: if each voter is more likely than not to be right, adding voters drives the majority toward the truth. They treat the independence hypothesis as a technicality somebody worried about. I want an in-class game that dismantles that gloss by having them lose, round after round, while every single one of them is individually a better-than-chance judge of the question at hand.

Please design the activity in full, meeting the following specifications.

1. Choose the parameters yourself — the reliability of individual signals, the number of jurors drawing on a common source rather than on their own evidence, the number of rounds — subject to one constraint I care about: every juror's individual reliability must exceed one half, while the group's expected accuracy over the session must be barely distinguishable from a coin toss. Show me the arithmetic that secures this, and tell me how many rounds I need before the running tally makes the effect visible rather than merely true in expectation.

2. Give me the procedure: how I, as a non-voting facilitator, generate the hidden state each round in a way the students cannot count, memorize, or reverse-engineer; how signals are dealt fast enough to sustain many rounds; a scoring rule tied to the pizza that makes them care without letting them collude their way around it; and the assignment of a clerk and a public running tally on the board.

3. The jurors drawing on the common source must not know that they are doing so, and the others must not be able to identify them. Decide whether ballots are secret or open, and defend the decision. Secrecy conceals the correlation; openness lets someone notice a subset that always votes alike. Tell me which of those serves the point better, since spotting the bloc may be precisely the discovery I want them to make.

4. Include a deliberation stage at the point where it belongs, and tell me what it should and should not be permitted to do.

5. Add a brief coda, if the clock allows, in which signal reliability falls below one half so that they watch the theorem invert; if there is no time, say what to cut to make room.

6. Write the debrief: map particular moments in the play onto particular hypotheses of the theorem; tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover; and state plainly which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce and which I must therefore supply by argument.

One further ambition. I would like them to leave with the sense that, from where they sat, there was no internal marker distinguishing the rounds in which they were converging on the truth from the rounds in which they were merely echoing one another. If your design can make that an experience rather than an assertion, build it in; if it cannot, say so rather than pretending otherwise.

Finally, please report how confident you are in your quantitative claims, especially the group accuracy figure, and single out the one point in the design about which you are least sure. Where your probabilities are rough estimates rather than computations, identify them as such.
