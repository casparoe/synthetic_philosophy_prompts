So I teach second-year epistemology — 16 students, and I've got one 90-minute seminar slot left at the end of term. They've done Gettier, the lottery paradox, some testimony, and absolutely no modal logic, and I don't want a single formula to go up on the board. I want to spend that session on Fitchs paradox of knowability, and I want them to walk into it rather than be told about it.

Here's the rough device I've been scribbling at, tell me if its workable and fix the holes. A set of sealed envelopes, each one containing a fact about the room that is true but that nobody in the room knows — say a number fixed by some blind procedure — plus a card that states both halves at once: that the number inside is such-and-such, and that no one here knows it. Reading the card kills the second half of it, but the envelope is plainly openable, so it was knowable the whole time. Then I want the scoring to turn on the group producing an inventory of the unknown truths in the room — points per item, and they lose those points if an item turns out to be something they already knew — with the catch that they can never check the inventory without destroying it.

What I actually need from you:

- the rules, and a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money (I can dangle a couple of participation marks or a chocolate bar, that's my budget);
- a procedure for generating envelope contents that are genuinely unknown to everyone including me. This is the bit I can't crack: if I write the cards then I know the answers, and if the answers are deducible from how I made them, that's knowing too. Solve it, or tell me straight that it can't be solved and give me the next best thing;
- a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90;
- the debrief, mapping what they physically did onto each step of the argument — the knowability assumption, the conjunction, the factivity of knowledge, and the slide to everything in here is known — and then making them choose which step to give up and pay for;
- what will blow up the first time I run it. I'm already braced for someone to rip an envelope open in minute two, for someone to insist that knowing the number is between one and six counts as knowing something relevant, and for someone to say the card was true five minutes ago even if it isn't now, which may or may not be a way out;
- and honestly, which part of the argument the game just can't carry and I'll have to supply by hand afterwards.

One thing to keep in mind: I'd much rather the collapse happen inside the room — no unknown truths, so we know everything in here, which is obviously false — than have anyone narrate it. If your design only ever gets them to feel the local point about one self-defeating card and never reaches the general one, say so rather than papering over it.
