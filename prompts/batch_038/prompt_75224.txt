I'm auditing an early modern philosophy course at my local library's continuing-ed program (long story, mostly I go for the biscuits) and I've got stuck on Berkeley, specifically the move in the Principles where he says you can't even frame the idea of a tree or a house or a star existing while nobody perceives it, because the framing of it is itself something happening in your mind. I know that's usually called the master argument and that it's supposed to be doing the heavy lifting for the whole "to be is to be perceived" business. But I'm not writing about whether it succeeds — our tutor has opinions about that and they're louder than mine. What I can't stop thinking about is the *shape* of the thing, and I don't have a word for that shape.

Here's what I mean. The claim is built so that the only way you could ever produce a counterexample to it requires you to perform the very act that, on the claim's own terms, turns your counterexample into an instance of the claim. So my attempt to refute doesn't just fail — it gets swallowed, and then handed back to me as fresh support. The proponent isn't left merely undefeated, they're left better off than before I opened my mouth. And I keep meeting this structure somewhere other than Berkeley. The amateur-psychology version: someone tells you you're furious with your father, you say you absolutely are not, and they say that's the repression talking. Some diagnostic or personality questionnaire where refusing to sit it gets scored as defensiveness, which then shows up in your results as confirmation. The Nagel-flavored one about bats, where any attempt you make to imagine what it's like to be a bat just produces more of your own imagining, and that's taken to prove you can't do it. And then there's the free will row, where someone says you can't sincerely assert determinism because the act of arguing for it already assumes you chose to argue — which feels related but somehow not the same, and I can't say why not.

So: does this have a name? I've half-heard of a few candidates — something about self-sealing arguments, no-true-Scotsman, begging the question, and a phrase like performative contradiction or performative self-refutation — but none of them seems to sit exactly on top of what I described, and I suspect the free will case is the odd one out. I'm not after a bare label, I want the carving. Please say what the structure actually is, go through those near neighbours one at a time and tell me how the thing I described differs from each, tell me which of them fits best and which of my examples really belongs to a different category, and — if nothing established covers it — coin a term with a definition and a couple of boundary cases showing what wouldn't count. There's also a wrinkle I'd like you to deal with: some arguments that look structurally identical to this are treated with real respect in philosophy rather than as tricks, so where's the line between the legitimate and the illegitimate version of the move, if there is one? Start with a single sentence giving me the bottom line — yes there's a name, or no there isn't — and then keep the whole thing under 450 words. Definately don't pad it out with a tour of Berkeley scholarship, I've got the textbook.
