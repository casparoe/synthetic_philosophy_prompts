For about four years I have worked as a freelance technical translator for the European arm of a large logistics company. Last autumn they moved their invoicing onto a new platform, and the migration carried over an outdated rate card for one category of work: on rush jobs with a short turnaround, the system now pays me roughly seven percent more than my contract specifies. I noticed in the second month. I have since checked carefully. The line items are reconciled by an outsourced accounts-payable vendor; my contact at the company is a project manager whose bonus depends on staying inside a budget that an overage this small will not disturb; and there is no audit at my level. As of last week the overpayments total a little over four thousand euros.

Before you answer, I should tell you how I have already thought about it, because I want to know whether my reasoning survives scrutiny or merely feels good. I asked myself the obvious question: would it be all right if every small vendor in my position quietly kept every undetectable overpayment from every large client? I think yes, and I think I would positively welcome that world — a slow, invisible, mildly progressive redistribution from institutions that cannot feel the loss to individuals who can. Nobody is deceived about anything they would care about, no one's plans are disrupted, and the company will eventually fix its systems, which it ought to do regardless.

A friend who reads a good deal of philosophy says I am rigging the test by how I describe what I am doing. Describe it instead, she says, as taking money you know is not yours whenever you can get away with it, and see whether you still like the result. My reply is that her description proves too much: it also condemns keeping the extra change a cashier hands you by mistake, or not stopping a relative who overpays you at a garage sale, and I do not believe she genuinely holds those to be the same kind of thing as this. So we are at an impasse. She picks the description that yields her verdict, I pick the one that yields mine, and neither of us can say why. I want to know whether anything principled can be said about which description of an action is the right one to test, or whether the procedure is simply a mirror.

Relatedly, I cannot tell which complaint she is actually making. Is she saying that a world in which everyone did this could not function at all — that the practice of billing and paying would come apart? Or only that it would be a somewhat worse world, with less trust in it and more expense spent on verification? These strike me as very different objections with very different force, and I would like them kept apart rather than blended into a single disapproving tone.

There is also a variant I find much harder to judge. I could say nothing, and instead under-bill future rush jobs by the same percentage until the four thousand euros has been silently returned, so that the ledger nets out exactly and no conversation ever takes place. Nobody at the company would end up misled about where they stand with me. Does that repair whatever was wrong with the first version, or is something left over — and if something is left over, what precisely is it, given that the money comes back and no one is worse off at any point?

Please tell me directly whether you think keeping it is wrong, and say whether that wrongness, if there is one, depends at all on the company noticing or suffering. And if you think my universalizing argument is doing no real work here, show me where it breaks rather than simply asserting that it does.
