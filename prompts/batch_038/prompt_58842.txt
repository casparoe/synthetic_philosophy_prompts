I am chairing a session at a departmental workshop on ancient ethics and its modern inheritors, and the assigned reading is the treatment of wine and supervised drinking in Plato's last and longest work. Two of the participants have been circling the same disagreement for weeks, and I want to see it staged properly in advance, so that on the day we can argue about the argument rather than about paraphrase. Please write a dialogue between them, set at the reception afterwards, with the punch bowl doing some of the work. Their positions are these, in a coinage of my own that I would like you to retain throughout: the **inoculation reading**, on which Plato takes wine to be an instrument of moral formation — a controlled dose of the thing that most reliably unseats self-command, administered under supervision so that the drinker practises holding firm inside the temptation rather than being kept at a safe distance from it; and the **assay reading**, on which Plato's interest in drinking is epistemic rather than formative — wine is a touchstone that discloses a nature already fixed, and the elaborate apparatus built around it exists to keep the test from spoiling the specimen and to make its result legible. Give the two of them names, and make them equally good at their jobs: neither may be permitted to win by forgetting something the other remembers, and neither may be reduced to a sceptic about the whole enterprise.

So that they can engage the material directly rather than gesture at it, here is what both are working with. The Athenian visitor opens by pressing his Dorian companions on the aim of their legislation, and his charge is that their common meals, their gymnastic discipline and their armed contests train citizens admirably against pain and fear while leaving them wholly untrained against pleasure and appetite. He then runs a thought experiment about a hypothetical drug that would induce fear rather than confidence, observing that a legislator could hand it out in small, watched doses to see whether a young soul can master what it feels; since wine works in the opposite direction, he treats it as the available analogue. Elsewhere the drink is presented as a Dionysian gift given to humankind not for its ruin but as a remedy, and specifically as a remedy for the sourness of age; the age-grading of who may drink, how much, and in whose company follows from this. The repeated qualification is that a drinking party is beneficial with a sober man in charge of it and ruinous without one, and that what the practice is meant to yield is a settled sense of shame and a bond of regard among those who drink together — which each side should also be pressed on: whether wine's work as a social adhesive belongs inside their reading or is a third thing that neither the inoculation nor the assay account captures. Each side owes an account of the texts that embarrass it. The inoculation reader must explain the refusal, in the earlier political work, to let the guardians drink to excess — including the quip that a guardian who needed watching would need a guardian of his own — and the portrait, in the drinking-party dialogue, of a man whose self-command is so settled that wine simply fails to take hold of him while he out-argues the whole table until dawn. The assay reader must explain the remedy-for-age claim, which sounds like something wine does to a drinker rather than something it discovers about him, and the insistence that the practice is supposed to produce shame and friendship, not merely register their presence or absence.

Aim for something between 1,000 and 1,400 words, with minimal stage direction and no narration beyond what is needed to keep the setting visible. Each character should at least once take up the other's strongest piece of evidence and turn it to their own account — the requirement of a sober supervisor is the obvious candidate, but the fear-drug thought experiment, the age-grading, and the immunity of the philosophical drinker are all available for redeployment — and each should concede one substantive point that costs them something. Do not declare a winner. Instead, have the final exchange isolate the question that would actually decide between them: whether a man who behaves disgracefully under wine is thereby marked out, on Plato's view, or thereby enrolled in a course of treatment, and hence whether the disposition that wine engages is a capacity strengthened by exercise under temptation or a native endowment that temptation merely reveals. Let each say which way they would bet on that question and why, and end there. One stylistic constraint, which matters for our purposes: where the characters refer to Plato, have them describe what he says in their own words. Do not put any Platonic wording in quotation marks, do not supply Stephanus numbers, and do not have either of them cite or allude to scholarship about the passage; if a character is unsure of a detail of the text, let them flag the uncertainty rather than fill it in.
