I hold a chair in the philosophy of medicine at a medical school and, by virtue of that appointment, sit as the lay member of the statutory tribunal that hears appeals against adverse coverage determinations under our national sickness insurance scheme. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to my two colleagues — a physician and a retired claims director — three days before we sit.

The scheme's governing regulation defines a compensable condition conjunctively, in two clauses: (a) "a significant departure from the statistically typical functioning of an organ or physiological system, measured against the reference class of persons of the claimant's sex and age," and (b) "which impairs the claimant's capacity to perform the occupational role for which the claimant is engaged." The drafting history is a compromise. Clause (a) was inserted by members who wanted a standard that was objective and answerable to measurement alone; clause (b) by members who held that nothing should be compensable unless it actually disables a person at work. Nobody appears to have noticed that the clauses can come apart in either direction, and we now receive such appeals monthly.

The appellant is a 51-year-old radiographer who works in a catheterisation laboratory, where radiation-protection rules oblige her to wear a lead-equivalent apron of several kilograms, over scrub suit and sterile gown, for procedures of up to three hours in a warm, poorly ventilated room. She reports severe flushing, drenching night sweats, and consequent sleep loss; her supervisor documents three occasions on which she had to withdraw from a procedure. Our medical assessor records that her hormone levels and menstrual history are consistent with the perimenopause and fall within the reference interval for her age, that no test discloses any pathology, and — this is important — that her symptom-severity scores place her in the top three per cent of symptomatic women of her age. The assessor further states that menopausal hormone therapy is the most effective available treatment for vasomotor symptoms and that the appellant is an eligible candidate with no contraindication. So the clinical merits are not before us; only the classification is. Her claim was refused on the ground that the perimenopause is a normal stage of life rather than a disease. Her representative adds that the scheme routinely funds cataract extraction, and that opacification of the lens is if anything more age-typical than vasomotor symptoms.

Next month we hear a 58-year-old railway signaller whose audiogram sits exactly at the median for his age cohort — his hearing loss is precisely what the reference class predicts — but whose occupational standard requires thresholds that the median ear of his age does not meet. His claim for hearing aids was refused under clause (a) alone, and he will cite whatever we say in the appellant's case.

The opinion should contain:

1. A one-sentence holding at the head: allow, dismiss, or allow on stated conditions, with the condition and the review period if it is conditional.
2. The reasoning that gets us there, written for two colleagues who have read no philosophy and will not thank me for any.
3. A direct resolution of the clause (a) question. If the answer turns on the choice of reference class — the transition is statistically typical for women of her age, yet her severity is atypical even within that cohort, and both facts describe the same woman at the same time — say so, state which fact is the relevant one, and defend the choice. Note that clause (a) is silent about the environment against which functioning is to be measured, and that the apron, the procedure list and the temperature of the laboratory are features of her working environment rather than of her body. Say whether that silence destroys the clause's claim to objectivity or merely leaves a gap we are obliged to fill.
4. A determination on clause (b), given that her employer could rotate her off the procedure list and thereby extinguish the incapacity without treating anything. Take a position on whether an incapacity produced jointly by a bodily condition and a job design is compensable under our regulation, and say whether the position changes when the job design is legally mandated, as the apron is, rather than merely convenient.
5. The strongest consideration on the losing side, in its best form and not as a straw figure, together with an explanation of why it does not carry the day — or, if it does, a candid revision of the holding in section 1.
6. A short general test, phrased so that the other two members could apply it without me in the room, that yields coherent results in both the appellant's case and the signaller's, and that tells us what to do with the cataract comparison. If no such test exists, say so plainly and state what we should ask the legislature to amend.
7. One further matter, to be treated as a live objection rather than a flourish: whichever way we decide, we will have made a value judgment about whose suffering counts as compensable, and we will have made it under cover of a definition that presents itself as factual. Say whether that is an objection to our decision or only to the drafting, and whether conceding it commits us to anything we should be unwilling to accept.

Two constraints. I want no literature review: no citations, no reading list, no names of theorists dropped in as authority — my physician colleague is allergic to them and they would move him not at all. And I want no survey of available positions: the document must commit, because the tribunal has to sign something on the day. Nine hundred words at most; if you must exceed that, exceed it in section 6 and nowhere else.
