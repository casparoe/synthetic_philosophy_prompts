For the past year I have been doing an independent study in epistemic logic with a graduate student at a university near my school, and our topic is the problem of logical omniscience. I follow why the standard possible-worlds account generates it: if an agent knows something exactly when it is true in every world compatible with what she knows, then everything true in all of those worlds is known too, which is to say every logical consequence of her knowledge. Actual agents are plainly not like that, so the account must be repaired somehow, and there seem to be many incompatible ways of doing it.

Each week my mentor gives me a scenario and reports only whether the agent in it should be modeled as knowing the consequence at issue. She will not state her criterion and says she will reveal it at the end of the term. Her record so far:

1. A club-level chess player who correctly believes the rules of chess and the exact position on the board: not credited with knowing that White has a forced mate in nine.
2. The same player in the same position: credited with knowing that if it is Black's move and Black's king is in check, then every legal Black move removes the check.
3. A warehouse clerk who believes five hundred conditionals of the form "if shipment n arrives, shipment n+1 is delayed," who believes that shipment 1 arrived, and who would immediately accept each link if the links were put to her one at a time: not credited with knowing that shipment 500 is delayed.
4. A seven-year-old who has never been taught multiplication but believes there are three apples in each of two baskets: credited with knowing that there are six apples in all.
5. A grandmaster who has drilled one particular endgame configuration hundreds of times: credited with knowing, immediately on seeing that configuration, whether it is won or drawn for the side to move.
6. An office worker who believes on the strength of one email that a meeting begins at three and on the strength of another that it begins at four: credited with knowing each of those separately, but not with knowing that the meeting begins at three and at four, and not with anything that turns on noticing the clash.
7. A language model trained on the full text of a mystery novel that answers correctly every direct question we put to it about who did what: not credited with knowing that the butler's stated alibi is inconsistent with the timeline of the murder, since no one asked it.
8. A research group that accepts the axioms of a formal theory and has years of funded work ahead of it: credited with knowing a theorem whose proof took the relevant community decades to produce.

This week's case is one I proposed and she agreed to rule on. A structural engineer correctly believes the load figures, the material properties, and the applicable code requirements for a particular beam; she has twenty years of experience with exactly this sort of check, which she can perform in roughly fifteen minutes using her spreadsheet. The model we are building, however, is meant to predict which members she will flag as unsafe during a two-minute walk through the site, before she has opened her laptop. Should she be credited with knowing that the beam fails the code requirement?

My own attempts at reconstruction have stalled. The seven-year-old seems to rule out any criterion on which conceptual mastery is a precondition. The clerk seems to rule out any criterion on which a cheap derivation is sufficient. And the research group seems to require that an extremely expensive derivation sometimes counts, which is hard to square with the clerk. I suspect the resolution has to do with the span of activity a given model is meant to describe, but I cannot get that to explain the grandmaster and the office worker as well.

What I would like from you:

- Lay out the candidate criteria that are consistent with all eight rulings, and state how each of them rules on the engineer.
- Identify which plausible criteria the record positively excludes, and say which ruling does the excluding.
- Give your best guess at her verdict on the engineer and at the criterion behind it, with the reasons you weight most heavily, and a rough confidence.
- Propose a ninth case I could put to her that would force the two closest surviving criteria apart, and explain why they diverge on it.
- Tell me honestly whether you think the record is governed by a single standing criterion at all, or whether she may simply be adjusting the idealization to whatever each model is for, in which case there is nothing to recover.

If any of these criteria correspond to familiar technical repairs of the possible-worlds account, note the correspondence briefly, but the criteria matter to me far more than the labels, and please do not compile a reading list.
