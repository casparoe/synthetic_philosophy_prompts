Ok, here's something I want a real argument for, not a survey — I know the standard moves and I want to be pushed on the non-standard one.

Setup. Let Kp mean: someone, at some time, knows that p. Knowability says every truth is such that it could have been known — if p is true, then possibly Kp. Now take some truth nobody knows and consider the conjunction of it with the claim that it's unknown. Knowledge distributes over conjunction and it's factive, so anyone who knew that conjunction would know both halves, and knowing the second half makes it false. So the conjunction can't be known; so by knowability it isn't true; so there are no unknown truths. I've got the proof, don't walk me through it.

The position I want you to argue for: the proof only gets traction because knowability is being read as a timeless property a proposition has across worlds, and that isn't what the verificationist is actually committed to. What they're committed to is a claim about discovery — every truth is such that there could have been a first moment at which it came to be known. On that reading the diagonal case stops being a contradiction. Coming to know "p and nobody knows p" at t is coming to know something that was true at t and stops being true at t, which is exactly how tensed and self-locating content behaves all over the place, and nobody thinks that's a pathology there.

Make the case. Specifically I want:

(a) an argument that the discovery reading is the natural one rather then a rescue invented under pressure — something about what a claim of the form "this could be known" is even for, who it's addressed to, what it's supposed to license.

(b) an explanation of why it doesn't need a restriction to specially well-behaved propositions, a non-classical logic, or an extra operator for pinning down the actual world — and whether giving up those moves costs it anything.

(c) a straight answer to the worry that kills it if the worry's right: that the discovery principle is too cheap. "There could have been a moment where this first came to be known" looks like its true of basically every truth for free, in which case the anti-realist has bought consistency by selling the thesis. So say whether the principle still does any work at all — does it rule anything out, is there a notion of an unknowable truth it can still condemn, does it still ground the connection between truth and evidence the anti-realist wants in the first place?

Be honest at the end about how much of anti-realism is left standing, even if the answer is that it's mostly gutted.
