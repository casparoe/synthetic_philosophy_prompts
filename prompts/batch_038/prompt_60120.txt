I've been chewing on the same problem for a couple months now and I think a story would actually help me more than another argument, so here goes. The problem: if the universe goes on forever and contains an infinite amount of happiness and suffering no matter what, then anything I do adds or subtracts some finite amount to an infinite total, and infinite plus ten is still infinite. So everything ties and nothing matters. People tell me there are fixes -- you compare lives day by day and go with whoever's ahead far enough down the line, or you discount the far future so the sum stays finite, or you invent numbers that are actually bigger than infinity (and smaller than any fraction) so tiny differences never fully wash out -- but every fix seems to break something else. With the "who's ahead later" rule I can picture two lives where each one is ahead of the other at different stretches, so neither comes out better, which feels insane for something as concrete as whether to donate or have a kid. And someone told me that if the good and bad stretches alternate and keep growing, you can literally reorder how you tally them and get whatever total you want, which if true is just wild.

Could you write me a short story, maybe 1200 to 1800 words, that lives inside this instead of explaining it? Here's the setup I keep scribbling on: an office, somewhere after the end, where clerks score lives that never actually stopped, so the ledgers have no last page. Two clerks who've used different tallying rules for centuries have to sign off on one file together, because there's a single slot open -- one person gets sent back, and the other doesn't. The file in question belongs to somebody whose life alternated between long stretches of misery and long stretches of joy, with each stretch twice as long as the one before, and the two clerks' rules genuinely rank the candidates in opposite orders. Somewhere in the middle I want one of them to notice that you can make the ledger say pretty much anything by changing the order you read the entries in, and I want them to be honestly unsure whether that's a cheat or the deepest thing they've ever found out. Keep it a workplace drama, not angels-with-clipboards comedy, keep the dialogue human -- nobody should explain the theory like a textbook -- and please don't wrap it up with a speech where someone articulates the moral.
