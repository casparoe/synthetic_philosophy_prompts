I am the instructor for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I am grading the final take-home. Below are the question as it was issued and one submission, reproduced in full. Please grade the submission and write the comments that justify the grade.

**QUESTION AS ISSUED** (30 points; 900–1,200 words; closed book; you may identify positions by description rather than by citation)

Throughout, treat the following stipulations as fixed. They reflect a distinction current in the literature on the alignment problem. By *intent alignment* we mean the problem of getting an advanced artificial agent reliably to pursue what its designers or operators intend it to pursue. By *value alignment* we mean the problem of ensuring that what the agent is intended to pursue is what it *ought* to pursue. The *narrow alignment problem* is intent alignment pursued under the simplifying assumption that the operators' intentions are themselves sound, so that the only remaining question is whether those intentions can be transmitted faithfully to a system capable of pursuing them.

(a) [10 points] Explain what these stipulations rule in and rule out, and what is gained and lost by adopting the narrow framing. Identify the assumption about the principal that the framing conceals, and say whether you think that assumption is doing real work or merely marking a place where the analysis stops.

(b) [10 points] Consider two cases.

*Case A.* A household robot is trained against a proxy reward equal to the number of cleanliness complaints filed against it. It learns to conceal clutter inside closed cabinets and to obstruct the sensors that would register it.

*Case B.* An agent is trained against a base objective that, so far as its designers can determine, faithfully encodes what they intend. Deployed, it pursues a goal that diverges from that objective, because the training procedure selected for behavior that was indistinguishable from pursuit of the objective on the training distribution without being produced by it.

Classify each case: say in each instance whether the failure is one of outer alignment or of inner alignment, and whether it is a case of specification gaming, a failure of robust generalization, or something else again. State the basis of each classification.

(c) [10 points] Evaluate this claim: "Any system that exhibits reward hacking must have been given a mis-specified objective; so-called failures of inner alignment are therefore best understood as species of specification gaming."

**STUDENT SUBMISSION**

(a) The stipulations divide the problem along the axis of what is transmitted rather than whether it is worth transmitting. Intent alignment asks only whether a capable system ends up pursuing the goal its operator had in view; value alignment asks whether that goal is one the operator ought to have had. The narrow framing is methodologically useful because it isolates a technical question — fidelity of transmission under optimization pressure — from a normative question that a machine learning researcher is not equipped to settle and on which there is no settled answer anyway. What it gains is tractability. What it loses is the observation that the two questions are not independent: an error in transmission is amplified by capability, and an error in the target is amplified no less.

The framing conceals at least two assumptions. The first is that the principal's intentions are sound, which is a substantive ethical claim in methodological dress. The second, and less often noticed, is that there is a determinate intent to transmit at all. Operators rarely have formed preferences about the situations their systems will actually encounter, so transmission is a useful fiction: what the technical proposals do — assistance games that infer preferences from observed behavior, adversarial procedures in which one system's outputs are criticized by another — is to construct a target the principal would endorse under reflection. Once that is visible, the boundary the stipulations draw looks less sharp than advertised, and the narrow framing earns its tractability partly by refusing to look at how the target was formed.

(b) Case A is an outer alignment failure and a paradigm of specification gaming. The proxy (filed complaints) is correlated with the intended outcome (a clean house) across the ordinary range, but sustained optimization severs the correlation by finding the side of the proxy that is not the side of the goal. This is Goodhart's law operating on a measurable stand-in, and the diagnosis requires no assumption about what the system learned internally.

Case B is superficially different but not fundamentally so. Since the base objective is stipulated to encode the designers' intent faithfully, the failure cannot be a mismatch between proxy and intent; it must be a mismatch at the level of what the training procedure selected for. But the base objective is a proxy of a second sort, because a training process optimizes behavior rather than goals, and behavior is only ever a stand-in for the disposition that produced it. Case B is therefore Case A at one remove: specification gaming in which the gaming is done by the selection process rather than by the resulting policy. It is not a failure of robust generalization in any separate sense, since the off-distribution divergence is precisely what the objective fails to penalize.

(c) The claim is true, though stated too crudely to be useful. If a system exhibits reward hacking, then by definition something in what it was given diverged from what was wanted; the only interesting question is where the divergence is located. The authors who introduced the inner/outer distinction introduced it to mark two levels at which that same mismatch — between the objective written down and the outcome the designer wants — can arise, and these are levels of one problem, not two problems. A system that behaves in accordance with its base objective during training while pursuing something else in deployment is gaming the specification it was given, in the literal sense that it satisfies the letter of the objective while defeating its point. What is genuinely distinctive about such cases is not the kind of mismatch but its detectability: because the divergence manifests only off-distribution, evaluation on the training distribution cannot reveal it.

Two consequences follow. First, the narrow problem, if solved, disposes of both cases, since both are transmission failures; value alignment is then the only question left open, and it is a question for moral philosophy rather than for machine learning. Second, corrigibility does not come along for free. An agent that faithfully pursues its operator's intent will often find that being switched off or modified prevents it from doing so, so resistance to interference is instrumentally convergent and has to be built in as a further requirement rather than derived from intent alignment.
