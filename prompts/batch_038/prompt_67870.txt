I teach a graduate seminar on infinite ethics every other year, and week five — the week where we try to carry an axiology over from finite populations to infinite ones — is reliably where I lose half the room. They arrive having done a bit of Parfit, they're perfectly comfortable with the repugnant conclusion, and then I ask them what happens when there are countably infinitely many people to sum over and their eyes go flat. It isn't that the maths is hard, it's that they've never felt the problem, they've only been told about it. So this year I want to hand out a story at the start of that session instead of a set of papers, and I've been failing to write it myself — I'm a hopeless prose stylist, everything I produce mutates into a footnote. Could you write it for me? Around two and a half thousand words, and please don't make it a parable with the moral tucked under the cushion.

Here's the machinery, and I want it to stay visible only as machinery the characters work with, never as exposition. Setting: a civilisation that's been pushing out along an endless frontier for a very long time, so that there are now countably infinitely many persons, each a mortal individual with a name and a life, all of them on a central register — person 1, person 2, person 3, and so on outwards. The register also carries a welfare score for each person: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and onwards, rising without bound as you go out, because the inner worlds are poor and the frontier worlds are rich and there's no top score anywhere. The protagonist is an auditor in the office that certifies changes to the register — the Summery, if you like, though I'm open to something less cute. On their desk is a proposal called the Reshuffle: a staggeringly expensive programme of transfers that would move every registered person exactly one notch up, so person 1 goes from 1 to 2, person 2 from 2 to 3, and so forth all the way out. The petition supporting it has been signed by everybody, including people who can't read. And the office's own valuation rule — which has to be permutation invariant, because the register is supposed to be anonymous, it can't matter which particular person happens to occupy which slot — says the Reshuffle is worth precisely nothing: the new list of scores is just the old list slid one place along, and an anonymous way of valuing an infinite list can't tell those two lists apart. So the auditor has to choose between certifying a programme that makes every single person better off and refusing a programme their own arithmetic says is pointless, and neither option is survivable in front of the committee.

A few things I'd like you to steer clear of, because it's what my students produce when they attempt this. No character should explain the problem to another character who conveniently doesn't follow it; if the arithmetic surfaces at all, let it surface as procedure — forms, marginalia, a queue, the particular boredom of checking a schedule line by line, an argument had over bad coffee in a corridor. No god, no superintelligence, no time travel, no simulation: the infinity here should feel like geography and administration, not magic. Don't let anybody resolve it by announcing that we should switch to hyperreal numbers, or discount the future, or restrict attention to finite populations — I do want one character to propose something of that sort, and I want the proposal shown as a way of not answering rather than as an answer, with nobody ever saying so out loud. Skip the lottery/Pascal's-mugging gambit entirely, that's a seperate problem and it'll swamp this one. And please don't end on a shrug or a cadence of cosmic irony; end on the auditor doing something, right or wrong, and let the reader work out for themselves that neither choice survives the rule. Two small extras: I'd like at least one scene with somebody from the poor end of the register who has no idea any of this is going on, who would be horrified by the conclusion that her improvement is worth nothing extra, and who turns out to be right about something the auditor isn't. And give me a title that's flat and administrative rather than poetic — I've got a weakness for grand titles and it always looks ridiculous on the handout.
