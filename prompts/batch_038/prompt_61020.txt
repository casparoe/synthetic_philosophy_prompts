I am the philosophy-of-science member of the Programme Review Panel of a national research council, and I have been asked to circulate a written analysis before next week's renewal session. Our panel decides five-year core funding for large interdisciplinary research programmes, and since a reorganisation of our terms of reference we are required to justify renewal decisions in explicitly Lakatosian vocabulary: each programme is treated as a research programme with a hard core of non-negotiable claims, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, background assumptions, instrumentation and modelling choices, and a positive heuristic that generates the next round of problems. Renewal is supposed to turn on whether the programme exhibits a progressive rather than a degenerating problemshift. In practice, of course, our minutes have never pinned down what counts as progressive, and the two factions on the panel now read our own precedents differently. Everything you need is summarised below from our minutes; please do not look for or invent external sources, case names, or citations, and do not pad the analysis with references to Lakatos's published works. Use your general grasp of the framework only.

Here are the five precedents that both factions claim to be applying consistently:

1. **2019 — Ocean–atmosphere exchange.** During the previous cycle the programme predicted a specific pattern of heat uptake at depth that no rival framework implied; a dedicated observation campaign confirmed it two years later. Renewed unanimously, the ground recorded as a confirmed prediction of a previously unforeseen fact.
2. **2020 — Collective decision-making in distributed systems.** The programme derived a family of new quantitative predictions about oscillatory coordination patterns. No corroborating measurement was possible because the required technique did not yet exist, and none had been obtained. Renewed, on the ground that the programme's content had expanded in a way its predecessor version could not generate, and that insisting on corroboration within a funding cycle would systematically penalise theory-driven work.
3. **2021 — Soil carbon cycling.** An observation campaign contradicted the programme's central estimate. The team introduced an auxiliary hypothesis about a previously neglected microbial pathway; the auxiliary implied a distinct, independently checkable signature, and a follow-up study corroborated it. Renewed, on the ground that the protective belt had been modified in a way that added testable content rather than merely absorbing the anomaly.
4. **2022 — Transfer of trained skills.** Anomalies accumulated across the cycle. The team introduced a free parameter whose value was fitted separately in each experiment and which implied nothing beyond reconciling the existing results. Defunded, on the ground that the modification had purchased consistency at the price of content.
5. **2023 — Statistical toolkit for networked epidemiology.** Two consecutive cycles produced no prediction of any previously unobserved fact. The programme's open measurement standard and analytic toolkit had, however, been adopted by eleven other council-funded programmes. Defunded by five votes to four, on the ground that uptake of a programme's instruments is a benefit to the research system but not evidence about the programme's own trajectory. The four dissenters recorded that a heuristic which keeps producing usable problems and tools for others is precisely what a positive heuristic is for.

The case before us next week has the following features. A programme in antimicrobial-resistance dynamics holds as its hard core that transmission is driven overwhelmingly by one class of contact events rather than by environmental persistence. Two years ago a multi-hospital genomic study found transmission clusters that sat badly with that core. The team responded by adding an auxiliary positing a neglected transfer vector; the auxiliary implies a distinct genomic and temporal signature, so it is independently testable in principle, but the longitudinal sampling protocol needed to test it takes roughly two years to run and has not been started. Over the review cycle now closing, the programme has produced no prediction of a previously unobserved fact. What it has produced is, first, a derivation from the modified model of a pattern in the existing genomic dataset that the earlier version could not generate and that had been observed for years but left unexplained by everyone — that is, a recovery of a known anomaly rather than a forecast; second, a surveillance metric now used by four other council programmes and by a national public-health body; and third, an announced prediction of a genuinely novel signature in one class of facilities, whose test is scheduled for eighteen months after our decision. One panel member adds a complication the minutes should record: in her view the original genomic anomaly was probably a sampling artefact, so the auxiliary was never needed as a rescue at all — and if no rescue occurred, the recovery of the known pattern is a bonus rather than a face-saving manoeuvre.

Acting as a philosopher of science who has worked both on the methodology of scientific research programmes and on the practical governance of research funding, think carefully and, before drafting, run a short brainstorming pass in which you enumerate the candidate decision rules that our five precedents might jointly express. I expect at least four: a rule requiring corroborated excess content; a rule requiring only uncorroborated excess content over the predecessor version; a rule that screens modifications for ad hocness and is otherwise permissive; and a rule that credits the fertility of the positive heuristic. Then deliver the analysis I will circulate, containing: (a) which candidate rules are consistent with all five precedents and which strain against particular ones — say explicitly whether precedent 2 and precedent 5 are load-bearing rules or tolerated outliers, since that is where the factions actually diverge; (b) a recommended verdict on the new case, renewal or non-renewal, and if renewal, with what conditions attached; (c) the closest analog among the five precedents, and the specific respect in which the analogy holds or breaks; (d) the substantive principle the panel would be committing itself to under each verdict, in particular whether "novel" in our terms of reference means unforeseen by anyone or merely ungenerated by the predecessor version of the theory, and whether corroboration must fall inside the funding cycle; (e) the single further hypothetical case that would most sharply discriminate between the two rules you take most seriously, described concretely enough that we could recognise it if a programme ever presented it; and (f) one paragraph stating the strongest case for the verdict you are rejecting, in terms its own advocate would accept rather than a strawman. Aim for roughly nine hundred words, be decisive, and do not conclude that the precedents simply underdetermine everything — if they do leave the case open, say precisely which choice the panel must make and what it costs.
