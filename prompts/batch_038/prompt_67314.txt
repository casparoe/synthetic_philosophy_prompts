I build anomaly detection for a wearable that flags possible health issues, and a work argument turned into something I can't get out of. One of our PMs came up with this chain, which he says proves there's no objective fact about bodily "dysfunction" at all, so health is just values all the way down:

(1) On the statistical view, a function is whatever a part typically does, and a part is dysfunctional iff it does that job below the typical level for its reference class (age, sex, and so on).
(2) A reference class is a statistical construct: you decide the membership, and the "typical level" moves with it.
(3) So for any condition C, there's a class in which C is typical — just take the people who have C.
(4) And medicine already does something like this: nearly everyone over 50 needs reading glasses, so inside the class "people over 50" presbyopia *is* the typical level, hence not a dysfunction by (1).
(5) So nothing is a dysfunction: every candidate condition is normal relative to some class, and if we reject the class we're just smuggling in a judgment about which classes are the right ones.
(6) If nothing is a dysfunction, then disease can't be a biological fact plus a badness evaluation — the only real content left is the badness.
(7) Therefore "disease" means nothing more than "a state we dislike," and the project of defining health without values fails on its own terms.

I'd really like to keep (1). I like the idea that there's a fact about function independent of what anybody wants, because that's the whole reason our threshholds mean anything instead of just being a popularity contest. And (4) looks plain true. So which step is broken, and what do I have to give up to fix it? Also — is this the same problem as an algorithm that has to pick a baseline cohort, or is that a coincidence my PM is overreading?
