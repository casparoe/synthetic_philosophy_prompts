I'm in my last year of high school, and as part of an independent study in medieval philosophy I've been working through the section of al-Ghazālī's refutation of the philosophers in which he takes up the case of fire and cotton, together with the reply Averroes wrote later in the twelfth century. I'm supposed to present a paper at a regional student symposium in the spring, and my topic has become the question of what, exactly, al-Ghazālī thinks he has shown. I follow the surface of the argument well enough: he says the connection between what we ordinarily call a cause and what we ordinarily call an effect is not a necessary one, because the two are different things and the existence of the one does not entail the existence of the other; God creates the burning at the moment of contact, and God could just as easily have created the cotton unchanged, or created satiety without eating, or death without a wound. What I cannot get a grip on is what work the argument is doing, since al-Ghazālī and the philosophers he attacks seem to agree on every ordinary claim about fire and cotton, including the claim that the cotton will burn.

To get at the difficulty, here is a distinction I've been trying to develop on my own, and I'd be grateful if you would tell me whether it survives scrutiny. Call the first thesis the denial of necessity: for any pair of natural events, it is metaphysically possible that the first occur without the second. Call the second thesis the denial of agency: no created thing is a genuine agent at all, bodies possess no causal powers of their own, and God is the only real cause. The second appears to entail the first, but the first plainly does not entail the second, since someone could hold that things really do have stable natures and real powers while insisting that those powers are not self-sufficient and that their arrangement could have been otherwise. My suspicion is that readers who label al-Ghazālī an occasionalist slide back and forth between these two theses, and that his own positive remarks about God's settled practice — the thought that we can still predict the burning because God's custom is uniform and because God knows in advance how He will act — sound much closer to the weaker thesis. So my question is whether the stronger thesis can be stated in a way that actually disagrees with the weaker one about something, rather than redescribing a regularity both sides already accept. Averroes's objection seems to be that it cannot, and that if you remove natures you remove knowledge along with them; I'm not sure whether that's a fair reading or a straw man.

There is a related puzzle that confuses me even more. Al-Ghazālī appears to work with a criterion on which a real agent is something that acts knowingly and willingly, and he faults the philosophers for honoring fire with the name of agent when fire has neither knowledge nor will. But human beings do act knowingly and willingly, and I understand that the Ashʿarite tradition to which he belongs nonetheless holds that the act itself is created by God and only acquired by the human being. I don't see how the same criterion can be used to strip fire of agency while leaving our own agency that precarious. Could you explain whether these are two independent doctrines that merely sit side by side in his system, or whether there is a single underlying principle about what causation is, and about what we can know of it, that generates both — and, if it's the latter, what that principle is? I'm not looking for a survey of the scholarly literature or for suggested readings; I mainly want the conceptual lay of the land, and I would especially welcome you telling me plainly where my necessity-and-agency distinction turns out to be a false one.
