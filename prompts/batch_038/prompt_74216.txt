This term I am running one fifty-minute discussion section on definite descriptions for about twenty second-year computer science students in a required logic-and-language module. They can read and write first-order quantifiers, they have had a semester of SQL, and they have had no philosophy. I do not want Russell's theory explained to me; I want a plan for explaining it to them.

The carrier I have in mind is an analogy they already own: a definite description behaves like a scalar subquery, in that a query matching no row returns NULL rather than an object, and a query matching several rows is an error rather than a value. Tell me whether that analogy can bear the weight of a whole section or whether it obscures what is distinctive about the analysis — above all that uniqueness is asserted rather than assumed — and, if it can bear it, give me the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what each depends on. Specify what the worked case must have and must lack, and say whether Russell's own illustrations (the present King of France, the father of Charles II) should appear at all and at which point.

Then map the misconceptions they will import from programming. My guesses are that they will treat NULL as a peculiar kind of object, and that they will take three-valued logic to settle the King of France question in favour of a truth-value gap. Say precisely where in the sequence each is headed off, and — separately — where the database analogy will itself mislead them and what I must say to repair it. Close with one question I can put to the room that discriminates genuine understanding from fluency with the notation, and tell me what I should refuse to cover at this level (names, Strawson, the acquaintance epistemology) and flag as a deliberate omission rather than an oversight.
