I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in environmental history, and our department runs an annual opening seminar co-taught with a group of earth-system scientists. This year the organizers have asked each of us to circulate a one-page statement of where we stand on the Anthropocene question — specifically on when the interval began and on who is entitled to say — in advance of the session, on the explicit understanding that the statements will be read aloud and then attacked. I would like a consistency audit of mine before I submit it, because the feedback I received on last year's version was that it was stratigraphically literate but politically wishful, and I would rather find the soft spots myself than have a sedimentologist find them for me in front of the room. Here is the position, stated as plainly as I can manage. (1) A boundary is a stratigraphic fact, not a verdict about importance: an interval earns formal status only if there is a physical, globally correlatable marker horizon, something a future geologist could locate in sediments or ice on any continent and tie to the same moment; narrative significance, however profound, is not a criterion. (2) The question a start date should answer is causal — when did human activity become the dominant force shaping Earth system processes, meaning atmospheric composition, sediment and nutrient flux, and the global distribution of species. (3) On the evidence, the answer to (2) is old: I find the early-Anthropocene argument convincing, that Holocene deforestation and rice cultivation reversed the orbital-cycle-driven decline in carbon dioxide and methane thousands of years before the industrial era; and I also think the seventeenth-century dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide associated with the post-Columbian collapse of Indigenous American populations and the regrowth of forest — the marker Lewis and Maslin call the Orbis spike, around 1610 — is the clearest single globally legible signal of a human-caused shift in the carbon cycle. (4) Nonetheless, if I were voting, I would support the mid-twentieth-century proposal: the bomb-test plutonium horizon of the early 1950s, with the type section in a lake core from southern Ontario. I think it is the best available candidate and I would not have had the working group choose otherwise. (5) I hold the Capitalocene diagnosis: the driver is a particular mode of production with a fossil-fuel metabolism — plantations, enclosures, coal — rather than an undifferentiated Anthropos, and the species-level framing spreads responsibility in a way that is analytically sloppy and politically convenient; but I also insist that whatever interval we name must be globally uniform, one boundary, one date, everywhere, because the whole value of a geological time scale is that it supplies a shared frame that does not bend to region or politics. (6) On who decides: I accept that the 2024 vote by the Quaternary subcommission declining to ratify the Anthropocene as a formal epoch was procedurally legitimate, and I accept the outcome; at the same time I believe the vote was a mistake driven by an institutional culture attached to the utility of the Holocene, and more fundamentally that stratigraphers are the wrong people to be settling when the human-dominated era of Earth history began, since the question is too consequential and too normative for a technical committee — yet I would resist handing any say in it to a non-geological body, whether a panel of historians or an intergovernmental forum, and I cannot presently say who else should have it. (7) In my own writing and teaching I use the term freely and without hedging, even though I hold (1) and accept the result described in (6).

Please audit the set rather than lecture me on the state of the debate, which I know well enough. Concretely: identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list of worries. For each genuine tension, say exactly what would have to be given up or reinterpreted, what the revision costs me elsewhere in the position, and whether the tension can be dissolved by distinguishing two questions I may be conflating — and if a distinction is offered, tell me whether it actually does the work or merely relocates the problem into a place where it is harder to see. I do not know in advance which commitment is the weak one, and I would rather the audit not be shaped by where my sympathies obviously lie, so please do not assume that (3) or (5) are the ones I will sacrifice merely because they are the fashionable positions in my department. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the largest share of the trouble, whether any of the remaining inconsistencies are ones I should simply live with and state openly, and whether the resulting position would still be recognizable as the view I set out here. I need no references, reading list, or bibliography; the reasoning is the point. Please write the response in American English.
