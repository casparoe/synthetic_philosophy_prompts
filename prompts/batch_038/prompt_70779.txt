**Question (20 points):**

Dennett describes the self as a center of narrative gravity. Explain the analogy and say what work it's supposed to do. Then: on this view, does the self exist? Finally, contrast Dennett's position with a narrative theory of personal identity, and say whether the difference between the two is substantive.

**Student answer:**

The analogy is to centers of gravity in physics. A center of gravity isn't an extra ingredient inside an object, it's an abstraction that physicists posit in order to predict how the object will move. It's not nothing, exactly — it's a well-defined posit that earns its keep — but you won't find it by taking the object apart. Dennett says the self is like that. It isn't a thing in the brain, there's no seat or location where it sits, and there's no self-shaped object anywhere in the skull to be found by the neuroscientist. What there is instead is a body that produces language, and out of all the stories that body tells about itself, plus the stories everyone around it tells, a character gets abstracted: the protagonist, the one everything gets ascribed to. That character is the self, and it's an abstractum, in the same way the center of gravity is.

So does the self exist? Not in the way ordinary language assumes. It isn't a substance, a soul, or a homunculus, and there's no Cartesian theater where the streams of processing come together in front of a witness. Dennett is basically a bundle theorist here, following Hume: the person is a lot of parallel machinery and the self is the unified character that the output gets edited into. But the analogy also shows why the self isn't real in any interesting sense. A center of gravity is a calculational convenience and so is the self — it's the phlogiston of folk psychology, a placeholder we use because we don't have a better story yet, and in the long run we should just stop positing it. This is the same place the Buddhist no-self doctrine arrives from the other direction.

The contrast with narrative theories of identity is smaller than it looks. Parfit is a narrative theorist too, insofar as he reduces identity to what can be said about a person's continued psychological connections; his view and Dennett's are two versions of one deflationary strategy. And the hermeneutic narrative people — Ricoeur, and the self-constitution crowd after him — also hold that the self is constituted by the story. So the disagreement is mostly verbal: everybody agrees the self is a narrative posit, they just differ on how much weight the first-person telling gets. If there's a real difference it's that Dennett wants the story to stay answerable to what the machinery is actually doing.

One thing that falls out, which I think counts in Dennett's favor: if selves are abstractions rather than inner agents, responsibility can't attach to anything inside the person. It has to attach to the persona. Being a person is a status the practice confers, and holding someone responsible is part of how that abstraction gets made concrete. Substance views have a harder time explaining why responsibility should track the substance.
