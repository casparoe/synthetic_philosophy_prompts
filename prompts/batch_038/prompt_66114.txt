For eleven years I have worked as an operating-theatre technician, and the work has left me with a question I cannot resolve on my own. I recently read a popular book about consciousness that described two rival pictures. The first it called the Cartesian theatre: the idea that all the parallel processing going on in a head eventually comes together somewhere, at some moment, into a single show watched by a single subject, so that there is a definite answer to "was she experiencing it then?" The second it called the multiple drafts model: the idea that there is no such place or moment, that many competing versions of "what just happened" are being assembled and revised in parallel, that only some of them ever become sayable, and that the question "when exactly did she see it?" may simply have no answer. I took the second picture to be the author's own view, though I could not always follow his reasons.

What puzzles me is that my whole profession seems to be built on the first picture, and to be built on it in three inconsistent ways. First, there is the monitor: electrodes on the patient's forehead, and a single number from 0 to 100 that ticks over every few seconds, smoothed across a stretch of the recent signal rather than read off the present instant. There is a target band for it during general anaesthesia, and the whole room treats crossing out of that band as a change in one thing — whether the patient is "under." Second, there is the testing: before the muscle relaxant is given, someone may ask the patient to squeeze a hand twice on command; and in some research a tourniquet is applied to one arm and the arm drained of blood before the relaxant goes in, so that arm stays unparalysed while the rest of the body cannot move. Patients whose isolated arm follows a verbal instruction in the middle of an operation are counted, in those studies, as having been aware. Third, there is the definition of the harm itself: "accidental awareness under anaesthesia" is settled afterwards by whether the patient can recall and describe events from the surgery. So the three tests regularly disagree — an arm answers, the number says 45, and the patient in the recovery room sincerely says she remembers nothing at all — and when they do, nobody concludes the concept has broken down. They just quietly rank the tests.

So I would like to know what this apparatus actually presupposes. Does the practice of the operating room require that there be a single, moment-by-moment, yes-or-no fact about whether one unified subject is conscious — in other words, a theatre — or could it be licensed just as well by a picture on which there is no such fact? And when the arm answers, the number does not, and the memory never arrives, is that three imperfect measurements of one thing, or three measurements of three different things?

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — no more than 100 words, giving your overall verdict on whether the theatre picture is required, optional, or contradicted by what we do.
2. **What the room presupposes** — about 350 words reconstructing the implicit theory of consciousness that makes the monitor, the command-following test and the recall criterion hang together as one enterprise.
3. **Strictly required, or merely congenial** — about 300 words separating what the practice could not function without from what it only happens to sit comfortably beside, and naming the places where it is underdetermined, so that a rival picture would license exactly the same conduct.
4. **Three tests, three verdicts** — about 300 words on the disagreement between responsiveness, recall and the number: whether the profession's implicit ranking of them is principled or merely practical, and what it would mean if they were tracking different things rather than one thing badly.
5. **A theatre-free operating room** — about 300 words on what would have to change in monitoring, in treating awareness as a harm worth preventing, in the way we take consent from a patient before inducing, and in what we say about the person whose arm obeyed while she did not, if the multiple drafts picture turned out to be right.

I have no academic training in any of this, so please define any technical term you need on first use. I am writing during a night shift and cannot look anything up, so please do not give me references, a bibliography or a reading list.
