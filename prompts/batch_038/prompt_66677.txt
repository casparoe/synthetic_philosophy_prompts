I am a novelist, five years into a book whose subject is what a life of labour is for. I have been reading two traditions that seem to me to talk past each other. On one side stands the inheritance of the Protestant ethic as Weber described it and its secular descendants: the conviction that work is the sphere in which a person is formed and justified, that a calling is discharged rather than enjoyed, and that a life without demanding labour is missing something constitutive rather than merely something pleasant. On the other side stands the older and quite different claim that leisure is the only realm of freedom, that labour is instrumental by nature, and that the political task is to shorten it — the claim behind the campaign for the statutory thirty-five-hour week in France, and behind the recoil from death by overwork in Japan. My difficulty is that every scene I have drafted argues for one side, and I can feel the argument arriving from outside the story. I want a case that is not a fable for either camp.

So I would like you to build me a thought experiment, to specification, in which the two accounts genuinely diverge — and to diverge in the direction that is hard to construct. A reduction of working hours produces a real loss that the leisure account, applied honestly, cannot register at all, and the loss must not be reducible to the survival of work discipline inside the workers' own heads. Please treat the following as binding.

1. The setting is an ordinary workplace, not an abstraction and not a dystopia. Choose it yourself, but it must be one where the trade is neither being automated nor losing its market, so that nothing outside the case explains what happens, and where long tenure is normal. Tell me which settings you rejected and why the one you chose is better.
2. The hours are cut, roughly by a third, with pay fully protected and headcount unchanged. Nobody is made redundant, nobody's pace is intensified, and no shift is reorganised to squeeze more out of the same people.
3. Exclude the overwork axis entirely: no illness, no exhaustion, no broken marriages, no early deaths. The case must not depend on anyone having been harmed by long hours in the first place.
4. No character may be a believer in the dignity of labour or a striver by temperament. Nobody pines for the old schedule, nobody's standing among peers turns on how much they work, and five years after the change the workers report, sincerely, that they are content.
5. The loss must not be a transition cost. Whatever is gone is still gone once the new pattern has fully settled.

Then do the checking work. State both positions as precisely as you can in your own words — I will supply the scholarship myself and want no citations or reading list. Give the verdict each account delivers on your case and the reasoning that produces it. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and show me the minimal repair: the smallest change that preserves the hours cut and the protected pay while eliminating the loss, so that I can see whether the loss is contingent or built into the shape of the arrangement. Say explicitly on whom the loss falls, and whether a version in which it falls only on people who are not the workers themselves would still count against the leisure position. Finally, put the strongest available attack in the mouth of a defender of leisure — including the charge that any such loss is nothing but the residue of a moral discipline the workers have absorbed without noticing, and that your constraint against internalisation has therefore quietly begged the question — and tell me whether that attack succeeds.

Aim for about a page of scenario and rather more than that of analysis. If you conclude that the specification cannot be satisfied as written, say so plainly, identify the constraint that must give, and show me the best case you can build with that one relaxed. What I most want to avoid is a scenario whose moral is smuggled in through the furniture.
