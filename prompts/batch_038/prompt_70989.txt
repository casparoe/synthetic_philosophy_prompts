For thirty-eight years I worked as an analytical chemist, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than I had previously thought the subject deserved. I host a small monthly discussion group at my house; the members are mostly former colleagues and neighbours, and the standard of argument is uneven. Last month's meeting produced an exchange between two of them that I have not been able to resolve to my own satisfaction, and I should like a referee's report on it.

The occasion was a circular that went out in our town asking residents of the lakeside subdivision to stop using phosphorus-bearing lawn fertiliser and to plant buffer strips, on the grounds that the lake has been in a turbid, algae-dominated condition for several years. The two disputants are Douglas, a retired civil engineer, and Nadia, a former schoolteacher who runs the local historical society. What follows is close to a reconstruction from the notes I kept — I take notes at these things the way I used to take them at the bench — and I have numbered the moves so that they can be addressed one at a time.

(1) Douglas opened by declining the request on quantitative grounds. The phosphorus reaching the lake, he said, is dominated by upstream agricultural drainage and by legacy sediment already in the basin; the two hundred households of the subdivision together account for perhaps one part in fifty of the annual load, and his own lawn for about one two-hundredth of that, so roughly one part in ten thousand of the whole. A perturbation of that size, he said, is below the detection limit of any sensible measurement of lake response: removing it would not shift the measured concentration by an amount distinguishable from year-to-year variation. Therefore his individual act causes no harm. He added that he was entirely willing to be shown wrong, but that he wished to be shown wrong with numbers.

(2) Nadia replied that this was a threshold error. The state of the lake is not a linear function of its load; it sits in one of two alternative stable conditions, and a sufficiently large reduction in loading can flip it back to clear water. The question is therefore not whether Douglas's contribution alters today's concentration measurably, but whether the sum of many such contributions crosses a critical loading. Douglas, she said, had quietly assumed a smooth dose–response curve in order to conclude that his dose could not matter.

(3) Douglas said he would grant the threshold outright and that it changed nothing. On Nadia's own picture what matters is whether the collective total crosses a critical value, and his contribution moves that total by far less than the uncertainty in where the critical value lies. Being generous, he said, suppose the uncertainty band in the critical loading is as wide as the entire subdivision's contribution; then his chance of being the difference-maker is at best one in two hundred. Multiplying a genuine harm by one chance in two hundred gives an expected harm far too small to outweigh the certain and modest cost of digging up his garden. He asked whether Nadia was prepared to hold that any act with a non-zero expected harm is forbidden, since on that rule he ought also to decline to drive to the meeting at all.

(4) Nadia said that framing was a dodge. The benefit he was weighing is trivial while the harm, if it arrives, is a lake that will not recover within the lifetime of anyone in the room. The proper comparison is not expected harm against certain benefit but the character of a principle: if every lakeside resident reasoned as Douglas does, the lake stays turbid with practical certainty, and Douglas cannot consistently want everyone to reason that way while exempting himself.

(5) Douglas answered that this smuggled in the result. The question, he said, is what he ought to do given what the other residents actually do; and if the other hundred and ninety-nine continue to fertilise, his abstention leaves the load exactly where it was, while if they all abstain the lake recovers whether or not he does. Nadia, he said, had presented him with two worlds — one in which everyone abstains, one in which no one does — and then reproached him for preferring the second, when in fact he is choosing inside the real one, where his act is a single term in a sum he does not control.

(6) Nadia then conceded the causal point and moved to different ground. Douglas's reason for acting, she said, is not the phosphorus at all; taking part in a collective practice one regards as wrong damages the participant, and even if his abstention changed nothing measurable it would remain true that he did not do this thing, and that fact about him carries a weight no expected-harm calculation registers. Douglas replied that this was either consequentialism in disguise — the value of clean hands being simply a feeling he wants to have, and so belonging in the same ledger as his garden — or else a claim that the rightness of an act is independent of its effects, in which case he did not see why he should be moved by it, the whole dispute having begun with the effects.

They have asked me to decide and I cannot. What I want from you is an adjudication, not an overview of the topic and not suggestions for further reading, which I am perfectly capable of finding myself. Specifically:

(a) Take the six moves in order and grade each — clearly right, defensible, weak, or clearly mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification for the grade. Where a good point has been met by an equally good reply, say so rather than awarding the round to either side.

(b) Settle the one sub-dispute that belongs to my trade rather than theirs. In move (3) Douglas converts Nadia's threshold into a probability of making the difference and multiplies. Is that a legitimate operation at all, and is his particular estimate — one in two hundred — the right figure, or has he taken the wrong denominator given the numbers he himself supplied in move (1)? If the multiplication is sound, state precisely what it entitles him to conclude; if it is not, say what has been misdescribed.

(c) Identify what each of them is right about that the other has not actually engaged with, and then name the single assumption on which the entire exchange turns — the one which, if I could get them both to look at it directly, might end the argument.

(d) Tell me whether Douglas can retain his car, his flights and his lawn and remain consistent. If he cannot, say which of the three he must surrender first and why that one rather than another.
