I chair patient-safety reviews across a four-hospital network. My facilitators draft two or three reports a week that go to the medical director and, when a claim is filed, to lawyers, and the drafts are full of conditional sentences that do not mean what their writers think. Two failures recur. First, a sentence whose antecedent we have already established to be false gets written in the indicative — "if the checklist was completed, the discrepancy was caught before the incision" — when the record shows the checklist was never opened. Second, our executive summaries reverse conditionals: a report establishes "if the interlock had been defeated, the press would have cycled," and the summary then reads "the press did not cycle, so the interlock was not defeated."

Write the triage procedure my facilitators should run over the conditionals in a draft: the questions to ask of each sentence, in order; what evidence licenses it as written; when it must be downgraded from *would* to *might*, or recast as a statement about our records rather than about the world; when it must be struck; which inference moves the procedure blocks by name, because our writers keep making them; and which sentences go to a human because the disagreement is about how much of the background we hold fixed rather than about the facts. It has to be runnable in fifteen minutes by a clinician with no training in logic or semantics, so keep the machinery implicit rather than explained, and mark the steps that are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume.

Then run it on three sentences from live drafts: "if the second nurse had been present at handover, the wrong-site mark would have been noticed," where all we have is eleven shifts in which two nurses attended handover and eight in which a marking discrepancy was noticed; "if the unit had been staffed to establishment that night, the error would not have reached the patient," which is the load-bearing line in a capital request I have already submitted; and the pair "if the interlock had been defeated, the press would have cycled" followed immediately by "if the interlock had been defeated with the guard in place, it would not have." Close with what the procedure cannot settle at all.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single distinction that carries most of the weight here — then give the procedure and the three applications, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
