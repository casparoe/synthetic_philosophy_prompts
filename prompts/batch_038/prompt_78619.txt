For some years I've been reading the rationalist corpus on self-knowledge and bias, and the accumulation of results — studies in which people confidently explain choices they were covertly manipulated into making, subjects whose reports about their own inner speech and dreams turn out to be confabulated under gentle questioning, the choice-blindness work in which people give elaborate reasons for a preference they demonstrably did not express — pushed me to a fairly specific thesis that I now hold. I want it stated tightly, because most of my disagreements with friends turn out to be about its scope. The thesis is this: the process that generates my first-person attributions of my own occurrent attitudes — my judgings, decisions, intentions, desires — takes as input only representations in a sensory format: inner speech, imagistic rehearsal of action, interoceptive and bodily feeling, perceptual evidence about what I have just done and what situation I am in, plus whatever background self-theory I carry. There is no non-sensory channel running from an attitude to a belief about that attitude; nothing acquaintance-like, no inner perception of the judgment itself. Three consequences I accept: first-person authority over my attitudes is a matter of my interpretation being unusually fast and unusually well-supplied with evidence, not of my access being of a different kind; confabulation about one's own decisions is the default operation of the system rather than a breakdown of it, differing from other-mind ignorance only in degree and in richness of input; and the felt immediacy of knowing what I just decided is an artifact of the interpretive process failing to represent itself.

What would count against this? I want a map of its exposure, not a survey. Please rank the defeaters explicitly, from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and for each say what the finding, argument, or case would have to look like and why a defender could not absorb it. I'm particularly interested in these pressure points, though I expect you to find others: whether self-reports about one's own decisions and judgments stay accurate at normal rates when the sensory-format intermediaries are experimentally suppressed or degraded — articulatory suppression, verbal shadowing, people who report having no inner speech or no visual imagery at all; the transparency route, on which I answer a question about my belief by attending to the world rather than to myself, and whether a bottleneck theorist can accommodate it without smuggling in a non-sensory input; whether the thesis can make sense of the way an avowal functions in practical reasoning as a reason-giving stopping point rather than as one more piece of evidence, and what it forfeits if it cannot; and cases — paralysis, deafferentation, or ordinary pre-behavioral deliberation — where there is arguably no behavior, imagery, or interoceptive signal left to interpret and yet self-attribution seems unimpaired. For each, tell me which a Carruthers-style defender would themselves concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss as fully compatible with it, and be honest about the base rates: does the thesis require an auxiliary assumption about how rich self-directed sensory input is, and is that assumption independently testable? Also tell me what the thesis predicts that its main rivals do not — acquaintance and inner-sense accounts on one side, constitutivist or avowal-makes-it-so accounts on the other — and which of those predictions could come out wrong rather than merely unimpressive.

Finally, the charge my interlocutor actually presses is that the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability: since any attitude report can be redescribed after the fact as an interpretation of some sensory input, no result can touch it. Address this head-on. State the strongest version of the Duhemian reply available to a defender, then say whether you think it succeeds, and whether a theory that advertises itself as continuous with empirical cognitive science is held to a stricter standard of riskiness than a purely metaphysical account of self-knowledge — that is, whether insulation here is a real defect or a normal feature of the kind of theory this is. Name at least two commitments on which the thesis genuinely sticks its neck out, and specify the observation that would sever each. For calibration on what moves me: I would update hard on a controlled demonstration that people reliably report, above chance and above what a well-briefed outside observer could infer from the same situation, decisions or judgments made under conditions where the sensory intermediaries are blocked, or where those intermediaries are systematically rigged to point at a different attitude than the one reported. I would also be moved by a decisive argument that the view cannot explain the epistemic difference between finding out what one's mind is and making it up. Please don't pad the answer with references or a reading list — I can't look anything up and don't need to; describe the relevant work by content. And don't be diplomatic: if the thesis is in fact well insulated and that is a problem, say so plainly.
