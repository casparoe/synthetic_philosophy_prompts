I am a junior and serve as recording secretary of my school's Student Activities Council, an eleven-member body (nine elected students and two faculty liaisons, all of them voting) that decides which student organizations receive a charter for the year. We meet twice a month, allow roughly twenty-five minutes per proposal, and hear about twenty proposals between September and April. I keep the tally sheets, and the minutes are posted publicly within a day. Any decision can be appealed to the Dean of Students. The Council cannot amend the handbook itself — only the Dean can do that, and a petition takes a full semester to work through — so whatever you propose has to be something we can adopt as internal practice on its own.

The handbook's chartering rule is conjunctive. A charter is granted if and only if three criteria are all met: (A) there is demonstrated student interest; (B) a faculty sponsor has committed in writing; and (C) the organization's activities are consistent with the school's mission and its available resources. Our long-standing practice is to take four separate votes: one on each criterion, and then one on the motion that the charter be granted.

At our October meeting the four votes came apart. The proposal was a charter for a competitive gaming club. All eleven members voted, there were no abstentions, and every member's four votes were internally consistent — each member voted for the motion exactly when they had voted yes on all three criteria. The tally sheet read as follows:

Member 1: A yes, B no, C no, motion no
Member 2: A yes, B no, C no, motion no
Member 3: A yes, B yes, C no, motion no
Member 4: A yes, B yes, C no, motion no
Member 5: A yes, B yes, C yes, motion yes
Member 6: A yes, B yes, C yes, motion yes
Member 7: A no, B yes, C yes, motion no
Member 8: A no, B yes, C yes, motion no
Member 9: A no, B no, C yes, motion no
Member 10: A no, B no, C yes, motion no
Member 11: A no, B no, C no, motion no

Criterion A carried 6–5. Criterion B carried 6–5. Criterion C carried 6–5. The motion to charter was defeated 2–9. Read criterion by criterion, the handbook requires that the charter be granted; read as a single question, the Council rejected it by a wide margin. No member voted against a criterion they thought was met, and only two members are on record as thinking the club should be chartered at all. We postponed the decision, and I have been asked to bring a standing procedure to the next meeting.

I have since learned that this pattern has a name — the doctrinal paradox, or discursive dilemma — that the two ways of resolving it are called the premise-based and the conclusion-based approaches, and that there is a general result to the effect that no aggregation rule escapes some version of the difficulty once a handful of plausible conditions is imposed. I am not asking for a survey of that literature, and please do not give me one. I need something that eleven people with no training in logic or voting theory can follow at a table in real time.

Please write the procedure I can put in front of the Council. It should address, in order:

1. What order the votes are taken in, and whether the vote on the motion should be taken at all once the criterion votes are known.
2. A check the secretary can run on the paper tally sheet, in under five minutes and without arithmetic beyond counting, that determines whether the meeting has produced a divergence before anything is announced.
3. What the chair does when divergence is found — announce one of the two results, re-open a vote, table the proposal, refer it to the Dean, or something else — together with a reason for that choice that does not amount to "because the handbook says so."
4. How the minutes should record a divergent case, so that a reader six months from now can reconstruct what the Council decided and what it did not. Please include sample minute language I could paste in.
5. How to handle a disjunctive rule rather than a conjunctive one. Our appeals procedure says that a student's appeal of a club's suspension succeeds if either the Council mishandled the procedure or the sanction was disproportionate to the violation, and I expect the same inversion can arise there.
6. How to handle a proposal split across two meetings — we sometimes table criterion C until the facilities office reports back — where members can see how their earlier votes constrain the later ones.
7. What to do about insincere voting: a member who anticipates the inversion may vote no on a criterion they believe is met in order to block an outcome. Should ballots be public or secret, and should the procedure allow a re-vote once divergence has been discovered? If some part of this cannot be fixed by procedure, say so plainly rather than hiding it.
8. Where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a person — the Dean, or the Council as a whole — because what remains is a question about what the Council is for rather than a question about counting.

Then run the procedure on three cases. The first is the October tally above. The second should be an appeals case of your own construction, using the disjunctive rule in item 5, in which the two routes again point in opposite directions. The third should be a case where the divergence only becomes visible at the second of two meetings. For at least one of the three, your procedure should tell the Council to do something I think is wrong: I wanted the gaming club chartered, and I would rather not hand the Dean a rule under which nine members' reasoned opposition is overridden by what looks to me like an arithmetic accident. I want that outcome stated plainly, not designed away.

Finally, two questions I will have to answer at the meeting, so please answer them in ordinary language:

- Is it accurate to say that the Council, as a body, granted a charter that not one of its members granted? Or does that description overstate what a group can be said to believe?
- Is there a principled reason to prefer the premise-based route to the conclusion-based route, or does the choice ultimately depend on what a body like ours is supposed to be — a fact-finder applying written criteria, or an assembly expressing the collective judgment of its members?

Constraints on your answer: the procedure itself should fit on one page, use no formal notation, and require nothing that cannot be counted off a paper ballot. Assume the chair is a sophomore who will be irritated by any instruction that requires pausing the meeting for an explanation.
