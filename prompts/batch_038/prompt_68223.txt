I'm second year in a political theory PhD and my chapter is basically about who gets to count as a knower in environmental disputes, so I've been going round in circles on Love Canal. Everyone I talk to reaches for the same two framings — plucky working-class housewives versus a state that kept saying there was no evidence of harm, or the citizen-science-hero story where the community's own door-to-door survey shamed the epidemiologists into doing their job — and both of those are fine, but they're journalism rather than argument, and my supervisor has now told me twice that the chapter needs a theory doing actual work. What I want to do instead is run the whole episode through Dewey's experimentalism, specifically the apparatus in The Public and Its Problems plus the account of inquiry in the Logic, and see how much weight it'll bear. Take the facts as roughly this, correct me where I've got them wrong but don't spend much time on them: Hooker Chemical dumped of the order of twenty thousand tonnes of chemical waste into an unfinished canal trench at Niagara Falls through the 1940s and early 50s, sold the land to the school board for a dollar in 1953 with, I gather, a caveat clause in the deed spelling out what was under it, a school went up on part of the site and houses were built all round it, and nothing much happened until 1977–78, when the state health department declared an emergency and evacuated the families closest to the canal; Lois Gibbs's homeowners association kept at it, Carter announced money in 1980 to buy out pretty much the rest of the neighbourhood — some eight hundred families in the end — and Congress passed Superfund that same year. The cleanup ran for two decades and the neighbourhood was demolished. Then there's the awkward bit for my purposes: the later health studies were a good deal more equivocal than the 1978–80 consensus implied, and some people say the miscarriage and birth-defect claims never really held up statistically. Treat that as contested if you like, I don't want the analysis hanging on it, but I do want you to take it seriously rather than write it out.

On the Dewey side, here's my rough gloss, and please fix it where it's sloppy before you use it. A public comes into existence when people recognise the indirect and far-reaching consequences of transactions they weren't party to and organise to control them; the state is that public organised through officials; under modern conditions the public is eclipsed, meaning it exists but can't find itself, can't identify its own members or its own problem; and the way out is communication, which is why Dewey thinks an expert class that isn't in ongoing communication with the people it serves turns into a closed caste that stops serving their interests at all. Then in the Logic: inquiry starts from an indeterminate, problematic situation, ideas work as plans of action and hypotheses rather than as pictures, the end state is a situation that's been made determinate, and claims hold only as warranted rather than as representations of how things really are. Democracy is the political analogue of that method — policies as hypotheses to be tested and revised, not dogma. Tell me if I've flattened anything important, especially about the difference between the public and the state, because I suspect that's where my reading is weakest.

So, first, do the mapping properly and don't blur the terms. At which moment, if any, does a public exist at Love Canal on Dewey's definition — the residents who lived on top of the trench for twenty-five years, the parents' movement, the homeowners association, or the national thing that formed after the news coverage? Are the pre-1977 residents a public in eclipse or not a public at all, and does Dewey's account have the resources to tell those apart? Where do Hooker, the school board and the state health department sit: if the state is supposed to be the public organised through officials, then when it announces there's no evidence of harm, is that the eclipse happening inside the state, or is it something the framework has no slot for? And run the 1978–80 sequence as an inquiry in the Logic's sense — are the community's survey and the state's epidemiology two competing inquiries into one problematic situation, or different kinds of thing entirely, and what does the communication requirement do to that question? Then turn it round, which is the part I actually care about. Does the buyout and demolition count as inquiry resolving the problematic situation, or as the situation being dissolved rather than made determinate, and is that a success or a failure on Dewey's own terms? Is Superfund an experimental policy, a hypothesis held open to revision, or is retroactive strict liability precisely the sort of settled dogma his experimentalism is supposed to rule out? And if the later health evidence really is equivocal, what does the framework say about a public that mobilised and got eight hundred families relocated on an assertion that may never have been warranted — is that a case where the warrant was relative to a genuinely indeterminate situation, so the burden of proof was the political question all along, or does Dewey simply have no way of handling a successful mobilisation built on bad knowledge? Finally, be honest about where the theory goes quiet: latency and time lags, harms you can't see or smell, a corporate actor that generates the consequences but isn't anybody's public, a resolution that ends up national and legislative rather than face-to-face and communal. If some other framework handles those better, say so in a sentence or two, but don't go theory-shopping and don't give me references or a reading list — what I want to know is whether Dewey survives contact with the case, or whether this is an episode he'd never have anticipated and the theory is doing less work here than it looks.
