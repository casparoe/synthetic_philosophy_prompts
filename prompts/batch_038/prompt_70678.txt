I spent forty years in surface physics, the last twenty of them manipulating individual atoms on cold metal with the tip of a scanning tunnelling microscope, and I have taken up philosophy only since retiring. I have been working through the special composition question — van Inwagen, Sider, and the replies to them — and I have reconstructed an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept and whose steps I cannot, so far, fault. It is set out below in the form I would put it to a colleague.

(1) Either composition is a genuine relation in the world, in which case for any things whatever it is a settled matter of fact whether they compose something, or composition is a manner of speaking and there are no composite objects in any serious sense.

(2) If it is a settled matter of fact, then in any continuous series of cases running from clear composition to clear non-composition there must be a definite point at which composition begins; otherwise the facts leave the question open somewhere in the middle of the series, which is simply to say that they do not settle it.

(3) For any candidate criterion of composition — contact, fastening, cohesion, functional integration, being the activity of a life — such a series can be constructed in which the criterion is satisfied in continuously varying degree, and in which there is no non-arbitrary point at which to say composition begins. From my own practice: I can deposit gold adatoms one at a time along a step edge, forty-one of them, at spacings I vary by fractions of an ångström. Nothing in the physics marks off the spacing at which a chain comes into being from the spacing one increment wider or narrower, and the transition from isolated adatoms to a conducting wire is a matter of degree in every quantity I was ever able to measure.

(4) Therefore no criterion of restricted composition — composition obtaining in some cases and not in others — settles the facts, and by (2) restricted composition is not genuine.

(5) What remains is that composition always obtains, or that it never obtains.

(6) Universalism cannot rescue the argument's target. If my forty-one adatoms compose something with a grain of dust in Andromeda, then the objects my practice singled out — this crystal, this adsorbate row, this cryostat — are no less arbitrary sums than any other, and the fact that I spent a career counting and characterising some of these sums rather than others turns out to have had no worldly correlate.

(7) Hence nothing composes anything. There are no crystals, no molecules, no instruments; and since persons are composite things, there is nobody who spent a career finding any of them out.

(8) The same form of reasoning, with the appropriate substitutions, appears to show that there are no bald men, no heaps, and no species — which is why I suspect I have made a structural error rather than a local one.

I refuse (7) and (8). I would rather keep molecules than keep any premise above, but I do not know which premise must go in order to keep them, and I am aware that a physicist's instinct for "the facts settle it" may be exactly the thing doing the damage here.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable to someone with my training? Then price the alternatives — state what each of the positions available to you must pay in order to reject the step you reject, and say which pays least. Finally, tell me plainly what would change your verdict: which consideration, if I pressed it, would drive you to a different diagnosis, and at which point you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Please keep the whole reply under 450 words. I have read enough philosophy in the last two years to recognise a hedge, and I would much rather have a short answer that commits than a long one that does not.
