A pattern runs through the institutions that gate commitments from which it is hard to go back. American medical schools ask applicants to describe their clinical exposure and treat a substantial record of shadowing hours as counting in their favour. Law firms hire summer associates in the expectation that many of them will be offered permanent positions. Teacher-preparation programs require a semester of student teaching before certification. Religious orders interpose postulancy and novitiate, often several years in all, between a candidate's arrival and their vows. The armed forces let a recruit sign an enlistment contract and then sit at home for months in a delayed-entry pool, in practice free to walk away almost up to the day they ship. In many jurisdictions an adoption is not final until a supervised post-placement period of several months has elapsed. In each case, the institution arranges for the person to sample the life before the commitment binds.

What puzzles me is that these same institutions routinely deny that the sample can do what the arrangement implies. Pre-health advisors tell applicants that no number of shadowing hours will show them what residency is actually like, and that the first year of clinical training will change them in ways they cannot now anticipate. Programs describe their own student-teaching placements as something other than the real job. Firms describe the summer as a sanitized and artificial version of practice. So the trial period is required, funded, and treated as decision-relevant by the very people who insist it cannot inform the decision.

Please address the following:

1. Reconstruct the view of self-knowledge and of practical deliberation that the trial-period arrangement presupposes. Does it require that there be a settled fact, in advance of the commitment, about whether the person will endorse the life they would thereby get — a fact that a short exposure can reveal? Or does it require only something weaker, such as that sampling raises the probability of a good match enough to justify the cost? Be precise about the difference between these two readings and about which one the institutional behaviour actually supports.

2. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably. Could the arrangement be doing no epistemic work at all — functioning as a screen for perseverance, a signal of commitment, a source of inexpensive labour, or a socialization process that produces the fit it claims merely to detect — without any view about self-knowledge being implicated? What feature would distinguish a trial period whose function is revelatory from one whose function is constitutive, and can you tell which of the cases above is which? Note that the adoption supervision period is not offered to the prospective parents as information; it is a check conducted upon them by a third party. Same structure, opposite justificatory direction — say what that does to the theory.

3. Ask whether the pattern is consistent with what these institutions do elsewhere. Nobody arranges a trial for the paradigm case of an irreversible, self-altering commitment, namely having a child; and some institutions deliberately invert the logic, designing initial military training so that it resembles the job it prepares you for as little as possible. What does the difference between letting people sample before they commit and taking care that they cannot presuppose about whether preferences are found or made?

4. Suppose we accepted that for certain choices there is no answer, in advance, to whether you will want the outcome, because the person who would have to want it does not yet exist in the relevant sense. What would have to change — in admissions criteria, in the architecture of professional training, and in the consent conversations that surround these decisions? Would the practice be abolished, or survive under a new rationale, for instance as a way of choosing for the sake of the transformation rather than in the light of it? If it would survive unchanged, say what that shows about whether it ever depended on the view you identified in part 1.
