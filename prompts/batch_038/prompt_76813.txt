I hold an appointment in philosophy and, by an unhappy coincidence of interests, one of the seven faculty seats on my university's Research Integrity Panel, which hears allegations of data fabrication and falsification. Our code states a conjunctive standard: misconduct is made out only if the panel finds both (F) that the data in the published work were fabricated or falsified, and (K) that this was done knowingly or recklessly rather than through honest error. Each member records a position on F, on K, and on the overall determination, and all three ballots are entered in the record. The bylaws contribute two clauses and nothing else: "the panel shall determine each question put to it by a majority of the members sitting," and "where the votes on any question are equally divided, that question shall be resolved in favour of the respondent." They are silent on the order in which questions are put, and silent on whether a vote on an element is taken by the whole panel or only by those members already satisfied as to some prior element.

Our record over four years is as follows, in each case giving how many members accepted both elements, how many accepted F alone, how many accepted K alone, and how many accepted neither.

1. Five accepted both, one accepted F alone, one accepted K alone. The panel made a finding; the opinion was unanimous.
2. One accepted both, one accepted F alone, five accepted K alone. No finding. The opinion observed that however culpable a state of mind may be, the act element had failed.
3. Three accepted both, two accepted F alone, two accepted K alone. Finding, over a dissent. The majority wrote that it was satisfied as to each element of the standard and that the determination followed. On the determination ballot itself the votes had run three to four against a finding. I believe this is a textbook instance of the discursive dilemma, and it is the precedent our office now cites most often.
4. Three accepted both, three accepted F alone, one accepted K alone. Finding. The dissenting member protested that once F had carried by six votes to one, the question of K ought to have been put to those six alone, among whom it was evenly divided, and that the bylaws then required the tie to be resolved in the respondent's favour. The majority's opinion said only that the panel was satisfied as to each element.
5. Four accepted F alone, three accepted K alone, nobody accepted both. No finding. A majority held that the data had been falsified, a majority held that the mental element was not made out, and the panel was unanimous that no misconduct had been established.

The case now before us is the first in which a member has recused himself, for co-authorship, so six will sit. The ballots are: two members accept both elements, two accept F alone, two accept K alone. F therefore carries four to two, K carries four to two, and the determination runs two to four against a finding. Two of the six sitting members joined the panel after case 4 and had no hand in any of these precedents.

I would like a careful analysis rather than a hunch. First, identify the candidate procedures that remain consistent with our record — including ones I may not have listed — and show, with the tallies worked through, what each implies for the pending case. Say explicitly which of our five precedents do the eliminative work, and whether any elimination depends on an interpretive assumption I should not take for granted; I have in mind particularly the reach of the equal-division clause, which was plainly drafted with full-panel votes in view and which, so far as I can tell, bites only if a question is put to a restricted subset of members. Second, predict the outcome and describe the path of reasoning the panel is likeliest to take in reaching it. Third, and more importantly, tell me what we should decide if we wish to be consistent with ourselves: name the closest analog among the five precedents, say what makes it closest rather than merely most recent, and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the pending case, state plainly what principle the panel adopts in each available direction — including which of the conditions that the impossibility result in this area shows to be jointly unsatisfiable (anonymity of the members' votes, systematicity across issues, consistency of the collective set) each choice would be the one to surrender. Fourth, describe a single further case, specified by its ballot profile, that would settle which procedure we are in fact running, and explain why that profile rather than another. Finally, address the awkward question raised by our changed composition: whether members who joined after case 4 are bound by it at all, and what consistency means for a body whose membership has partly turned over.
