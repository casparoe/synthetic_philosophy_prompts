Okay so background: I've been reading LW and the Alignment Forum since approximately forever, and the thing that always stuck with me is logical uncertainty — the fact that an ideal Bayesian is logically omniscient, and therefore a useless model of an actual reasoner, because there are true statements I'd bet my life on that I can't prove and false ones I'd bet my life on that I can't disprove. My sister's a number theorist and when I tried to explain this to her over dinner she basically said "that's not a real problem, just put a prior over models and update," and I froze, because I could feel that the answer was *no, its not about which model is actual, it's about what it costs to drag consequences out of the model you already have* — but I couldn't articulate it in a way that landed. Fiction is a better vehicle. So I'm commissioning you.

Role: you're a working SF writer who happens to have read enough decision theory to be dangerous. Think whoever wrote the good parts of *Permutation City*, but with more patience for characters and less appetite for monologue. I want a short story, roughly 2,000–2,500 words, that dramatizes the idea of a prediction market in *logical* statements — a place where prices are posted on open mathematical claims, people trade them, and the whole design goal is that nobody who has to actually do the reasoning with finite time and a finite skull can systematically bleed the market for unbounded profit. Coherence is the thing you get only in a limit nobody can reach.

Setting seed, change whatever you want: there's an institution — call it the Exchange, the Consistory, the Ledger, your pick — that has been posting prices on unproven claims for generations, long enough that the prices have acquired social weight (marriages, tenure, land disputes, all quietly settled by reference to the board). Your protagonist is a trader or an auditor who spots an arbitrage she can *see* and cannot *cash in*, because cashing it requires being smarter than the rules permit. The juicy version, if you want it: the claim she's circling is self-referential — a statement about the Exchange's own future price for itself — and the free money is sitting in plain sight, uncollected, because collecting it is a computation nobody in that building could finish in a lifetime. I want the reader to feel what it's like to have a belief system you can't compute: to be able to point at the exact spot where you're incoherent and still be unable to fix it.

Constraints, please actually follow these:
- Before the story, give me four to six candidate plot engines — the mechanism that generates the conflict, two or three sentences each — then say which one you picked and why. Spend real effort on this part; the engines should be genuinely different, not six costumes on the same idea.
- No character ever says "logical induction," "Dutch book," "Bayesian," or anything that sounds like a lecture. The rules get taught exactly once, by watching someone lose money.
- The price behavior has to be internally consistent. If you need to, work out the numbers on scratch paper before writing. I will notice if a price moves for no reason.
- Don't use real open conjectures and resolve them, and don't state real theorems as if they were uncertain. Invent your own claims.
- Ending stays unresolved. Nobody brilliant arrives and does the computation.
- No citations, no bibliography, no invented paper titles or fake authors anywhere.
- After the story: a ~250 word author's note saying which technical properties you dramatized faithfully, which ones you bent for the sake of the plot, and one line each on why. Be honest here — if you cheated, say where.
