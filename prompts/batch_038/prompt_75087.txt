I'm a physics grad student and my roommate is in year four of a philosophy PhD, writing something about Leibniz and individuation, and she's been running me through thought experiments over dinner for months. I've started keeping notes because I want to catch her out, and I think I finally have a case that will do it — but I want to check my reasoning before I spring it.

Her verdicts so far, roughly in the order she gave them:

1. The Max Black universe — nothing exists except two exactly similar iron spheres, same diameter, same colour, same temperature, sitting some distance apart in otherwise empty space — is genuinely possible, there are two spheres, and they're not identical. She treats this as settled and gets impatient with people who want to resist it.
2. Same universe, except the two spheres are exactly superimposed, occupying the same region. Impossible. But she insists this has nothing to do with indiscernibility — it's just that two material things can't be in the same place at once. Two different arguments, and she gets annoyed when people conflate them.
3. The variant where one sphere has a hairline scratch: not a case at all, that's just two discernible spheres, you've changed the subject.
4. Kant's two hands in an empty universe: discernible. Handedness is intrinsic to the hand, she says, you don't need anything outside it to tell a left glove from a right glove. She got quite heated and I didnt follow half the argument.
5. A world just like ours in every qualitative respect except that the stuff making up you and the stuff making up your duplicate are swapped: not a different world. If two worlds agree on everything qualitative they're the same world, there's no further fact about which hunk of matter is really which.
6. When I tried to bring in my own field — surely the electrons in an atom are actual indiscernibles, so physics already decided this — she said yes, exactly, the principle's been false for a century, and then said something about their being only "weakly discernible" by some relation she wouldn't spell out.
7. Her favourite: the two square roots of minus one. As far as anything the field structure of the complex numbers can say goes, i and −i are exactly alike — there's an automorphism swapping them — and nobody thinks that means there's only one of them. So there are real, uncontroversial, actually existing indiscernibles, and anyone defending the principle owes an account of why the mathematical case is different.

Here's the case I've been saving: take Black's universe and delete the distance. Nothing exists except two exactly similar, partless, non-spatial simples — no space for them to be apart in, no time for one to be earlier than the other, no causal contact, no shared parts, nothing at all relating one to the other. Same nature, twice over. Two things, one thing, or a description that hasnt actually described anything? My guess is she says two, because of 6 and 7. But 5 makes me nervous: if a world is just a way for things to be qualitatively, and I can't say how the two-blob world differs qualitatively from a one-blob world, it looks like I haven't described a world — which would make the case bogus. And I suspect 1 has been resting on the distance between the spheres the whole time without her noticing.

So: what are the candidate principles she could be running that fit all seven verdicts? I'm assuming it's more than one, since 5 and 7 seem to pull in opposite directions and I can't see a single rule doing both. What does each of them say about the stripped-down case, and which way do you bet she goes — and what's the strongest reason to bet that way rather than the other? Then the thing I actually care about: what follow-up would force her off the fence? Ideally one more case I can put to her at dinner that the candidate rules disagree about, so her answer tells me which one she's got. And if it turns out her seven verdicts aren't jointly consistent at all, say so plainly and tell me which one she'd most likely give up and why that one.
