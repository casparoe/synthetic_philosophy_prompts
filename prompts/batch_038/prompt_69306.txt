For the past nine years I have led a Tuesday-morning reading group at the continuing-care community where my mother has lived since her stroke two winters ago. This autumn the group has asked for six sessions on Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute, and I have agreed, although I no longer hold that the text means what I was trained to think it means. I need help preparing for the discussion rather than for a scholarly audience.

My thesis, which I intend to state plainly in the first ten minutes, is this. Cicero's answers to the four charges against old age — that it withdraws one from active affairs, that it weakens the body, that it cuts off pleasures, and that it brings one near to death — are not symmetrical. He concedes the second and third almost wholly and then revalues them; the real argument is carried by the first and the fourth. And the first, the claim that age still does great work because it supplies judgment and authority rather than strength, rests on a condition Cicero never states as a condition: that there be an arena in which an old person's word is owed a hearing. His examples are men holding office or commanding estates. He wrote the dialogue in 44 BC, in his sixty-third year, addressed to Atticus, while himself shut out of the republic he had spent his life in; on my reading the speech he gives to an eighty-four-year-old Cato is an act of self-appointment, and the consolation it offers does not travel to readers who have no deliberative office left to hold. What survives for us is the long passage on farming, where the good lies in the practice itself rather than in being heard.

The group runs fourteen to eighteen regulars, median age about eighty-five. Nobody has Latin, and I will not use it. Two are former academics: a retired clinical psychologist who reads the empirical literature on cognitive reserve and late-life depression, and a retired structural engineer who wants to know whether an argument is load-bearing or decorative. Several are widowed, one has Parkinson's with early cognitive symptoms, one is a former hospital chaplain. They are not hostile to Cicero; they are hostile to being consoled by him. They have been told for thirty years that memory fails only where it is not exercised, and they have watched it fail regardless. Cicero hands them the point, since he remarks early on that the fault lies in a man's character and not in his age, which several of them will hear as blaming the unhappy for their unhappiness. They will not object in my vocabulary — no one will accuse me of anachronism or of over-politicizing the dialogue. They will say that Cato had slaves, that Cicero had estates and a name, and that old age is easy to bear while people still rise when you come into the room.

Please prepare me for the discussion as it will actually go:

1. The eight or ten objections this group will raise, in the order they will arise, phrased in their idiom rather than mine.
2. A ranking by how much damage each does to the thesis as I have stated it, which is not the same as by how loudly it will be voiced. Tell me which to concede outright and concede early, inside the opening ten minutes, so that the rest of the hour is not spent defending; which to concede in part while holding the thesis; and which I can answer.
3. For each, a reply deliverable in under two minutes — the room tires quickly and a lecture will lose them — with an explicit note on where the reply is soft, and on whether the honest answer is that my reading leaves Cicero less useful to them rather than more.
4. The objection the retired clinical psychologist will make, and make politely, from evidence rather than experience. Assume her data are correct and tell me what remains of the thesis afterward.
5. Which one of the four charges I should stop defending in front of this audience altogether, and which one I can still defend without insulting anyone present.
6. The question I am hoping nobody asks, which concerns my mother: she sits in the second row, and last month she said within a nurse's hearing that she was done with things and simply waiting. If someone puts that to me and asks whether my reading is a way of not answering her, tell me whether a defensible response exists, and what it is.

Do not direct me to the secondary literature and do not propose handouts with passages in the original. I want the objections, the replies, and a clear account of which of my own commitments will not survive the first quarter hour.
