I moderate a couple of panels a year for a science-writing festival, and last month one ran long and the mics stayed hot through the argument, so I have it more or less verbatim. It was about Malthus, and I still can't tell who landed what. Can you go through it move by move?

The two were Ruth, an economic historian, and Dev, who builds integrated-assessment-style models. Dev opened.

(1) Dev: the man is falsified, full stop. He said the food supply grows in a slow arithmetic way while population grows geometrically, so any improvement gets eaten by more mouths. Instead population went up more than tenfold and calories per head rose with it. A theory whose central prediction is the opposite of what happened isn't 'right but early,' it's wrong, and calling it early is just politeness to a dead clergyman.

(2) Ruth: wrong about the future, yes, but he was describing a pattern that had held for essentially all of recorded history up to the year he was writing. For millennia, better harvests and better technique showed up as more people at roughly the same living standard, not as richer people. He named that regularity and he named it accurately. The trap broke around 1800. Being the guy who wrote up the trap in the decade it ended is bad luck, not bad science.

(3) Dev: a theory that fits everything before it's stated and nothing after isn't a theory, it's a description of the past wearing a lab coat. And note he had a hatch built in. He allowed that the labouring classes could escape the misery check by 'moral restraint' — marrying late, staying chaste until then — and said if they didn't, famine and disease would do the trimming for them. Heads the mechanism runs, tails the mechanism runs. That's the definition of unfalsifiable.

(4) Ruth: actually the restraint clause is where he stuck his neck out, not where he hid it. He made a live empirical claim — that the poor would not in practice restrain themselves, because they're improvident and short-sighted — and that claim was flatly false. Fertility collapsed across the rich world within a couple of generations, without any famine making them do it. So the theory was refuted precisely at the point where he committed, and you're annoyed that he committed at all.

(5) Dev: then we agree, and 'right but early' is just a nicer word for wrong.

(6) Ruth: no, because you have to seperate the parts. Mechanism: diminishing returns to labour on fixed land. Still true, still in every textbook, just swamped by other terms. Parameter: how fast that mechanism bites, which is a function of how quickly technique improves — he assumed slow and roughly linear. Timing: when the constraint binds. A model can have a correct mechanism and a bad parameter and you don't get to say the mechanism was the error. Same structure as the old Simon-Ehrlich metals bet, incidentally: Simon won because the ten-year window he happened to pick was one of the best for him, and people who've rerun it across every window from 1900 onward find he loses most of them. Window choice isn't mechanism either.

(7) Dev: two problems. First, that decomposition is infinitely elastic — any busted forecast can be laundered by blaming a parameter, so tell me what it rules out. Second, and more important, the Malthus mechanism isn't a mechanism with a knob on it, it's an arrow pointing the wrong way. Boserup's whole point in the sixties was that population pressure drives agricultural method, not the reverse: density shortens the fallow, forces you into annual cropping, forces fertiliser and weeding and irrigation, and that's where invention comes from. He didn't get the parameter wrong, he got the direction of causation wrong, and the escape was Boserupian.

(8) Ruth: Boserup explains intensification inside the trap, not out of it. Her own account is that as density rises, output per acre goes up while output per hour of work goes down — people farm harder and stay just as poor. That's the trap absorbing shocks, which is Malthus's claim, not a rival to it. Nobody escaped by working harder per bushel. The escape came from outside both theories: fossil energy, synthetic nitrogen, and then a fertility transition neither of them derived. And he did make one committed, testable, non-parameter claim: that parish relief bid up the price of provisions and swelled the number of claimants without making the poor better off. That's a specific policy prediction with a specific causal story attached.

Dev's last line was that this concedes everything — right about all of pre-1800, silent about all of post-1800, so he's a historian we keep mislabelling as a forecaster.

So: rate each of the eight moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating — I want the arguement broken into pieces, not a summary of who sounded better. Then say which specific points Ruth wins, which Dev wins, whether either of them actually answered (3) and (7), and who's more right overall. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing. I'm asking because I'm also working on a piece about people who claim freshwater aquifers are the new fixed land, and I'd like to know whether 'right but early' is ever a legitimate verdict or just a way of keeping a theory alive past its expiry date — and if it's sometimes legitimate, what has to be true for it to be.
