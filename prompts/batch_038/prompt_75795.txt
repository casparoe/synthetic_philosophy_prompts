A course I'm auditing on the origins of the Cold War leans on a single analogy: the escalation of 1945–48 was a security dilemma — each side's defensive precautions read as offensive preparations by the other, producing a spiral neither wanted. The lecturer uses this to argue that asking who started the Cold War is simply the wrong question, since a spiral has no first mover worth naming and the participants were trapped rather than culpable.

Audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the defensive posture, what plays the misperception, what plays the ratchet that makes each round harder to reverse — and then where the mapping strains: can it still count as a security dilemma when one side holds a nuclear monopoly and the other holds a preponderant conventional army together with political control of the territory between them, and when each side professes to know the other's long-run intentions from its doctrine rather than having to guess at them? Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from the ones that are merely interesting, say whether the trapped-rather-than-culpable verdict survives the audit or whether the analogy was carrying it all along, and note what the analogy quietly assumes about states being unitary actors oriented toward the status quo. If you think there is a better analogy for 1945–48, offer one and state what it does and does not license.
