I'm about 200 pages into a novel and I need a philosopher's number, not a philosopher's shrug. I've read enough about Nagel's four kinds of luck to be dangerous, which is exactly the problem.

The setup, roughly: two brothers, same household, same temper, both drink too much at the same party on the same night, both know perfectly well they shouldn't drive, both drive. One of them, on an empty road, gets home. The other one, three minutes later on the same route, kills a kid on a bike. That's the textbook version and I know it's textbook. Here's what I've done to it in the book, which is where I actually need help: the brother who kills the child is never caught — no witness, no camera, nothing — and the event remakes him. He spends the next thirty years doing slow, unglamorous good, and by the end he's a substantially better man than he was. The brother who gets home safely is never touched by anything at all and stays exactly the petty, careless, drinking man he always was. Both know what happened. Neither of them ever says a word.

So the proposition I want a credence on: at the end of the book, the unlucky brother deserves more blame than the lucky one. Deserves it, meaning there's a fact of the matter about blameworthiness that comes out that way — not merely that we'd find it fitting to feel different things toward them, or to treat them differently, but that the unlucky one is more blameworthy, full stop.

Give me the number. Then break it open, because a bare number is no use to me. I want the sub-questions with a rough probability on each and a line or two of justification:

- whether resultant luck is real once you've genuinely controlled for intention, foresight, and degree of negligence — or whether the pull of "the one who killed the kid is worse" is really a confusion between moral blame and horror at an outcome;
- how much of it turns on what blame is even aimed at: the man at the wheel that night, or the person the whole life adds up to. My book bets hard on the second one and I want to know whether that's a defensible position or just a novelist's convenience;
- whether thirty years of decency can actually reduce the blame, or only make it inappropriate for anyone to press it;
- whether the control principle survives at all here, given that their temperaments, the party, the road, the bike and the kid's decision to ride out at that hour were all handed to them from nowhere;
- and whether ordinary people's judgements really do track the outcome in cases like this, or whether the psychology is doing something else entirely that we're mislabeling as a moral intuition.

Show me how those combine into your number rather than just listing them, and tell me which one your answer is most hostage to — the single sub-question where, if I could convince you I'm right about it, your number moves furthest. Also: my editor reads the case and says 0.9, I say 0.45, and I suspect almost the whole gap between those two is sitting in one of your terms. Tell me which one, and tell me if I'm wrong about that too, because that's the fight we're actually having at 11pm most nights.
