I am in the final year of my degree and currently taking a course on the ethics of war. We have just moved from the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants to the revisionist critique of it, and for my seminar paper I tried to construct an argument in the revisionists' own idiom. When I ran it to the end, however, it appeared to show that the revisionist position collapses back into the Walzerian view it was built to overthrow, and I have been unable to locate the bad step. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The argument is meant to be internal to the reductionist program: it grants the reductionist her premises and claims she ends up where she started.

(1) On the reductionist account, what combatants may do in war is fixed entirely by the ordinary morality of harming — liability, necessity, and proportionality, applied case by case to individual persons rather than to states.
(2) A person is liable to be killed in defense of others only if she is morally responsible for an unjust threat.
(3) Moral responsibility for an unjust threat comes in degrees, and liability comes in degrees with it; a negligible degree of responsibility grounds at most a negligible degree of liability, which is not enough to make lethal force permissible.
(4) In most wars, most combatants on both sides bear at most a negligible degree of responsibility for the injustice their side is doing: they are conscripted, or taught a state-controlled account of the war's origins, or are too young and too epistemically cut off from independent information to assess their government's case.
(5) So most combatants on both sides are not liable to be killed, and killing them is impermissible.
(6) The reductionist's own account therefore hands the ordinary soldier, on both sides of the line, exactly the same practical verdict: she may not kill the enemy, because the enemy is not liable to be killed.
(7) A symmetric structure of in bello permissions, in which combatants on either side face the same permissions, is precisely what the moral equality of combatants asserts.
(8) Hence the reductionist critique of the moral equality of combatants, pursued rigorously, re-establishes the moral equality of combatants.

Of these, I am most attached to (4), because the empirical claim about conscription and propaganda is what made revisionism attractive to me, and to (2), because abandoning it seems to abandon the individualist insight altogether. I wrote (3) mainly to handle the conscript case and could live without it.

Please tell me which step or steps actually fail, and for each one: why it looked plausible enough for me to write it down; what the reductionist pays to give it up; and whether giving it up rescues the revisionist conclusion or merely replaces this argument with a different problem. If you think the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to surrender, say so and price the options. I would also like to know whether anything survives the diagnosis — whether the argument, even if it does not refute the reductionist position, still identifies a genuine difficulty for it that the traditional doctrine handles better.

Since I intend to defend this in seminar, please also be explicit about how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, and single out the point you are least sure of: if there is a premise where you think a respectable case could be made either way, flag it rather than presenting the argument as decisively broken.
