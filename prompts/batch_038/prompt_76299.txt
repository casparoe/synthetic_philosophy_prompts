You are a philosopher of technology preparing a specification document for an interdisciplinary working group (legal theory, AI ethics, and engineering) that intends to design a liability regime for autonomous systems. Before the group compares candidate proposals, it wants to agree on what any proposal must deliver. Think carefully before drafting, and begin with a short brainstorming section: list the hard cases and the conceptual pressure points you can think of, note which of them seem to pull in opposite directions, and only then commit to the requirements list.

To keep the exercise honest, work from the following running case, which you should treat as fixed stipulation rather than as a description of any actual product.

**The Corridor Case.** A hospital corridor robot, fully autonomous, is tasked with moving linens between floors. Its navigation stack was trained and verified on data collected in corridors with a particular geometry: doorways of a standard width, floor markings of a standard reflectivity, and a maximum observed pedestrian density. On the day in question, a temporary partition has been erected for renovation work, the floor has been waxed to a higher gloss than anything in the training distribution, and eleven visitors are waiting at a lift. The robot's obstacle classifier returns a confidence of 0.91 that the space ahead is traversable. It proceeds. It strikes a visitor, who suffers a permanent injury. Subsequent reconstruction establishes the following, and your account must be able to accommodate all of it:

1. No human being present at the time could have intervened, and no human not present could have foreseen this specific conjunction of conditions.
2. The engineers' design documentation explicitly recorded the gloss-of-flooring variable as an unmodelled risk, rated low, and accepted under an internal risk-acceptance policy that was itself reasonable by the standards of the field at the time.
3. The classifier did not malfunction. It behaved exactly as specified, tested, and documented. Every unit test passed.
4. The visitor was herself partly responsible: she stepped out from behind the partition while looking at her phone, in violation of posted signage.
5. A human porter performing the identical task in the identical corridor, on the identical day, would — on the balance of expert testimony — very probably have noticed the wax sheen and paused. But a human porter would also, across a year of such shifts, have caused a greater number of minor incidents than this fleet causes.

**Your task.** Write the desiderata: the specification that any adequate theory of how responsibility is allocated when an autonomous system causes harm must meet in order to count as a serious candidate. This is a requirements document, not a survey of proposals; keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating considerations, not on scoring contenders.

For each desideratum, supply:

- **The requirement itself**, stated precisely enough that one could tell whether a given theory satisfies it or fails it.
- **The motivating case or consideration** — what goes wrong, concretely, if a theory neglects it. Use the Corridor Case where it applies, and invent further compact cases where you need pressure from a different direction.
- **Its status**: whether you take it to be non-negotiable for any theory in this area, or whether it is a demand that reflects the prior commitments of a particular camp (and if so, which camp, and what that camp would have to give up to drop it). Be candid where you suspect a requirement is a prejudice rather than a genuine constraint.

Your list must include, at minimum, requirements addressing the following — though you should not treat this enumeration as complete, and you should say what important requirement it omits:

**(a) The distributive questions.** A theory must be able to distinguish *different things* that get conflated under the single word "responsibility." Specify which distinctions it may not collapse, and say for each why collapsing it produces a substantive error rather than merely an infelicitous vocabulary. Address at least: causal contribution as against attribution; the backward-looking as against the forward-looking; role-based obligations as against outcome-based accountability; and culpability as against the mere obligation to repair. Then say which of these the Corridor Case actually raises, and which it merely appears to raise.

**(b) The aggregation questions.** Harm produced by a large sociotechnical system is typically the joint product of many small, individually reasonable decisions distributed across designers, trainers, testers, procurers, deployers, maintainers, and users. A theory must have something to say about whether responsibility can be divided, diluted, or lost across such a distribution. State the requirement, and state what you take to be the correct verdict about whether a genuine *remainder* can survive the exhaustion of every individual allocation — and if you think the idea of a remainder is confused, say so and explain what is really going on when people insist there is one.

**(c) The epistemic questions.** Requirements concerning what an agent had to be able to know or control in order to be a candidate for responsibility at all, and how those requirements behave when the relevant ignorance is itself produced by the division of labour rather than by individual negligence. Address the engineer in clause 2 specifically: does documenting a risk discharge it, transfer it, or transform it?

**(d) The normative-uptake questions.** A theory in this area is not merely descriptive; it is meant to feed institutions. State what a theory must deliver in order to be *actionable* — to generate determinate verdicts in novel cases, to be administrable by bodies with limited investigative capacity, to give designers ex ante guidance they can actually follow — and be clear about which of these you regard as constraints on truth and which as constraints on usefulness, since conflating the two is a recurring error in this literature.

**(e) The comparative baseline.** Clause 5 of the case is included deliberately. State what a theory owes us regarding the choice of baseline: whether the relevant comparison is against a world in which the harm does not occur, or against the human-practitioner alternative that the system displaced. Explain why the answer matters, and whether a theory that cannot settle it is thereby inadequate or merely incomplete.

Then, in a clearly separated section:

**Joint satisfiability.** Identify any subset of your desiderata that you believe cannot be simultaneously satisfied, and argue for the incompatibility rather than merely asserting it. If you think the list as a whole is jointly satisfiable, identify the pair that comes closest to breaking and explain exactly what a theory must sacrifice to hold both. Treat this section as potentially the most important part of the deliverable: if a genuine tension exists, the requirements list is itself a result, not a preamble.

**Most exclusionary requirement.** Name the single desideratum that you expect to eliminate the greatest number of otherwise attractive theories, and explain the mechanism by which it does so.

**Purposive caveat.** State explicitly what your list is a list *for*. Adequacy is relative to the purpose a theory is meant to serve, and a specification that does not fix that purpose is incomplete. Declare the purpose you have assumed, and identify which of your requirements would be demoted or promoted if the purpose were instead, say, the allocation of compensation to victims rather than the assignment of blame, or the governance of future system design rather than the adjudication of past harm.

Conventions. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no invented attributions — the group wants your reasoning, not your references. If you invoke a named doctrine or a familiar distinction from legal or philosophical practice, characterise it in your own words and say what work you are using it to do; if you are unsure whether a term is standard, flag that uncertainty rather than dressing it up. Do not quote any source. You may coin terminology where the existing vocabulary is inadequate, provided you define each coinage and mark its boundaries against the nearest established notions. Aim for a document a sceptical colleague could argue with requirement by requirement.
