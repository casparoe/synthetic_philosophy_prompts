I've spent the past year reading through what Athens' assembly actually did — no training in any of it, I simply got absorbed — and without deciding to I've been sorting its decisions into three bins. First bin: it was genuinely Athens deciding, and the decision stands as theirs. Second bin: it was never a decision at all, so nothing done in its name counts as carrying out Athens' will. Third bin, the awkward one: formally a decision, but degraded, the institution pointed at something it wasn't built for.

Here are my placements. The decree to kill every adult male Mytilenean sat in bin one for about a day, and when the assembly reversed itself I don't think that left Athens holding two conflicting acts — the second vote erased the first — and yet Athens is still stained by having voted it. The decision to sail against Sicily I keep in bin one even though Alcibiades inflamed the crowd, Nicias tried to kill the plan by demanding a force he hoped would frighten them off, and the Athenians afterwards turned on the very men who had urged it: bad as that deliberation was, Athens owns it and its ruin. The single vote condemning the Arginusae generals collectively, over the objection of the one member of that day's presiding committee who refused to put the motion, goes in bin two: no verdict, so what followed was killing. The trial of Socrates I also put in bin two, which I know is strange, since everything there was done strictly by the book — proper charges, a large jury, a regular count — and still I can't call it something Athens is entitled to stand behind. The last ostracism, which fell on a man nobody thought worthy of the honor of exile, goes in bin three.

My own tentative principle is that an assembly's decision counts only if the same body, calmer and better informed, would still stand behind it. But that seems to sweep Sicily into bin two, which I won't accept, and it makes the Mytilene reversal do all the work by accident.

Is there one principle that sorts my five cases into the bins I've put them in, stated precisely enough that someone else could apply it? If not, which placement does the best available principle force me to give up, and does one of the three bins collapse into another? Then tell me what it delivers for two cases I haven't listed: a modern legislature that passes a consequential bill on a voice vote with almost nobody in the chamber and repeals it a week later; and a referendum carried by a campaign its own authors later admitted was funded on false claims, with no second vote ever held.
