I write about physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I've hit a wall on a piece about the block universe. I started with what I thought was a cute little argument to give readers a feel for why presentism is in trouble, and my editor — who has more philosophy training than me, unfortunatly — says it proves too much, and now I can't tell which of us is right. Here it is, roughly:

1. Presentists say that only what exists now exists. The past is gone; the future hasn't happened.
2. Anything that exists or happens does so for some stretch of time. Existence isn't instantaneous: a heartbeat, a spoken sentence, a chemical reaction all take time.
3. So if the present is going to hold anything at all, the present has to have a nonzero duration.
4. But any stretch of time with nonzero duration has an earlier part and a later part, and those parts aren't going on at the same time as each other.
5. Whatever isn't going on at the same time as now is either past or future, not present.
6. So the present has zero duration. It's a knife-edge instant.
7. And a zero-duration instant can't hold any events, processes, or changes, because by (2) nothing exists for zero time.
8. So nothing is present.
9. By (1), nothing exists.
10. Something exists — at minimum, me, filing this on deadline. So presentism is false.

Here's the part my editor won't let go. Steps (7) through (9) don't just kill presentism, they kill everything. If existing takes time, then eternalism is in exactly the same boat, since every moment of the block is just as durationless as the present is — which makes the block a stack of empty slices, and there goes my whole piece. I can't argue with that, which makes me suspect its smuggling something in somewhere.

So: which step is the bad one, and why did it look innocent when I wrote it down? Is this a straight equivocation, or is it valid with a premise that's just expensive to hold onto? I'd really like to keep (2), because it seems to be the only reason anyone ever wanted a thick present in the first place, and (10) is not up for discussion. Then walk me through what each candidate repair costs — the presentist who says the present really is a knife-edge and takes the consequences, the presentist who insists it's a short but genuine interval, the eternalist who shrugs at (8), and whatever you think the honest fourth option is. And please keep it at the level of a smart reader with no philosophy background: I'm going to paraphrase your answer, not quote it, so jargon gets me nowhere.
