Retired biochemist here, 34 years at the bench, and only started reading philosophy seriously about two years ago (mostly on my wife's recommendation). I've hit a pattern I keep seeing and cant find a word for, and I'm honestly not sure a word exists.

Here's what happens. A mechanism gets worked out for some capacity that used to be described in the vocabulary of purposes, goals, skills or intelligence — the sort of thing you'd naturally say something "does" rather than something that just "happens". And once the mechanism is in hand, something quiet goes on: the higher-level word stops being an independent check on the mechanism and turns into a name for whatever the mechanism does. It gets redefined from underneath. So the mechanism can no longer fail to account for the phenomenon — not because it won the argument but because the phenomenon was resized to fit it.

Two instances, both on my side of the fence:

1. Enzyme specificity. For a long time people spoke of enzymes "recognising" their substrates. That's an active, almost cognitive-sounding word, and you could at least imagine recognition going wrong, or being shallow, or being the wrong model of what's happening. Then lock-and-key, induced fit, the structural work — and now recognition just means whatever the binding geometry and the rate constants give you. Nobody says the structural account captured recognition. Recognition has come to mean the structural account. Ask whether the enzyme "really" recognises its substrate and there's no independent fact left over to check the answer against.

2. Same shape, different century, in the machine-learning arguments I read now. A system does something we'd have called inferring, or noticing, or getting the gist of a sentence. Then the internals get opened up, a circuit or a feature gets identified, and the debate slides from "does it understand anything" to "fine, define understanding as what this circuit does." The word folds into the mechanism, the mechanism becomes true by stipulation, and anyone still resisting looks like they're just squeamish about vocabulary rather than making a claim.

Why I'm asking rather than just accepting it: I've read enough Dennett to know about the inversion idea — Darwin getting design out of a mindless process, competence without comprehension, Turing pulling off something parallel for mind. I thought that was my answer and then realised it isn't quite. The inversion is about which direction the explanation runs, and it's supposed to be a genuine discovery. What I'm describing happens afterwards, and it's about the higher-level concept losing its independence so the explanation stops being the kind of thing that could come up short. It feels more like a bookkeeping change than a discovery, and nobody votes on it.

So — does this have a name? I've got candidates and none sit right. Reduction, but I don't mean the derivation, I mean the semantic aftermath of a successful one. Eliminativism, but nobody's denying the phenomenon exists, they keep the word and hollow it out. Operationalism, close, except that was a program people signed up for, whereas this happens with no one deciding to. Some kind of self-sealing or unfalsifiability move, but there's no argument being immunised against a counterexample, there's just a definition drifting. Are any of these actually the thing, or the nearest thing to it, and how exactly does my case differ from each? If none of them is it, what would you call it — give me a definition with edges: a clear instance, a clear non-instance, and the borderline ones, because that's the part I actually care about.

And one more thing I'd like an answer on, because part of me suspects I'm being nostalgic for a vocabulary that never earned its keep: is this a defect at all? Or is this simply what mature science looks like once a mechanism lands, and the feeling that something was lost is just the feeling of losing a bad way of talking?
