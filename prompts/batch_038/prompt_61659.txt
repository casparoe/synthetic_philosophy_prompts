im in a scottish enlightenment seminar and for our last session everyone has to contribute something to a little end-of-term zine, and i'd much rather do a poem than another 2000 word response paper. i want it to be a pantoum — five quatrains, 20 lines, with the usual rule that lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the final stanza bringing lines 1 and 3 of the very first stanza back around to close the circle. i picked that form on purpose: every line in a pantoum is secondhand, and what i want the poem to make you feel is that the voice you experience as your own conscience is assembled out of sentences you originally overheard other people saying about you. so the repeats cant be decoration. the speaker is the judge-inside-the-chest, first person, but the lines he keeps reusing are ones he learned by listening — early on they still carry the crowd's tone, later theyre his, addressing you directly, and by the end it should be impossible to say which of the two is doing the talking.

subject is Adam Smith's impartial spectator, and i need it right rather than gestured at, because half my seminar gets this stuff wrong:

- sympathy for Smith isnt pity, its fellow-feeling with any passion whatsoever, and it runs on imagination rather than contagion: i can only feel what i would feel in your situation, never what you actually feel. that gap is asymetrical and its the engine of the whole book.
- which makes approval a kind of concord. the person acting has to come down, has to flatten his feelings to a pitch a bystander can go along with, and the bystander has to strain his imagination upward as far as it will stretch. self-command lives in that lowering, and the poem should make the lowering cost something.
- we get no direct view of our own character at all; we first see ourselves in other peoples faces and only later build an inner version of that looking. get that in, but please dont lean on the word mirror.
- there are two sets of spectators and they can disagree: the actual people around you, who get warped by party and fashion, and the imagined one you carry, who sometimes ends up testifying for you, hesitantly, when literally everybody else is against you. the disturbing part is that the second one was manufactured out of the first.
- and since the judge inside is a construction of the person being judged, the correction never fully lands — self-deceit survives to the very end. i want the poem to admit that instead of resolving it.

for concrete imagery use the disproportion Smith is famous for: the little finger youd lose a night's sleep over versus a hundred million strangers swallowed up at breakfast and forgotten by noon. one stanza's worth, no more — i dont want a poem about earthquakes.

the last line needs to land so that someone whos never read Smith hears it as comforting and someone who has finds it genuinely creepy. modern plain english throughout, no 18th century pastiche, no naming Smith or the book, and dont use the phrase impartial spectator anywhere in the poem. just send me the poem, no gloss or explanation — i know the theory, i just cant write verse.
