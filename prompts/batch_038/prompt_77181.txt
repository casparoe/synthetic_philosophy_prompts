I sit on the audit committee of a regional health authority. Last month we received two memoranda, written separately by the two analysts who produced this year's outcomes audit for a vascular emergency treated at nine hospitals across our region. There are two operative approaches, which I will call X and Y. X is available at only two of the nine hospitals — the regional hubs — while Y is available at all nine. The audit compares survival to discharge, and patients are classified by route of arrival:

| Route of arrival | X: patients | X: survived | X: rate | Y: patients | Y: survived | Y: rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Brought directly by emergency ambulance | 350 | 90 | 25.7% | 120 | 40 | 33.3% |
| 2. Scheduled admission after outpatient assessment | 90 | 85 | 94.4% | 330 | 300 | 90.9% |
| 3. Presented at a district hospital, then transferred onward | 160 | 70 | 43.8% | 60 | 20 | 33.3% |
| **All routes combined** | **600** | **245** | **40.8%** | **510** | **360** | **70.6%** |

Standardized to the combined distribution of routes across both procedures, the rates come out at 55.3 percent for X and 55.1 percent for Y.

The analysts then argued about what the audit should say. I was in the meeting; I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can, and I am the person who has to write the committee's summary. Neither of them is my side, and I honestly cannot tell who is right.

1. Nadia, a statistician seconded to us from a university, opened by saying that the raw comparison cannot be published as a comparison of the procedures, that the thirty-point gap is a composition artifact, and that any analysis worth the name conditions on route of arrival — after which X comes out ahead in two of the three routes.

2. Owen, who runs our decision-support team, replied that "X comes out ahead in two of the three routes" is a shape, not a finding. He asked her to name the single figure she proposes to put on the front page, and pointed out that in the route carrying 42 percent of all cases the procedure her analysis favors loses; so if the committee is deciding where to send patients and what to fund, the largest group of patients is the group her analysis goes against. He said the only figure describing the system as it actually ran is the raw one.

3. Nadia said she does have a figure, and it is neither of his. Standardizing both procedures to the combined case mix gives 55.3 percent for X and 55.1 percent for Y: a difference of two-tenths of a point across 1,110 patients. Her claim was that the whole of the raw gap is composition, that there is no difference to find, and that the honest headline is neither X nor Y but "no detectable difference," with the referral pattern reported separately as a finding about the service rather than about the surgeons.

4. Owen said the standardization is a trick of the same species as the thing it corrects. Route of arrival is not a patient characteristic in the way age or sex is. A patient arrives as a transfer because they presented first at a district hospital and because the hub that receives transfers is a hub that performs X; the route is downstream of which procedure you were going to get. Adjusting for it adjusts away part of the very thing the audit is supposed to measure. If the way the service routes patients flatters Y's numbers, that routing is part of what Y delivers and part of what it costs, and you cannot subtract it and call the residue the effect of the procedure.

5. Nadia said that if you do not subtract it, you are not estimating the effect of the procedure at all but the joint effect of the procedure and the referral map — which may be exactly what the committee wants, but then the audit must say so, and must stop using the language of one approach being better or worse. She added that neither of them can settle whether route is a confounder or an intermediate without a causal model of the whole pathway, that no such model exists, and that the stratified tables are in the appendix at all only because the protocol's authors took route to be a confounder — a step nobody ever argued for.

6. Owen said nobody assumed anything: the stratified tables were requested by the clinical leads, who want to know how each approach performs on comparable patients. And her standardized figure has an arbitrary choice buried inside it. She weighted by the combined case mix; weight instead by the case mix of the two hub hospitals, or by last year's mix, or by any plausible counterfactual mix, and the ordering can move. So she has not escaped arbitrariness, she has moved it one level down where nobody checks it. The raw figure at least is exactly what it looks like: survivors over patients.

7. Nadia said that transparency about a quantity that answers no one's question is not a virtue; that every number they publish is an estimate of something, and the only real question is whether that something is what the committee will end up deciding; and that she would rather defend a number whose target she has been forced to state than one whose target is smuggled in.

8. Owen said then state it and publish it and he would drop the matter — but that this is not what her memorandum proposes. Her proposed headline is no detectable difference, which the press office will render as surgeons cleared, and which converts an absence of evidence into evidence of absence in an audit with 1,110 patients and no randomization.

9. Nadia said she will write it as an equivalence claim if he will let her state the precision, and that the difference they can detect is two-tenths of a point. Owen said: with which weighting?

That is where it stopped. I would like the following, in order.

First, check the arithmetic before you adjudicate anything. Confirm or correct the raw rates and the two standardized rates; two of the moves above depend on them, and if any figure I have given is wrong I want to know that first.

Second, go through moves 1 to 9 and rate each one — clearly mistaken, weak but pointing at something real, partly right, mostly right, clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification. Where an objection lands but the conclusion drawn from it does not follow, say so explicitly rather than grading the move as a single unit; several of them seem to be two claims wearing one coat.

Third, answer the four questions they were circling instead of asking: (i) does the raw marginal comparison measure anything at all about the two procedures, and if so precisely what; (ii) does the legitimacy of adjusting for route turn on whether route is a confounder or an intermediate, and what should the audit do given that nobody knows which; (iii) is the weighting objection a genuine problem for the standardized figure, or a tu quoque that leaves the raw figure just as arbitrary; (iv) is "no detectable difference" defensible on these data, and what would have to be true of the numbers for it to be defensible.

Fourth, say what both of them are missing — including anything visible in the table itself that neither one mentions. And if you think the question the committee is actually asking is ill-posed, say that plainly and say what question it should be asking instead.

I cannot look anything up, and I do not want a reading list, references, or names of methods I would have to go and read about: I want the reasoning done in front of me, out of the numbers given and whatever you already know.

Please write the whole response in British English.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which single further fact — about the referral geography, the case mix, the precision of these estimates, or anything else — would most likely move you to a different verdict on the exchange; and where do you think your own reasoning here is weakest, or most likely to fail? I would rather you mark the soft joint yourself than have me find it later, in front of the committee.
