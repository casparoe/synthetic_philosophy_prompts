A framing document I'm reviewing introduces inverse reinforcement learning with an analogy: recovering a person's reward function from their behavior is like recovering a chess engine's evaluation function from a database of its games — feed in enough positions and moves and the weights fall out. The document then argues by extension that scale dissolves the worry about whose values get encoded: given enough human behavioral traces (purchases, dwell times, suggestions accepted and declined), a capable learner will converge on what people actually want, and what remains is an engineering question rather than an ethical one.

I don't think the analogy can carry that weight, but I want the audit rather than my hunch. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the engine, the evaluation function, the database, and whatever stands in for winning — then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the scale conclusion from those that are local color. I'd also like to know what happens when the analogy is pressed rather than waved: in the chess case, how many distinct evaluation functions are compatible with one and the same game database, and does that result transfer to the human case or does it dissolve there? Finally, tell me whether any version of the conclusion survives the disanalogies, and if this comparison is broken, what would fit the target better.
