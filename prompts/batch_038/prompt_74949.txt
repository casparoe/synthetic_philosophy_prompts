I'm putting together a piece on the whole great-man vs structural-explanation debate, using the obvious test case: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. I've come to think the question is usually framed badly and I want you to argue for a reframe I only half believe, so push it as hard as it'll go.

Here's the reframe. The counterfactual isn't one question, it's a nested stack of them, roughly: (1) does Weimar collapse into some kind of authoritarian regime at all? (2) is that regime racial-imperial and revisionist about Versailles? (3) does it end up pursuing the mass murder of European Jews? (4) does it start a general European war? My hunch is that structural conditions do a huge amount of work at (1) and (2) and close to nothing at (3) and (4). Which suggests the great-man side should just *concede* (1) and (2) outright instead of fighting for them, since everything actually worth defending sits at the bottom of the stack.

So: argue that this concessive strategy is the strongest available version of the great-man position. Make the case that a great-man theorist who insists Hitler was necessary for the collapse of Weimar is playing a losing hand and discrediting the thesis for everyone else, whereas one who stakes the claim on (3) and (4) is on much firmer ground. I also need you to show why conceding the top of the stack doesnt quietly slide into structuralism — i.e. why "he didn't bring the regime about, but he determined its character at the points that mattered" is still a genuine great-man claim rather than structuralism with a personality footnote. And deal with the replaceability worry head on: the party wasnt short of antisemitic radical authoritarians, so what does the rest of the stack look like under one of them, and is there any principled reason to think the answer differs at (3) and (4)?

No bibliography and don't bother with a lit review, I'll handle refernces myself — talk about positions and arguments by description, not by name.

- Bottom line — two or three sentences
- The concession — why giving up (1) and (2) costs the great-man side nothing, about 250 words
