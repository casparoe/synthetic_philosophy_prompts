For the past four years I have run the research and evaluation function of a foundation that funds interventions to raise girls' participation in secondary-school physics, computing, and engineering across roughly a dozen countries — Sweden, Denmark, the Netherlands, Ireland, Germany, and a small cluster of others. Two of the countries in our portfolio sit far lower than the Nordics on the standard composite indices of gender equality, and in both of them women's share of engineering degrees is conspicuously higher than in Stockholm or Copenhagen; our country directors notice this constantly, and it has turned our annual strategy retreat into something of a ritual. Since 2019 that retreat has contained the same argument, conducted between the same two people — a developmental psychologist and a sociologist of education who serve on our scientific panel — and it has never once produced movement in either direction. I have come to suspect that the two of them are not answering the same question, and what I want is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a summary of the findings on either side.

The positions, as they restate them each year, are these. The psychologist holds that a substantial part of the divergence in subject and career choice reflects average differences in interests that are not merely the sediment of prohibition: on her account, constraint compresses the expression of those differences, so as legal and economic barriers fall the gap should widen rather than narrow, which she takes the cross-national data to show — measured differences between men's and women's preferences are larger in richer and more equal countries, and on the STEM version of the finding, women's share of STEM study is lower where the equality indices are higher. The sociologist holds that the same tables are equally consistent with the opposite mechanism: in societies where occupation, lifestyle, and identity are chosen rather than inherited, gender becomes one of the few readily available axes along which a distinctive self can be constructed, so differentiation should intensify as constraint loosens. He points to work reporting that the stereotype associating mathematics with men is stronger, not weaker, in more egalitarian and developed countries, and that this alone can account for the pattern without appeal to latent dispositions. He adds a measurement objection she regards as evasion: the composite equality indices score a fixed set of female achievement gaps — earnings, parliamentary seats, tertiary enrolment — and contain nothing about how boys and girls are actually addressed in classrooms, households, and career guidance, so the independent variable in these analyses is not the variable anyone in the room cares about. Both of them then say, in effect, that the numbers are the numbers.

The episode that convinced me the stalemate is structural rather than informational happened in my first year on the job. The most widely cited cross-national study of the STEM version of the paradox was corrected after researchers elsewhere could not reproduce its reported figures and it emerged that its dependent variable had not been women's share of STEM degrees at all, but a previously undisclosed and unvalidated measure of each sex's propensity to complete such a degree. Neither of my panelists moved. One said the correction proved the analysis had been sloppy all along; the other said it proved how readily a well-established result gets sacrificed to replication politics. If a published correction to the central piece of evidence produces no updating in either direction, I doubt we are watching an empirical disagreement at all. My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I want it tested rather than flattered — is that the dispute is really two questions sharing a heading: one about the distribution of latent dispositions and the conditions under which they are expressed, the other about what a preference has to be like in order to justify the outcome it produces, with a buried third question about whether the causally relevant variable is equality, affluence, or something the indices never measure. So: diagnose it. How much of this disagreement is verbal, or about which instrument counts as measuring the thing, and how much is substantive? Is there a genuinely empirical question here that, if settled, would break the tie — and given that the available variation is cross-national and heavily collinear with wealth, what design could settle it, if any? Where does the argument bottom out in commitments about human nature that neither side can argue for and neither would abandon? And tell me what progress would look like given your diagnosis: a distinction, a better instrument, a different unit of analysis, a change of question, or nothing at all — and if nothing, why not, and what our panel should be doing with that hour instead.
