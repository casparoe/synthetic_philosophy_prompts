I'm rebuilding the first few weeks of my graduate philosophy-of-psychiatry seminar around a puzzle I haven't managed to settle for myself, so what I want from you is a reconstruction, not a survey. No reading list, no citations, no telling me to consult the literature — I know the literature. I want to know what you think.

The object is the American diagnostic manual, the DSM, and specifically the machinery by which it gets made and then used. Let me lay out the parts, because the puzzle is in the combination rather than in any one of them.

1. Each disorder is given as a checklist of symptoms plus a threshold count, and every one of them carries a further requirement that the symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment. So the manual builds in a distinction between having the symptoms and having a disorder.

2. Before publication the association runs field trials in which multiple clinicians independently evaluate the same patients, and then it publishes the agreement numbers — including for its own headline categories like major depression and generalized anxiety, where agreement turned out to be poor — and ships the manual anyway.

3. Every chapter ends with leftover categories for people who are visibly suffering but don't meet any of the listed sets. The manual is full of these escape hatches.

4. It describes itself, over and over, as a provisional heuristic rather than a picture of nature. Whole diagnoses get folded together (Asperger's collapsed into an autism spectrum), subtypes get deleted, an exclusion for bereavement gets removed so that grieving people can be diagnosed depressed. The current version is explicitly a living document that gets revised online between editions.

5. And yet: insurers won't pay without a code from it, schools won't allocate support without one, courts treat a diagnosis as evidence about what a person is like, and there's a whole back section parking dimensional models and conditions for further study that clinicians are told not to officially use but that everyone discusses, researches, and applies.

6. The revision process takes public and professional comment, including from advocacy groups made up of people who hold the diagnoses. Once a name exists, communities form around it, prevalence numbers move, people come forward describing themselves in the manual's own vocabulary — and the evidence base for the next revision is partly made of reports from people the previous edition recruited.

Here's what I want you to do with that. Reconstruct the theory of what a psychiatric category *is* that this machinery presupposes. Go part by part: what does the reliability trial presuppose about two clinicians tracking the same thing, what does the distress-and-impairment clause presuppose about there being a value-free baseline of normal suffering, what do the leftover categories presuppose, what does the advocacy-comment step presuppose about who gets to author a classification? Then be strict about the difference between what the practice actually requires and what it merely sits comfortably with — several rival accounts probably license the same manual, and I want to know which parts of it are genuinely underdetermined.

I know the looping-effects idea, and I don't want a book report on it. What I want to know is whether the manual's own procedures presuppose that classifying people is an intervention on the people classified, or whether the whole thing could survive a firm denial of that.

Then tell me what would concretely have to change — in the trials, the leftover categories, the comment process, the insurance hook — if we swapped the implicit theory for a thoroughgoing conventionalism on which categories are useful stipulations we adopt, or for a strong realism on which they're natural kinds we're badly approximating. And end by telling me where you think I've overstated the tension, because I suspect I have somewhere.
