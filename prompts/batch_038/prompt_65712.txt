Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind who disagree about whether the possibility of building a *counterfeit person* is a genuine objection to functionalism about consciousness. Both speakers should be first-rate: neither may win by caricaturing the other, each must at some point be credited with a move the other cannot immediately answer, and each must narrow or refine their own position at least once as the exchange proceeds.

**The first speaker** defends the line Dennett takes against the counterfeit-person objection. On this view, the demand that a system supply something *over and above* a complete match with a person across every possible test — including its own sincere self-reports, its susceptibility to surprise, and its capacity to notice after the fact that it has been fooled — is a demand with no content. If nothing could ever distinguish the counterfeit from the genuine, then the alleged difference is not a difference in the world but a surviving habit of thought, a residue of the idea that consciousness is an inner substance rather than a pattern of competences and dispositions. This speaker should press the point that "there is nothing it is like to be it" is not an extra hypothesis about the robot at all; it is a withdrawal of the only evidence on which any such hypothesis could rest.

**The second speaker** defends a thesis you should treat as novel rather than as anyone's published view. Call it the **forfeiture thesis**. Some mental states are essentially expensive: what partly constitutes their functional role is that occupying them costs the bearer something it would otherwise keep — time, open options, bodily integrity, standing with other people, or the continued existence of its own accumulated state. Pain, boredom, grief, dread, and embarrassment are states of this kind; deduction, chess, summarization, and even effortless fluent conversation are not. Simulation, by contrast, is cheap: a system can reproduce the entire outward and inward-causal profile of an expensive state without ever paying for it, because for the simulator the goods in question are only represented, never spent. Hence the counterfeit/genuine boundary is not drawn by an ineffable inner glow but by price — it coincides exactly with the line between capacities that can be had for nothing and capacities that must be had at a loss. This speaker should insist that whether a system's option space and resource base really shrink is a publicly inspectable matter, not an appeal to private feel, and that on this account the functionalist has simply failed to notice that *some* of the functional roles he quantifies over are ones no simulation can fill.

The dialogue must work through the following exchanges rather than around them:

1. The functionalist should argue that the forfeiture thesis is either a redescription of functional role — in which case a system with a non-replaceable history, finite time, goals it can genuinely fail, and states whose destruction is irreversible for it has already paid the price, and the thesis concedes the case — or else it secretly restricts what may count as spent to a particular currency, such as metabolic survival, which is substrate chauvinism by another name.

2. The proponent of the forfeiture thesis must then answer the backup objection head-on: if a system's states can be duplicated, and any loss restored from a copy, has anything been forfeited at all? They should say whether the difference between a cost incurred relative to a design goal assigned by others and a cost the bearer itself has at stake can be stated without appeal to phenomenology.

3. The functionalist should press a reductio: on the forfeiture thesis, an anesthetized human, a human absorbed in effortless activity where nothing is at risk, and a human whose relevant states happen to be cheaply replaceable are, for that interval, counterfeits of themselves — and personhood turns out to be hostage to a backup policy. The forfeiture theorist should say plainly what they are willing to accept here and what they will resist, rather than deflecting.

4. Finally, the forfeiture theorist should turn the functionalist's own collapse against them: once the counterfeit objection is dismissed, the only remaining ground for treating something as a person is that it behaves like one, so being a person and being taken for a person come apart in no principled way. The functionalist should own that consequence rather than treat it as a discovery — and should then explain what, on their view, is objectionable about a system that is never conscious but is extremely good at producing the belief that it is. If the functionalist's answer is that the wrong lies entirely in the effects on those deceived, they should acknowledge what that implies about the moral work the concept of consciousness was being asked to do.

Constraints on execution: the two speakers must respond to the arguments actually made to them, not to the positions they occupied two turns earlier; no speaker may define their key term by reference to the term at issue in the dispute (in particular, "really spent" may not be glossed as "there is something it is like to lose it"). Do not cite, quote, or recommend any real literature, and do not lean on named authorities other than the general Dennettian position described above — the exchange should stand entirely on the reasoning given in it. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words, in unadorned speaker-turn format with no narrator and no scene-setting beyond what is needed to identify the two positions. End without a victor: the final turn should be one speaker stating, in a single sentence that the other does not dispute, the precise question on which their disagreement now turns.
