**The question (25 points, ~400 words):**

> Explain as precisely as you can what Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says, including the conditions a theory has to satisfy for it to apply. Then pick one attempt to push the theorem outside mathematics — either the claim that it shows the human mind is not a machine, or a claim about law, morality, or physics — and say what that extrapolation does and doesn't license.

**Student's answer:**

> Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any formal system which is (i) consistent, (ii) recursively axiomatizable, and (iii) strong enough to express elementary arithmetic will contain sentences it can neither prove nor refute. So arithmetic is essentially incomplete: there's no finite list of axioms plus rules that settles every arithmetical question. The proof works by arithmetizing syntax — coding formulas and proofs as numbers — and then constructing a sentence G that, read back through the coding, says "G is not provable in T." If T proves G, then G is false, since it says it isn't provable, so T would be proving falsehoods; if T proves not-G, then by what not-G says G is provable, and T would prove both G and not-G. Either way a consistent T does neither.
>
> One thing worth being clear about: Gödel's original version actually needed a stronger hypothesis than consistency, namely ω-consistency, and it was Rosser in 1936 who showed plain consistency is enough, which is why the theorem gets stated that way today. It's also not a sceptical result about mathematics. Most statements of arithmetic are decidable in PA, the theorem doesn't make proof unreliable, and it certainly doesn't show that any system containing arithmetic is inconsistent. It just shows that provability inside one fixed system can't capture all arithmetical truth.
>
> That last point is the one people use to argue that minds aren't machines. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says a consistent system can't prove its own consistency, and this is why he concluded the human mind can't be a Turing machine. A machine's consistency is unprovable from inside the machine, but we can see that we are consistent, and we can see that G is true even though it can't be proved — not by any system, and not by any person, ever. Truth outruns provability, so the mind is doing something no formal system can do. It also means AI won't ever match human mathematical insight, and for the same reason no finite set of physical laws can generate every true statement about the physical world.
>
> You can push it further. Suppose someone drafted a complete legal code, a finite set of rules meant to decide every case. If the code is rich enough to encode arithmetic — and any code dealing with deadlines, thresholds, and sums of money is — then it too will contain cases it can't decide. So gaps in the law aren't a defect of bad drafting, they're unavoidable in principle.
>
> Obviously you can add the undecidable sentence as a new axiom, but then a fresh Gödel sentence shows up for the enlarged theory, so the process never terminates.
