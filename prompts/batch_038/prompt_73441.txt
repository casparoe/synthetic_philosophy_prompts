Ok, I've talked myself into a position about the classical/intuitionist quarrel and I want to stress-test it before I defend it in front of anyone who actually works on this.

The position: the intuitionist is not offering a rival theory of what makes mathematical sentences true. What Brouwer and Heyting and everyone downstream of them are doing is laying down a discipline on assertion — you may assert φ only when you're in a position to hand over the relevant construction — and intuitionistic logic just falls out of that discipline as a byproduct. On this reading classical logic isn't false, and its undecidable sentences aren't neither-true-nor-false either; they're simply not the kind of thing anyone is ever licensed to assert. The supposed metaphysical debate about mathematical truth is a category confusion, and the two camps don't even disagree about the same propositions — they disagree about what you're allowed to say. Call it the discipline view. Nothing ontological is at stake.

What would count against it? I want the exposure map, not a defense of me.

- Which formal results or features of the logics themselves tell against it? The double-negation translation and the Glivenko-style results cut both ways as far as I can tell: they make the gap look purely syntactic, which seems to help me, but they also make it look like the two systems are just different games with different subject matter, which undercuts the idea that intuitionists are only policing assertion. Which is it? And given the pile of intermediate logics sitting between classical and intuitionistic, if more than one of them is compatible with any plausible gloss on 'proof' or 'construction', does that mean the discipline view underdetermines intuitionistic logic specifically? Fatal, or merely awkward?
- The different constructivist schools accept mutually incompatible extra principles — continuity-style assumptions and choice sequences on one side, Markov's principle and Church's thesis on the other — and they all claim to be running the same assertibility discipline. Does my view have anything to say about which extras are right, or does it just shrug? If the intuitionist's actual mathematical commitments cost them theorems they'd like to keep, can a pure licensing story generate those costs, or does it need substantive claims about what mathematical objects are — in which case the view fails to describe the thing it claims to be reading?
- Dummett's route grounds something like my view in a theory of meaning plus arguments about manifestation and acquisition. What goes wrong if those arguments prove too much — i.e. if the same discipline applied to claims about the past, about other minds, or about arithmetic sentences nobody will ever settle, forces revisions that no intuitionist would sign up for? Is that a defeater for my view or only for their argument for it? Worth distinguishing.
- What does the discipline view predict that the rivals — realism, anti-realism as a full-blown semantics, pluralism about the logical constants — don't predict? Is there anything that would come as a genuine surprise on my view, or can it absorb every outcome as 'well, that's just licensing talk'?
- Where would a competent defender of this kind of reading concede ground, and where would they dig in? Sort the defeaters from 'this kills it' to 'uncomfortable but survivable', and give me an honest verdict on whether I've qualified the thing so thoroughly — no metaphysics, no disagreement, just permissions — that there's nothing left for it to be wrong about. If it is that insulated, tell me whether that's a real problem for a claim of this kind or just what any claim about how to read a philosophical tradition ends up looking like.
