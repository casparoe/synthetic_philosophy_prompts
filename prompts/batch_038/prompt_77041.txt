I am a novelist, and the book now occupying my third draft is set in an invented Adriatic port town during the summer of 1914 — the town is mine, the summer is history's — and concerns the fortnight in which a garrison's departure for the front passes, in the minds of the people watching it, from unthinkable to unremarkable to inevitable. The manuscript rests on a single structural conceit: every chapter opens with a page from some contemporary's diary or letter stating what that person expected to happen next, and only afterward does the narration turn to what did happen. I devised the device before I could say why. A friend who teaches the philosophy of history supplied the vocabulary — the chapters are engineering against what the psychologists call creeping determinism, that drift whereby an outcome, once known, quietly rearranges its antecedents into a sequence that appears never to have admitted an alternative, so that contemporaries who hesitated between five futures are remembered as walking, more or less willingly, toward the one that arrived.

That conceit was attacked and defended at a workshop reading last month by means of two analogies, and because they licensed opposite recommendations, I want them audited rather than admired. An editor with a cartographer's turn of phrase said that a narrative of the past is a map: no map reproduces the terrain, every projection distorts, and the question to put to a map is never whether it simplifies but whether it gets the traveler where she needs to go. On that view the three readers who reported afterward that my book felt inevitable were registering not a flaw but the signature of a good projection, and the diary device should be cut for pace. My friend answered from a different source domain: a reconstruction of the past is a detective's case, assembled from traces toward an answer that is determinate whether or not anyone finds it, and the detective's besetting sin is precisely the tidy story that fits — the convenient culprit — against which the profession has evolved procedure, the discipline of writing down what one expects before opening the envelope, the alibi that survives contact with the timeline. On that view the felt inevitability is the smell of the convenient culprit, and my conceit is exactly the safeguard it purports to be, though she conceded it may be inert for a reader who already knows how the summer ends.

What I want from you is the audit, not a verdict tilted toward whichever side sounds more principled. For each analogy, give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — identifying what in the source plays the terrain, the projection, the traveler's destination, the trace, the culprit, the rules of evidence, the moment of disclosure. Then the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the use the analogy is being put to and those that are merely decorative differences between a map and a manuscript. I am especially interested in what each source quietly exports to a target that cannot receive it: a map is checked against a landscape that persists and can be walked, and cartographers possess a formal instrument, Tissot's indicatrix, for measuring how much a given projection distorts area and angle at each point, whereas the past offers neither walkable terrain nor any metric of narrative distortion; a detective's answer is fixed before anyone's judgment is formed, whereas a reader of 1914 arrives with the judgment already in place. Then tell me whether either licensed conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and which analogy better accounts for the only piece of evidence I actually possess — three readers who found a book engineered against inevitability inevitable. If both fail, say so plainly, and offer a replacement only if it can carry a specific load: I want to know whether any comparison in this neighborhood can support the claim that narrative form is not itself the distortion, or whether that claim must be argued on some other ground entirely. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning laid bare, and I would rather have one analogy dismantled thoroughly than both handled diplomatically.
