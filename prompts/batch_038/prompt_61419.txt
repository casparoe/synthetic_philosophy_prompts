I am a novelist at work on a book whose second strand is set in England between 1651 and 1662, and one of its principals is a philosopher-geometer closely modelled on Thomas Hobbes — a man who believes, sincerely and for reasons he can articulate, that he has squared the circle. I want him to be a thinker rather than a joke, but I also want his scenes to withstand a specialist reader. To that end I asked a friend who teaches the history of early modern science for a short briefing note on Hobbes's geometry. It arrived last week and runs, in substance, as follows:

> "Hobbes took geometry to be the only genuine science, and for an interesting reason: its objects are of our own making. A line is generated by the motion of a point, a surface by the motion of a line, so in geometry alone we know the causes of what we reason about, and demonstration is nothing but reasoning from the causes of generation to their effects. Natural philosophy studies bodies whose causes we did not make, so it can only work backwards from observed effects to possible causes and never reaches certainty; civil philosophy, by contrast, is properly demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves by covenant. This kinematic foundation he took over from Galileo and above all from Cavalieri's method of indivisibles, which he adopted essentially wholesale.
>
> "From the same principle he drew a hard line against the new algebra. Hobbes was a nominalist: definitions in mathematics are stipulations about names that we are free to fix. It followed, for him, that a proof carried out in symbols proves nothing about anything — algebra is at best a bookkeeping device that helps you guess where to look — and that anyone passing off Descartes's analysis or Wallis's arithmetic of infinitesimals as geometry was a fraud. This is also why he felt licensed to invent his own quadrature: if the definitions are ours to fix, the result comes with them.
>
> "He published his squaring of the circle in *De Corpore* in 1655. John Wallis, Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford, exposed it that same year in the *Elenchus geometriae Hobbianae*. Hobbes, to his credit, struck the faulty steps before the English translation appeared in 1656 and thereafter treated the episode as closed; his subsequent mathematical output was chiefly polemic, an attempt to settle scores with Wallis rather than a renewed assault on the problem. His exclusion from the Royal Society was principally a consequence of the damage the quadrature affair did to his standing as a geometer.
>
> "My advice for your novel: the mathematics is incidental — a bad day at the desk, a technical slip — and tells you nothing about the philosophy, which is internally coherent. Write him as a man whose method was sound but whose vanity outran his skill, and as a conservative who simply missed the boat to the calculus."

I am inclined to read this charitably, because it gives me a tragic figure rather than a lunatic, and I am aware that this makes me a poor judge of it; equally, I do not want the opposite failure of turning him into a martyr persecuted by a philistine establishment. So I would like a careful audit rather than encouragement.

Please go through the note claim by claim and sort what is there: what a historian of seventeenth-century mathematics would accept as it stands; what is a fair compression of a subtler position; what is quietly wrong; and what is false. I am particularly uncertain about two moves. The first is the assertion that his kinematics is Cavalieri's method of indivisibles adopted wholesale — my vague sense is that generation by motion and composition out of indivisibles are not the same doctrine, and that Hobbes may have insisted on the difference, but I cannot say why. The second is the inference from his nominalism to the licence to stipulate results; I suspect the note is making his theory of names do work it cannot bear, and that a nominalist about universal terms need not be a conventionalist about demonstration, but I would want that spelled out rather than assumed.

Then tell me which of the note's errors are load-bearing for its central conclusion — that the quadrature was a technical slip philosophically incidental to a coherent method. If the attempted quadrature was itself an application of the constructionist and kinematic commitments he defended in *De Corpore*, does that conclusion survive, and in what weakened form? I would also like the record set straight on his conduct after 1656: whether he genuinely withdrew and moved on, what he in fact did, and how one characterizes a thirty-year persistence without either hagiography and the cheap alternative diagnosis of senility. And on the Royal Society: whether that is the right explanation of his exclusion, or whether it compresses several distinct obstacles into one convenient motive.

Finally, some practical help. Give me three or four sentences my philosopher-geometer could plausibly utter in 1657, after Wallis's attack has landed, that a specialist would recognize as faithful to Hobbes's actual position on the status of algebraic analysis, on infinite quantities, and on what he takes himself still to owe the reader — and tell me what he could not say in those years without misrepresenting the view. Please mark clearly where you are reconstructing an attitude from the shape of the position rather than reporting a documented statement, and where you are simply uncertain; I would much rather have the gaps flagged than smoothly filled, and I am not asking you for sources or a reading list, only for the reasoning.
