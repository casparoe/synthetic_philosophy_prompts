I am a first-year philosophy graduate student. In this week's metaphysics seminar the sentence "no two things are exactly alike" — the line everyone traces back to Leibniz — did a great deal of work, and it seemed to me to do different work each time. First, when Max Black's two-spheres universe came up (a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, of the same diameter, colour, and temperature, at some distance from one another), our seminar leader invoked the slogan to declare the case impossible rather than a counterexample: each sphere, he said, stands at some distance from the other, so each has a relational property the other lacks. Second, minutes later, the same sentence was offered as a premise for a bundle theory of substance: if two objects shared all their properties they would simply be one object, so there is nothing to an object over and above its properties. Third, at the end of the hour it was deployed against haecceitism about possible worlds: two worlds that are qualitatively identical are not two worlds, hence there is no such thing as a merely numerical difference between possibilities.

My suspicion is that those three uses need three distinct principles and that at most one of them is defensible, but I cannot state the differences cleanly. Please give me a menu of the precise claims the slogan could be taken to assert, ordered from weakest to strongest. For each reading, say explicitly: what the quantifier ranges over (all objects, only concrete ones, only objects in the actual world, or objects in every world); what is admitted as a property (intrinsic monadic properties only, or relational and spatiotemporal ones as well, or properties whose formulation picks out a particular object); whether it is advanced as a necessary truth or a contingent generalization about what happens to exist; and whether it is a modal thesis about what could be the case or a methodological demand that any numerical difference admit of a qualitative ground. Then attach a verdict to each — trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false — and say which of the three seminar uses, if any, that reading actually licenses.

Handle the trivialization problem head-on rather than mentioning it in passing. If properties such as being two metres from that sphere, or being identical to itself, are admitted, the principle looks guaranteed to hold of any pair of distinct things whatsoever, and I cannot see how to rule them out without presupposing the distinctness they were meant to establish. Tell me whether a non-arbitrary restriction exists; if you think it does not, say so plainly rather than offering the usual gesture at "pure" or "non-trivial" properties. Relatedly, mark in each of the three uses whether the argument passes silently from a claim about how things are alike to a claim about how many things there are.

Close by taking a position instead of leaving the menu with me: which reading you would sign on to, whether Black's case is a genuine counterexample to it, and which of the three jobs the slogan was asked to do in my seminar it could actually have done, with your reasons.
