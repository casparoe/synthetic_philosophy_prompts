I am a staff software engineer at a mid-sized payments company, and for the last two years I have owned our incident postmortem process. After any production incident above a certain severity, a facilitator runs a review, writes it up, and the head of platform signs it off before it is published internally. We produce four to six of these a month; each costs two to three hours of senior engineering time; the readers are mostly engineers who were nowhere near the incident, and occasionally auditors and executives. I have no philosophy training beyond what I have picked up from arguing with colleagues.

My team is split down the middle about what the document is for, and a colleague with a history degree tells me the split is an old argument in the philosophy of history about whether explaining a particular event means subsuming it under a general law or means telling a story that can be followed. Our reliability engineer's position: a postmortem explains an incident only if it delivers a general claim — conditions of this kind, in a system like ours, produce failures of that kind — which we could have used to anticipate this incident and can use to anticipate the next one; anything less is storytime. Our support lead's position: every general claim we have ever managed to write is either vacuous (communication broke down; the assumption was never tested; the change was not reversible) or a restatement of this one incident wearing a rule's clothing, and what actually changes how people behave is the ordered account — what each person knew when they acted, what looked reasonable from where they were standing, and how, once it had gone that far, it could only have gone that way. He says a good general claim is something you read off the story afterwards, never something you start with.

Both are describing real failure modes in what we produce. As evidence: across our last fifty postmortems, the "general lesson" field contains one of about six sentences some forty times over.

I would like a decision procedure I can put into the template and that a facilitator with no philosophical training can run against a draft in about an hour. Specifically:

1. The questions to ask of a draft, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each.
2. A test for whether a proposed general claim is genuinely general rather than a description of this incident with the serial numbers filed off. I gather the relevant notion has something to do with whether the claim supports counterfactuals and travels to cases we have not seen, but that is not something a facilitator can apply as stated. Please make it operational: what to ask, which answers fail, and what to do about a claim that holds for our systems but not for anyone else's.
3. A test for whether a stretch of narrative is doing explanatory work or only filling pages — and the converse check, on whether our story format has already settled what counted as a cause before anyone argues about it.
4. Where the procedure should stop with "there is no general claim here, and that is the correct answer", and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values (what we owe the named individuals, what we show auditors) rather than of fact.
5. Which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. I would rather see those flagged than smoothed over.

Please also say plainly where the comparison with explaining the past breaks down for us: outages recur, we can run experiments, our records are machine logs rather than testimony, and we have a commercial interest in the lesson being cheap. If those differences change what the procedure ought to require, say how.

Then run the procedure on three incidents.

Case A: an outage after a configuration change that was correct in isolation but drove a retry storm into a downstream service with no idempotency guarantees.

Case B: a trivial technical fault — a certificate expired on an internal endpoint — that became a four-hour incident because the on-call engineer saw the alert, recognised it as one that had fired falsely three times that month, and did not escalate for two hours. Every general claim we write for this one turns out to be about alert quality or about trust, and neither seems to be about this incident.

Case C: a two-hour outage caused by undocumented behaviour in a third-party vendor's API, which the vendor has since changed and will not confirm in writing. My instinct, and my CTO's, is that we must publish a general lesson about vendor dependencies. Tell me if the procedure instead says the honest output is a narrative and no law; if it does, give me the argument I can make in the review meeting.

I want a verdict rather than a balanced summary: close by saying which of my two colleagues is closer to right about what the document is for, or what the correct synthesis is, and let the procedure reflect that verdict instead of hedging between them.

Please write the whole answer in British English. Name the positions and the thinkers where that genuinely helps me follow the reasoning, but do not give me a reading list, page references or quotations — I have no way to check any of them and will treat unverifiable specifics as noise. Around 1,800 words, with the procedure itself short enough to fit on one page.
