Pretend you're a philosopher of history / social epistemologist who's spent a lot of time on testimony and archives, and think carefully — I want the disambiguation, not a both-sides shrug. Brainstorm the candidate readings before you commit to verdicts on any of them.

Situation: I sit on a small-town historical society board. A group of families, mostly descendants of the mill workers, carry a pretty consistent oral tradition that the 1911 fire was set deliberately by the owner for the insurance money, and that the coroner's inquest finding of faulty wiring was paid for. The paper record — inquest transcript, local newspapers, the company's own ledgers — says accident. They want a plaque that says arson; some of us want one that just names the dead. At the last meeting a fellow board member waved the whole evidentiary question away with "history is written by the victors," meaning roughly: the record is only the owner's side of it, so the families' memory should get the plaque.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making — about who controls the archive, whose testimony survives, who gets to frame the narrative, whether extant sources are systematically skewed, whether living memory outranks documents — running from near-tautology to radical. For each reading, say whether its true, whether it's actually relevant to our case, and specifically whether it would license preferring the families' memory over the inquest record. Then tell me which reading my colleague was probably invoking, and where the slogan starts to undercut itself if you push it hard (I have a feeling it does, given that we know there was a fire at all only from the same sort of documents).
