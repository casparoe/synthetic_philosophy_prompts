I have spent the last two years as a desk officer in the European integration office of a Western Balkan candidate government, drafting our national position on the chapter of the accession negotiations dealing with the judiciary and fundamental rights. Last month I sat through a bilateral screening session in Brussels, and afterwards, largely out of curiosity, I put side by side the Commission's annual progress report on my own country and its annual rule of law chapter on a large founding member state against which an Article 7 procedure has been open for years. The comparison has unsettled me, and I have been unable to articulate exactly why until now. I should say that I came to this work as a lawyer rather than as a political theorist; a former professor of mine once remarked, half in jest, that enlargement is the last place where the claim that history has a direction is still official policy, and I did not understand what he meant until I read these documents.

Here is what puzzles me, and I want to state the practice as precisely as I can, because I suspect the philosophy is in the details. Before accession, my government is assessed against the political criteria laid down at Copenhagen in 1993, which require "stability of institutions guaranteeing democracy, the rule of law, human rights, respect for and protection of minorities" — note that the standard is not present compliance but durability. We are examined chapter by chapter through screening reports, interim benchmarks, annual country reports, conditional pre-accession funding, and the current methodology expressly contemplates suspending negotiations and reopening closed chapters in the event of serious backsliding. After accession, the same Commission publishes, since 2020, an annual rule of law report covering every member state, with country-specific recommendations since 2022 — but the instrument is described as "preventive and dialogue-based" and carries no leverage at all. The treaties permit the suspension of certain rights of membership, yet contain no mechanism for expelling a member; the finding of a serious and persistent breach requires unanimity among the others, so two states under procedure shield each other, and no suspension has ever been adopted. Meanwhile, the only serious experiment with post-accession conditionality — the monitoring mechanism imposed on Bulgaria and Romania when they joined in 2007 — was allowed to lapse in 2023 and has not been repeated for any subsequent candidate. And a candidate must accept the whole body of EU law and commit to the single currency, while several long-standing members hold treaty opt-outs from parts of that same body, so the threshold at the door is one that some of those already inside do not themselves meet.

My question is what this arrangement presupposes about political development, and whether it presupposes anything at all. It looks to me as though the whole apparatus only makes sense on the assumption that liberal democratic institutions are a terminal condition: once a state crosses a certain threshold, its order becomes self-maintaining, monitoring becomes courtesy rather than condition, and reversibility ceases to be a live possibility. If that is right, I would like the implicit theory reconstructed as carefully as you can — what exactly the endpoint is supposed to be, what is claimed to happen at the moment of crossing it, and what makes the crossing one-way. Which rival accounts does the practice quietly exclude: a view on which no constitutional order is self-sustaining and every polity requires permanent external maintenance, a view on which institutions are equilibria that can unravel without anyone deciding to unravel them, or a view on which there is no endpoint but only a standing risk of decay? Please be scrupulous about separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined. In particular, I can imagine the deflationary answer: the asymmetry is not a philosophy of history but the simple fact that leverage evaporates at the moment of accession, and conditionality is only ever as strong as the prize still on offer. If that is the whole story, why does the pre-accession process insist so heavily on the irreversibility of reforms — demanding proof about a country's future precisely where the Union's power to respond to a reversal is at its greatest? Is the practice, in the end, internally coherent, or does it assert two incompatible things about the same object? And if the Union were to abandon the presupposed view in favour of one on which every member state remains, permanently, a candidate, what would concretely have to change in the treaties, the reporting cycle, and the closing of chapters?
