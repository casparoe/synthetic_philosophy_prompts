The room I have to persuade is not the room I trained in. In about six weeks I'm presenting a working paper at a law school workshop on evidence and proof — twenty minutes of questions after a forty-five-minute talk — to roughly eighteen people: two professors who write on the admissibility of expert testimony (one of whom has argued at length that cross-examination is a weak instrument for exposing bad science), a retired federal magistrate judge, a philosopher of law who works on the epistemology of legal proof and probabilistic standards of persuasion, three products-liability litigators from a firm down the street, a computer scientist in the school's law-and-technology program who has actually read the AI-oversight literature, a scattering of law students, and one colleague from my own lab who will be friendly. I was talked into this slot by a friend on the faculty and have never practiced law, so my worry is not that they'll be hostile but that they'll be precise about procedure in ways I am not. The paper was supposed to be co-authored with an economist who pulled out in March, which means the section on incentives is mine alone and is the part I'm least sure of.

The thesis is deliberately provocative, and I need the objections aimed at exactly this version of it rather than at AI safety via debate in general. Standard formulations ask whether an unaided human judge can recognize the truth of a claim that exceeds her own competence. I argue that this is the wrong question, and that the right one is whether the protocol makes a false claim more expensive to defend than a true claim is. I define the cost of defending a claim as the expected number of further commitments the claimant has to make — and then keep mutually consistent — before either the claim is exposed or the question budget runs out. My contention is that for a true claim about a verifiable fact that tree is shallow and self-consistent, whereas holding a randomly sampled subtree of a false claim consistent requires progressively more coordinated fabrication, so the cost curve is far steeper; a judge who is only marginally better than chance at rating any single exchange can therefore drive the error rate down by sampling questions rather than by understanding the domain. I say explicitly that this, and not anything about the judge's competence, is what I take the complexity-theoretic argument behind the original proposal to be doing. From there I draw the design conclusion: oversight protocols should be built to maximize the cost of defending a lie — randomized question selection, binding prior commitments, escrowed predictions, forced specificity, consistency checks across replayed sessions — while measures aimed at improving the judge's own competence (training, tooling, decomposition, monitoring the model's reasoning) are second-order. One slide says the protocol should be designed like cross-examination and not like peer review, and I fully expect that to be the most quoted thing in the room. My empirical support is thin and I know it: small synthetic tasks, language models as the two debaters, crowdworkers as judges.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight to ten objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the ones I'd get from an alignment-research crowd, and not generic skepticism about AI risk — ordered by the product of how likely each is to come up and how hard it will be for me to answer, with each phrased the way that person would phrase it, since the magistrate judge and the admissibility professor will not put the same concern into the same words. For each objection, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is softest, and a flag for whether I should partially concede it in my first five minutes to buy credibility. I especially want the objections that turn my own courtroom analogy against me: what adversarial procedure is actually for, whose burden it allocates and how, what happens when both advocates are licensed to mislead within bounds, what appellate review adds that a single round of debate lacks, and what the picture looks like when the fact-finder cannot follow the evidence at all. Close with the question I'll be hoping nobody asks, and say why it's dangerous. Wherever my argument or your replies depend on a claim about how legal procedure or legal epistemology really works, tell me plainly whether that claim is settled among people who know the doctrine or genuinely contested; I don't want to walk in asserting as background consensus something the evidence professors regard as an open fight, and equally I don't want to hedge on something that is in fact uncontroversial. Please write the whole thing in American English.
