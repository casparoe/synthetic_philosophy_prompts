I am a senior at a small independent high school, and in June I will give the student address at our baccalaureate service: four to five minutes, spoken aloud, to about three hundred people — my classmates, their parents and grandparents, and the whole faculty. The English version of our school's motto is painted along the wall behind the platform where I will stand, and it is printed on the program: "Everything that is true can be known." I have loved that sentence since I was eleven years old.

Over the winter my older sister, who is two years into a philosophy doctorate, walked me through why it cannot mean what it looks like it means. Her reconstruction, as best I can hold onto it: suppose some truth is in fact known by no one. Then the compound claim "this is true, and no one knows that it is true" is itself a truth, so by the motto it could be known — but anyone who knew it would know both halves, and knowing the first half destroys the second. So either there are no unknown truths at all, which is absurd, or else some truths cannot be known, which is the opposite of the motto. She said both ends come from the same argument, and I am not certain I have the direction right, so please correct me if I have garbled it. I believe it is the one people call Fitch's paradox of knowability.

Please write the address. Here is what it has to do and what it must not do.

1. It must work as an actual baccalaureate speech — warm, addressed to eighteen-year-olds who are leaving home next month, funny once or twice in a dry way, and readable aloud in about 700 words, with sentences that survive being spoken. No formal notation, and the words "epistemic," "verificationism," "anti-realism," and "proposition" are all banned. "Paradox" may appear at most once.

2. It must not assert anything that would be false if the argument above is sound. That has two consequences I keep tripping over. It cannot endorse the motto in its literal universal form, even as a flourish. And it cannot give the audience a single concrete example of a truth no one will ever know, because if the argument is right nobody could be in a position to name one. The speech has to make the class feel the existence of such truths without ever pointing at one. I have tried three drafts and every one of them cheats here.

3. It must not announce that the motto on the wall is wrong. The school is not going to paint over it, I am fond of it, and the people who endowed it will be in the second row. What I want instead is for the speech to arrive at some restatement of the same hope that the argument leaves untouched — a version of the sentence the class could carry out the door with them. If you judge that no such restatement is both honest and stirring, say so plainly rather than manufacturing one.

4. Two pieces of our actual year to build on. There is a tradition in which each senior writes an unanswered question on a card, and the cards go into a tin that is sealed at graduation and opened by the class's twenty-fifth reunion. And our Latin teacher, who has been at the school for thirty-eight years and retires this month, has told every class she ever taught that what is on the wall is a translation, and that translations leak. Please leave [NAME] placeholders for anyone you mention; I will fill them in.

5. The tin is the thing I most want you to use carefully, because it is a trap: those are questions whose answers will very likely be known, just not yet, and the speech should not pretend otherwise. "Not yet known by us" and "not knowable by anyone" are different claims and the audience deserves to feel the difference without being told it in those words.

6. The service is interfaith. Some of the room will say the truths left over are known by God; please do not make that the speech's resolution, since the argument is about what can be known at all, and I would rather acknowledge that people in the room hold this than borrow it as an escape hatch.

My sister will be checking. If the speech depends on a particular way of answering the argument — narrowing which truths the motto ranges over, reading knowability as something other than a claim about minds like ours, or simply conceding that some truths are beyond us — I want that choice made deliberately and visibly to her, not smuggled in through a metaphor.

After the speech, add a short separate note answering three things: which response to the argument the speech in fact presupposes and why you chose it; where you think the speech is most likely to be wrong or to be caught out, especially anywhere it comes close to breaking rule 2; and what argument or consideration would change your mind about the ending — that is, what would have to be shown to make you write the closing restatement differently than you have. I would rather have a speech with its weak point named than a confident one that is quietly incoherent.
