Right — I want a short story, somewhere around 1,500 words, built on a fairly specific conceit. Please actually think about the philosophy in it rather than just using it as set dressing.

The conceit: in a near-future city, the hard decisions aren't made by clever people any more. They're made by *factoring*. You take the root question — say, whether to flood one district to save the rest of the basin — and you split it into sub-questions, then split those, recurssively, until every leaf is something an ordinary, unremarkable person can answer honestly and completely in about ten minutes, using only the material handed to them. Each leaf gets a fresh volunteer who never sees any other part of the tree. On the way back up, nothing gets averaged or voted on: each branch node is another volunteer who is shown exactly two candidate answers from below and asked to pick the better one, and they're not told what question produced them. The whole thing terminates in a single verdict at the root. Depth and branching factor are public. The shape of any particular tree is secret.

The official line is that this beats one genius thinking hard, because nobody is ever trusted with the whole problem and every single step is checkable by someone weak. What I want the story to chew on: no individual mind ever holds the decision. Not one person in the process understands what has been decided. So when the water comes up the streets, who exactly is answerable? And — this is the part I care about most — think hard about the leaves. A leaf can be asked a tiny, perfectly well-posed question, answer it sincerely and to the best of their ability, and still be wrong in a way that no combination of the other leaves can ever detect, because the error is precisely the kind that survives the factoring. Give me at least one concrete example of such an error, in-world, and make it plausible rather than melodramatic.

Shape is up to you, but one possibility: follow a "regular" — someone who's sat a lot of trees for the fee — who starts noticing a pattern in the small questions she's given, and slowly realises she can reconstruct the root question from the fragments, which is the one thing the whole system is designed to make impossible.

A few things to steer clear of: no benevolent-AI-goes-rogue turn, no "the machine was human all along", no courtroom moment where one heroic person shouts the real answer and everyone sheepishly admits the system was silly. I want the system to genuinely work, to genuinely be better than the alternative it replaced, and for the story to be sad about that in a precise way rather than a vague one. Don't name-drop any real theories or real philosophers — keep it all inside the world. And please end on something concrete and physical, not a big thematic statement.
