You are a philosopher of physics who also reads distributed-computing papers without needing a glossary. Think this through in stages: first state the argument's premises explicitly for yourself, then translate, then write the residue report.

I am a staff engineer working on globally replicated storage systems. My working vocabulary consists of Lamport's happens-before relation, causal versus concurrent events, logical clocks, consistent cuts and snapshot protocols, linearizability, and the standing assumption that there is no global clock — together with the fact that a snapshot is only one of many admissible cuts through a concurrent region, selected by convention rather than by anything in the physics of the system. I have no philosophy training, and I would rather not be given one.

Restate the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for four-dimensionalism — the argument running from the relativity of simultaneity to the conclusion that events far outside my light cone are already real, including Penrose's variant in which two pedestrians passing on the street have planes of simultaneity that disagree about whether a decision in Andromeda has been made — in that vocabulary, premise by premise, as faithfully as you can. My own working hypothesis is that 'the present' maps onto a consistent cut returned by a snapshot algorithm, and that 'real' maps onto membership in the union of admissible cuts rather than onto membership in any single cut. Tell me whether that mapping is correct, and whether the argument still delivers its conclusion once it is stated that way.

Then give me the residue report. Which premises translate cleanly? Which translate only by being distorted — and for each of those, state the distortion openly instead of quietly smuggling in spacetime talk and calling it translation? Which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I want special attention on two places: what 'real' becomes when the only notions available to me are state, causality, and admissibility; and what becomes of the claim that the future is open, given that in my framework the future is not a set of events but a set of possible executions.

Finally, take a side. Is the residue evidence that my vocabulary is missing something — some genuinely modal or ontological notion that concurrency theory cannot express — or evidence that the argument's crucial step was less substantive than it looked, so that once the inexpressible terms are stripped away there is nothing left to argue about? Give me a definite answer rather than a balanced summary of both options.
