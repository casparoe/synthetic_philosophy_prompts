I convene a graduate reading group on social epistemology, and next term's speaker defends reductionism about testimony in an unusually strong form: on her view a hearer is entitled to believe what she is told only where she has case-specific, locally available reasons for taking that particular speaker to be sincere and competent on this occasion — cues she can read in the exchange itself — and a general inductive record for testimony as a source does no justificatory work at all. It is, in other words, local reductionism pushed considerably further than its usual defenders push it. She has also said in print that she is unmoved by the developmental objection, since on her account a child learns to read those cues perceptually rather than on anyone's say-so.

Please draft eight questions for the hour, ordered so that each one narrows an exit the preceding answer is likely to open: begin with whether ordinary monitoring actually tracks sincerity and competence to any useful degree, and what her requirement amounts to if it does not; move to domains in which no local cue tracks competence at all, such as a patient assessing an oncologist's pronouncement; then to the bootstrapping of whatever background sensitivity she concedes to the child, and whether conceding it quietly restores the general inductive base she has rejected; and close on what distinguishes a positive-reasons requirement from a mere defeasible presumption of truthfulness, given that her opponents insist the presumption is all they ever claimed. For two of the eight, of your choosing, supply the reply you expect from her and the counter-question it should draw.

Write the whole thing in British English. Before the questions, state which of them you expect her to absorb most comfortably and why, and say what she could answer that would show you had misread the position badly enough to warrant scrapping or rewriting one of the eight.
