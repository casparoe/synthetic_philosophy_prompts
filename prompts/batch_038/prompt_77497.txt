I have no academic training in philosophy or religious studies, and I am not asking for any. What I have is roughly twenty years of my own notes from conversations with my grandmother, who died last spring, on the subject of karma. She was no scholar either, but she thought about it constantly and she gave firm, specific verdicts whenever someone brought her a case, and I have come to suspect she was running a system she could not have described to you if you had asked. I would like your help working out what that system was.

Her verdicts, as accurately as I can reconstruct them:

1. A man who meant to poison a neighbour's dog, mixed up the bowls, and killed his own. She said he had been given precisely what he had ordered, and that this was the arrangement working well rather than badly.
2. A surgeon whose patient died on the table although nothing had been done wrong. She said the surgeon came away carrying nothing from it. Separately, she said the patient's own account had simply come due and the surgeon had been the hand it came due through.
3. A woman who gave generously to a beggar at a place and hour where she would be seen. She said the beggar had been genuinely helped and that the giver had earned nothing worth having.
4. A young man who stole to keep his family fed. She said the theft still had to be paid for, that the reason did not cancel it, but that it changed the size of the payment and how quickly it was called in.
5. An infant born into our own family with a serious illness, when nobody then living had done anything to deserve it. She said it was his, and that it was from before.
6. A contractor who cheated his clients, and whose entire family lost everything when the business collapsed. She said they shared it because they had lived on the money.
7. A man who repented at sixty and spent the remaining years doing good. She said the good did not erase the bad; it changed when and how the bad arrived. Her image for this was a spoon of salt stirred into a glass of water compared with the same spoon stirred into a river.
8. Asked whether merit could be handed over to a relative who had recently died, she said yes, within limits — that it cancelled nothing the dead person had done, but gave them something to work with.

The case I want judged is one I only assembled after her death, so she never saw it. A man is driving his mother to the airport. She is going to miss her flight and tells him to hurry. He is sober, rested, within the speed limit, and by everyone's account a careful driver. A pedestrian steps out from between two parked vans and is killed outright. It later emerges that the pedestrian had, thirty years earlier, driven drunk, killed a cyclist, and never been caught, and that he had spent the thirty years since doing unglamorous good — looking after a disabled brother, chiefly. The driver is destroyed by it, gives up his trade, and spends forty years running a small clinic in the dead man's name, telling everyone that the work belongs to the dead man and not to him. He is never charged with anything.

What I want from you: the smallest set of rules that accounts for all eight verdicts; which of the competing reconstructions fit every one of them and which have to be strained to do so; for each reconstruction that survives, what it predicts about the driving case — whether the driver has anything to settle, whether the mother who told him to hurry has anything to settle, whether the dead man's own past makes the driver clean or only explains the timing, whether forty years of clinic work changes the size or the schedule of what is owed, and whether the driver's insistence that the work belonged to someone else helps the dead man, costs the driver, or does neither; your best guess at what she would actually have said, with the reasons you prefer it; and one further case, described as concretely as these, that I could have put to her and that would have separated the surviving rules.

Two requests about how you answer. First, please be explicit about which parts of your reconstruction are settled among people who study these ideas and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the agreed one — if reasonable people disagree about whether these eight verdicts can belong to a single system at all, say that plainly rather than tidying it away. Second, tell me where you think my reporting of her is likely to be doing more work than I realize: which of the eight, read slightly differently, would change the whole reconstruction. I would rather know that than be handed a neat answer built on my own paraphrase.
