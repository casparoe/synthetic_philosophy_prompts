I'm a fourth-year PhD student in atomic physics. My thesis concerns indistinguishability in trapped-ion and cavity-QED systems, so I work daily with symmetrization, occupation numbers, Fock states, permutation invariance, and exchange interactions; I'm fluent in all of that and only semi-literate in metaphysics. I know roughly what haecceitism, grounding, and weak discernibility are supposed to mean, but I have no instincts about which of those notions is respectable and which is a verbal tic, so wherever you need metaphysical machinery, please introduce it in terms I already have. This semester I have been auditing the metaphysics seminar, and we spent several weeks on Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and on Black's symmetrical universe of two exactly similar iron spheres in an otherwise empty world. I found the discussion exasperating in a particular way. I already possess a working set of intuitions about when there are two things and when there aren't, formed at the bench rather than in the armchair, and I now strongly suspect they do not hang together. What the seminar never did was treat those intuitions as data. So I would like you to do that: here are my verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. Two electrons in the same trap prepared in a spin singlet. There are exactly two of them; the number is not in doubt for a moment. But there is no fact about which is which, and I do not experience that as a loss. Each stands to the other in the irreflexive relation of having opposite spin, and that strikes me as all the individuation the situation either admits of or requires.

2. Two bosons occupying the same single-particle mode — the n = 2 Fock state of a cavity, say. Here "two" reads to me as a count of excitations, not a count of individuals, and I actively resist saying there are two things. Pressed, I would say there is one mode with occupation number two.

3. Black's universe. Two spheres, and not one sphere described twice. But I want to insist that the two-ness is secured by nothing more than the irreflexive relation of being some distance apart. If you tell me that this is exactly as thin as what secures two-ness in case 1, I accept that gladly.

4. The same universe, except that the spheres move so that their histories are exact mirror images and they periodically exchange positions. This is a genuinely different possible world from the static one, because in it something changes over time and in the other nothing does.

5. Permuting the particle labels in my two-electron state. Same physical situation, end of discussion. I would not tolerate a formalism in which the permuted state counted as a distinct possibility.

6. The Gibbs paradox and the division by N!. Here I am openly of two minds and I know it. Part of me reads that factor as a confession that permutations are not real differences, which is why two volumes of the same gas gain no entropy on mixing. Another part reads it as bookkeeping required to make entropy extensive, carrying no metaphysics at all, since the same factor falls out of plain combinatorics with no mention of indistinguishability. I hold both readings and could not say which I would defend under cross-examination.

7. Two coins on my desk, or two ions of the same isotope in my linear trap that I call "left" and "right." Obviously two individuals; no metaphysical discomfort whatever. But my collaborator's objection lands. After a swap-type gate exchanges their motional states, "left" and "right" cease to track anything, and my confidence evaporates even though the number is still two and the ions are still identical. So I am unsure whether 7 is a real verdict or merely case 1 in better lighting, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on.

What I want from you, in this order. First: is there a single criterion — of when numerical distinctness is grounded, or of what counts as an individual — that delivers these verdicts, or most of them? State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without consulting you, and build it clause by clause, telling me which verdict each clause exists to capture. Second: which of my verdicts can it not accommodate? If you think two of them are flatly incompatible, and I suspect 1 against 2 and 4 against 5 are the candidates, say which one has to go and why the loss is more than verbal. Third: apply your principle to two situations I have not listed. One is a universe containing two exactly similar spheres whose orbits are exact mirror images of each other, so that the history taken as a whole is permutation-invariant even though the spheres never coincide. The other is a single excitation delocalized across two spatially separated modes, where I cannot tell whether my instinct to count one thing there is a fact about the world or an artifact of there being one field quantum. Tell me what your principle predicts in each case and whether I should welcome the prediction. Fourth, and this is the part I actually care about: give me the price. I would rather have a lean principle that overturns one of my verdicts than an epicycled one that saves all seven; but if you think the baroque principle is the correct one, show me what each piece of added complexity is buying, and convince me it is not simply padding over the conflict. I am not after a survey of who said what — I have read enough of that literature to be more confused than when I started. I want the principle, its clauses, its predictions, and its cost.
