I am assembling a set of cases to test a specific claim about practical rationality: that an agent can be rationally required to act in a situation where the reasons do not distinguish between the acts available to her — that is, that choice without preference can be mandatory, rather than a merely permissible arbitrary picking that leaves nothing further to explain. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

**The scenario.** In one paragraph, set in a realistic professional or institutional context (a selection committee, a clinic, a trading desk, an emergency service — no trolleys, no science fiction, no deities, no predictors of any kind), describe a single agent who:

(i) faces two available acts that are exactly equal in every respect that bears on choice;

(ii) has established that equality herself, by reasoning from all the information available to her, and knows that no further evidence, no additional deliberation, and no refinement of the criteria could break it — the tie is robust to reflection rather than an artifact of ignorance, and the symmetry is produced by her reasoning about reasons, not by a physical arrangement. This is where the case must part company with the familiar fable: no equidistant haystacks, no two identical glasses of water, nothing whose symmetry is merely geometrical;

(iii) faces a hard deadline after which both acts cease to be available and a strictly worse outcome obtains, so that inaction is not a way of preserving the tie — and she knows this;

(iv) has no access to the usual exits: no chance device to hand, no third party authorized to decide for her, and no opportunity to have bound herself in advance, since she could not have foreseen that she would end up in this situation.

The case must be a pure tie and not a moral dilemma: neither act is forbidden, and there is no residue-earning or regret-earning feature that distinguishes them.

**The banned dissolutions.** The bind may not be dissolved by any of the following, and your write-up must state explicitly, for each, whether the case as built blocks it and how: that the agent will in fact be moved by a whim or passing impulse; that she can randomize; that she can adopt a resolute plan or a pre-commitment rule; that she can form a second-order preference over her own first-order preferences; and that the deadline supplies a decisive reason — you may grant that it supplies a reason to stop deliberating and act, but it must not be allowed to supply a reason selecting one act over the other. You may also not stipulate a psychology that makes picking impossible for her. If the block on picking is to be real, it has to come from the structure of the situation rather than from a declared incapacity.

**The three verdicts.** After the scenario, take each of the following positions in turn and give the verdict it delivers on your case, together with the reasoning that gets it there:

- Orthodox expected-utility maximization, on which two acts of equal expected utility are both permissible and no rational defect arises.
- A view on which an act is rational only if some reason selects it, so that a requirement to act coupled with an exhaustive tie is a genuine bind rather than a harmless symmetry.
- A voluntarist view on which the agent can simply pick, and on which picking in the absence of preference is a full exercise of agency rather than a failure of it.

The design target is that the third view comes out badly: picking must be unavailable or compromised in your case, and the reason must be visible in the structure of the scenario rather than asserted.

**What the case is for, and what counts as cheating.** The point is to establish whether a mandatory tie-break is constructible at all. So the verdicts must be generated by the theories out of the details of the case. A scenario that simply announces that the agent is required to choose, or that declares the two acts equally good by fiat rather than showing the equality arising from the agent's own reasoning, fails.

**Then close with four short sections.**

1. Which single feature of the case does the work — the one that could not be removed without collapsing the bind — and what the case becomes if you remove it.
2. Whether the bind survives idealization: does it still bite if the agent is made perfectly self-transparent and free of sub-personal noise, and does it still bite if she is left an ordinary, noisy human being?
3. The strongest attack available to a defender of the view that comes out badly — including the objection that no two real options are ever exactly alike, and the objection that an agent who must act always finds that acting beats not acting, so the tie was never total — and your assessment of whether the attack succeeds.
4. Whether the constraints, taken jointly, are satisfiable. If you have had to soften one, say which and why; if you think the design is impossible or trivial as specified, say so and name the constraint you would drop first.

I am not looking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list; you may name positions if it helps, but the analysis must stand on its own. Roughly 700 to 1000 words in total.
