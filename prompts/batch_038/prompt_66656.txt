I run a ninety-minute session in a "governing emerging tech" workshop series at my university next month, and the slot I've been handed is officially titled "the AI alignment problem." The room will be about twenty-five people: mostly public-policy and IR master's students, three or four CS undergrads who've had one ML course, one philosophy PhD student who will absolutely try to drag us into metaethics, and two people from a national AI office who've come to work out whether any of this is actually regulable or whether they're being sold a moral panic. There's a whiteboard, one marker that may be dying, and no slides — the projector in that room has been broken since spring and nobody's fixing it. So it's me, a board, and ninety minutes.

The thing I actually want the room to leave with isn't a definition. It's an answer to two questions that I think get collapsed into one in most write-ups: what is alignment actually being asked to *do* — what's the deliverable, and what would count as success — and is "the alignment problem" one problem or four or five problems wearing a trenchcoat. I've tried this session once before and it failed in both directions at once. Half the room heard me describe a Terminator scenario and got politely embarrassed on my behalf; the other half kept insisting the whole thing is already handled by RLHF plus a red team and an evals vendor. The CS undergrads got annoyed because they felt I was hand-waving about what training even is, and the philosophy student spent forty minutes trying to establish that no system can be aligned to anything since value isn't real. So I need an arc that survives all four of those failure modes.

Plan the session for me — don't explain alignment to me, I already think I know it, I need the teaching plan. Specifically:

- The dependency structure. Which distinctions have to be in place before which others, and what breaks if you reorder them. I keep flip-flopping on whether capability-vs-goals has to come before specification-vs-generalization, or whether the "aligned to what, and to *whom*" question has to come first so the policy people have a hook — tell me which order actually holds up and why, and where the second and third distinctions can be safely deferred to week two.
- One example that carries the whole thing. Not a list of case studies, one. Tell me what that example has to have and what it has to lack. My instinct is something small and undramatic — a deployed system that does competently and repeatedly something nobody on the team intended, with a paper trail showing what was specified versus what got learned — but I can't tell whether that's too tame to survive the terminator-expectation, or whether going bigger is exactly what makes half the room check out.
- The misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each gets headed off, because I don't want to leave them all for the Q&A. At minimum: it's a robot-uprising problem; it means "make the AI nice"; RLHF already solved it; alignment is just the off-switch/control problem; and the one I expect from the two government people, which is that if we can't measure it then it isn't a policy object at all. Also tell me which of these I should deliberately *not* refute head-on because arguing with it makes it stronger.
- A commitment from me on the one-problem-versus-several-problems fork, and which way to teach it to *this* room, with the reason. I think the honest answer is several, but I worry that decomposing it into five named sub-problems in ninety minutes makes it look like a research programme with no bottom, which is precisely the "moral panic in disguise" read I'm trying to avoid.
- One check I can run in the last five minutes — a question, a two-sentence task, whatever — that would tell me they got the *idea* and not just the vocabulary. If they can recite "specification" and "generalization" back at me without being able to apply either to something they've never seen, the session failed, and I want to be able to detect that in the room rather than in the feedback form.
- What I should leave out at this level and announce that I'm leaving out, so the CS students don't think I'm hiding the technical content and the philosophy student doesn't think I'm dodging the normative one.
