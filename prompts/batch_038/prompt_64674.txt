I spent most of my working life in experimental physics and have come to decision theory late, where one result keeps nagging at me: Ellsberg's two urns. In the first, fifty red and fifty black balls; in the second, one hundred balls in an unknown split. People will bet on red in the first and refuse the second, although the payoff is the same under any symmetric belief about the second. I am already acquainted with the standard Bayesian repairs — a second-order prior over compositions, or the observation that the choice itself transmits information — so please do not recite them. I want a case put to the theory.

Take the U.S. credit crisis of 2007–2009, and specifically the supervisory stress test of early 2009. The facts I will supply: in August 2007 BNP Paribas halted redemptions on three funds, explaining that liquidity had evaporated in parts of the U.S. securitization market to the point where certain assets could not be fairly valued regardless of their quality or rating. In February 2009 the Federal Reserve and the other U.S. banking agencies assessed all nineteen domestic bank holding companies holding at least $100 billion in assets under a worse-than-expected macroeconomic scenario; the results were published in May, the scenario implying roughly $600 billion of losses across 2009–2010 and an additional $75 billion of common equity at ten of the nineteen firms. Bank shares rallied and credit spreads narrowed markedly on publication, even though that aggregate capital figure was small beside losses already recognized. Bernanke later described the exercise as designed "to reduce the uncertainty about potential losses and earnings prospects."

Analyze this episode as a deliberate intervention on Ellsberg's second urn. Begin with a disciplined mapping: who exactly is the ambiguity-averse bettor here — the money-market lender, the repo counterparty, the fund manager — what plays the role of the urn, what are the balls, and what does a published supervisory scenario do to the composition? Then state what each account predicts about the market's response: a Bayesian whose mean estimate has barely moved should be nearly unmoved, whereas a maxmin agent should be moved a great deal, and say which pattern the episode exhibits. Next, identify the rival mechanisms the case cannot rule out — adverse selection in the funding market, coordination and flight to quality, and above all the government backstop bundled with the disclosure, which reprices the tail rather than removing ignorance — and say what evidence would discriminate among them, or whether any could. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory: does it vindicate treating ambiguity as a distinct object of normative and policy attention, or does it suggest that "ambiguity" is a property of disclosure regimes rather than of decision problems? Where the theory is simply silent, say so. If you think later European exercises of the same design bear on the diagnosis, use them only if you are confident of the facts, and flag them otherwise.
