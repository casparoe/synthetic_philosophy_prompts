You are to take on the role of a decision theorist who works at the intersection of the formal theory of choice functions and Herbert Simon's account of bounded rationality, and who is accustomed to defending unfashionable positions in seminar. I am a high school senior writing an extended essay for a philosophy-of-economics course, and I would like you to think carefully and then construct the strongest available argument for a thesis I will state precisely below. Please begin with a brief brainstorming section — two or three paragraphs canvassing the lines of attack that are open to you, with your assessment of which are most promising and which are tempting but weak — and only then write the argument itself.

The thesis is this: satisficing against a fixed, pre-committed aspiration level is not merely a second-best substitute for maximizing expected utility, to be tolerated only because human agents are slow, tired, and computationally limited. For an agent who does not control the menus she is shown, threshold-based choice has a structural advantage that maximization cannot replicate, and there are conditions under which an agent who could compute the true maximizer without any effort at all would still do better to bind herself to satisficing.

I want the argument organized around a property I will call menu-invariance. A choice rule is menu-invariant when, whenever the option it would select is present, it selects that option regardless of what else is offered alongside it. An absolute-threshold satisficer has this property by construction: the aspiration level was settled before the menu was seen, so appending a fourth alternative, deleting one, repackaging two of them, or reordering the list cannot shift the outcome. A maximizer, by contrast, selects whatever ranks highest relative to the menu actually presented to her — and real menus are assembled by other people who have reasons of their own.

The concrete case I would like you to work with is this. My school's robotics team orders parts from an online supplier, and I have watched three of us make the same purchase decision three different ways in a single evening, depending on which comparison table the site happened to show us and which add-ons appeared in the sidebar. We were not confused about what we needed; we had a budget and a specification. Yet the selections moved.

Two things I want you to handle explicitly rather than step around. First, the objection I expect to face is that a genuinely rational maximizer is immune to all of this by definition: context effects and decoys are simply instances of faulty reasoning, and the choice of a fully rational agent depends only on the intrinsic features of the options, never on how they are displayed. If that objection succeeds, my thesis reduces to the uninteresting claim that poorly executed maximization is worse than well-executed satisficing. Explain why it does not succeed, or explain why it does not carry the whole weight of the case against me. Second, aspiration levels are not in fact fixed: Simon himself treated them as adjusting upward and downward in response to experience, which would reintroduce precisely the menu-sensitivity I am claiming satisficing escapes. Say what remains of the thesis once that is granted.

Please do not let the argument rest on the observation that people get tired or make arithmetic errors. I want an explanation of why the threshold rule is structurally better situated, not merely easier to execute. Define any technical term the first time you use it, and state your assumptions where they are doing load-bearing work. If the thesis has to be narrowed in order to be defensible, narrow it and tell me exactly how far you narrowed it.

I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or references of any kind; I want the reasoning. Aim for roughly 1,300 to 1,600 words, and close with a single paragraph identifying the point at which your argument is most exposed and what would have to be true for it to hold.
