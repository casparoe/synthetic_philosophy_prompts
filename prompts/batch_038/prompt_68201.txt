ASSIGNMENT (600 words maximum). Explain the higher-order approach to phenomenal consciousness. Your answer should cover (a) what, on this approach, makes a mental state conscious rather than unconscious, and why its proponents deny that consciousness is an intrinsic property of states; (b) at least one serious objection and how the view's defenders respond; (c) how the approach relates to global workspace theory and to the neuroimaging evidence cited on both sides.

STUDENT ANSWER. The higher-order approach starts from a simple observation: a great deal of mental processing in us goes on without our being aware of it in any way, and yet some of it is conscious. So consciousness cannot be just a matter of what a state represents, or of how much processing goes into producing it. What has to be added is awareness of the state itself. Rosenthal formulates the requirement as the transitivity principle: a state is conscious only if the subject is in some suitable way aware of being in that state. On his version, that awareness is supplied by a higher-order thought, a thought whose content is roughly that I am in such-and-such a state. Because the awareness is carried by a distinct state aimed at the first one, the view treats consciousness as relational rather than intrinsic: nothing about the functional makeup of the target state on its own settles whether it is conscious; what settles it is whether the right sort of representation is directed at it. Rosenthal is careful about the regress. If the higher-order thought had itself to be conscious in order to do its work, we would need a further thought to make it conscious, and so on without end. His answer is that the higher-order thought need not be occurrent at all: it may be a standing disposition to think that one is in the state, and that is what stops the regress. Lycan's higher-order perception theory stops it differently, by treating the higher-order state as a kind of inner sense whose deliverances are non-conceptual, more a scanning of one's states than a judgment about them. The standard objection is misrepresentation. A higher-order thought might be caused by something other than the state it represents, leaving the subject with a higher-order thought about a state they are not in. Rosenthal grants that this would produce a conscious state with no first-order target, but argues we could never detect it, since from the inside an empty higher-order thought is indistinguishable from a matching one; the possibility is therefore not a reductio. The concession is costlier than he allows. Once a higher-order representation can generate consciousness on its own, the theory no longer has a principled account of why any particular first-order state is unconscious rather than conscious, since correspondence between the two levels is no longer required. Empirically the view has been developed by Hakwan Lau, who argues that the neural signature of conscious awareness tracks prefrontal activity rather than activity in the sensory areas carrying the stimulus information. This also explains blindsight better than rival accounts do: in blindsight, visual processing continues without the higher-order representation, so the patient can guess accurately while sincerely denying that he sees anything. Global workspace theory is best understood as the mechanistic version of the same idea. A state becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely enough to be reported and used by other systems, that is, when other parts of the mind are informed about it, and reportability is just what higher-order theorists mean by awareness of a state. The two families therefore agree on the architecture and disagree mainly about where in the brain to look for it.
