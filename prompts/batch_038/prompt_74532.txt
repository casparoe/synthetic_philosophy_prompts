**Context.** I am the instructor for an upper-level undergraduate seminar of sixteen students on nineteenth-century critiques of mass society. In week five the students sat a 45-minute closed-book in-class essay. The essay question was worth 25 marks, and I am now moderating a second reader's assessment of one script before the marks are released. I have not yet graded this one myself and I would prefer your read to be independent of any expectation about how good or bad it is.

**The question as the students saw it.**

> In *Two Ages* (1846), Kierkegaard argues that "the public" is a product of the press rather than a group that the press merely reports on. Explain this claim: what sort of thing the public is on his account, how it differs from other collectivities, and what role "leveling" plays in his diagnosis of the present age. Conclude by stating what you take to be the practical upshot of the diagnosis for the individual reader. (45 minutes, closed book; roughly 500–700 words.)

**The student's script.**

Kierkegaard's claim is not that the press reports badly, but that it brings into being a kind of agent that had not previously existed. The public, on his account, is an abstraction: it is not a people, not a generation, not a community, not any actual gathering of persons. Nobody has ever been in the same room as the public. It exists only insofar as a daily press addresses a mass of readers separately, at a distance, in the same words, and then treats their dispersed assent as though it were the verdict of a single subject. Antiquity had crowds but no public, because the ancient people assembled, acted, and could be held answerable for what it had done. The crowd is therefore simply the pre-modern name for what the modern age calls the public: the press did not so much create a new category as enlarge an old one, replacing the shouting assembly with the silent reader.

The distinguishing feature of the present age, for Kierkegaard, is that it is reflective rather than passionate. Nothing in it is decided; everything in it is discussed. Reflection generates chatter, and chatter generates the journalist, who is answerable for nothing he prints and who can retract it tomorrow at no cost to himself. This is the mechanism through which leveling operates. Leveling is the process by which the press makes everyone hold the same opinions: distinctions of quality between better and worse judgments are erased, the reader is supplied with a settled view, and in due course individual opinion converges on what the papers have already said. The leveled person no longer has views of his own but only the views of the public; and since the public is nowhere, he too is nowhere.

What follows practically depends on a point that is easy to miss. If the public is unreal — a phantom with no location and no voice of its own — then nobody belongs to it in the way a person belongs to a family or a city. The public cannot act, and so it cannot be blamed; and if no individual is the public, then no individual is answerable for what is done in its name. This is why the diagnosis issues in a program: the press must be constrained, since it is the instrument that manufactures the phantom, and where the press is restrained the small face-to-face community in which individuals are known to one another can be rebuilt in its place. On this reading Kierkegaard's complaint is ultimately political and his remedy institutional.

**The rubric I gave the students.**

- 21–25: identifies precisely what sort of entity the public is, explains why the press constitutes rather than describes it, distinguishes it from other collectivities, handles leveling as a diagnosis of reflection rather than a slogan, and derives a defensible upshot.
- 15–20: the main claims are right and the exposition is clear, but at least one distinction is imprecise or one inference is left unsupported.
- 9–14: recognises the key terms but conflates them, or reports the diagnosis without explaining the mechanism.
- 0–8: generalities about media and modernity with little specifically Kierkegaardian content.

**What I need from you.**

1. A mark out of 25, with the band it falls in and a short justification of the band rather than of the number.
2. Point-by-point comments written in the second person, as I would write them in the margin and in the summary at the end of the script. Each comment should be anchored to a specific claim in the answer — quote back the student's own words where that makes the target unambiguous.
3. An explicit list of the claims you judge to be correct and worth credit, including any that are correct in a way that many scripts get wrong; say briefly why each earns credit.
4. An explicit list of the claims you judge to be incorrect, conflated, or not supported by what precedes them. For each, state what the correct position is and where the step goes wrong — as a conceptual matter, not merely as a matter of phrasing. Distinguish errors of substance from errors of exposition.
5. The arithmetic: allocate the deductions across the identified weaknesses so that your mark can be reconstructed, and say which single weakness most damages the script.
6. One or two sentences on what the student would have had to add or retract to move the script into the top band, given the same time limit.

Please do not cite or discuss secondary literature, do not supply Danish terminology unless you are certain of it, and do not reconstruct passages from the text. Where you are unsure whether a claim in the script matches Kierkegaard's wording, grade the conceptual content of the claim on its merits and say that you are doing so. Comments should be specific enough that the student could act on them without another conversation, and neutral in tone: no praise or criticism that is not tied to a particular claim.
