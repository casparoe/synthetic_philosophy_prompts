I am working through John Stuart Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861) on my own, and I have reached Chapter XVIII, "Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State." I want help with a particular stretch of it. To be explicit about what I am not after: I know the standard summary of this chapter, and I know the much-quoted sentence in *On Liberty* about despotism being a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians. I am not looking for either, and I am not looking for secondary literature, a reading list, or names of commentators — I want close reading of the words in front of me, plus whatever you already know of Mill's general framework. Where you think one of my questions rests on a misreading, say so before answering it; and where the passage genuinely does not decide between the readings I propose, say that too rather than forcing a verdict.

Here is the text, from the chapter's opening pages. Mill has just set aside the case of small naval posts like Gibraltar and Aden:

> Outlying territories of some size and population, which are held as dependencies, that is, which are subject, more or less, to acts of sovereign power on the part of the paramount country, without being equally represented (if represented at all) in its Legislature, may be divided into two classes. Some are composed of people of similar civilization to the ruling country, capable of, and ripe for, representative government, such as the British possessions in America and Australia. Others, like India, are still at a great distance from that state.

> England has always felt under a certain degree of obligation to bestow on such of her outlying populations as were of her own blood and language, and on some who were not, representative institutions formed in imitation of her own; but, until the present generation, she has been on the same bad level with other countries as to the amount of self-government which she allowed them to exercise through the representative institutions that she conceded to them. She claimed to be the supreme arbiter even of their purely internal concerns, according to her own, not their ideas of how those concerns could be best regulated. This practice was a natural corollary from the vicious theory of colonial policy–once common to all Europe, and not yet completely relinquished by any other people–which regarded colonies as valuable by affording markets for our commodities that could be kept entirely to ourselves; a privilege we valued so highly that we thought it worth purchasing by allowing to the colonies the same monopoly of our market for their own productions which we claimed for our commodities in theirs. This notable plan for enriching them and ourselves by making each pay enormous sums to the other, dropping the greatest part by the way, has been for some time abandoned. But the bad habit of meddling in the internal government of the colonies did not at once die out when we relinquished the idea of making any profit by it. We continued to torment them, not for any benefit to ourselves, but for that of a section or faction among the colonists; and this persistence in domineering cost us a Canadian rebellion before we had the happy thought of giving it up. England was like an ill brought-up elder brother, who persists in tyrannizing over the younger ones from mere habit, till one of them, by a spirited resistance, though with unequal strength, gives him notice to desist. We were wise enough not to require a second warning. A new era in the colonial policy of nations began with Lord Durham's Report; the imperishable memorial of that nobleman's courage, patriotism, and enlightened liberality, and of the intellect and practical sagacity of its joint authors, Mr. Wakefield and the lamented Charles Buller.

The biographical fact I will supply, since it bears on the questions: Mill spent roughly three and a half decades as a career official in the East India Company's office in London, rising to head it, and in 1858 he argued strenuously against transferring the government of India from the Company to the Crown.

My questions, which I would like answered in order and with each answer anchored to specific words in the quotation:

1. The first sentence defines a dependency in purely juridical terms — subject to sovereign acts, not equally represented — but the division into two classes is made on a civilizational basis. I have been calling these the juridical axis and the developmental axis, and my worry is that they come apart: a people can be ripe and unrepresented, which is precisely Mill's own description of the colonies before the change of policy. Does the two-class distinction govern who may rule, or how they may rule? Is the second class defined by what India is, or by what England may permissibly do there? Tell me whether my two-axis framing is a fair description of the sentence or a distinction I am imposing on it, and if it is imposed, whether the imposition changes what the chapter is doing.

2. Mill writes "capable of, and ripe for, representative government" — two conditions, not one. What does "capable" add to "ripe"? For India he asserts only distance, and says nothing about capacity. Is that omission deliberate — a refusal to assert incapacity — or does he think distance settles the matter so that capacity is beside the point? Relatedly, is "at a great distance from that state" a temporal estimate (far along a road everyone travels) or a typological one (a different kind of polity)? What in the passage decides between these, and what does each imply about how long the arrangement is meant to last?

3. "Enriching them and ourselves by making each pay enormous sums to the other, dropping the greatest part by the way" is a strange economic claim. What exactly is Mill asserting — that mutual monopoly was negative-sum for both parties, with the loss occurring in the apparatus of restriction and enforcement? Is that coherent on its own terms, given that the "enormous sums" evidently did land somewhere, namely with particular merchants, shipowners, and planters? Why does he render what looks like a class benefit as a frictional loss?

4. He then replaces the motive: the meddling continued "not for any benefit to ourselves, but for that of a section or faction among the colonists." What theory of political action does this require — that policy outlives its rationale and runs on habit alone? If Mill condemns the meddling as meddling, does he need it to be self-interested at all, and what work is the abandonment of the profit motive doing: excusing England as corrigible, or indicting her for dominating without even a reason? Note also that the beneficiary is now a colonial faction, which makes England the instrument of a local oligarchy rather than the principal. Does that change who the adversary of a dependency is?

5. The elder-brother simile makes colonial resistance, not moral reasoning, the cause of reform — England is "wise enough not to require a second warning" — yet the next sentence dates the new era to an English inquiry. Which is doing the explanatory work, or are these a sequence in which resistance creates the occasion for advice? What does the simile concede about the epistemic standing of the ruled, namely that the only argument a domineering power reliably hears is force? And given that Mill's considered view on India is that it should be administered by a trained professional corps rather than by the metropolitan public, is that concession one he can afford?

6. Durham receives "courage, patriotism, and enlightened liberality," while Wakefield and Buller receive "intellect and practical sagacity," and Mill calls the latter the report's "joint authors." What is accomplished by distributing the virtues this way and demoting the named author of a famous state paper to the role of moral patron? Is this courtesy, or does it encode a theory of how sound colonial policy actually gets produced — noble sponsorship plus expert drafting — that is continuous with his argument about India? Does the epithet "the lamented" attached to Buller carry any argumentative weight, or is it elegy?

7. Finally, a larger hypothesis I want tested. Suppose the claim is that in this passage Mill is not really arguing about India at all: India enters only as the label for the second class, while all the argumentative labor concerns the first, and the lesson drawn there is that a free state's characteristic failure is not exploitation but a temperamental inability to leave people alone — a failure cured empirically, by being defeated, rather than by reasoning. On that reading, whatever Mill concludes about India would be inferred from the pathology of class one rather than from any property of class two. Is that reading available on the text as quoted, and what does it cost? Or is the two-class structure irreducible, so that anything said about India must be sourced in India's own characteristics?
