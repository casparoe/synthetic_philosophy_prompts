I'm three years into a philosophy of biology PhD and I've watched the same fight happen at least once a semester in our cultural evolution reading group, with nobody converting anybody and the exact same two hours of talk each time. Roughly three camps:

1. The replicator camp. Culture counts as genuinely Darwinian only if there are memes in something like Dawkins' or Dennett's sense — discrete, high-fidelity copied items, so that lineage structure is a real feature of the world and not a bookkeeping convenience. Everything else follows from variation plus differential retention.

2. The dual-inheritance camp (Boyd, Richerson, Henrich and the people who cite them). Discreteness is a red herring. What matters is that transmission is systematically biased and faithful enough at the population level for cumulative adaptation to accumulate. Imperfect, gradient, partly continuous copying is fine because the models deliver the goods anyway.

3. The attractor camp (Sperber, Claidière, and the reconstruction people). Nobody copies anything, strictly speaking. Every transmission is a reconstruction out of memory and inference under cues, so the stability of a cultural item is explained by attractors — the shape of the minds plus the ecology — and not by replication at all. On this view, selection talk is a straitjacket that hides the actual mechanism, and camp 2 is camp 1 in denial.

Plus a fourth group who think all three are telling unfalsifiable just-so stories, since any stable pattern whatsoever can be relabelled as high fitness after the fact.

What I can't figure out is what kind of disagreement this even is, and I want a diagnosis rather than a ruling on who's right.

Some of my own stuff to complicate it. For the last year I've been building a corpus of a few thousand deliberately-passed-on items — recipes, workout routines, folk melodies, a couple of long internet formats with fixed refrains — and instead of scoring each transmission event with one global fidelity number, I estimate edit distance component by component against the most plausible parent. The distributions keep coming out bimodel: inside a single item, some chunks get copied near-verbatim (a measurement, a refrain, a disclaimer, the exact wording of a punchline) while others drift freely (the framing story, the order, the ornamentation). Which chunk sits in which regime varies by item type, and it shifts along a lineage — a drifting chunk seems to freeze once it gets welded to something with a fixed form.

So my working hypothesis, which I want you to try to break: the debate is stalled because all three camps are quantifying over the wrong object. They're asking whether cultural items replicate, but replication is a per-component property with a distribution over it, so each camp is right about a subset and overreaching about the generality, and the dispute dissolves into a research program about which components freeze and why.

Is that actually right, or is it just a peace treaty vague enough that all three sides can absorb it unchanged — which would itself be the diagnosis? Or is the real fault line something else: an equivocation on 'replicator', a fight about what counts as an explanation (formal model versus mechanism), a disagreement about which cases are admissible as data, or a hidden formal question — how much transmission error a cumulative process can soak up before adaptation stops — that a theorem could settle and that would leave the philosophy beside the point?

Tell me what would count as progress given whichever diagnosis you land on: a distinction, a measurement protocol, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing, with reasons. And seperately: could my per-component fidelity data in principle discriminate between camps 2 and 3, or is it compatible with both?
