Write me a dialogue between two economic historians who are argueing about a case I made up. They're both sitting in the same county record office working on the same town, and theyve come to opposite conclusions about it.

Here's the case, and I want you to actually use these details as the substance of the argument rather than swapping in your own examples. Ferrymouth is a mid-size port town. In 1911 a landslide closed the rail pass for most of a season, and the only contractor who could get freight moving before the autumn harvest happened to hold narrow-gauge rolling stock, so the town's freight tramway got built narrow gauge instead of standard. Over the next forty-odd years everything else grew up around that choice: dockside crane spans, warehouse door widths, the turning circles at the two big mills, even the plot boundaries on the eastern industrial estate. In 1953 the tramway company's own engineer filed a report recommending conversion to standard gauge — it's four pages long and mentions road haulage exactly twice — and the board turned it down as too expensive. In 1958 the tramway was ripped out entirely and freight went over to lorries. Meanwhile nearby Kessing made the standard-gauge choice in the same year, under similar pressure, and it too was lorry-dominated by 1957.

Historian A thinks this is a clean case of lock-in: a small, contingent event in 1911 shaped the town's physical layout for half a century and made reversal ruinously expensive, and the 1953 report is evidence that the alternative path was still genuinely open and then got closed. Historian B thinks the outcome was overdetermined: regional road-building programmes, the collapse of the coastal coal trade, and steadily improving lorry technology all point the same way, so Ferrymouth lands on lorries with or without the gauge decision — and Kessing is the proof.

Both of them at full strength, please, no strawmen, and they should be reacting to what the other one just said rather than delivering two parallel speeches. A needs a real account of why the Kessing comparison isn't as clean as B thinks it is, and B needs an actual answer to the 1953 report that isn't just waving it away. Give them names.

Then — this is the bit I care most about — push them into the methodological weeds. What would the record office have to turn up for either of them to concede? Have them fight about how you'd even specify the counterfactual in the first place (change what exactly, and what do you hold fixed?), about whether one neighbouring town can carry the evidential weight B is putting on it, and about whether "the same outcome would have arrived anyway" is a claim you could ever test or just a way of dressing up what did happen as inevitable. It's fine, even good, if they don't settle it, but by the end each of them should have been forced into saying something more precise than they started with.

Try not to lean on the usual stock examples — QWERTY, VHS vs Betamax, the lightbulb — except maybe once, in passing, as something one of them is thoroughly sick of hearing. No footnotes, no reading list, no name-dropping of books; it's a conversation, not a seminar handout. Somewhere around 900–1300 words.
