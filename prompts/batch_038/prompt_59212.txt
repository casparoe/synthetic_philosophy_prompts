I haven't scored it and I'm deliberately not telling you how I'd have scored it — I'd rather your read be independent and I'll compare afterwards. Here's the question as it appeared on the exam:

"Question 2. The Uniform Determination of Death Act provides that 'An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead.' Many commentators insist this is not two competing definitions of death but one definition with two routes to determining it. Explain that reconciliation as a defender of the whole-brain criterion would give it. Then explain one serious objection to the reconciliation as it arises in controlled donation after circulatory death, and say what a defender can and cannot reply. End by marking which parts of your answer you take to be settled and which are live disputes."

And the student's answer, transcribed from the blue book with the spelling cleaned up but nothing else changed:

"Both prongs track the same thing: the irreversible loss of the body's capacity to function as an integrated whole. On the account Bernat gives, death is the loss of the functioning of the organism as a whole, and the brain is the critical organ because its activity is what makes the rest hang together as one organism instead of a collection of organs that happen to be in the same body. If you accept that, the circulatory prong isn't a rival definition at all. Circulation and respiration matter only derivatively — they matter because without them the brain is destroyed within minutes, and because a body whose circulation has stopped will lose integrative unity whether or not anybody is testing its brainstem reflexes. So declaring death at circulatory arrest is a fast, practical route to the same finding the neurological tests reach slowly and expensively. The reflex testing and the apnea test are how you determine that finding directly when the heart is still being driven by a ventilator. (And to be clear the Act is a model statute adopted state by state, not federal law — New Jersey even has a religious exemption letting families refuse a neurological declaration, so 'legally dead' isn't uniform across the country.)

This also shows why persistent vegetative state isn't death. Those patients have lost the capacity for consciousness but their brainstems still work, they breathe unassisted, and their bodies remain integrated. On the whole-brain account they're obviously alive.

DCD is where it gets hard, but I think it shows the reconciliation is basically fine. In controlled DCD the patient has a devastating injury but is not brain dead, support is withdrawn in the OR, and after the heart stops the team waits a few minutes — usually two to five — before declaring death and procuring organs. The objection people raise is that in that window the patient isn't yet dead on the whole-brain account, since the brain takes longer than a few minutes to be destroyed, and the dead donor rule says you may not kill one patient to save others, so DCD violates it. But that objection assumes 'irreversible' has to mean 'impossible to reverse.' What the waiting period establishes is that circulation will not come back on its own — spontaneous autoresuscitation after five minutes of asystole doesn't happen — and the team is not going to restore it, since not restoring it is the whole plan. So the cessation is irreversible in the only sense that matters here, and the reconciliation survives intact.

One thing people forget: Bernat and others point out that a brain-dead body on a ventilator still regulates temperature, fights infection, heals wounds, digests, and there are documented cases of such bodies sustaining a pregnancy for months. That's evidence the body is still an integrated organism, which is exactly what the whole-brain criterion denies. The law went the other way mostly for practical reasons — the 1968 Harvard committee that first proposed treating these patients as dead was explicit that reducing the controversy around obtaining organs for transplantation was one of its aims — so the whole-brain standard has been a rule of convenience from the start.

Settled: that PVS is not death, and that the Act's two prongs are meant as two determinations of one thing. Contested: whether the whole-brain criterion is philosophically defensible, which I don't think it is."

Two things I care about most, and please don't skip either. First, be scrupulous about the epistemic status of your own criticisms. This area has a settled core and a genuinely contested periphery, and I've watched graders penalize a student for stating a contested position too confidently while letting the identical move slide two pages later when they happened to agree with it. So every time you flag something, mark which it is: a claim the field treats as settled, in which case the student is simply mistaken, or a live debate, in which case the only fault is overconfidence rather than error. Where you yourself are unsure which category something falls in, say so instead of sorting it confidently. Don't present your own preferred line on the disputed parts as the consensus view. Second, no references, no reading list, no suggested further reading — I know this literature and I don't want to spend Thursday checking whether a citation you produced actually exists. If you think a view has been attributed to the wrong person, name the view and say who you think actually holds it; if you're not confident about the name, describe the position and say you're not confident, rather than guessing at one.
