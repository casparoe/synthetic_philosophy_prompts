I run an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on suffering and justification, and next week's session is deliberately off-syllabus: instead of the usual pairing of Rowe's fawn with Plantinga's defense, I'm putting Marilyn McCord Adams's account of horrendous evils alongside Ivan's chapters in The Brothers Karamazov — the tavern conversation with Alyosha and, in the background, the Grand Inquisitor. My reason for the substitution is that Ivan's catalogue seems to me designed around exactly the category Adams isolates: not bad experiences added to a life that remains good on the whole, but cases that give prima facie reason to doubt whether the victim's life could be a great good at all — the serf boy torn apart by the general's hunting dogs in front of his mother, the five-year-old shut overnight in the cold privy by her own respectable parents. And Ivan's refusal is not that these evils lack an explanation; he grants that a justification may exist and even grants, at least rhetorically, that God may exist. He says the price is too high. That sounds to me less like the evidential argument from evil than like Adams's complaint that a justification addressed to the whole cosmic order cannot do the work that a sufferer needs done, and I want the session to turn on whether that resemblance is real or a coincidence of vocabulary. A student objected that I'm reading a contemporary analytic apparatus back onto a nineteenth-century novel, which is a fair challenge and one I'd like help meeting.

Could you write the reading? Three things, in this order. First, set out Adams's structural case for why the familiar goods-givers — the free will defense, soul-making, the value of moral growth and genuine responsibility — fail specifically for horrendous evils rather than merely proving insufficient in degree: the incommensurability of the goods on offer with the harm suffered, the point that a good the victim does not partake in cannot compensate her, and the difference between supplying a reason why God permitted something and rendering the victim's own life, taken as a whole with that evil inside it, a great good to her. Second, map Ivan's argument onto that structure and separate what genuinely anticipates Rowe-style reasoning — the move from evils that look pointless to the claim that some are, and the skeptical-theist rejoinder about our epistemic position — from what is distinctively Ivan's and, if anything, shared with Adams rather than with the atheists. Third, and this is the part I most want pressed: Ivan insists that the mother whose child was tormented has no standing to forgive the tormentor, and that a final harmony bought by the victims embracing those who destroyed them is not a harmony he would accept at any price. Adams, meanwhile, wants the defeat of a horrendous evil to come from inside the victim's life and to be something the victim can take up into her own story. Are those the same demand stated twice, so that Adams vindicates Ivan against the traditional theodicist, or are they opposed, so that Adams's requirements are precisely the purchase Ivan refuses to authorize? Take a side, and say what turns on it.

Then tell me where the alignment breaks, and don't soften this part. Zosima and Alyosha are supposed to answer Ivan, and the novel's counterweight is diffuse and largely non-argumentative — everyone answerable for everyone, Grushenka's onion, the kiss that ends both the poem and the tavern scene, the speech to the schoolboys at Ilyusha's stone. If Adams's own answer runs through divine identification with the sufferer and the sufferer's incorporation into a story she can embrace from within, Dostoevsky's Alyosha ought to be closer to Adams than Ivan is. Is he, or does the novel answer on a different axis entirely, transforming the accuser rather than answering the accusation? And which side do you think the novel's form takes, given that Ivan gets the arguments everybody remembers and the rebuttals get gestures: aesthetic accident, or a claim about what theodicy can and cannot do? I'm not asking for a bibliography, page references, or quoted passages from either the novel or Adams — I know you can't consult them and I'd rather have an argument that stays general where the text is uncertain. But because this is going in front of students, please be explicit about your confidence as you go: mark clearly which claims about the novel's details and which attributions to Adams you're sure of and which are reconstructions, and finish by naming the single point in your reading you're least sure of and what would settle it.
