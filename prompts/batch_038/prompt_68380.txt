I'm putting together a talk for a grad reading group and I built this argument as a warm-up, intending to knock it down in five minutes. I've now been staring at it for two days and I can't knock it down.

(1) Dennett's ladder sorts minds by what does the selecting: genes across generations for Darwinian creatures, the animal's own reinforcement history for Skinnerian ones, an internal model of the environment that kills off bad options before the animal commits to them for Popperian ones, and the pool of externally designed mind-tools — words, notches, diagrams, abacuses — for Gregorian ones, the rung named after Richard Gregory.
(2) A selection process is any process in which variants get generated, some get eliminated, and the survivors get retained and passed on.
(3) Genetic inheritance, operant conditioning, the generate-and-test of internal hypotheses, and the generational winnowing of tools and techniques are all selection processes in exactly that sense.
(4) This is not a distortion of Dennett, it's his own thesis: the same substrate-neutral algorithm runs at every level and in every medium, and that's precisely why he thinks Darwinian thinking is a universal solvent rather than a special theory about finches.
(5) So the four rungs differ only in the timescale and the storage medium of one algorithm, and a difference only in timescale and medium is not a difference in kind of mind.
(6) Hence a chemotactic bacterium, a pigeon, a chimp fishing for termites, and someone reading a physics preprint all have the same kind of mind; they differ only in how much design information is parked where.
(7) But that can't be right. The point of the ladder was supposed to be that some creatures can be destroyed by an untested hypothesis and others can't, and that only some creatures inherit competences designed by other creatures rather than by their own genes or their own trial and error.
(8) So the taxonomy is either trivial — it just says selection happens at several scales — or it's false, and neither is a thing you want to conclude in a warm-up exercise.

Premise (4) is the one I'd defend hardest, since giving it up looks like giving up the most interesting part of the whole project.
