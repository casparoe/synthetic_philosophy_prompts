I am a novelist in the last year of a long book, and the section that gives me the most trouble needs an epigraph I cannot borrow. I would rather commission the poem than quote one, and I am asking you to write it. Please take the role of a poet who also happens to have a philosopher's grip on the metaphysics of time travel — someone who has read Lewis on the paradoxes closely enough to know that the case my book is actually about is not the grandfather case at all, and who can carry that knowledge lightly enough never to explain it to the reader.

The book's machinery, so that the poem has something true to answer to. My narrator, at nineteen, receives in the post a packet of forty pages, unsigned, with no return address: what reads like the first draft of a novel. She keeps them, types them out, revises them across two decades, and publishes them under her own name at forty-three to a reputation she never explains. At seventy-one she wraps the original forty pages — untouched, exactly as received — and posts them to the street address she herself lived at in 1974. She always meant to send precisely what she got, and she does. The pages therefore have no author: nobody composed them, though they were certainly read, copied, and mailed. My novel never suggests that anything here goes wrong, or that a second, different version of events was narrowly averted; the history is one and settled. But it devotes a chapter near the end to the narrator's unshakable conviction at seventy-one that she could have burned the packet instead — and it declines to tell the reader whether that conviction is a confusion or a fact about her.

The commission, in six points.

1. Form: a pantoum of exactly four quatrains, sixteen lines. The second and fourth lines of each stanza recur as the first and third of the next, and the final stanza closes the circle in the traditional way — its second line is the poem's third line and its last line is the poem's first line. The pattern is therefore A B C D / B E D F / E G F H / G C H A. Keep to a loose four-beat line; rhyme or don't, as you judge. No title, or at most a short one.

2. The recurrence must do philosophical work rather than decorative work. My reading of the form is this: the lines that come back are the single settled history — the same events, externally ordered, admitting no second draft — while the new lines in each stanza carry the narrator's own experience of those very events, which is the same sequence lived in a different order. A reader who has never met the distinction between personal time and external time should simply feel two orderings braided together; a reader who has met it should be able to point at the braid. Each recurring line must change its sense on return, since the form's whole pleasure is that the words stay put and the meaning moves.

3. The word "can" must appear at least twice, and the two occurrences must bear senses that come apart: once as local ability — she has the matches, the nerve, the free afternoon — and once as compossibility with everything that has in fact happened. If you can manage it, seat at least one of them in a recurring line, so that the same handful of words means one thing on first appearance and the other on return. The poem must not adjudicate between the two senses.

4. This is a bootstrap case, not a consistency case. No slain ancestors, no altered past, no branching, no message that fails to arrive from a future that then never happens. But the poem should let the reader hold both halves of the puzzle's status at once: nothing in the loop contradicts itself, and yet something in the loop is left unexplained.

5. Prohibitions, which I will enforce with a red pencil: no clocks, no hourglasses, no gears, no vehicles, none of the tired furniture of the genre; and do not use the words paradox, loop, timeline, destiny, fate, or eternity. Keep the diction domestic and physical — paper, ink, envelopes, stamps, stairs, hands, a kettle — and the syntax plain enough to be read aloud at the front of a chapter.

6. Please think this through before you write a line. Work out your line inventory in advance: which lines will recur, what each must mean on first appearance and what on return, and where the two senses of "can" can sit without forcing them. Draft at least two versions of the closing stanza and discard one. Do that work privately; I want the finished poem, not the scaffolding, except as itemized below.

Deliver exactly two sections, in this order, under these headings.

**The pantoum** — the finished sixteen-line poem and nothing else.

**Notes on the choices** — 350 to 500 words of prose paragraphs, no lists, written to me as a client who will cut the poem if the notes fail to convince her. Cover: which lines carry personal time and which carry the settled history, and how the final stanza's return differs in effect from the earlier ones; the occurrences of "can", quoting each briefly and naming the sense it bears, together with what would be lost if the two senses were collapsed into one; where the poem registers that the loop is consistent, where it registers that the loop is unexplained, and which single line you consider load-bearing for the second; the strongest form of the objection that you have traded on an ambiguity in "can" rather than on a genuine context-sensitivity, and whether your poem invites or resists that objection; and one recurring line you rejected, with the reason.
