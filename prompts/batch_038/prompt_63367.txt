I'm running a small workshop where two people have been going at eachother for a year over Bertrand's paradox, and I've finally talked them into producing a joint statement instead of another round of emails. One is a Jaynes type: thinks the chord problem is well-posed after all, because the statement of the problem already carries invariance requirements that pin down a unique measure, and because you can check it by actually building the randomizer (his favorite move is a machine tool cutting rods from a stockpile, plus a straw-drawing experiment). The other is a van Fraassen type: thinks that's just the principle of indifference in a symmetry costume, that cube-factory-style cases show indifference hands you incompatible answers depending on whether you parameterize by edge length, face area, or volume, and that priors come out of the agent's own commitments rather than out of the bare statement of a problem.

Write the document they could both put their names to. I want:

1. a numbered list of claims both can honestly sign — including exactly what they agree about the three classic answers to the chord problem, and what they agree a fully specified physical selection mechanism settles vs. leaves open;
2. a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording the owning side would accept for its own view, and each flagged as substantive or at least partly verbal;
3. the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody — and for each, a concrete case or experiment both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

If you think a chunk of the fight is really about what "the problem" is even referring to, say which chunk and why instead of smoothing it over.

And one thing I really want: don't hand me a document that's secretly a victory lap for one camp. So alongside it, tell me which lines you suspect are slanted, which of the two you found easier to write sympathetically and what that says about the drafting, and what argument or consideration would make you redraft a given claim. Where is your characterization of one of these positions most likely to be wrong?
