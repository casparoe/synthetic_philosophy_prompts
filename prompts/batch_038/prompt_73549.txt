**The exam question.**

> (a) State Berkeley's so-called master argument and explain how it is supposed to establish that sensible objects cannot exist unperceived. [8 points]
>
> (b) A critic objects that the argument begs the question against the materialist, on the grounds that it assumes that whatever is conceived is an idea in the mind of the person conceiving it. Explain this objection as clearly as you can, and say whether Berkeley has a reply to it. [10 points]
>
> (c) What role does God play in Berkeley's account of the existence of sensible things when no finite mind perceives them, and what does this tell us about the scope of the conclusion Berkeley's argument is entitled to? [7 points]

**The student's answer.**

Part (a). Berkeley holds that the objects of sense are ideas, and that an idea cannot exist otherwise than in a mind that perceives it. The master argument takes the form of a challenge to the materialist: try to conceive of any sensible object — a tree, a book, a mountain — existing without being perceived by any mind at all. Berkeley thinks this cannot be done, because in the very act of conceiving the object one is conceiving it, and what is conceived is an idea in one's own mind. The materialist is therefore caught in a manifest contradiction: the object she is asked to conceive as unconceived is, in the conceiving of it, conceived. Berkeley takes this to show that the materialist's positive claim — that there is an unthinking substance in which sensible qualities inhere, and which supports them whether or not anyone perceives them — is not merely false but unintelligible, since it purports to describe a state of affairs that cannot coherently be conceived.

Part (b). The objection is that Berkeley assumes what he needs to prove. The materialist is not claiming to conceive of an object, conceived as an idea, existing unperceived; she is claiming to conceive that there is an object which no one perceives. In the first case the property of being conceived attaches to the object itself; in the second it attaches only to the content of a thought about the object. If Berkeley slides from the second formulation to the first, he begs the question, because the materialist denies from the outset that the objects of sense are ideas in a mind. My view is that Berkeley has a partial reply, but not the reply his rhetoric suggests. He cannot simply insist that the materialist's distinction is unavailable, but he can argue that the materialist cannot specify any object of sense except through its sensible qualities, and that the earlier argument from the inseparability of the secondary qualities from the primary has already shown that extension and figure are mind-dependent in the same way colour and sound are. On this reading the master argument is not a freestanding proof; it is the capstone of the earlier arguments, and it has force only for a reader who has already granted that premise. That is a real limitation, since the materialist who is unmoved by the earlier arguments will be unmoved by this one too.

Part (c). God's role is to perceive everything at all times. The tree in the quadrangle does not pop in and out of existence with the comings and goings of undergraduates, because when no finite mind perceives it, God does. This shows what the master argument is really entitled to: since everything that exists depends for its existence on being perceived, and God perceives everything, the whole of reality consists of ideas together with the minds that perceive them. The master argument does further work at this stage as well. Because ideas are inert and cannot be the cause of anything, and because the ideas of sense are markedly more vivid, more regular, and more independent of my will than the ideas of imagination that I produce myself, the master argument shows that the ideas of sense must be produced in us by some other will, and that this will is God's. So the master argument supplies Berkeley with both the refutation of matter and the demonstration of the existence of God, and the two conclusions are inseparable.
