I'm a novelist. The man at the center of my new book is a philosophy professor who has talked himself into superrationality: he cooperates in one-shot dilemmas with total strangers, won't check what sort of person he's facing, tips exactly what he takes people like him to tip (a figure he derives from his own inclination), and tells his dean that people like him are already on that committee. I havent decided yet whether he's a saint or an articulate cheap skate.

Separately I'm doing a forty-minute in-conversation slot at a festival with a decision theorist who defends much the same view, and I dont want to spend the time interviewing my own character. Her habits: she says a superrational pair is one program running on two machines, and when someone presses the causal-independence point she tells them they're "still thinking like a billiard player."

Give me five numbered questions, a sentence or two apiece, nothing before the list and nothing after it. Each item is the question plus the follow-up aimed at the answer she's likeliest to give, and the billiard-player deflection should get harder to reach for as the list goes on. I want to hit: what her advice becomes once she knows her opponent flips a coin or is an ordinary causal decision theorist, and whether that turns the whole thing into a claim about her beliefs rather than a special kind of rationality; how "people like me" gets chosen without being gerrymandered after the fact to cover whatever you already wanted to do; the many-player cases — the Luring Lottery, where readers who had just read about superrationality mailed in googolplexes and wiped out the prize, and the Platonia telegram setup, where twenty people win only if exactly one of them acts and the superrational move is to randomize at one in twenty, which sounds like conceding that identical reasoning should produce different actions; whether what she's defending is evidential decision theory inside a symmetric game with a better name on it; and, last, what could possibly count as evidence against it.

No softballs about what drew her to the field, no history of the idea — I can read that up. And if one of the questions would also settle whether my professor is a fraud, build that into the wording instead of tacking on a note.
