I chair the little committee that's rewriting our animal-use policy this semester, and one sentence has ended up doing nearly all the work in our meetings: Bentham's line that the question isn't whether they can reason or whether they can talk, but whether they can suffer. Since half the committee has never read past the slogan, I've been pasting in the fuller footnote, which runs roughly:

"It may come one day to be recognized, that the number of the legs, the villosity of the skin, or the termination of the os sacrum, are reasons equally insufficient for abandoning a sensitive being to the same fate. What else is it that should trace the insuperable line? Is it the faculty of reason, or, perhaps, the faculty of discourse? But a full-grown horse or dog is beyond comparison a more rational, as well as a more conversable animal, than an infant of a day, or a week, or even a month, old. But suppose the case were otherwise, what would it avail? the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

The trouble is that Marcus and one of my grad students are using the same sentence in opposite directions. Marcus uses it to close down the evidence question: whenever someone points out that the distribution of consciousness across animals is massively underdetermined — insect nociception, bee learning data, the whole mess with crustaceans — he says the question is whether they suffer and it is, so the empirical worry is just a way of stalling. My student uses the identical line the other way: if suffering is the criterion, then the empirical worry IS the entire ballgame, and the slogan gives you no route around it. I don't think both of them can be right, and I can't say which one is wrong without first saying what the sentence is actually claiming.

So: take it apart for me. I want the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, and I mean genuinely distinct — not "it puts the emphasis on suffering," but things like "capacity for affectively valenced experience is sufficient for moral considerability," "rationality and linguistic competence are not necessary conditions for moral considerability," "there is a sharp threshold, not a continuum, at which moral status begins," "the evidence about who suffers is easier to get than the evidence about who reasons," and so on. For each reading, tag it as a platitude, substantive but defensible, contentious, or false, and then tell me which of them would actually license Marcus's dismissal.

Some things I specifically want the menu to address:

- Whose question is it? Is Bentham asking a question about moral status, about what a legal system can administer, or about how we come to know who's conscious — and can one sentence be doing two of those at once without equivocating? If a reading only makes sense as a claim about legislation, flag it, because that changes what the slogan can be used to shut down.
- Which notion of suffering does each reading need? Nociception, valenced experience, some felt-badness that persists over time, or the having-of-interests? Note the same footnote says these animals have none of "those long-protracted anticipations of future misery which we have," so whatever he means by suffer it can't be that — does that squeeze out any of the readings?
- The "insuperable line" bit. Is there a reading on which the line is a real threshold rather than a point on a spectrum, and does any such reading survive the possibility that suffering comes in degrees or in importantly different kinds? If the line is really about drawing administrable categories rather than about where moral standing stops, say so explicitly.
- Whether the sentence is a thesis at all. Maybe it's a purely dialectical move — knock out reason and discourse as candidate criteria by showing they either fail to discriminate or discriminate on features everyone agrees are arbitrary — and carries no positive content whatsoever. If that's right, what's left of it as an argument, and can the hollow version still license ending a discussion?

And sort of seperately: does the truncation matter? Everybody quotes the last sentence and drops "What else is it that should trace the insuperable line?" plus everything before it. Tell me whether any of the readings only works in the fuller context, or only works in the one-sentence popular form.

Format: a numbered menu, one reading per item, each with its verdict tag, and then a couple of paragraphs on which reading Marcus was actually invoking, whether my student has the better of it, and whether there's a reading that's both true and useless for the purpose it's being put to. No bibliography, no citations — I know that literature and I'll fill it in. What I need is the disambiguation clean enough that I can hand it to a committee that is mostly veterinarians and lawyers.
