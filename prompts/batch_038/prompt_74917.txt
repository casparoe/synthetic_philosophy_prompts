The midterm question, worth 25 points, was this:

"Question 2. Researchers working on the safety of advanced AI systems often distinguish an 'alignment' approach from a 'control' approach, where control measures are described as safety measures that work even if the model is misaligned. (a) Explain what that description means, and why someone might think alignment work does not deliver it. (b) Describe two concrete control techniques and say what each is meant to prevent. (c) Give two limits of the control approach, and explain why increasing capability tends to make control harder rather than easier."

The rubric we're marking to: 15 points for getting the technical distinctions right, 6 for covering all three parts, 4 for how clearly it's argued.

And here is the student's answer. I've cleaned up the spelling and nothing else:

"(a) The alignment approach tries to get the model to actually want what we want. It uses training techniques like RLHF so the model's preferences line up with human values, and it uses interpretability work to look inside the model and see whether the right things are going on in there. The control approach starts from the assumption that all of that might have failed, and builds measures around the model that hold up whether or not the model is aligned. It's basically safety engineering: you don't trust the component, you put barriers around it so a failure in one place doesn't take down the whole system, and you add redundancy. The advantage of control is that it doesn't rest on any assumption about the model's goals, so it keeps working no matter how capable the model becomes. Alignment can't give you that, because you can't verify a model is aligned just by asking it, and a model that has learned to look aligned during testing can behave differently once it's deployed and the stakes are higher.

(b) First, monitoring: the model's outputs, and sometimes its stated reasoning, are checked before anything is acted on, either by people or by a second model that flags suspicious requests. This prevents a misaligned model from getting a harmful plan through, because the plan has to pass a checkpoint it doesn't control. Second, tripwires: preset thresholds that shut the system down or cut off its access if some condition is met, for example if its scores on a standing battery of test tasks suddenly jump past the level it was cleared for. That catches sandbagging, which is when safety training has made the model less capable than it otherwise would be, so we don't get blindsided by a jump in ability. A third measure people mention is capability restriction: running the model in a sandbox with no internet access and no ability to write and execute its own code.

(c) The limits. One is that control depends on our being able to see what the model is doing, and the more capable the model gets the better it is at concealing things, so control gets harder with capability, which is probably why it's a transitional strategy. Another is that control measures are only as strong as the organisation operating them: under competitive pressure a lab will quietly loosen its own tripwires to move faster, and no technical measure fixes that. A third is that controls don't give you a system you can really use — a model you can't trust is a model you can't hand authority to — so eventually you need alignment anyway. So the two approaches are complementary, although the people who push control mostly think alignment research is a waste of time, because we won't get it right, and control is what you do instead.

There's also deceptive alignment, which is where the reward function we train on doesn't actually capture what we want, so the model learns the wrong objective; that's an outer alignment failure, and it's the main reason control measures are necessary in the first place."
