I am taking a philosophy of cognitive science seminar this term, and my paper is supposed to assess whether the brain literally computes or is merely usefully *described* as computing. I have been reading accounts of implementation in the wrong order, because I cannot yet say what would make any of them adequate. I would like the adequacy standard first.

Begin with one sentence giving your bottom line: which single requirement in the list does the most to eliminate candidate theories, and whether any subset of the requirements cannot all be satisfied at once. Then give the list and the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of what I asked.

The theory is being hired for a specific job, so grade every requirement against it: it must underwrite implementation claims that a third party could in principle confirm or refute by inspecting a physical system, with no decisive work done by the ascriber's interests, purposes, or interpretive choices. A theory on which any sufficiently state-rich open system implements any finite automaton you like fails this job. A theory on which nothing implements anything unless someone has adopted a description of it fails it too, in the mirror image way. Both failures make my paper's central claim true or false by stipulation, which is exactly what I want to avoid.

Present the requirements as a numbered list. For each one: state the requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it (concrete enough that I can see what a theory is obliged to say about it), tag it as non-negotiable given the job above or as a demand peculiar to a particular camp (formalist, mechanist, semanticist, deflationist, dynamical-systems), and say what flunking it looks like in practice.

At minimum the list should adjudicate all of the following; I would like more besides.

- Triviality and its cure: what has to be added to a state-to-state mapping so that a convective pattern in a star, whose microstate transitions happen to be mappable onto the transition table of some program, does not thereby run that program.
- Genuine pluralism without permissiveness: the same neural tissue is truly describable as reinforcement learning, as approximate Bayesian inference, and as a feedback control loop. If the ascriber's interests may not decide, what does?
- The grain problem: spikes, subthreshold membrane dynamics, nonlinear integration inside single neurons' dendritic trees, intracellular chemistry. What determines which of these is the level at which the system computes, if not explanatory convenience?
- No natural interface: textbook automata come with distinguished inputs and outputs; a brain is in continuous closed-loop exchange with a body and a world. Either show that an interface is dispensable or explain how it gets fixed here.
- Counterfactual support and redundant backups: suppose a machine's fault states would be corrected by an outside technician, but no fault ever occurs, so the technician never acts — and suppose further that the machine would have run the same program with the technician absent all along. Does the backup's presence change what the machine implements? Which counterfactuals belong in implementation conditions, and what grounds them in the machine rather than in the technician?
- Capacity versus activity: the idle laptop, the anesthetized cortex, the sleeping hippocampus. Which sense does the empirical claim about the brain use, and does a theory have to pick?
- Co-implementation and underdetermination: one physical process mappable onto two programs with non-isomorphic transition graphs, or onto two that share every actual trajectory and differ only counterfactually. Must the theory say both are implemented, that one is, or that the question is ill-posed?
- Undesigned programs: the theory must let a system implement a program nobody wrote, anticipated, or could recognize, since evolved circuitry is the paradigm case for me.
- Determinacy under noise: realizers are noisy and analog. What makes a physical process determinately *this* discrete computation rather than a near neighbor, and how much noise an implementation claim can absorb before it fails?
- Sufficiency: whatever the brain is said to compute must be enough to account for the capacities we explain by computing. An account that lets a system implement the right computation while that computation does no explanatory work has not supported my paper's claim, only decorated it.
- Risk: the theory must specify what kind of finding would show that a given system does *not* compute — no stable discrete states, dynamics not decomposable into parts whose states determine transitions, and so on.

Then tell me which of these cannot all be had at once, and which one has to go. I suspect the bind is between the demand that the ascriber do no decisive work and the demand for genuine pluralism, but check that, and say so plainly if the real conflict sits elsewhere — a list that turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable is a result I can use, not a disappointment.

Last, and briefly, score the main contenders against the list: formal mapping accounts, mechanistic accounts on which computing is a functional kind of causal organization, semantic accounts that require representations with content, deflationary views on which computation is a description we impose rather than a feature of the world, and dynamical-systems views that deny the brain computes at all. Two or three sentences each, and name the first requirement each one flunks. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is an afterthought.

Do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list. Attribute positions to camps and arguments, not to papers.
