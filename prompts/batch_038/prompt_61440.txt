I run a three-person analytics team inside a regional health system — me, one MPH who's had maybe a semester of micro, and a data engineer. Roughly once a month somebody from the clinical side or the ops side brings us a proposal that leans on an inference from what patients chose to what patients want, and often one step further, to what would make them better off. "Interpreter uptake is under five percent of eligible visits, so patients obviously don't want it." "Sixty-eight percent of the people offered a telehealth follow-up picked telehealth again next time, so that's their preference." Up to now the most rigorous thing anyone on my team has been able to say is "hmm, not sure that follows," and I'm increasingly worried we're being used to give a quantitative-looking seal of approval to decisions that were already made.

I want you to write us a decision procedure we can actually run on a proposal in about two hours, grounded in what revealed preference theory genuinely licenses — and I mean genuinely licenses, not the pop version where "choice reveals preference" is waved around like it's an empirical discovery rather than partly a way of defining the term. Be explicit that there are two seperate jumps, choice → preference and preference → good-for-the-person, and build the procedure so those two are never collapsed into one step, because I'm pretty sure that's where most of what crosses my desk goes wrong. Include whatever the consistency machinery (the weak/generalized axioms, the rationalizability results in the Afriat line) can and cannot buy you, and be straight with me about the fact that "this data is consistent with utility maximization" is often a near-empty claim — I want the procedure to know when consistency is informative and when it isn't.

Constraints and format:

- Questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what evidence would satisfy it. Not a list of considerations, a sequence.
- Mark the exits clearly: where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with "we cannot tell from this data and here's what data would settle it," and where it hands the thing to the committee because what's left is a values question and not a factual one. The final output gets read by a committee that includes a physician and a patient representative, so the write-up has to be legible to people who will never see a subscript.
- Our data is almost all administrative EHR and scheduling data. No price variation, no meaningful budget sets, almost nothing randomized, occasionally a real A/B test if someone in digital has been feeling ambitious. Most of the textbook tests assume you can vary prices or budgets and watch the choice flip. Tell me what the fallback is when you can't, and how much weaker it is. Don't quietly pretend the fallback is as good.
- We also sometimes have patient-reported experience scores and, for a couple of services, repeated symptom ratings over time. Build in somewhere for the question of when reported experience can override or supplement choice data — and handle the obvious traps, like the fact that what people retrospectively rate an episode as having been like is not the same thing as how the episode went, including the peak-end and duration stuff. If you think those traps are overblown, say so and say why.
- Flag every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedure costume. I'd rather have five honest hand-waving points than twenty fake-precise ones.
- End with a short section on what the procedure could never deliver no matter how good our data got — the ceiling, not the current shortfall. I want to be able to say no to a request on principled grounds instead of just "not with what we have."

Then run the whole procedure, step by step and not just the conclusion, on three live cases:

1. Ops wants to cut the in-person interpreter service to on-demand video only, on the grounds that among patients flagged as limited-English-proficient, fewer than five percent of visits actually used an interpreter even though one was offered and funded.
2. A program director wants to expand telehealth follow-ups for a chronic-condition clinic, citing that 68% of patients who were offered a telehealth slot chose a telehealth slot again at their next follow-up.
3. The one I'm braced for: my own team's flagship metric. We rank clinics by the share of patients who re-book with the same clinician at their next visit, and we've been telling the board for two years that this is a revealed-preference measure of how much patients value continuity. If the procedure kills it, kill it and tell me exactly which step it dies at.

Two pages maximum for the thing the analysts actually use, with as long an appendix as you need explaining why each test is in there and what it's guarding against.
