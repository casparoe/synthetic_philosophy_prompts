I retired three years ago after thirty-six years as a systems physiologist — first modelling solute handling in the kidney, later advising on the control logic of closed-loop infusion devices used in intensive care. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind and of artificial intelligence more seriously than I anticipated, and I have become preoccupied with the frame problem: what began, in McCarthy and Hayes's formulation of 1969, as a technical difficulty about writing down the effects of an action without also writing down the indefinitely many things the action does not change, and which philosophers then recast as a puzzle about how anything ever manages to confine its attention to what is relevant. I have followed the technical side far enough to know that non-monotonic formalisms were developed for the purpose, that a famous shooting scenario exposed their shortcomings, and that by the late 1980s most workers considered the narrow problem largely handled. It is the wider problem I cannot stop thinking about, because I keep finding what appears to be the same thing in my own professional history, and I cannot tell whether I am seeing something real or merely colonizing a respectable name for a mundane fact about models — namely that models leave things out.

What follows are the judgments I have already made. I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or a survey of who said what; I want your own analysis.

(i) Our group designed the control logic for an infusion device running a one-compartment pharmacokinetic model. Given that model, its dosing decisions were excellent. It failed in evaluation because the patient was concurrently receiving a second agent that displaced the first from plasma protein binding, and nothing in the model represented that another infusion might be present at all. My verdict: the frame problem, and a paradigmatic instance of it — though I have read that some would file this under a different heading, unforeseen enabling conditions rather than unrepresented non-effects, and I do not know whether that distinction should matter to me.

(ii) A colleague ran an eddy-resolving ocean model that was, so far as anyone could determine, correctly specified: nothing relevant had been left out. It advanced four simulated days per day of computing time and was therefore operationally useless. My verdict: not the frame problem. A cost, not a framing.

(iii) I once visited a robotics group that maintained an action library in which every action carried a hand-maintained list of the several thousand conditions it did not alter. The system planned correctly. It was merely slow. My verdict: no failure occurred here, and yet I am unwilling to say that they solved the frame problem, and I cannot account for my unwillingness.

(iv) A chess engine misses a tactic lying one ply beyond its search horizon. My verdict: not the frame problem, even though a relevant consequence has gone unrepresented. The bound is known, symmetric across the tree, and the engine's evaluations are calibrated to it; the device in (i) carried no such acknowledgment.

(v) Two of my students built structurally identical models of two different fisheries, each omitting a slow-growing predator as negligible. In one case the predator mattered and the collapse prediction was wrong by nearly an order of magnitude; in the other the omission was harmless and the model remains in service. My verdict: the first modeller's framing failed and the second's did not. I hold both verdicts, although their training, their evidence, and their reasoning were, so far as I could tell, indistinguishable.

(vi) A senior technician, preparing a specimen exactly to protocol, stopped and called me because the sample smelled faintly of ammonia — a consideration appearing nowhere in the protocol, her job description, or any model we owned. My verdict: this is what solving the frame problem looks like. I also want to say she did not reason her way to it; she noticed. I do not know whether noticing belongs inside the principle I am after or outside it.

(vii) A conversational system gives me a competent, well-organized answer about the design of a sampling protocol and omits a consideration any graduate student would have raised; when I mention it, the system integrates it at once and explains why it matters. My verdict: I am inclined to call this the frame problem, but a younger colleague insists it is ordinary ignorance of a fact, no different in kind from not knowing a rate constant. I find his argument better than my instinct and do not know which to abandon.

My tentative principle is that the frame problem arises exactly where a representation carries a completeness commitment it cannot support: where a model implicitly asserts that what it enumerates is all that changes, or all that matters, without acknowledging the bound and without provision for revising it. That saves (i) and (iv) and dissolves (ii), and it makes (vii) turn on whether a fluent system's presentation of an answer carries such a commitment — which may be just why its confident omissions irritate me. But (iii) and (v) break it, since in (iii) the commitment is discharged in full and in (v) the commitment is identical on both sides.

So: state a principle, or a small number of competing principles, precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have never seen without asking you again, saying for each clause which of my verdicts it exists to capture. Tell me which verdicts I must surrender — I expect (iii) and (v) cannot both survive — and what I should then say about the pair. Give your principle's verdict on (vi) and (vii), and on two situations of your own choosing that I have not thought of, preferably ones that discriminate among the candidates you offer. And tell me whether the principle I am groping toward is about representations, about agents, about the practices of a discipline that hands relevance judgments down to its members, or about the structure of the world: these four keep sliding into one another in what I have read, and I suspect I am equivocating.

One final request. I know this is contested ground — that some hold the residual puzzle to be an artefact of an obsolete formalism, and others hold that disposing of the formalism leaves the real difficulty untouched. Please mark clearly, throughout, which of your claims represent settled agreement among people who work on this and which are live disputes, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. If the view you favour is a minority one, say so plainly.
