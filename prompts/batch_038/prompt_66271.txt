Davidson's radical interpreter has no prior knowledge of the speaker's language and no independent access to the speaker's beliefs; her evidence consists in the speaker's attitudes of holding sentences true. (iii) Does the underdetermination of an interpretation by all possible evidence threaten that conclusion, or the programme of radical interpretation more generally? Explain.

**The submission** (complete; roughly 800 words)

The starting point of Davidson's programme is that a theory of meaning for a speaker's language should take the form of a Tarskian truth theory, and that its adequacy should be tested against evidence available to someone who does not yet understand the language at all. That evidence is of a particular kind. The interpreter cannot observe meanings, and she cannot ask the speaker what her sentences mean; what she can observe is which sentences the speaker holds true — roughly, which sentences she is disposed to assent to, or to act on, in which observable circumstances. A candidate truth theory is then confirmed by checking that, for each sentence, it entails that the sentence is true just when the speaker holds it true in those circumstances, and the theory is confirmed or disconfirmed as a whole rather than theorem by theorem. It is worth insisting that this is a reconstruction of what any correct theory of meaning must be able to do, not a practical manual that anyone could follow; Davidson's radical interpreter is a device for displaying the structure of meaning, not a field guide.

The difficulty is that the evidence underdetermines the assignment in a systematic way. To know which sentences a speaker holds true we would already need to know what those sentences mean; to know what they mean we need to know which beliefs the speaker expresses by holding them true. Davidson's answer is a division of labour. The interpreter first fixes the speaker's beliefs by ordinary empirical observation — what she attends to, what she avoids, what she says when the bowl is filled, when the predator appears — in the same way we infer the beliefs of a dog or an infant. With that body of belief in hand, she then chooses, for each sentence, the truth condition that makes the belief expressed by holding it true come out true. This choice is governed by the principle of charity, which instructs the interpreter to select that interpretation on which the greatest number of the speaker's beliefs are true. Charity, on this picture, is a rule of method rather than a claim about the world, and its justification is inductive: creatures who have survived and coordinated successfully are as a matter of fact fairly reliable perceivers of their surroundings, so maximizing truth is the strategy most likely to yield an interpretation that works. In later work Davidson supplements this with the idea of triangulation: content is fixed only where two creatures are reacting to a common object and to each other's reactions to it, which is what stops the causal history of a held-true sentence from being arbitrary. It is also worth noting that Davidson takes a version of Quine's principle of humanity for granted here — we interpret others by imputing to them the pattern of beliefs and desires we ourselves would have in their situation, so that what charity aims at is similarity to the interpreter rather than truth.

The second half of the programme turns this methodological constraint into an epistemological result. Charity as described can only deliver beliefs that are true from the interpreter's point of view, since the standard of truth used in constructing the interpretation is the interpreter's own. Davidson's response to the charge that his anti-skepticism is therefore merely relative is the argument from the possibility of an omniscient interpreter. Suppose a being who knows everything there is to know about the world sets about interpreting a speaker. Such an interpreter has no false beliefs of her own to project, and she cannot assign as the truth condition of a sentence the speaker holds true a condition that does not obtain. So a speaker whom an omniscient interpreter could understand would be right about the world, not merely right by someone's lights. We are interpretable, and so we are in principle interpretable by such a being; hence most of what we believe is true. This is the sense in which Davidson says that coherence yields correspondence: the holistic constraint on a belief system, together with the constraints of interpretation, guarantee the system's fit with the world, and truth turns out to be, as the pragmatists held, what would be believed at the ideal limit of inquiry. What is ruled out is not local error — the argument says nothing about particular beliefs and leaves plenty of room for mistakes — but massive, global error, which is exactly the skeptic's target.

The question is how much of this survives the underdetermination that the programme itself begins from. Even with all possible evidence in hand, more than one truth theory will fit: the same pattern of holding-true can be generated by pairing sentences with different truth conditions, provided beliefs are adjusted in compensation, which is just what the interdependence described above permits. Davidson accepts the Quinean conclusion that there is no fact of the matter which of these theories is correct. It is hard to see how the anti-skeptical result can survive that concession. If which beliefs to attribute is indeterminate, then whether those beliefs are true is indeterminate too, and the omniscient interpreter does not help, because an omniscient interpreter would face the same plurality of empirically equivalent theories and could choose among them only arbitrarily. Underdetermination therefore bites twice. It leaves no determinate content for the claim that most of our beliefs are true; and it vindicates the description of charity given earlier, as a heuristic whose deliverances are artifacts of our method rather than discoveries about speakers. Davidson's argument against conceptual relativism — that a language which could not be interpreted at all would not be a language, so there is no room for an untranslatable scheme — rests on the same apparatus and is exposed in the same way: if interpretation is indeterminate, the notion of a scheme's failing to be interpretable loses whatever determinacy it had.
