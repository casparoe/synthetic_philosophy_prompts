In three weeks I give a departmental seminar — forty-five minutes of talk plus half an hour of discussion — defending a version of the procreation asymmetry that departs from Benatar's axiology. My view, in outline:

(i) It is prohibited to bring into existence a person whose life, on the information available at the time of the decision, carries a substantial probability of falling below a threshold T, where T marks the point at which the person could not reasonably want the life they would be given.

(ii) The prohibition has the force of a restriction rather than of a weighted consideration: it cannot be traded off against creating further people comfortably above T, nor against any aggregate gain elsewhere.

(iii) There is no symmetrical requirement to create people above T, however good their lives would be and however many of them there might be.

(iv) My explanation of the conjunction is that moral complaint runs from a standpoint. I accordingly grant standpoints to merely possible persons for the purpose of grounding restrictions, while refusing to grant them for the purpose of grounding requirements. I further argue that formulating T ex ante — in terms of probability and available information rather than in terms of the life actually lived — is what allows the view to evade the non-identity problem.

The room is mixed, and its two halves will object in different registers. Present are two population ethicists who work on impossibility results and on person-affecting views, and who are deeply suspicious of any asymmetry not derived from a single underlying axiology; a larger contingent working on climate ethics, global health, and the governance of long-run risk, who will want to know what the view implies for fertility in a warming world, for embryo selection, and for the fifth child; a Kantian who holds that the framing already treats a prospective person as a project; and a visiting welfare economist who does cost-benefit analysis and will want to know exactly where T sits, and what my restriction buys that an ordinary constraint on maximizing a social welfare function would not.

Prepare my question-and-answer sheet — a prep sheet, not a defense of the view and not a survey of the literature. I want the eight or nine objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper, each tagged with the part of the room it comes from and phrased in that part of the room's idiom, since the specialists and the applied contingent do not ask questions the same way. For each objection give me: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes; one sentence I should concede before answering; and a candid note on where the reply is soft, rather than where I would like it to be strong. Then tell me which pair of objections is most dangerous in combination, which single objection is fatal if it goes unanswered, and the question I will be hoping nobody asks — together with what I should say if it comes anyway.
