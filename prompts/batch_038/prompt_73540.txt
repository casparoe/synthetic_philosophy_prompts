You are a philosopher of biology who has taught the functions debate repeatedly to mixed rooms of philosophers and working biologists, and who is good at diagnosing where a distinction stops being merely unfamiliar and starts being genuinely hard. Think carefully about the design problem below before you write anything.

**Context.** I am the instructor for a 75-minute weekly section of a second-year philosophy of biology course: twenty-four students, whiteboard only, no slides. Roughly two-thirds have one semester of organismal biology plus one introductory philosophy course; the remaining third are biology majors who are confident they already know what a function is. Three weeks from now they write a short paper in which they must take a trait with a complicated history, say whether it can malfunction, and defend that verdict against at least one rival account of function. This section is where they are supposed to acquire the machinery for that.

**The idea to be taught.** The distinction between etiological (selected-effect) accounts of biological function and causal-role accounts — the Wright/Cummins pair, as refined by proper-function-style views — and, more pointedly, what each account says when one trait has two histories, so that the two accounts stop coinciding. I do not need the philosophy explained to me, and I do not need citations, readings, or attributions of any kind.

Begin with a diagnostic of about two hundred words, before any plan: state precisely what the two accounts disagree about in a two-history case, identify the two or three places where the concept is genuinely hard rather than merely new, and say which features of my case below are actually doing philosophical work. If you judge that a feature is not doing work, say so plainly.

Then give me the teaching plan, addressing each of the following.

**1. Sequence.** The order in which the components must be introduced, with a sentence for each stating what depends on it and what collapses if it arrives later. Take an explicit position on four decisions: whether the case goes on the board before or after the two accounts are named; whether malfunction is the entry point or the payoff; whether type-level and token-level function claims must be separated before the case appears; and whether the standard heart-and-pumping illustration should be deployed and then broken, or never mentioned at all.

**2. The carrying case.** I have constructed the following. It is not a report of a documented species; treat every stipulation as given and reason from it.

> In a genus of plateau frogs, protein P is expressed only in the gut in the lowland relatives, where it acts as a digestive protease. In the plateau lineage, a duplicate copy of the P gene is expressed in the skin's mucous glands, where it binds to nascent ice crystals and arrests their growth, allowing the frogs to survive skin freezing at −4 °C. The uplift and subsequent colonization are recent, on the order of a few million years. The duplicate is still expressed in the gut at reduced levels, and knocking it down measurably impairs digestion, so the old role has not lapsed. The duplicate also sequesters calcium, reducing the calcium available for muscle contraction. Two variants are common in the population: Variant A has lost ice-inhibition entirely but is a better protease than either the duplicate or the ancestral copy; Variant B retains ice-inhibition and has lost protease activity. Neither variant is rare, and the fitness data are noisy — A does badly in the coldest years, B badly in warm wet ones.

Tell me which features are load-bearing for the distinction and which I should cut for time; whether the case is too large for seventy-five minutes; and, in general terms, what a carrying case of this kind must have and must lack. If you think a real, documented case would serve better, state in two sentences the properties it would need — do not name one unless you are confident it is documented.

**3. Misconception map.** Separate what the students walk in with from what my sequence will itself manufacture if run in the wrong order. I anticipate at least these: function identified with present contribution; selected-effect read forward-looking, as usefulness rather than history; teleological smuggling, in which evolution is said to want things; the charge that the whole debate is verbal; and malfunction glossed circularly as failure to do what the trait does. For each, give the point in the plan where it must be headed off and the minimal move that does it — a question, a distinction, an inscription on the board. Then name any misconception I have not listed that this particular case will provoke in the biology majors, and say when it will surface.

**4. Board plan.** What is written down and in what order, what stays visible for the remainder of the hour, and what is erased.

**5. Check.** One question I can pose at the end whose answers discriminate between students who have grasped the distinction and students who have only acquired the labels. Sketch what a label-only answer looks like and what a real one looks like.

**6. Declared omissions.** What I should leave out at this level — middle positions between the two accounts, systems or organizational approaches, the semantic machinery proper-function views are usually built to support, pluralist reconciliations — together with one sentence I can say aloud about each so the students know the map is not exhaustive.

**7. Timing.** A minute-by-minute allocation, and the single thing I should abandon if I am ten minutes behind at the halfway mark.

Write as a plan, not as a lecture: numbered stages, decisions with reasons, and no exposition of the material itself.
