I am revising the opening chapter of a monograph on dispositional properties, and I made the wrong choice about the order of work: I read the candidate analyses before I had settled what I was going to demand of one. My editor wants the chapter to open with a specification rather than a polemic, so before I go back through the field I want the criteria written down. Give me the list of requirements any account of what it is for an object to have a disposition must meet in order to count as adequate, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, so that I can then hold the simple conditional analysis, Lewis's reformed version that builds the retention of an intrinsic basis into the antecedent, the accounts that treat the conditional as a placeholder rather than a reduction, and the view on which every property is dispositional, against the list. A short verdict on each of those families at the end is welcome, but the weight belongs on the criteria; a table is acceptable for the list itself provided every row carries its motivating case.

The list should cover, at minimum, the following — and tell me what else belongs on it. Which interference cases the account has to get right, and get right in the right way: the wire whose disposition to conduct is switched off by an attached device at the moment it is touched and quietly restored afterward; the fragile glass that is packed so that striking it will not shatter it; the venom whose lethal effect is neutralized by an antidote already circulating in the victim's bloodstream; and the mirror-image case, in which an object produces the characteristic manifestation without having the disposition at all because something else brings the manifestation about. Which distinctions it may not collapse: losing a disposition at the moment of the stimulus versus having the manifestation blocked while the disposition remains intact; a single-track disposition versus one with many stimulus-manifestation pairs; the strength or degree of a disposition versus the reliability with which it manifests; a disposition's being had versus its having a categorical basis; a disposition that happens never to manifest versus one that could not. Which phenomena it owes us an explanation of: why citing a disposition seems to explain the behavior, why the ascription survives the total absence of any manifestation, why such ascriptions appear unembarrassed in scientific generalizations and in hazard regulation. Which results from practice it must be consistent with, including one I keep running into in safety engineering, where a substance is classified on the basis of its behavior in a standardized laboratory test under a fixed ignition source and a controlled atmosphere, and that classification is then treated as a property of the substance which travels with it into storage and transport even though the particular warehouse it ends up in may have a suppression system that makes ignition there impossible. I would also like you to take up three cases of my own construction and say whether each needs its own entry on the list or whether the standard taxonomy absorbs it: a masker that operates at a distance, so that the counterfactual about the object comes out false only in virtue of a monitoring and suppression system a kilometer away, with no local or intrinsic difference in the object itself; a masker built permanently into the object's own structure, so that no possible circumstance would produce the manifestation, even though the object is otherwise indistinguishable from a paradigm bearer; and a masker that blocks the manifestation most but not all of the time, so that the object manifests only rarely.

Then do the meta-work, which is what I actually need. Mark every requirement as non-negotiable or as the partisan demand of a particular camp: I suspect that the insistence on reduction, on intrinsicness, and on every disposition's having a categorical base all belong in the second column, and I want to be told where I am wrong. Say which requirements are in force only if the aim is reductive and which bind even for an account that merely wants to systematize dispositional talk without analyzing it away. Flag any subset of the non-negotiables that cannot be jointly satisfied, and if there is such a subset, state it as the main result of the exercise rather than as a caveat — I already suspect that requiring the ideal-conditions clause to be simultaneously complete, non-vacuous, and free of dispositional vocabulary asks for something no account can deliver, and if that is right I want the requirement list itself to be the finding. Finally, tell me which single requirement eliminates the largest number of candidates, so that I know where to concentrate the chapter. Please write in American English, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I have the literature.
