A number of psychology and biomedical journals now run registered-report tracks: an author submits the hypothesis, the planned sample size, and the complete analysis plan — including every exclusion rule and secondary measure — before any data are collected. Reviewers assess that Stage 1 document, and if it is accepted the journal grants an "in-principle acceptance" guaranteeing publication of the eventual paper whatever the results turn out to be, provided the authors carried out the plan and flagged any deviations. These same journals continue to ask for p-values and continue to treat the 0.05 threshold as the mark of a finding.

What theory of evidence does the timing requirement presuppose? Why should the moment at which an analyst settled on a test, rather than the test itself, change what a body of data shows? Reconstruct the implicit account — in particular, what it assumes about facts that are not present in the data set, such as when one would have stopped collecting or how many analyses one was free to run — and identify the rival account of evidence on which those facts are simply irrelevant once the actual data and the actual model are in hand.

Then separate the two halves of the practice: does the publication guarantee rest on the same presuppositions as the pre-registration, or on a different one about what a literature is supposed to be a sample of? Is demanding pre-registered plans while still reporting significance tests internally coherent? And distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with — could a thoroughly Bayesian evidential standard license pre-registration too, and if so, would either half of the institutional design become redundant under it?
