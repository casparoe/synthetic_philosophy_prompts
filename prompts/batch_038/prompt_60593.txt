Our department awards a single prize each year for the most important paper by one of its members, and this year the two shortlisted results turned out to be near-simultaneous and arrived at independently. One member of the committee argues the prize should therefore be split, and his case rests on an analogy he reaches for often: discovery is like mining. The vein is already in the ground and the miner does not make it; Newton and Leibniz struck the same lode of calculus; and what separates them — the better survey, the earlier letter, the claim staked before the other party had finished packing — is luck and logistics rather than contribution. On that picture, crediting one of them is like crediting a prospector for a nugget his rival would have turned up next season. Another member says this is precisely the analogy the literature on multiples should dismantle: there was no vein, and the two calculi were not one and the same discovery at all.

Please audit the analogy as it is being used. Set out the correspondences explicitly — the vein, the miner's tools and training, the survey, staking the claim, assaying the ore, the lode being worked out — and say where each pairing fails. Above all, what assumption does it make about how discoveries are individuated, and about what renders two independent bodies of work one and the same discovery: is that something the mining picture earns, or something it borrows from elsewhere and then uses to settle the question? Separate the disanalogies that bear on the committee's decision from those that are beside the point, and say whether the conclusion — split the prize, because priority tracks luck rather than contribution — survives once they are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it. If some other comparison would bear the weight better, such as a field as fruit that ripens, or independent discoveries as convergent evolution, say what that one would license instead. Please write in British English.
