I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter that puts the classical Indian pramāṇa debates into conversation with the contemporary epistemology of testimony, and I have come to accept a fairly specific Nyāya thesis that I now want stress-tested. The thesis is that verbal testimony (śabda) is an irreducible pramāṇa — a knowledge source in its own right, not reducible to inference — and the argument that has convinced me is the one Navya-Nyāya presses against the Buddhist reduction. Here is my reconstruction of it, in case you want to attack the premises rather than the conclusion. The Buddhists count only two pramāṇas, perception and inference, on the ground that there are only two kinds of knowable object, the particular and the universal, and they treat testimonial knowledge as inference: the hearer takes the utterance as a mark and infers the content, with the speaker's reliability supplying the connection. Nyāya replies that inference requires the reason to be cognized as pervading what is to be established, so the Buddhist owes the hearer an actual cognition of the speaker's trustworthiness functioning as the mark. But there are ordinary cases in which a hearer comes to know what a sentence says while having no cognition whatever of the speaker's reliability — an unfamiliar voice overheard from behind a curtain, a text whose author is unknown, a small child taking in what a caregiver says long before she has any concept of a reliable informant — and in such cases the resulting cognition can still be knowledge: it is true, it is not produced by any detected defect, and it issues in unhesitating successful action. The supporting doctrine is that the speaker's being an āpta is a causal condition of the cognition's amounting to knowledge without needing to be apprehended by the hearer, since pramāṇas are individuated by their distinctive causal operation, and the immediately operative cause in the testimonial case is the hearer's cognition of the word meanings and their syntactic combination, not any cognition of pervasion. I want to know what would count against this position — not a general survey of the debate, but a map of its exposure.

So: tell me what would have to be true, or what would have to be shown, for the thesis to be false, and sort the candidates by how decisive they are. Please work through several distinct lines of attack rather than one. There are the classical ones I half-expect — the Buddhist insistence that everything in my P4 cases is really a mixture of perception of sounds, memory of conventions, and inference; the Mīmāṃsā alternative on which testimony's validity is intrinsic to the cognition and merely presumed until defeated, which may absorb my thesis without needing a separate pramāṇa at all; the worry that the word-meaning conventions themselves are learned by inferring usage from observed behaviour, so that testimony looks inference-dependent at the acquisition stage even if not at the delivery stage. There are also contemporary ones: local reductionism about testimonial warrant, which holds that a hearer is entitled only where she has positive, often monitoring-based reasons; the argument that a global default entitlement is the only non-circular option, which may make my position true but uninteresting; the empirical literature suggesting that hearers, including very young children, spontaneously track informant reliability and selectively withhold trust, which looks like evidence against the empirical premise that reliability-tracking is absent in normal testimonial exchange. And there is the structural worry I find most disturbing: if an unapprehended but truth-conducive causal process suffices for pramāṇa-hood, what stops the taxonomy from exploding — why are memory, recognition, and presumption not pramāṇas on the very same criterion, and is Nyāya's blocking condition (that a pramāṇa must deliver something not already apprehended) principled or ad hoc? A related case I would like assessed is the speaker who is unreliable and says something true by luck: my view seems committed to denying the hearer knowledge there, and I want to know whether that is a cost or a virtue, and what the rival accounts say about the same case. For each candidate defeater, say explicitly whether a committed Naiyāyika would concede that it counts against her — perhaps while insisting it is answerable — or would dismiss it as resting on a rival's question-begging individuation of the pramāṇas, and say why. I also want the discriminating question answered: what does the irreducibility thesis predict or commit me to that a well-formulated reductionist rival does not, so that I can tell whether there is any real difference between the views or only a verbal one. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — the appeal to unapprehended causes and to the immediately operative cause looks like it can defuse any counterexample in advance — and if it has, whether that is a defect for a theory of this kind or entirely appropriate for what is really a claim about how to carve up knowledge sources.
