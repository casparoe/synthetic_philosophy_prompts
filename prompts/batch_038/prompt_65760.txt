I am not a philosopher, and what I need from you is a teaching plan rather than an explanation.

Some background, briefly, because it determines the room I will be standing in. My mother held a seat on the board of our county historical society for twenty-two years. When her dementia diagnosis came in the spring I moved back to take over her house, and somewhere in the confusion the chair appears to have assumed that her obligations transferred to me. They did not, exactly, but I have not corrected anyone, and so in seven weeks I am giving the society's annual autumn session: ninety minutes, a lectern, a whiteboard, a projector that works only from one particular laptop, and a room that holds perhaps sixty people. Three weeks after that, the county commission holds its long-postponed hearing on the future of the old woolen works at Dunmore Falls, which is why the society asked for this session in the first place and why the room will not be a friendly one.

The audience: the society's volunteers, most of them over sixty, two of whom have spent a decade transcribing the mill's payroll ledgers; four county commissioners; a planner's consultant; a representative of the firm that wants to buy the site; and roughly a dozen high-school students fulfilling service hours. Nobody has read Nietzsche. A handful have heard the name and associate it with something alarming.

The idea I want to teach is the three relations to the past that he distinguishes in the second Untimely Meditation of 1874, on the use and disadvantage of history for life — monumental, antiquarian, critical — and specifically the claim that each is a way of *needing* the past rather than a kind of historical assertion, and that each has a characteristic way of going wrong. What I want the room to leave able to do is notice which mode a given statement about the mill is operating in, including their own statements. The hearing is going to be a fight among people who do not realize that they are having different kinds of argument, and that is the whole point of my having an hour and a half.

Here is what I need.

**Sequence and dependency.** Tell me what order the three modes must go in and what each one requires to be in place first. My draft order is monumental, antiquarian, critical, which I suspect is wrong for this room: the volunteers' self-image is antiquarian, and if they hear themselves described in the first ten minutes they will stop listening and the rest of the hour is wasted. Tell me whether anything has to precede the three modes at all — the opening image of the grazing animal, the idea that memory is simultaneously an enabling power and a burden — or whether that is throat-clearing I should cut for time.

**The carrying example.** I have been assuming I need three separate examples, one per mode. I suspect instead that I should use a single site and show it three ways, and the mill is the obvious candidate: the founder's statue on the lawn, the 1840s counting house, the company cemetery behind it, the ledgers in our own archive, the strike of 1913, the state of the river below the outfall. Tell me whether one site can genuinely carry all three modes or whether the distinctive failure of each mode needs its own case. If one site, say what that site must contain and what it must lack. And say whether I should use the mill at all, given that it is the subject of the hearing, or whether I need a neutral warm-up object first and should move to the mill only once the distinction is secure.

**The misconception map.** My guesses, in no order: that the three modes are three political positions, with monument-lovers on one side and demolition people on the other and archivists uncomfortably in the middle; that Nietzsche is telling them the past is bad and that they should stop; that antiquarian history is harmless nostalgia, which is the belief this room most needs disabused of, because its characteristic failure — keeping everything and thereby becoming unable to value anything, a town that has turned itself into a museum — is precisely what these volunteers do; that critical history is simply the honest or the brave one, so that its own failure mode, cutting away the ground you are standing on and judging a past by a present that is nothing but that past's product, will not be visible to them at all; and that for life means something like well-being or mental health. Give me the ones I have missed. Then rank all of them by how much damage they will do in this particular room, and for each say the exact point in the sequence at which it should be headed off — before the mode is introduced, inside it, or afterwards in the cleanup.

**My fourth mode.** This is the part I most want your judgment on, because I have not found it in anything I have read and I cannot tell whether I have located a real gap or simply failed to read closely enough. For two years I have been writing down what people in this county actually say about the past, and a very large group of those statements fits none of the three. They are not seeking models to emulate; they are not preserving an inheritance; they are not sitting in judgment on an ancestor. They are settling a current account. The past is used as evidence that one's own side was right and that the other side has always been wrong, and the speaker's real interest is not in the dead but in the living descendants of the people being scored against. The 1913 strike, in this use, is neither a lesson nor a wound nor a verdict on the founders; it is a receipt. I have been calling it the ledger, and I have come to think it is the commonest use of history in this county, commoner than any of Nietzsche's three. Tell me whether it is a genuine fourth relation, or whether it is one of the three in a pathological state — and if it is pathological, which one, and why that diagnosis is more than a relabeling. Then tell me whether it belongs in a ninety-minute session at all, and if it does, whether it should come at the end as an extension of the scheme or be planted at the beginning as the thing the audience will recognize most readily in themselves and only afterwards be re-diagnosed.

**The check.** One question I can put to the room near the end that would tell me whether the distinction has landed rather than whether people have been agreeable. I want it framed around a statement about the mill that they will not have heard before, and I want you to tell me what the wrong-but-plausible answers will sound like, so that I can tell the difference in real time.

**What to cut, out loud.** I assume the unhistorical and the superhistorical, the diagnosis of an over-saturated age, the notion of plastic power, the Greeks, and all of Nietzsche's quarrels with his own contemporaries have to go, and that I should announce the cuts rather than let people discover a hole in the argument. Tell me which of those, if any, is load-bearing enough that the three modes will not stand without it, and give me the sentence to say when I set the remainder aside.

Do not explain the three modes to me. I have read the essay twice and know roughly what they are; what I lack is any sense of how to hand them to sixty people who did not come to be taught. And do not recommend readings, either for me or for the room — I am not distributing a bibliography, and I would much rather you said nothing about sources than approximate them. If you think my own grasp of the material is shaky somewhere in particular, say so and say where.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the sequence you propose, and single out the one judgment in your plan that you would be least surprised to find was wrong once I am actually standing in that room. I would rather be shown where you are guessing than have the guess smoothed over.
