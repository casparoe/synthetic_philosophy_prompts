The question (self-contained — students were told to assume nothing beyond what's in it):

"Consider this idealization; call it HCH. A human is asked a question Q. Instead of answering directly, they may pose two sub-questions Q1 and Q2 to two copies of themselves, who are stipulated to be perfectly honest and perfectly accurate, receive answers A1 and A2, and then produce an answer to Q on the basis of A1 and A2 alone. Each copy may do the same thing recursively, to unbounded depth. At every leaf an unaided human answers a question directly, with no help at all.

Now consider a different picture, factored cognition: a cognitively hard task is tractable just in case it can be broken into subtasks each of which an unaided human can perform, and the outputs of those subtasks can be recombined into an output for the whole task.

Discuss: (a) Is HCH's answer to Q well-defined — does the setup pick out a unique answer, or a unique best answer, for each Q? (b) Can HCH ever reach an answer that no unaided human could reach, given enough time and paper? (c) What does the relationship between your (a) and your (b) imply about using HCH as the target that an AI's judgments or goals should be aligned to?"

The student's answer:

"(a) Not automatically. Nothing in the setup says which sub-questions get chosen, and different decompositions of the same Q can lead to different final answers — for 'should the city build the bridge?' you could split it into cost and safety, or into who benefits and who pays, and there's no reason those have to converge. So HCH gives a set of candidate answers rather than a function from questions to answers, unless you add a rule for picking decompositions, or you take the best over all of them. That's a genuine gap in the idealization.

(b) No. Every step in the tree is performed by something with exactly unaided-human competence: the leaves are unaided humans by stipulation, and each internal node just reads two short answers and combines them. The tree can be arbitrarily deep and the total number of human-steps can be astronomical, but no individual step ever demands more than ordinary human cognition. So HCH can't answer anything an unaided human couldn't answer given enough time and paper: you could in principle simulate the entire tree yourself, working up from the leaves one node at a time. Depth buys convenience, not a new class of answers.

(c) Given (b), aligning to HCH is safe in the sense that matters. HCH never transcends human judgment, so we're not aiming the system at anything no human could have endorsed. And given (a), HCH is set-valued, so the right target is some member of the set — or the most common one — rather than trying to specify a unique answer. The two issues are independent: stipulating a decomposition rule to fix (a) wouldn't change (b) at all."

Grade it, then write the comments.
