I'm one of five people drafting the statute and the charging policy for a proposed hybrid chamber — two international judges, three domestic ones — for a conflict that's frozen rather than finished. My piece is jurisdiction: what conduct can be charged, and under what label. The file allready has three things the victims' groups most want charged and each one is a legality problem: (1) the blowing of the dam and the poisoning of the river downstream, which our NGOs want charged as ecocide, as a crime in its own right; (2) the decision to invade in the first place — aggression — against the people at the top, except that our chamber would come into existence after the invasion and only one side's leadership can realistically be reached, since the other side's use of force was self-defence; (3) sexual violence by a battalion-level commander under a command-responsibility theory whose edges genuinely weren't settled when he did it.

Two local groups have already said in the press that we're planning to do what they claim was done at Nuremberg and Tokyo: invent the charges afterwards and let the side holding the guns do the trying. I don't want an essay about nullum crimen, I know the slogan. I want a charging procedure a prosecutor on my team can actually run on a proposed count before it goes into an indictment. Constraints: our line prosecutors are domestic and competent but have never done international work; they should be able to get through one count in about two hours; whatever they charge has to survive a jurisdictional motion to dismiss; and the deputy prosecutor is a national of a state that armed one side, so the symmetry question isn't academic here, it's staffing. Also, the current draft has a clause — Article 11 — saying the chamber may apply the law as it stood at the time of the conduct, or as it stands at the time of charging where the conduct was criminal under both. Nobody on the team can tell me whether that's a legality rule or a loophole. Your procedure needs to say how to read it and what it lets you do.

So: the tests to run in order, what a sufficient answer to each looks like, where the procedure stops at a flat no, and where it stops and hands the thing to a human because the question is one of values or politics rather than law. The distinction that has to survive the simplification is the one between charging an old act under a new name and criminalising conduct that wasn't criminal when it happened — the Nuremberg tribunal leaned hard on that for its war-crime counts and I want it operational, not gestured at. I also need the middle case handled: the norm arguably existed in custom at the time, but a battalion commander couldn't reasonably have known it applied to him. And tell me what to do with the move where you say the thing was lawful under domestic law at the time but so grossly unjust that the legality is beside the point — who signs off on that, and how rarely it should ever be used.

Then run it on all three counts, with a verdict on each, and for any count that survives, what the wording of the count itself should say to blunt the ex post facto motion. I expect the aggression one to come back as a no, or as a not-by-this-route, and I'd much rather hear that now than at the motion stage.

Wherever people who actually work in this field disagree — whether custom alone can create a new crime, whether a court the aggressor state never consented to can try aggression, whether the Tokyo dissent was right — say it's a live dispute, and don't hand me your side of it dressed as settled law. Then separate for me which parts of the procedure you've given are real settled practice and which are your own reconstruction; I need to know what I'd be defending as a drafter versus inventing. No reading list, and don't name cases or article numbers unless you're certain of them — if you're not certain, describe the thing instead. An accurate description beats a citation I can't check.

Format, in this order:
- The screen — the two or three questions that decide whether a count is even worth further work. ~150 words.
- The procedure — numbered steps with pass conditions and marked sign-off points. ~700 words.
- Three counts, run — the three above, with verdicts and any count wording. ~700 words.
- Where this procedure lies to you — the steps that look mechanical but aren't, and what it can't settle at all. ~250 words.
- Settled vs contested — ~250 words.
