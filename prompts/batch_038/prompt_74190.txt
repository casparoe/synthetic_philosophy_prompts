A friend and I have just talked ourselves hoarse over Dennett's colour phi argument, and I should like the case against it made properly rather than merely asserted.

The set-up, so that you need not guess at it: a red dot is flashed at one position on a screen and, a short interval later, a green dot is flashed a few degrees away. At suitable separations and intervals subjects report a single spot travelling smoothly between the two positions and changing colour abruptly halfway along the path. The second colour cannot have been registered before the second dot appeared, so the mid-path change seems to call for one of two stories: either the subject really did have an experience as of a stationary dot and the record was rewritten afterwards (Dennett's Orwellian option), or consciousness of the sequence was held in abeyance until enough information had arrived to compose it, so that the rewritten experience was never had at all (the Stalinesque option). Dennett's conclusion is that no observation could separate the two — the rivals agree about every physical event in the brain and about how things would seem to the subject, differing only in whether they call a given stretch of processing pre-experiential or post-experiential — and that this underdetermination shows there is no determinate moment at which an experience occurs.

Argue that the underdetermination claim is false. I want the argument rather than a survey: say what the Orwellian model is thereby committed to there being, namely a window in which the unedited content is still live somewhere in the system, and what could be detected in that window that could not be detected on the Stalinesque story; sketch the kind of procedure that would exploit the difference, and be explicit about which measure does the discriminating; and explain why the obvious rejoinder — that any probe inserted to catch the window simply supplies more material for the same process — does not restore equivalence. Finish by stating what would have to be true of experience for Dennett to be right, that is, for the two accounts genuinely to disagree about nothing at all.

Please write your answer in American English.
