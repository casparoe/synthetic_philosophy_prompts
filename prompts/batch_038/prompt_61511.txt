I'm on the standards panel of a small nonprofit fact-checking outfit — call it the Ledger — that publishes ratings of claims made by politicians, agency heads, and advocacy groups. Our scale has four labels: Accurate, Mostly Accurate, Misleading, and False. Everything hangs off whether the claim was true when we checked it. We explicitly don't grade why the speaker said it or what they had to go on.

Two cases landed on the same week and they're pulling in opposite directions.

Case one: in the first weeks of an outbreak, a state health director told reporters that people without symptoms were probably a significant driver of transmission. The data was thin at the time — she admitted afterwards she was working off two small reports and a hunch from a similiar pathogen. She turned out to be right, and by a wide margin. Under current rules that's a clean Accurate.

Case two: a legislator cited a peer-reviewed study with a large sample, quoted it correctly, and drew the conclusion the authors themselves drew. The paper was withdrawn six weeks later for fabricated data. His claim was false. Under current rules that's False — the same label we give to people who invent numbers out of thin air, sitting right next to it on the page.

Half the panel wants to build the speaker's epistemic position into the ratings: grade what they were in a position to say, not just what happened to be true. The other half says that's unadministrable — we can't interview people's brains, we'd be grading whatever access to evidence they had, and we'd let lucky guessers off the hook while punishing the careful ones. Our main funder has floated a seperate "process" badge that would run alongside the truth rating, and one of my colleagues thinks that's the obvious fix. For what it's worth I lean toward the epistemic side, but I think the administrability objection is close to right, and I'll be sitting next to both camps on Tuesday.

Draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand. I need an actual recommendation on what the rating policy should be — keep four labels, recut them, add a dimension, whatever you argue for — plus the reasons, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Don't give me a survey of what philosophers think the norm of assertion is; I want something a newsroom of six people could actually apply on deadline. Say explicitly how each of the two cases above comes out under your proposal, and whether the health director case comes out differently if the stakes were lower (a city council remark rather than a public health emergency). Two pages max.
