I'm trying to work out whether corruption has a fixed content or whether it's a label applied by whichever side happens to hold the state apparatus, and the American spoils system keeps coming up as the case that settles it for me one way or the other. Could you run it through the definitional debate properly?

The facts I'd want you to work from, so you're not guessing: from Jackson onwards federal office went to party men in return for campaign money and local party work, and rotation in office was defended as a republican virtue rather than apologised for; the reform movement built up through the 1870s and got dismissed by the patronage side as snobbery; a disappointed office seeker shot Garfield in 1881 in the belief he'd be thanked for it; and the civil service legislation that followed, signed by a president who had himself been thrown out of the New York custom house for running it as a patronage operation, put competitive examinations in place for about a tenth of federal jobs, with coverage expandable by later presidents. Postmasterships and custom house posts left outside the classified service stayed political for decades, and the traffic moved into contracting and campaign money. Also worth holding onto: the people who had used patronage to get federal office in the South after the Civil War, Black officeholders above all, were among those the new merit rules shut out.

So. Pick the account of corruption that does the best work on this — the public office one, the public interest one, some dependence or institutional account — and say which it is and why. Then tell me who in that episode was corrupt on your account, and whether anyone was. The patronage men plainly didnt think so, and their self-description has real force: reciprocal obligation, ordinary people able to hold office, a system older and arguably more democratic then the examination-based one that replaced it, and an exam standard that was itself a pretty particular cultural artefact. Does your account let them say that coherently, or does it judge them from the outside — and which of those is the cost?

Then run it back the other way. Does the episode confirm the account, embarrass it, or show it was never designed for cases like this? In particular: would an account that can't tell the spoils system apart from a functioning gift economy be of any use in places where the distinction is genuinely contested, and what does the pendleton episode say about whether these standards travel at all? Say where each account goes quiet rather than forcing it to speak.
