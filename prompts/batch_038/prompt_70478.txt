So a bit of background first, because I want to be upfront that I'm not coming at this from a philosophy department. I tend bar four nights a week at a place off the highway, and about a year ago a regular left a beat-up old book on the counter and never came back for it — turned out to be a translation of Plato's Laws. I never finished college (did a semester of intro stuff at the community college, mostly slept through it), but I've been reading this thing on my phone between shifts, and Book 1 has got me properly stuck. The part that hooked me is the whole business about drinking parties, which I did not expect to find in Plato at all. I got into an argument with a coworker last week about whether "moderation" is something you can actually be trained into a person or whether it's mostly luck and temperament, and I kept thinking this passage is on my side but I can't for the life of me say exactly how.

Here's the bit. It's the free Loeb translation by R.G. Bury that I found online, Plato, Laws, Book I, right around 647e–649a. First he imagines a drug that doesn't exist:

"Well then: in the case of fear does there exist any specific, given by God to men, such that, the more a man likes to drink of it, the more, at every draught, he fancies himself plunged in misfortune and finally, though he be the bravest of men, he arrives at a state of abject terror; whereas, when he has once got relieved of the potion and slept it off, he always becomes his normal self again?" — "What potion of the kind can we mention, Stranger, as existing anywhere?" — "There is none. Supposing, however, that there had been one, would it have been of any service to the lawgiver for promoting courage?"

Then a page or so later, at 649a, he flips it round onto the drug that does exist:

"Be it so, O lawgiver, that for producing fear no such drug apparently has been given to men by God, nor have we devised such ourselves (for quacks I count not of our company); but does there exist a potion for inducing fearlessness and excessive and untimely confidence,—of what shall we say about this?" — "Presumably, he will assert that there is one,—naming wine." — "And is not this exactly the opposite of the potion described just now? For, first, it makes the person who drinks it more jovial than he was before, and the more he imbibes it, the more..."

And earlier, way back at 626e, he's got this line that seems to be the frame for everything: "It is just in this war, my friend, that the victory over self is of all victories the first and best while self-defeat is of all defeats at once the worst and the most shameful." From there he keeps going for another page or two and lands on the drinking party being an actual civic institution, run by a sober supervisor, for producing temperence in the citizens. That's the part I can't get my head around.

Here's where I'm lost. The made-up fear drug looks like it would be useful as a *test*: give a man a cheap, safe, reversible dose of terror and find out whether he's got any guts, no risk to anyone. But wine is the opposite of that drug, by his own account — it produces fearless overconfidence. So my first read was that he's setting wine up as the *bad* one, the thing the lawgiver should be worried about. And then somehow it comes out the other end as a training program. Where exactly does the argument turn? Is the trick that because wine reveals what a man is like when his self-control is stripped away, a supervised dose is both a test *and* a rehearsal? Those feel like two totally different jobs to me — one tells you something true about a person, the other changes the person — and I can't tell whether he's sliding between them on purpose or whether he thinks they're the same thing.

Second thing, and this is my own idea so feel free to tell me it's dumb. I keep picturing two versions of the sober supervisor. In one version he's like a gym spotter: the training only works because somebody else is there to grab the bar when your arms give out, so you can push past the point of failure safely. In the other version he's more like a nurse running a drug trial: small controlled doses, watch the reaction, write down who cracked and who didn't, no intent to improve anybody. Those imply really different pictures of what the drinking party does to a person, and I can't tell which one the text is committed to. Related to this: earlier in Book 1 there's that image of the soul being jerked around by cords or strings of pleasure and pain, with one cord being the pull of judgment that you're supposed to go along with against the others. If wine strengthens the pleasure cords and slackens the judgment cord, how on earth does drinking strengthen the judgment cord? My repair is that you can't learn to haul against a cord you've never felt pull — but is that actually his view, or am I just pasting a modern habituation story onto him?

Third, and this is the part that bugs me from behind the bar. If the payoff is that a man learns what he's like when he loses control, then somebody has to genuinely lose control for the institution to be worth anything. Does that mean his argument *requires* some citizens to get wrecked — that a few people have to come out of the parties worse for it so the rest can be educated? Does he notice that cost, wave it off, or does he think nobody actually gets damaged because the supervisor keeps the doses small? I ask because I've watched the "supervised exposure" theory fail on real people for a decade now, usually the ones who were sure it was working.

A couple of small ones while you're in there: why does he toss in that aside about quacks not being part of the company — is that just a joke, or is he ruling out the type of person who'd actually sell you such a potion? And later in the book he seems to change the story and treat wine as a kind of medicine for the grumpiness of old age, which doesn't sit obviously with the training-program version — is that a different argument or a development of the same one? Also I keep wanting to know whether what he wants out of all this is *knowing your limit* or *raising your limit*, since those seem to be the two things the bar could offer and they don't come as a package.

Please don't send me off to articles or reading lists — I can't look anything up at work and honestly wouldn't. Just work with what's in the passage and tell me what you think he's doing, where I've misread him, and whether the spotter-versus-nurse distinction survives contact with the text.
