For two years I have been the volunteer coordinator of a dream-recall circle: roughly thirty people, none of them academics, who keep audio diaries and send me their recordings each week. A psychologist at a nearby university has offered to take the archive for a study of recall, but she has asked me first to sort the collection, because as it stands it mixes together submissions she considers different in kind. Two of us will be doing the coding, and she has made it a condition that we be able to apply the same written rules to the same recording and reach the same verdict most of the time. That is where I have run into difficulty, and it is not a difficulty about filing.

Some years ago I read an argument, I believe by a philosopher named Malcolm, to the effect that a dream cannot be an experience in the ordinary sense at all: since nobody can check what supposedly went on in the sleeper's head while the sleeper was asleep, and the only evidence anyone ever has is what the person says afterwards, talk of something having been lived through during the night has no content beyond the fact of the later report. I then read a response, I believe by Dennett, which took that argument seriously enough to float the possibility that a dream's content is assembled at the moment of waking — something like a recording that is only played back when the eyes open — so that there was no experience during the sleep to report in the first place. What has stayed with me is that I cannot see what evidence could ever tell the ordinary picture apart from that one. And yet I am being asked to sort thirty people's diaries as though the distinction made a difference.

I am not asking you to settle the question, and I do not want a survey of the debate or any references. I want a triage procedure I can run on each submission.

It should be an ordered list of steps, each with the test to apply and a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer; it should say which submissions get set aside as not codable and why; and it should say where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to the psychologist because the question is about what her study is trying to measure rather than about the facts of the night. Wherever a step is really a judgment call, please label it as one instead of dressing it up as a rule.

The constraints are these. I have about ten minutes per submission and roughly ninety arrive each month. We have no laboratory: six participants own a smartwatch that logs movement and heart rate overnight, two have spent a night with a borrowed home EEG headband, and one has a housemate who sometimes stays awake and takes notes. Recordings range from forty seconds mumbled into a phone at five in the morning to fifteen-minute accounts recorded over breakfast. Several people re-record and revise their accounts, and a few narrate the same dream repeatedly over weeks, each time differently.

Then please run the procedure on four cases. First: a participant who wakes at 5:10 a.m. and speaks for two minutes with unusual fluency and detail, whose smartwatch shows a stretch of stillness and variable heart rate ending seconds before the recording, but who, asked about it three days later, adds a scene she had not mentioned and moves the whole thing from her childhood house to her present one. Second: a participant who reports knowing, inside the dream, that he was dreaming, and who during his night with the headband had agreed with his housemate to move his eyes left and right three times if he reached that state; the housemate's written note records that she saw the movements under his closed lids at the time. Third: a participant who tells me every morning that he must have dreamed, because he wakes exhausted and his jaw aches, but who has never once produced content, and who wants his entries counted all the same. Fourth, and I expect I will not like the result: my own recordings, which I consider the most vivid in the collection and which I make within a minute of opening my eyes, together with those of a participant whose accounts are equally immediate and who believes firmly that his dreams sometimes foresee events — one of which he says matched a news story a week later.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. If applying a strict requirement that something about the sleep itself be checkable would empty the archive, say so plainly; if the procedure has to loosen that requirement, say exactly where it loosens and why the loosening is principled rather than merely convenient. And separate out, for each question the procedure asks, whether it is an empirical question, a conceptual one, or one about the psychologist's purposes.
