Three months ago I joined the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized regional museum, and at our last meeting the chair — a donor with a physics background and a long-standing impatience with connoisseurship — put forward a proposal that has split the committee and that I have been asked to answer in writing. I am not yet able to evaluate it, which is why I am asking.

His argument runs as follows. Lab-grown diamonds are chemically, optically, and structurally identical to mined ones; they are separated from them only by growth traces that instruments, not eyes, can read; and the price gap between the two is the residue of decades of cartel marketing and an advertising campaign everyone has heard of. A perfect forgery of an old master, he says, is exactly the same kind of object: indistinguishable from the original at the level of anything a viewer could perceive, separated from it only by historical traces no one can see, and priced differently solely because of an institutional fixation on pedigree. He therefore proposes that the museum stop funding provenance research and attribution disputes, acquire first-rate period copies and disclosed modern reproductions for the study collection, label them honestly, and redirect the savings to conservation and free admission. As a demonstration he wants to hang a disclosed copy beside the original in next year's show and invite visitors to record which they prefer.

I find this rhetorically irresistible and intellectually suspect, and I would like an audit of the diamond analogy before I commit anything to paper.

(a) Set out the correspondence explicitly. What in the museum case plays the role of the crystal lattice, the carat weight, the grading certificate, the mine, the growth process, the cartel, the engagement-ring convention, and the resale market?

(b) Separate the disanalogies that bear on his proposal from the ones that are merely decorative. Four candidates occur to me, and I want to know which are load-bearing: that a disclosed lab-grown stone still sells for a meaningful fraction of what a comparable mined stone sells for — enough to support an industry — whereas a disclosed forgery appears to collapse to a fraction of the original too small to support one; that a diamond's scarcity is manufactured and a dead painter's output is not; that the copy he proposes would have been produced under a false claim about its origin, whereas a licensed generic is produced under a true one; and that identifying a lab-grown stone is itself a reading of its formation history, so even the diamonds are not identical at the level the trade cares about.

(c) Identify what the analogy quietly assumes that the target case lacks — in particular, any assumption that the value of an object is a function of properties it carries around with it rather than of the event that produced it.

(d) Give me a verdict on the funding proposal rather than a meditation. Is provenance and attribution work doing something that perceptual equivalence cannot replace, or is the museum in fact paying a pedigree premium of the same kind the gem market pays?

(e) If the diamond is the wrong source, propose a better one, and say plainly what the replacement licenses — I expect the chair to observe that any substitute will also break somewhere, and I want to be able to answer that.

One complication I should flag. Several works under discussion are not modern fabrications but seventeenth-century workshop replicas, made by pupils under a master's supervision and sometimes finished by the master's own hand, which entered European collections two centuries ago under the master's name. Tell me whether the analogy is meant to cover those too, or whether they belong to a third category that neither original nor forgery captures — and what that does to his argument either way.
