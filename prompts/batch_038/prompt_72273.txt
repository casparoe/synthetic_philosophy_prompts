I work in macroeconomics: I build, estimate, and defend medium-scale general equilibrium models, and my professional life is spent in the space between household-level behavior and aggregate time series. I am fluent in that vocabulary — aggregation and its failures, representative agents versus heterogeneous-agent models, structural versus reduced-form parameters, the Lucas critique, identification, steady states and linearization around them, policy invariance — and I have no real training in philosophy of science, though I know the standard labels and have read enough around the edges to suspect that a certain philosophical dispute is my own methodological quarrel with the names changed.

The task is to test that suspicion by translation. Take the four positions below and restate each of them, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use:

1. The classical account of theory reduction as derivability (Nagel): the higher-level theory is recovered from the lower-level one by way of bridge laws linking the two vocabularies, and reduction succeeds when the derivation goes through.
2. The multiple-realizability argument for the autonomy of the higher-level sciences (Fodor): the same higher-level property is realized by indefinitely many distinct lower-level configurations, so no manageable bridge law exists, and the higher-level generalizations earn their keep regardless.
3. The causal-exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism (Kim): if the physical level is causally closed and every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, then a higher-level property either is that lower-level cause or does no causal work at all.
4. The asymptotic or singular-limit conception of emergence (Batterman): certain macroscopic regularities are not obtainable from the microscopic theory at any finite order, arise only in a limiting regime, and are conspicuously insensitive to microscopic details across systems that share the limit.

For each of the four, keep three things strictly separate: what translates cleanly into my modeling practice, with the corresponding economic notion named; what translates only if you let either the philosophy or the economics bend out of shape to make the fit; and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary, where the philosopher is doing something my language cannot do.

Then give me the residue report and take a position on it. In particular:

- Use the Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu result as your first test case. Aggregate excess demand inherits only continuity, homogeneity of degree zero, and Walras's law from individual optimization, so the aggregate behavior of an economy of utility maximizers is nearly unrestricted. Is that a genuine economic instance of multiple realizability, or does it fail to be one because the aggregate still supervenes on, and is fixed by, the micro parameters together with the distribution of endowments? Make the difference between those two readings precise, and tell me whether supervenience is doing any work here that "determined by the deep parameters" is not already doing.
- Use the representative agent as your second test case. There is a well-known construction in which the representative agent's preferences conflict with those of every individual household in the economy, and a related literature showing that structural parameters recovered from aggregate data need not match the parameters of the underlying heterogeneous economy. Does the representative-agent model count as a reduction of the macro to the micro, as a re-description that merely dresses aggregate behavior in micro clothing, or as something else? Whichever of the four positions you invoke to classify it, say what that classification predicts about my practice that I could actually check.
- Address a worry I have, which may simply be a confusion. We can almost never identify the deep micro parameters from aggregate data, and we routinely linearize about a steady state and treat the linearized system as if it carried the modal behavior of the whole economy. Both look like epistemic or approximative failures rather than metaphysical ones. Does the dispute contain resources for separating "we cannot derive it" from "it is not derivable"? If so, which position supplies them, and what does holding onto that distinction cost me in my day job?
- Say whether Wimsatt's criterion — a system is decomposable exactly when the behavior of the whole is invariant under redistribution and rearrangement of its parts — is anything more than a formal restatement of what an economist already means when aggregation succeeds.

Finally, your verdict on the residue. Where the translation fails, and I expect it to fail hardest on downward causation, on the claim that levels are real rather than bookkeeping, and on whatever is distinctive about the singular-limit picture, tell me whether that failure marks a defect in my vocabulary, something my modeling language cannot represent but ought to be able to, or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it looked, so that its untranslatability is a symptom rather than a loss. Do not settle this by sorting items into two columns without arguing for the sorting.

Two rules of conduct. First, no smuggling: if you make a philosophical position look cleaner or more defensible by importing an assumption from my framework — optimization, equilibrium, rationality, the working presumption that what cannot be identified does not exist — flag the import at the point where it occurs and say what the position looks like without it. Second, no bibliography and no reading list; I want the argument, and I would rather you reason from what you know than point me at sources I cannot check.
