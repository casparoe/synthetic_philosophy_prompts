I sit on the decision-support committee of a mid-sized regional health system. Last quarter we piloted an internal prediction market in which employees could buy and sell shares paying out if a new outpatient clinic opened by its announced date. It ran six weeks, closed at 71 cents, and our project director now wants us to adopt that price as the system's official probability estimate in next month's stage-gate review, and to do the same for future capital projects.

Three members have objections I want engaged rather than waved away. First, nearly everyone who traded works here and reads the same weekly status reports; if they share a common prior and all know one another to be rational, they should not be able to agree to disagree, and the no-trade results go further still, suggesting that purely speculative trade among such people should not occur at all — yet volume was heavy. Second, two accounts generated roughly sixty percent of it, one belonging to the director himself, whose bonus turns on hitting the date. Third, once a price becomes our official estimate it stops being a measurement and starts being an incentive.

A fourth member replies that heavy volume is precisely what makes the price informative, and that if prices already reflect everything worth knowing, no one will have any reason to pay to find things out.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, recommending adopt, decline, or adopt with conditions, with reasons the director can actually answer. Use these sections in this order: **Recommendation** (three sentences, including the number we should enter in the stage-gate model if not 0.71); **What the volume means** (about 200 words, weighing the competing readings of the same data); **The case for deferring to the price** (about 150 words); **The case against** (about 150 words); **Conditions** (numbered, one sentence each, only if we adopt); **What would change our minds** (about 80 words).
