For a school project I've been reading about low-impact AI — the idea that instead of trying to tell a really capable AI everything we value, you give it a goal plus a penalty for side effects, so it gets the thing done without rearranging the rest of the world on the way. The technical versions I've seen compare the world it ends up in against a baseline where it did nothing at all, then penalize it either for how much less of the world it can still reach afterwards, or for how much worse it would now do on a bunch of other goals it could have had. Known problems: the do-nothing baseline seems arbitrary since the world changes anyway, the penalty can make an agent refuse to do big good things (curing a disease changes an enormous amount), and sometimes it can dodge the penalty just by undoing its mess afterwards.

Hobbes died in 1679 so obviously he never saw any of this, but I think he'd have a lot to say. What would he have made of it? Specifically: would he think you can pin down what counts as a side effect without a full account of the good, or would he say the question only gets a determinate answer once some sovereign has authorized what counts as harm? And would he think the penalty design is the right shape at all — rules backed by punishments rather than trying to make the agent good — or a confused one?

Take a side and argue it from things he actually held (authorization, his denial that there's a highest good, the Leviathan as an artificial person). Don't hedge by giving me both readings and letting me choose; I want your best single answer with reasons, and a short note wherever you're extrapolating rather than following him.
