Write a piece of deadpan bureaucratic satire in the form of an annex to the spending-appraisal guidance manual of a fictional British government body, which has formally adopted the maximisation of expected value — with no cap on the payoff function and no weighting of probabilities — as the sole test for approving expenditure.

Invent the department, the manual, the annex number and the Accounting Officer who signs it off; use no real institution, real document or real person. A body with a mundane remit will work better than a grand one, since the mismatch between, say, regional drainage or school meals and payoffs measured in googols of future lives does some of the comic work for you.

Format the piece as genuine-looking guidance prose: a short preamble, numbered paragraphs under dry sub-headings, one worked example with a small table showing the arithmetic, and two or three footnotes of the kind that clarify nothing. Around 800 words. The register should be uniformly flat and courteous, so that the most extreme recommendations arrive in exactly the same voice as a note about which template to use.

The humour must come from the decision theory being correct and then being followed without flinching. Please include the following beats:

1. A paragraph explaining why the Department declines to bound the utility function — the argument being that any saturating payoff function will systematically fail to rank a large enough prize above a small enough probability, and is therefore "unambitious" — and treating risk-neutrality as a matter of policy compliance rather than conviction.
2. A paragraph on discounting: the social time-preference rate is set to zero for all outcomes, on stated grounds, and the consequence is worked through honestly.
3. A worked example comparing two competing bids, one a certain and modest benefit to identifiable living people, the other a programme with a negligible probability of an astronomical payoff. Show the multiplication, let the certain bid lose, and record the result in the tone of a spreadsheet note.
4. A paragraph on any bid whose expected value is infinite: automatic approval regardless of cost, followed by the difficulty that two such bids cannot be ranked against one another, and the Department's tie-breaking rule, which should be purely procedural and faintly absurd.
5. A project whose payoff series converges only conditionally, so that its expected value depends on the order in which the outcomes are listed. Two Directorates rank it differently because one lists outcomes by magnitude and the other by date of occurrence; the annex's remedy is to require bidders to declare a "canonical ordering" in their submission.
6. A paragraph anticipating the objection that the guidance is fanatical — that it obliges the sacrifice of certain, modest goods for negligible chances of vast ones, and that it would refuse a trivial payment to avert a certain catastrophe — which concedes the characterisation in a single sentence and then passes without transition to a procedural matter.
7. A short appendix recording a dissenting note from an unnamed analyst, together with the Accounting Officer's one-line response. The dissent should raise the real objections — bounded utility, probability weighting that is non-linear in risk, the absence of any evidential basis for probabilities of that order, and the point that an appraisal which can always be answered by positing a larger payoff disciplines nothing at all — and should be treated with complete administrative courtesy.

The piece must remain legible to a reader with no philosophy: the civil-service comedy should carry it even where the technical content does not. It must also be internally consistent and free of technical error, so that a decision theorist recognises that every absurdity is entailed rather than invented. Do not wink, do not append a line explaining the joke, and do not cite or name any real papers, authors, theories or publications.
