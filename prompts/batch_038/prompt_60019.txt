I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine whose readers are scientifically literate but not specialists, and I am reporting a piece on a claim I find hard to make vivid: that the most methodologically rigorous philanthropic funders can allocate worse than less rigorous ones, not because of any lapse in rigor but because of it. I want to open the piece with a designed case rather than with an argument, and I need that case built to specification.

The scenario: a large health-focused grantmaker that allocates by comparing cost per unit of outcome across candidate programs, and that is unusually good at doing so. Engineer the case so the funder's decisions get worse as its methodology gets better.

Constraints:

1. The funder is competent, honest, and acting on advice a good analyst would give: estimate cost-effectiveness, run sensitivity analyses to identify which inputs actually move the ranking, then direct measurement effort at exactly those inputs. No corruption, no laziness, no hidden spreadsheet. The deterioration must be a predictable consequence of that practice.
2. The mechanism is the measurability tilt. Because measurement effort follows estimated leverage, and leverage is itself estimated in the terms of the metric the funder already uses, the loop is self-reinforcing: programs legible in those terms acquire progressively better estimates and progressively more money, and confidence in the ranking rises for that reason alone.
3. The divergence between the metric and the goal must be legible from inside the case. A reader should be able to see the ranking move further than the evidence warrants using only documents the funder itself produces — its own program reports, its own revised estimates — not a catastrophe known only to me as narrator.
4. Realistic and institutional: a grants committee, three or four programs of the sort real funders actually consider, including one whose benefits are diffuse and slow. No science-fictional devices, no perfect information, no stipulated disaster. Describe the programs functionally; do not name real charities or people.
5. Keep it inside present-day global health. No future people, no existential or astronomical stakes.
6. One paragraph for the scenario. At most three numbers, and arithmetic a reader can follow mentally.

After the scenario, show that it meets the specification. State what the funder's method delivers at each of two or three rounds of methodological improvement; what a critic of measurability bias predicts at each round; and what a defender of rigorous cost-effectiveness analysis says at each step — including the standard appeals to sensitivity analysis, discounting for uncertainty, and tolerating a high failure rate on individual grants in exchange for occasional large successes — and why none of these replies repairs the case. Then tell me which single feature is doing the work, what happens to the verdict if I delete it, and the strongest line available to a sceptical reader who says I have described bad practice rather than the method. If the case only works because I have assumed the metric diverges from the goal rather than shown that divergence arising from the measurement regime itself, say so plainly; I would rather learn that before publication than after.

Begin with the scenario itself. No preface, no summary of the assignment, no restatement of what I have asked for.
