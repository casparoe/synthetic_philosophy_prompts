Something keeps turning up in the papers my intro students write on Glaucon, and I still don't have a word for it. I'm after the carving here, not just a label.

The Gyges setup assumes the shepherd knows he's getting away with it: invisibility, no witnesses, no reputational cost, and the question is what he does once he's certain nobody will ever find out. But my students keep writing about cases where nobody arranges to get away with anything — they arrange not to find out, and the arrangement is what does the work. Two of the better ones from last term: a manager who routes the compliance reports to a standing committee that hasn't met in four years, so the data exists, is technically available to everyone, and is read by no one; and a design team that splits a system so each component looks fine in isolation, with the harm showing up only at the seam between them, where no single person ever sits.

In both, the ignorance is built into the structure rather than being a failure of anybody's curiosity, and it isn't a matter of there being too many hands to pin blame on — often there's exactly one person who set it up, and they may be perfectly sincere about not knowing.

So is there an established concept for this? I know the neighborhood: willful blindness in the law, agnotology, the problem of many hands, and those experiments where people pay not to learn whether their choice hurts someone. But they all seem to live either at the level of individual psychology or at the level of diffuse responsibility, and this is a third thing, seperate from both. Which of them is nearest, how does what I've described differ from each, and if none of them is it, can you coin a term, define it, and give me the boundary cases? Does it still apply, say, when the informational arrangement is a foreseeable side effect rather than the point?
