I built this for a stats-and-ethics seminar, it seemed airtight going down, and now I can't find the hole in it and I also can't accept where it ends up. Numbered so you can point at things.

(1) "The world is getting more violent" can't just mean more violent events, becuase event counts rise whenever populations rise, and how many people there happen to be has nothing to do with violence. So the only non-trivial thing the claim can mean is that each person's risk of being victimized is going up.

(2) Same going the other way: the world is getting less violent iff per-person rates are falling.

(3) On every event-based rate anyone actually tracks — homicide, battle deaths, deaths in massacres and in deliberately inflicted famine — the rate now is way below 1950, way below 1900, way below medieval Europe.

(4) And a world rate can fall even when the rate rises in almost every country, so long as it falls in the most populous ones and their share of humanity grows. Fine: (2) doesn't care, such a world is still getting less violent.

(5) So the world is getting less violent, and not by a little.

(6) But (2) is neutral about which harm you plug in, so run it on anything. A country quintuples its population over a century and its prison population triples. Per-capita imprisonment fell, so by (2) it got less carceral — even though more people are in cages than at any point in its history and not one of them got out.

(7) Same with torture, starvation, displacement, and, on a structural definition, with exploitation and with the deaths a society simply stops bothering to prevent. Enough population growth converts any amount of extra suffering into moral progress.

(8) Worse, (2) makes the verdict controllable by the denominator. A government that wants to count as less violent doesn't have to stop killing anyone; it can subsidize births or annex a populous peaceful region. Quintuple the population with homicides flat and you've cut violence by four fifths, which on (2) is a bigger achievement than the smaller state abolishing homicide outright.

(9) None of that can be right, so (2) is false.

(10) Except (1) is what got me to (2), and (1) still looks flatly obvious to me: absolute counts are hostage to demographics, and that's the whole reason to want a rate in the first place.

So either (1) doesn't support (2) and I can't see why not, or (2) is right and (9) is a prejudice I should drop. Tell me which. I want the exact step where it breaks, or an explicit "it doesn't break, here's what you're committed to"; why that step looked harmless when I wrote it down; and prices — what giving up (1) costs, given that the point of (1) was to keep demographics out of the verdict, and what giving up (9) costs. And if there's some repaired version of (2) that survives (6)-(8), say what it turns out "the world is getting less violent" actually means on the repair, because I suspect it's not what people think they're claiming when they say it.

Two more things. Is the definitional problem in (7) — whether structural stuff counts as violence at all — the same problem as the counting problem in (6) and (8), or a separate one that would survive even if I fixed the counting? And someone in my group says (3) is just false for the last several years so the whole thing is moot, which feels like a dodge but I can't articulate why.

For the record I'd rather keep (1) than (5). I know that's a preference and not an argument.
