You are a moral philosopher who works on collective harm, thresholds, and expected-value reasoning in ethics, and who is equally at home with the conceptual arguments and with the empirical questions about supply chains and voting behavior that those arguments depend on. Please think carefully before answering; I would rather have a considered audit than a quick one.

I read an essay in a general-interest magazine arguing that individual consumption choices are morally irrelevant and that only political action counts. It is well written, and its author plainly knows the literature, but something about it has bothered me since I finished it and I cannot locate the problem. I should say where I stand: I want to keep believing that what I eat and whether I fly matter, and so I cannot fully trust my own reaction — it may be motivated reasoning rather than philosophy. Here is the central argument as I have written it out:

> A chicken processor ships in batches of tens of thousands of birds, and a supermarket restocks against those batches; whether you buy one more breast or one fewer, the order does not change, so no extra bird is raised and none is spared. The structure is the same for driving and flying. One tank of gas makes no measurable difference to the climate system, so no individual emission ever makes anyone worse off. Economists have a name for this situation: it is an externality, a harm that is real but diffuse, and therefore not attributable to any particular actor.
>
> Defenders of individual duties know all this. They concede that your act causes no harm and then insist that it is wrong anyway, or they retreat to a probabilistic reply: your purchase may have only a one-in-ten-thousand chance of tipping the store into an extra order, but a small probability times a large harm still adds up, so you bear a small expected harm. That retreat does not work. Expected value describes what a rational agent should believe beforehand; it is not a causal force in the world, and afterwards there is exactly one history, in which your purchase either triggered the order or it did not. Expected harm is a bookkeeping device, so the probabilistic reply relocates the problem instead of solving it.
>
> What is left is political action — voting, organizing, changing the rules — because that is where the levers actually are. Admittedly, no single vote has ever decided a national election. But that only sharpens the point: the collective is the only unit of moral analysis worth taking seriously, and the individual disappears into it.
