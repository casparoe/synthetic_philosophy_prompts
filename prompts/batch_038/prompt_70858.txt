Our social choice study group built an arguement that lands somewhere none of us want to be, and I can't find the leak.

(1) Gibbard–Satterthwaite: any method of picking one winner out of three or more candidates from the voters' rankings, where everyone is free to rank however they like, is either dictatorial — one particular voter's top choice always wins — or manipulable, meaning that at some profile somebody gets a winner they like better by lying on their ballot.

(2) Random ballot fits that description: three or more candidates, unrestricted rankings, exactly one winner, and every candidate can win. And it isn't dictatorial, since no fixed voter's ballot settles anything — we just draw a name out of a hat and elect that person's favorite.

(3) So by (1), random ballot is manipulable.

(4) But its not. My ballot changes anything only if my name is the one drawn, and if it is, my top choice wins whatever everyone else wrote. So lying can never leave me better off. Random ballot is strategy-proof — maybe the most strategy-proof method anyone has ever written down.

(5) (3) and (4) can't both hold, so at least one of the premises above is false.

(6) The group's takeaway is that "dictator" and "manipulable" are terms of art that have floated free of anything we actually care about, so the theorem is true and pointless. That's the step I won't sign, but I can't say why.

I'd rather give up (1) or (2) than (4), since (4) feels like arithmetic to me. So which one goes, and what do I pay for each option? Don't stop at the diagnosis, though — if random ballot really does slip through the theorem, tell me what the theorem is still forbidding, because my prof keeps insisting the bite lands exactly where I think it doesn't. Also, one guy in the group said random ballot is just a dictatorship with extra steps, a fresh dictator every election, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a joke or the answer.
