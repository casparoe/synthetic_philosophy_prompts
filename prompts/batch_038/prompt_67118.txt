There's this argument about memetics that's been running for decades and I've now managed to get personally tangled up in it — I co-teach a seminar with an anthropologist, we ended up on opposite sides, and a year of trading arguments hasn't budged either of us. So I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who's right, and definitely not a survey of the field. No reading lists or citations, I can't use them.

Her side, roughly: cultural transmission is way too low-fidelity for anything in it to count as a replicator. There's no discrete unit that gets copied, no principled way to individuate a meme from a variant of itself, and what actually persists across generations is better described as a set of attractors — the shapes representations keep getting pulled back toward by human cognition and by whatever biases learners bring to adoption. Selection is real, replication isn't, so the gene analogy is a category error in a lab coat.

My side, roughly: the fuzziness complaint proves too much. Genes recombine, there are transposons, and 'gene' gets individuated differently depending on whether you mean the developmental or the evolutionary sense — nobody concludes from any of that that selection isn't a replicator-level process. Ideas get copied, they vary, and the variants get retained at different rates, which is the whole formal content of the Darwinian scheme. Insisting on crisp token identity before you'll permit the word 'replication' is its just moving the goalposts.

We've chewed through the same examples over and over — urban legends, folk melodies, religious rituals, the layout of the QWERTY keyboard — and every single time the example comes out looking like support for whichever side you already held. That's the part that makes me suspect we aren't really disagreeing about the examples at all.

My hypothesis about where the fault line actually is, and I want you to attack it rather than agree with it: the fight is over an unstated requirement that cultural transmission be causal-historical. Her camp, I think, needs something stronger than the fact that learner output reliably tracks teacher input plus differential retention — it needs the resemblance to be explained *by the copying event itself*, so there's a token-to-token chain you could in principle trace, a lineage. My camp is happy with tracking. If that's the real split, then most of the standard moves on both sides (fidelity percentages, whether a tune is one meme or a family of them) are downstream noise, and the thing genuinely worth arguing about is whether tracking-without-lineage can bear the explanatory weight memetics wants to put on it.

So: is that right? Tell me what kind of disagreement this is — substantive, partly verbal about 'replication' and 'gene-like', two camps answering different questions under one heading (one about formal population models, one about the metaphysics of units), a hidden empirical question that transmission data could settle in principle, or an ill-posed question that ought to be retired. Use what each side actually says. And if you think my causal-historical hypothesis is wrong, tell me what the unstated premise really is and what would expose it.

Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on: what would count as progress? A distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or nothing, in which case I'd rather hear that flat out, and hear whether my co-teacher and I should just agree to coexist, which I suspect we wont.
