I help run a grad reading group in a biology department and last week a 40-minute argument about whether some story about human jealousy is a real adaptation or a just-so story got killed in about ten seconds, when one guy said "survival of the fittest is a tautology, you can explain anything after the fact, so it isn't a theory at all." Then he said Popper was on his side. I think he's half right about that — Popper apparently said something like it at one point and later changed his tune and treated Darwinism as more of a metaphysical framework or research programme than a testable theory — but I never tracked down where he said either thing, so please don't make your answer depend on the sourcing.

What I want is that one sentence pulled apart. "It's a tautology" seems to cover at least a few different claims and I'm pretty sure he was sliding between them: fitness is just defined as reproductive success so the sentence is true by defintion; the principle is necessary but still does work, the way F=ma is sometimes said to; the theory is unfalsifiable in principle as opposed to merely hard to test in our case; fitness values get assigned post hoc so no prediction could ever fail; the principle is true but explains nothing. Lay them out as a menu I can bring to next week's meeting, and for each say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or just false — plus which one people usually mean when they say this at a party, and which one he'd have needed for his move to go through, i.e. to dismiss the jealousy story rather than just the Spencer slogan.

Related: does any of these readings license dismissing a specific adaptationist hypothesis? My suspicion is no — the general principle being analytic says nothing about whether a claim about a particular trait in a particular species is testable — but I can't say where exactly the gap comes from, and whether it's a scope move, an equivocation on "theory", or something else.

Finally, another friend thinks he has a test that settles the whole thing: if it's really tautological, you shouldn't be able to predict in advance which of two beetle strains will leave more offspring in a given environment, and if you can predict it, it's not tautological. Tell me whether that test discriminates between the readings above or just confuses the status of a principle with the status of its instances.
