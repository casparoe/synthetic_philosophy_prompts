Hey — putting on your social-choice-theorist-cum-political-theorist hat for this one, please. Situation: I've just moved onto the standards desk at a fact-checking outfit, and the single most complained-about category of headline we run is anything that attributes a view to "the people" — *Americans want a ceasefire*, *the public has turned on X*, *this is what the will of the people looks like*. Half the complaints are transparently motivated, but a chunk are actually interesting: they claim our wording smuggles in a thesis nobody on the desk could defend if you pinned them down. My editor has asked me for an internal memo on what it takes to license that kind of attribution, and before I write a word of the memo I want the spec, not the memo.

So: write the desiderata. What does any account of the will of the people have to deliver before it's usable by us — which phenomena it has to make sense of, which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it can't collapse, which formal results it has to be consistent with? Give me the case or the consideration behind each requirement, because I have to defend these to a newsroom that wont take "philosophers say so" for an answer. A few I already suspect belong on the list and want you to either vindicate or throw out: it has to separate what people would say if you asked them from what they stably hold (the non-attitudes problem); it has to survive the fact that majority preferences can cycle, so whoever controls the agenda can manufacture a winner (Riker's line of attack); it has to handle the boundary problem, since who counts as "the people" is usually the very thing being fought over; it has to permit issue-by-issue verdicts instead of one global yes/no; it has to be auditable, meaning a reader could reconstruct from the story alone which procedure produced the attributed will; and — this is the one I most want pressed — it has to be bootstrap-resistant, so that our publishing "the people want X" can't itself be what makes X wanted, which would let the claim certify itself. Add whatever else is genuinely load-bearing that I've missed, and tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidates.

Then the part I actually care most about: mark which requirements are non-negotiable for our purposes and which are really just the taste of a particular camp — aggregative/majoritarian accounts, deliberative ones, referendum-based ones, counterfactual-majority views, and the populist-constructivist line where claiming to speak for the people is what constitutes the people in the first place. Score those briefly against your list, but don't let the scorecard eat the spec; the weight should stay on the criteria and their motivating cases. And flag any subset of your own desiderata that you think cant be jointly satisfied — auditability plus bootstrap-resistance plus sensitivity to intensity of preference looks suspicious to me — because if the requirements list turns out to be a kind of impossibility result in disguise, I'd much rather know that before writing the memo than after. Please brainstorm out loud first, dump the candidate requirements untidily, then prune and organise; don't hand me a polished list on the first pass. Finally, be honest about your own confidence: tell me how sure you are of the overall shape of the list, single out the one requirement — or the one joint-unsatisfiability claim — you're least sure of, and say what evidence or argument would settle it.
