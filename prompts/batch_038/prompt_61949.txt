My roommate is in his second year of an economic history PhD and his partner is finishing a masters in philosophy of science, and last Thursday over takeout they got into a long argument about whether Malthus was "right but early." I was just sitting there eating and I dont have a horse in this race, but I'm taking a survey course on the history of economic thought this semester and the whole thing left me more confused than when they started, partly because both of them sounded convincing at different points. I wrote down the exchange as best I could remember it the next morning, so the wording is mine but the moves are theirs. Could you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what?

Here's how it went. (1) Roommate (call him Dev) said the phrase "right but early" doesn't even apply to Malthus, because Malthus never dated anything: his claim is an atemporal tendency, that population, if unchecked, outruns the means of subsistence. If you now say he had the mechanism right but the timing wrong, you're crediting him with a theory he never held, and the timing part is not some detachable auxiliary assumption — the claim that food output grows more slowly than population *is* a claim about what technology will do, so it's core, and it's false. (2) Partner (Nour) said that's too fast, because the mechanism genuinely ran for most of human history: for basically all of recorded time, extra food meant extra people rather than richer people, real wages hovered near subsistence, and pre-industrial England fits that pattern almost exactly. So Malthus described a machine that works, and it stopped working around 1800. A theory that's accurate inside its domain and gets superseded outside it isn't refuted, it's a limiting case, the way Newtonian mechanics survives inside relativity. (3) Dev shot back that the Newton analogy is smuggling in the whole argument: relativity tells you *in advance* where Newton stops holding and derives the correction from the same framework, whereas nothing in Malthus tells you when the trap opens. Bolting on "...and this only holds until sustained technological progress plus a fertility decline" after the fact is an ad hock rescue of the most blatant kind, and by the same maneuver you could rescue anything. (4) Nour said there *is* an internal candidate for the switch, and Malthus's own critics supplied it: Boserup's argument that population pressure itself induces agricultural intensification and invention, so the thing Malthus thought would starve people is also what pushes them to feed more people. Plus the preventive check is part of his own model — people respond to the cost of children — and the demographic transition is exactly that response firing at scale. On this reading, falling fertility isn't a counterexample to the theory, it's the theory's second term doing its job, and the framework also predicts the trap should be loosest where that response comes latest, which is roughly the pattern across countries today. (5) Dev said now Nour was using two incompatible rescues at once. If the preventive check explains the transition, then Malthus's *own* explanation of it — the "moral restraint" he added in the later editions, which he leaned on a postulate about the passion between the sexes staying roughly constant — is not what happened at all. Nobody in 1970s South Korea was practicing prudential abstinence; fertility fell because the returns to schooling and to women's wages changed and because child mortality dropped. So you've kept the equation and thrown out the behavioral postulate, and the postulate was the piece Malthus claimed was empirically certain. He then added the Julian Simon–Paul Ehrlich bet: Simon dared Ehrlich to stake money on resource scarcity, Ehrlich picked five metals over ten years, all of them fell in real terms, and Ehrlich had to pay up — which, Dev said, is what happens when the smartest Malthusians of a generation make a public, dated, checkable prediction and lose it. (6) Nour said the wager was never a test of Malthus, since he predicted nothing about the price of any specific commodity over any specific decade, and Ehrlich's choice of basket was his own; but also, and here they got a bit heated, Nour half-conceded the deeper point — "early" isn't a verdict you can just award, because either the tendency is permanent and it's falsified from 1800 onward, or it's conditional and you owe an independently specified condition, and if you can specify that condition you already have a better successor theory that Malthus is a special case of. Being absorbed as a special case isn't being right-but-early. Dev said sure it is, that's what being right-but-early means in every other science, and they never did settle it.
