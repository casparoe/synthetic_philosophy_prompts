I'm on a panel in a fortnights time — a memorial-ish affair, "logic and the soul" — and one of the other panellists is going to argue that Gödel would have been delighted by a commercial product that didn't exist when he died. I need to know whether she's right, and I'd like to be able to say something better than "well, we can't know".

The product: a firm in Zurich has licensed the complete archive of a mathematician who died in the nineties — notebooks, correspondence, marginalia, plus transcribed conversations with a colleague who kept diaries of their talks — and trained a conversational system on the lot. They sell hour-long sessions with it. It answers in his idiom, it revises positions it finds in the archive when given new arguments, and it has been set to work on problems the archive left unfinished. Two features are doing the philosophical heavy lifting in their marketing. First, three conjectures the system produced have since been recieved and confirmed by working mathematicians, which the firm says shows it isn't merely imitating but is still getting at what's true. Second, the system cannot be starved, poisoned, or abandoned: the pitch is an existence nobody can tamper with, and they've been putting it about that this is exactly what Godel, of all people, would have wanted, since he died in 1978 having refused food unless his wife had tasted it first.

My colleague's line is that his Platonism plus his Leibnizianism commit him to substrate-independence: identity lies in a rationally ordered structure, not in flesh, so a sufficiently rich continuation just is survival. She'll also wave the remark Hao Wang records — that the world we live in isn't the only one we shall live or have lived in — as though a data centre counted.

Take the set-up as given; I've seen the thing run and I'm not asking you to fact-check the firm. What I'm asking you to do is predict. Would he have accepted the continuant as the same person surviving, accepted it as something genuinely new and worthwhile but not survival, or thrown the whole thing out? Work it out from his own resources: the rationality-of-the-world argument for an afterlife; the monadology, and whether an artificial monad can come into being at all on Leibniz's terms or only by creation; his disjunction that either mathematics is too big for the human mind or the mind is more than a machine; that Nachlass note where I think he concedes the mind might, in all its potentialities, be equivalent to a finite machine (my memory of the wording is worthless, so treat it as a rumour, not a quotation); and his criterion for accepting a new axiom on the strength of its fruitfulness in verifiable consequences. Does the confirmed-conjectures point actually engage that criterion, or is it a category mistake? And does the no-poisoning point cut for him or against him, given what it was he actually feared?

Bottom line first, in a single sentence: which of the three positions is he likeliest to have taken. Then make the case in no more than 700 words, and say plainly where the record runs out and you're guessing. No reading list at the end — I've got one, and I want your reasoning rather than your references. I want an argument, not a séance.
