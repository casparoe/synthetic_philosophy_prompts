My brother Danny is getting married in about three weeks and I'm the best man, so I'm giving the toast at the rehearsal dinner. He's a family law attorney — divorce, mostly, fifteen years of it — and there's this running thing in our family that he has never once actually told a lie. Not to our parents, not to his ex-girlfriends, not to me when I was 19 and asking him whether I should drop out of community college. But he says true things in a way that sends you somewhere other than the truth, constantly, and everyone who knows him knows it. Two examples so you get the flavor. Our mom asked him, about six months before he got engaged, whether he was seeing anybody, and he said "I'm not living with anyone," which was completely accurate and also, we later found out, completely beside the point. And when I asked him whether my girlfriend at the time was right that I was being a jerk about her work schedule, he said "you're not a jerk," and I felt great for about a day and a half.

The room is going to be maybe eighty people and it's really two rooms pretending to be one. Half are from his firm and his law school friends, and they do this for a living — they'll get a joke about how a true sentence can still be a kind of dodge, they'll enjoy it more than anything else I say, and at least two of them will try to correct me about the details afterward. The other half are our relatives and Priya's family, including her parents, whose first language isn't English and who I don't think should have to sit through a puzzle to get the point. My mom is going to cry no matter what I say, that's not something you can control. Also there will be like four kids at the front table who have already been promised that someone is going to mention the time Danny got his head stuck in a railing, and I've been told by three separate cousins that if I don't do it I'm the villain.

So here's what I want. Write the toast, four to five minutes when it's said out loud at normal speed, and build it around that distinction — between never saying a false thing and never misleading anybody — but as a story and a joke, not as an explainer. Nobody should feel like they were in a lecture, and the words "philosophy" and "ethics" should not appear at all. The lawyers should hear the actual idea clearly enough that they respect it; Priya's dad should be able to follow the whole thing from the concrete examples alone. And it has to land somewhere real at the end, because the thing I actually believe is that Priya has made Danny want to be honest and not just accurate, which is a different skill and I don't think he had it before her. I don't want to say that as a compliment about her fixing him, though — he's my brother, that would be cruel, and also she'd hate it. Don't mention his ex-girlfriends, his job at the firm before this one, or our dad. And please don't end it with anything like "so join me in raising a glass" being the whole point — I'll do the glass-raising part myself, I just need the speech that comes before it.
