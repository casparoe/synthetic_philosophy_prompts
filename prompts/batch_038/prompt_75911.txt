So, background: I never went to uni, spent most of my working life as a shift supervisor in a food warehouse, and about four years ago I started a Tuesday night reading group in the back room of our community centre. Eight to twelve of us, all ages, nobody else in the room has any formal philosophy training. This year we did Augustine's On Lying and the shorter one he wrote later against lying to that guy Consentius, both in some free old translation I found online, and honestly it wrecked us. We finished last week and now I've promised them a "final exam" for fun, because half the group has got competitive and one of them, a nurse, wants to put it in a portfolio she's building for a night-school application. So I need one proper essay question, with a model answer and a marking scheme I can actually use to grade people. It's meant to feel like a real exam: 45 minutes, 20 marks.

The thing is, I don't want a question you can ace by writing three paragraphs saying Augustine thought lying is always wrong. That's the one bit everyone already knows and it's the least interesting bit. What kept us going till closing time was stuff like: on his definition it seems you can lie while saying something completely true, and you can say something flatly false and not be lying at all, if your heart and your mouth don't match up — which would mean nobody can ever tell from the outside whether you lied. And then he seems totally fine with just shutting up, or hiding the truth, or steering someone off the question, which struck me as odd, because hiding it usually does exactly the same damage to the person as telling them a falsehood would. And there's the bit where he says the midwives in Exodus and Rahab were rewarded for being merciful and not for the lie itself, and the bit where he ranks lies from worst to least bad but then says even the least bad one is still a sin. And the Priscillianist business — those heretics apparently taught that you could lie to conceal your faith, and Augustine wouldn't let his own people lie to catch them, which to me looks like he'd rather let the bad guys win. What I want the question to do is force whoever answers it to explain how Augustine can hold all that together: why the line gets drawn between asserting what you don't hold and merely concealing what you do, when from outside the two look the same. Bonus if it makes them say whether the line actually holds up or whether he's just moved the problem somewhere else.

So please write: the question itself, self-contained and with whatever setup a person needs, since not everyone in my group will remeber every example; a model answer of around 650 words that I can read beforehand so I know what good looks like; and a marking scheme that breaks the 20 marks into specific must-hit points, says what each is worth, and — most important for me — spells out the difference between an A answer and a B answer in terms of the actual moves rather than the vibe, plus a list of the mistakes that should cost marks, e.g. if someone slides back into treating lying as just "saying something false," or treats Augustine as an early Kant, or reads the midwives passage as him approving the lie. No reading list or citations please, I couldn't check them and neither could my group. And if you think my reading of anything above is wrong, say so straight — I could of got the wrong end of the stick on the concealment point.
