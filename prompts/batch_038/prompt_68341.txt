I'm a staff engineer at a mid-size payments company and I'm on the little committee rewriting our incident handbook this quarter. We're pretty orthodox about one thing: after an outage we write a postmortem, and the rule is that it has to be "blameless." In practice that means the doc doesn't name the person who pushed the bad config — we had one in March where a contractor's change took payouts down for about forty minutes and the writeup never mentions him — and we describe things in system terms rather than agent terms, like "the deploy went out without the canary step becuase the checklist didn't require it," not "X skipped the canary step." Every action item has to be about tooling, guardrails, docs, or defaults. Never about somebody being more careful next time. The reason our current handbook gives is that if people think they'll get blamed they hide stuff, and then we learn nothing and the next one is worse.

The thing bugging me is that the same company runs a performance cycle with soft rankings and individual bonuses for "ownership," has managed people out for repeat mistakes, and the CEO spends half the all-hands praising named individuals who rescued a launch. Nobody on the committee sees a problem, and when I tried to raise it I got the distinct feeling I was being dense. So I want to know what picture of responsibility the blameless norm only makes sense under. Is it actually a claim that the guy who pushed the config wasn't responsible — that once you lay out how he was hired, trained, tired, rushed, and incentivized, there's nothing left over to hold against him — or is it just a bet that blaming makes outcomes worse even when it's earned? Those look like totally different commitments and I genuinely can't tell which one our handbook is making, or whether a handbook could even register the difference from the inside. And can the norm live next to the rankings and the shout-outs, or does consistency force the company to pick one and eat the cost?

The other thing I want pinned down: suppose we went all in on the strong version, the one where nobody ever ultimately deserves blame *or* credit for anything, since nobody chose the character that produced the act. What would actually change in the incident process, and what would stay word-for-word identical? My suspicion is the answer is "almost nothing, the docs would read the same," which would make the whole debate cosmetic — but that seems like a lousy reason to stop asking, and I'd like someone to tell me where I'm wrong. I've been listening to too many free-will podcast episodes on my commute, for context, and it's making me see this everywhere. Don't send me a reading list, I just want the reasoning worked through.
