My mom has this picture she's been running for thirty-some years about my brother. He died at four months old, and she still talks about it in the same way: every pregnancy is a draw from a lottery, she and my dad drew the ticket that was him exactly once, so there was never a version of their life with the same baby in it who might have lived, and no way to have him again. She mostly uses it against herself, as a reason the grief can't be pointed at some rerolled future. Meanwhile I'm in an ethics seminar where we've been sitting on the non-identity problem for three weeks, and basically everyone in there — including me, which is the embarrassing part — argues with the exact same picture, just aimed the other direction: any policy that shifts when people end up conceiving draws a different ticket, so a different person, so nobody who's glad to be alive was harmed by the thing that made them exist. Same lottery, opposite conclusions, and nobody in either place seems to notice they're using it.

Can you audit the lottery picture for me? I want the mapping actually spelled out — what plays the ticket, what's the draw, what are the odds printed on the box, who's the lottery commission — and then where the mapping fails. My suspicion is that it smuggles in at least three things: that the tickets are individuated before the draw and each one is already a determinate possible person, so there's a fixed finite pool of them; that drawing is a single discrete event rather than a smeary process spread over hours or days; and that the improbability of a ticket somehow tells you about the value or replaceability of what you end up holding. But I also want you to sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for what my mom is doing versus the ones that matter for what my seminar is doing, because my guess is that most of them are inert for one and load-bearing for the other, and I can't tell which is which.

The part I actually can't work out is this. There's a line of objection to origin essentialism I keep running into: if a different sperm had fertilized the egg, fine, different person — but gametes differ from each other by tiny amounts, so you can build a chain from the actual one to a wildly different one where every single step is a trivial difference, and its not obvious where the personhood is supposed to flip. A seminar mate of mine says this vagueness is precisely what the lottery hides, since a real lottery has sharp tickets and no borderline draws. Does that land? And if the origin of a person is vague or tolerant rather than sharp-edged, which of the two conclusions is in worse shape — my mom's about grief, or the seminar's about harm? Last thing: if the lottery can't carry either of them, is there a better analogy? I've been toying with conception as dealing from a deck that gets reshuffled while you deal, and with conception as a chemical reaction whose range of conditions can still yield the same product, but I have no idea whether either is an improvement or just vaguer in a way that feels deep.
