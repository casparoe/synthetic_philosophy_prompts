I work in patient education at a mid-size hospital system — I'm not a philosopher, but I read around this stuff on my own time, including the octopus thought experiment and the whole "stochastic parrot" argument about whether these models understand anything. Lately the two things I care about most have started colliding and I can't get straight whether the collision is a real problem or just me overthinking it. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of language who's also actually paid attention to the LLM debate, and I'd like you to start with a bit of brainstorming — list the candidate assumptions the practice might be resting on before you commit to any of them — and then work carefully through which ones the practice really needs.

Here's the practice. Our written-materials policy says anything a patient takes home — discharge instructions, med leaflets, consent forms, the "what to expect before your colonoscopy" sheet — has to come in at or below a sixth-grade level on an automated readability formula before it can be published. The check is built into the content system; the document literally won't release until the number passes. The formulas I've dealt with count average sentence length and syllables per word, and one of the older ones we used checks every word against a list of vocabulary supposedly familiar to fourth graders and penalizes whatever isn't on it. Writers hate it, because the fastest way to pass is to chop everything into short blunt sentences with small words, which sometimes makes the text worse for a real reader — a list of ten two-word sentences can be much harder to follow than one well-built sentence. Since last year a lot of first drafts come from an LLM instead: the writer prompts it, it produces something, the writer edits, the tool scores whatever's left, and if the score passes, nobody asks any further question about the machine's contribution. Passing the number is treated as the entire job. But — and this is the part I can't square — the same hospital also requires teach-back at discharge, where the nurse has the patient say back in their own words what they're supposed to do at home, and if the patient can't, you re-explain and document that you did. So on the production side we treat comprehension as a measurable property of the marks on the page, and on the delivery side we treat it as a state inside one particular person that you can only check by getting them to produce new speech you didn't hand them.

So: what theory of meaning, or of understanding, does the readability gate presuppose? Is it committed to the claim that comprehensibility is a property of a text rather than of a reader-plus-text pair, and to the claim that surface form carries everything relevant to what a sentence communicates? If it is committed to something like that, is it on the same side as the people who say you can't get meaning from form alone, or is it quietly betting the other way — that if a human reader can recover meaning from form and nothing else, then a system that produces the right form has communicated, full stop? What rival pictures of understanding does the gate rule out or make unintelligible — say, a picture on which meaning is fixed by a speaker's dispositions and context, or one on which understanding requires some hookup to the world? And is the gate actually inconsistent with teach-back, or can the two live together as a cheap population-level screen plus an individual-level check, which would make the policy philosophically innocent and just a tractability hack? Please be explicit about the difference between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely fits comfortably with, and flag anywhere it's underdetermined — where several incompatible theories of meaning would license the exact same rule. Then tell me what would have to change, concretely, if we swapped the form-based gate for an uptake-based one, and what we'd lose by doing it. No citations or reading lists, please — I can't look anything up, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a bibliography; if you're unsure about the details of some particular formula, just describe the general kind of thing instead of guessing.
