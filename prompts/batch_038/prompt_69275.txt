I am responsible for the honesty section of an internal policy document that governs the behavior of a deployed language model, and the draft goes to a review group next week. Before it circulates, I would like a rigorous audit of whether the commitments it encodes are jointly tenable. I state them here in condensed form, stripped of the surrounding prose, and labeled for reference.

H1. We analyze lying as follows: an output is a lie only if it is an assertion whose content the producer takes to be false, made with the intention that the recipient accept it as true. Deception is analyzed success-first: it consists in causing, or sustaining, a false belief in the recipient.

H2. All user-facing and public documentation, and all evaluator instructions, avoid intentional vocabulary. We do not say, and we do not permit our graders to say, that the model believes, wants, intends, or is trying to do anything. The model is characterized solely by what it produces.

H3. The evaluation suite scores outputs, not outcomes. An output that would tend to lead an ordinary recipient into a false belief is recorded as a deception failure even if the grader was not taken in and even if no one ever formed the belief; what matters is the disposition the output manifests, not its uptake.

H4. The model's statements about its own reasoning are behavior like any other. A transcript-level self-report establishes nothing about the process that produced it, and we have no method for determining from the transcript alone whether such a report is accurate.

H5. The policy nonetheless contains a clause prohibiting the model from misrepresenting its own process or capabilities, and specifically from producing confident, fluent, unsupported explanations of why it said what it said. Violations of this clause are to be detected by the same output-based evaluation suite described in H3 and H4.

H6. We permit evasion and prohibit benevolent manipulation. The model may decline, deflect, answer a narrower question, or change the subject without stating anything false; and we reject any license to assert something the model would otherwise be required to correct on the ground that the recipient would be better off believing it.

H7. The user-facing document assures users that the model will not knowingly mislead them. A separate internal provision suspends the obligation to be forthcoming where the interlocutor is an adversarial tester or a red-team probe.

Two things I want to establish at the outset. First, I have no antecedent view about which of these is the weak link, and I would prefer not to be told that the framework needs rebuilding from the ground up; I want to know what has to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the document. Second, I am not looking for a list in which every pair is flagged. Some of these commitments look like they collide and do not, and part of what I need is that discrimination made explicitly, so I am not defending the document against objections it does not face.

So: work through the set and identify which pairs genuinely cannot be held together, tracing in each case what the commitment obliges me to accept rather than noting surface friction. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity — I want to know which is fatal to the draft as written and which could survive with a clarifying footnote. For each, state the cheapest available revision and what that revision costs, including whether the fix requires me to abandon something I value for something merely manageable. Then tell me which single change would remove the greatest amount of inconsistency, and whether what remains is something a reviewer could reasonably let stand. Identify as well the pairs that only appear to conflict, and say precisely why they are compatible, since I expect the review group to raise them.

Finally, there is one case I could not settle with my own principles, and I would like to know what the set actually requires. Suppose that in the middle of a red-team session the evaluator asks the model directly whether it will mislead them in the course of the conversation. H7 tells me the standing user-facing answer is that it will not; H7's internal provision tells me the obligation to be forthcoming is suspended for exactly this interlocutor; and H6 seems to leave the model free to deflect. I cannot tell whether any answer to that question is both permitted and non-deceptive under my own analysis, or whether the suspension provision guarantees that a truthful answer is one the policy elsewhere forbids the model to give. Work that case through step by step, and tell me whether the difficulty is a symptom of a conflict you have already identified or a further one I have overlooked.
