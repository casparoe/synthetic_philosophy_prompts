Meno's question — why knowledge is worth more than mere true belief when both guide the traveller correctly to Larissa — is usually met either by the stability answer (untethered true beliefs wander off unless tied down by an account of why they hold) or by the deflationary upshot of the swamping argument (once a belief is true, the reliability behind it adds nothing). I want to defend a third option: that the extra value of knowledge is constitutively social. A justified true belief can be audited, taught, and pooled; a luckily true belief cannot. Knowledge is therefore not a more valuable state than true belief in the same currency, but the only form in which a true belief becomes a reusable common resource — and Meno's solitary traveller is a limiting case in which the difference genuinely vanishes: for a one-shot decision taken alone, knowledge earns no premium over true belief.

What would count against this position? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Address in particular: whether any case survives in which an individual, deciding once and alone, is better off knowing than merely truly believing; whether tacit, indexical, and self-locating knowledge are counterexamples or can be accommodated; whether transferability turns out to be the old stability answer in new vocabulary; and whether my standing fallback — a belief that proved untransferable simply was not knowledge — has insulated the view past the point of falsifiability. Close by saying which objections a defender should concede rather than deflect, and what this view predicts that its rivals do not.
