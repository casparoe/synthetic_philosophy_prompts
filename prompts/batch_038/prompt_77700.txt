My supervisor runs a Friday decision-theory reading group and she has this habit of announcing flatly what she'd do in each puzzle before whoever's reading it out has finished. Her track record so far, as best I've got it in my notes:

Newcomb, the standard one, near-perfect predictor that reasoned its way through her decision procedure the day before: she takes the opaque box only, doesn't even pause. The smoking lesion — a gene that causes both the urge to smoke and the cancer, with the smoking itself doing nothing: she smokes, and says anyone who doesn't has confused evidence with reasons. We then gave her a Newcomb where the box-filler never modelled anyone's reasoning at all but worked off a scan of the player's genome, which happens to correlate strongly with what they'll do: she takes both boxes, and says it's the smoking lesion in cardboard. Death in Damascus she did the week after: Death turns up, is startled to see her, says she'd been certain they'd meet tonight in Aleppo, never misses, and the meeting place was written down before she was born and can't now be altered; the fast camel to Aleppo costs £100. She stays, obviously, the fare being wasted either way. Then she spends ten minutes on the part that actually interests her, which is that if you woke her at a minute to midnight and asked again she'd give the same answer, and that a theory whose advice you expect to regret the moment you've followed it isn't a theory but a trap. One-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact copy of herself, scanned and run in a box, no communication: she cooperates. The same dilemma against a stranger who is merely similar — same city, same training, choices that correlate, but no copy and no predictor anywhere in sight: she defects, and says the two cases aren't remotely alike however much the matrices look it.

Next week it's my turn to bring the case and I've writen one that's Damascus with an addition, and I'd rather work out her verdict beforehand than be surprised in front of everyone. Same setup, same speech from Death, same £100 camel. But at seven, an hour after Death has gone, a soothsayer comes to the door. He's explicit that he has no idea where Death will be tonight and that nothing he can tell her bears on it: he isn't a predictor, he's an archivist, and he has never modelled anybody's reasoning. For £1 he'll sell you a page of his register. In three hundred years of travellers who've had that speech in Damascus, the register records what each of them did, and — from tests administered years before their Damascus night plus the record of what became of them and of everyone who depended on them — what sort of decision procedure they were running. The pattern has no exceptions: everyone who stayed was a person whose procedure had served them, and everyone relying on them, badly; everyone who fled was a person whose procedure had served them and their dependants well. In my version the traveller has a daughter whose trust fund she manages and a business partner mid-negotiation, so what sort of agent she turns out to have been isn't only a fact about her. So it's: stay as she said last month and keep the £100 and the bad news, or pay for the camel and die in Aleppo with the good news.

What I want, roughly in this order. Which rules are jointly consistent with her six verdicts — name them properly, evidential, causal, functional or logical, some stability or ratifiability constraint, or whatever hybrid she's actually running — and say which verdict knocks out which. Then what each surviving rule says about my case and exactly where they part company, because I suspect at least two of them do. Then your best guess at her verdict and the reason you'd give for it: which of the six carries the most weight here, and does the soothsayer's page change anything at all given that she's already ruled on Damascus without it? And if her precedents genuinely don't settle it, what should she say to be most consistent with herself, and what principle is she committing to on each side — I'd like to be able to put that to her directly. Finally, what case should I bring the week after that would tell the surviving rules apart.

Please write it all as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I'll be reading it on the train.
