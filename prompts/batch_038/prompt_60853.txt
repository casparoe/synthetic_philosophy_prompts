I run a tiny outfit that keeps a public ledger of long-run forecasts — the big dated ones: fisheries collapse by such-and-such year, topsoil gone by 2050, this metal runs out, that debt spiral hits, we cross this threshold in the 2040s. Every entry has a stated window and a stated mechanism, and when the window closes we publish a verdict: confirmed, falsified, or still live. That third category is where I'm losing my mind. About forty entries a year come up for review and a big chunk of them look dead on arrival, but thier authors write in to say we're calling it too early — the mechanism is sound, only the timing was off, give it another twenty years. Half my panel wants to log a miss as a miss. The other half is terrified we'll end up punishing anyone who thinks in centuries, and they have a point: Malthus looks like the poster child for a guy who was right about ten thousand years of history and wrong about the last two hundred.

So write me a procedure my reviewers can actually run on an entry whose window has closed and whose predicted event didn't happen. Ordered questions, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the point where the entry gets logged as falsified, and the points where the call is genuinely a judgment about how much slack a theory has earned rather than something you could look up. Constraints: the reviewer is smart but not an economist, forty-five minutes per entry, two people on it, and the forecaster files a written reply that has to be addressed point by point. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of science actually has to say about rescue versus revision — core claims versus auxiliary assumptions, what it means for a theory to get sharper instead of vaguer, why re-dating a prediction isn't automatically cheating but sometimes is — not in our gut feelings.

Then run it on three entries, showing the answers you'd write down at each step:

1. Malthus. Core claim: population presses hard on the means of subsistence and gets held down only by misery and vice, with the geometric-ratio-versus-arithmetic-ratio argument doing the work. The record: two centuries of real wages and food output climbing way faster than population. The defense has to include both flavors — the one his own later editions handed him, where he adds moral restraint (marrying late, or not at all) as a third check, and the modern one, that the trap was real for essentially all of human history and we only broke out around industrialization, so the theory was right about everything except the timing of the escape.

2. A 1970s entry claiming a specific industrial metal is exhausted by 2000 absent recycling. It missed. The authors now say the mechanism held and that substitution plus new extraction tech moved the date. Note that they never specified substitution in the original writeup.

3. One I like and expect you to kill: a forecast filed in the nineties that a slow-moving fiscal threshold gets crossed in the 2040s, where the authors argue the mechanism has been confirmed at every step and only the date slipped. I want to know exactly which question in the procedure does the damage, because I need to find out whether I'm motivated-reasoning in its favor.

Finally, flag which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, and be straight with me about what no procedure can do here — specifically whether "true but early" can ever be told apart from "true but never testable," and what we should print for the public when it can't be.
