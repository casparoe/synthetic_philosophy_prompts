I'm chairing the Q&A after a departmental lecture next month. The speaker is a strategic studies professor — I'd rather not name him here — whose whole argument is that nuclear deterrence is made more robust, not less, by deliberately pushing some of the authority to use force away from the political centre: pre-delegation to commanders in the field, launch-on-warning postures, that kind of thing. His line is that a threat which depends on one sane person deciding calmly under enormous pressure is exactly the threat an adversary can discount, so a bit of engineered loss of control is what buys credibilty. I've read enough to know this sits in the Schelling tradition — brinkmanship as "manipulating the shared risk of war", "the threat that leaves something to chance" — but I dont want the session to turn into twenty minutes of him explaining Schelling to the audience.

Could you draft me eight to ten questions that actually put pressure on the position? Under each one, give me a line saying what I'm trying to force out of him, and then the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give — he's very smooth, so I want the second question already in my hand.

At minimum I want one question each on:

- the difference between generating risk on purpose and merely being unreliable, which to a lay audience sound like the same thing;
- whether his argument survives if the other side can work out how much of the delegation is real and how much is theatre, since the commitment only does its work if it's observable and being observable is precisely what makes it dangerous;
- crisis instability: if both sides delegate and each is worried about losing its forces in a disarming strike, has he designed a machine that fires more readily in exactly the situation he's meant to be deterring;
- the evidence problem — every success of his view is equally consistent with sheer luck, and the events that would refute it are the ones that havent happened;
- who is culpable when a junior officer's delegated decision kills a city, and whether spreading the blame thinly is a feature of the arrangement or an evasion of the question.

He also has one stock answer for anything about accidental war: nothing has gone wrong in decades, so the safety record speaks for itself. Give me a single question that makes that answer useless without me coming across as a heckler.

Close with the question about what would count as evidence against his position, and make it hard to dodge with a general remark about uncertainty. No reading list and no suggestions about what I should prepare beforehand, please — just the questions and the reasoning behind them. And write the whole thing in British English.
