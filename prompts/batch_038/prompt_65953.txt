I volunteer as the "research lead" (read: the only person who will actually open the spreadsheet) for a small invertebrate-welfare project, and for about a year now our group has been deadlocked on the same argument. I don't want a referee — I want a diagnosis. You should think of yourself as a philosopher of mind who also does serious work on decision-making under uncertainty and on moral weight, someone comfortable moving between the two literatures in a single paragraph. Please think carefully, and actually start by brainstorming three or four candidate diagnoses before you commit to one, because I've read the standard position summaries and I don't need another one.

Here's the shape of it. One camp — call them the scale-first people — reasons multiplicatively: take the probability that an insect is sentient, multiply by the moral weight you'd give it conditional on sentience, multiply by how many there are and by how much bad experience a typical insect life contains. They'll happily grant a low probability (Dana has 0.3, but says even 0.05 does the work) and a tiny weight (one ten-thousandth of a human, sometimes less), and then point at the old back-of-envelope figure of something like ten quintillion insects alive at any given moment, plus the black soldier fly larvae being reared by the billions for aquaculture feed and processed by the ton, plus insecticide applications over cropland. Their line is that the arithmetic swamps the epistemology, so arguing about the quality of the insect evidence is beside the point. The other camp — the evidence-first people — says the probability framing is the wrong frame entirely. What matters is whether there is valenced experience integrated enough that a life can go badly *for the subject*, and they think the insect data (mushroom bodies, attention-like modulation, central-place navigation, nociceptive responses that don't persist or generalize much) supports at most a very thin kind of affect. On their view moral weight isn't a discount factor applied to a full-strength subject; it should track the richness and duration of what's actually there. They also hammer on two things I find harder to answer: that most insects die in the egg or within days of hatching, so a standing population of 10^18 massively overcounts lives containing any experience at all — stock is not flow — and that abundance estimates from sweep-netting, canopy fogging and biomass allometry don't agree with each other within three orders of magnitude.

The thing that convinced me this is worth diagnosing rather than just waiting out: our funder asked for a single number, the probability that a worker honeybee has valenced experience, and the two camps returned 0.3 and 0.005, and we then burned three hours arguing about how to combine them — linear pooling, geometric pooling, defer to whoever's read more papers — and got nowhere. My working hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis: the dispute actually decomposes into four separable parameters (probability of sentience, weight conditional on sentience, number of subjects, amount of bad experience per subject-life), and the two camps disagree about all four while arguing as though it were one variable. But maybe the real fault line is only in one of them — maybe they agree on three and the entire fight is about how to handle a parameter nobody can measure, or about what the word sentience is being used to pick out, or about whether this is an axiology question or a prioritization question wearing an axiology costume. Tell me which. Is any part of it merely verbal, and if so which part? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work, and is it answerable with anything like current methods, or is the disagreement about standards of evidence and which findings count as data at all? Are they answering different questions under one heading? Is the impasse a sign the question is ill-posed or just genuinely hard? Then give me what would count as progress *given your diagnosis* — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question, a different unit of analysis — or make the case that no progress should be expected and say why. End by telling me how confident you are in the diagnosis you land on, and single out the one step in it you're least sure about; if you think I've mischaracterized either camp in a way that changes the answer, say that too rather than working with my version.
