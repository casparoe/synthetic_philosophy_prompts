I direct public affairs at a national statistical agency — the body that publishes the official figures on inflation, unemployment, and population, and that administers the decennial census. Somewhere between two and four times a month, a claim enters wide circulation that misstates one of our numbers or misdescribes our mandate, and my staff of eleven has to decide within a few hours whether to respond, in what form, and at what level. At present we decide by instinct and seniority, and the resulting pattern looks arbitrary in hindsight: we have issued stern corrections on trivial points and stayed silent on serious ones. I want to replace that with a written procedure, and I want it grounded specifically in what Arendt argues about lying and truth in politics in "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics" — the arguments in those essays, not the general reputation. Responses go out over my signature; the permanent head of the agency countersigns anything touching a live political controversy; and in a fast-moving story we usually have ninety minutes, not days.

Three features of Arendt's account are the ones my staff repeatedly collapse into each other, and I want the procedure to hold them apart. First, her contrast between the traditional political lie — which aims to get one particular falsehood believed in place of one particular truth, and often to keep something secret — and the mode she associates with modern image-making, in which the objective is not belief at all but the audience's loss of any reliable way of orienting itself by facts. These call for different diagnostics and different responses, so I want the procedure's opening move to be a classification test that a press officer with no training in philosophy can run in under two minutes, with explicit tie-breakers and a stated default when the classification remains unclear. Second, her observation that a falsehood frequently carries more plausibility than the fact it displaces, precisely because facts are contingent and could have been otherwise. Wherever the test finds the false claim more plausible than the truth, the procedure cannot assume that restating the correct figure will do anything; it should say what we do instead, and it should say plainly where we have no good option. Third, the asymmetry she draws between lying and truth-telling as kinds of action — that the liar is free to invent what an audience wants to hear while the truth-teller is bound to what has already happened and is therefore structurally on the defensive. The procedure should contain an explicit check that a proposed response does more than assert that we were right.

The deliverable should be a numbered sequence of questions, each with a specification of what counts as a sufficient answer, and each marked with the ways the sequence can terminate: no response, response in a designated form, or escalation to the permanent head or to ministers because what remains is a question of institutional role rather than of fact. Every step must be self-contained enough that a journalist could be read it verbatim as an explanation of why we did or did not respond. Alongside the procedure, I want a separate list flagging each step where the apparent test is really a discretionary judgment in procedural clothing — I would rather my staff know where the procedure is pretending to be mechanical. Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot handle at all, and with your own assessment of which link a hostile reviewer from the inspector general's office would identify as the weakest.

Apply the procedure in full to three cases. (1) A senior legislator, campaigning for a policy my analysts privately regard as sound, tells the chamber that our unemployment figure understates joblessness by nearly half because of how we treat people who have stopped looking for work; the claim misdescribes our methods but points at a real limitation we have documented ourselves, and my press office would prefer not to antagonize him. (2) A foreign state broadcaster circulates a story that our census systematically undercounted one ethnic group in order to depress its representation; the story is false, it is constructed so that each denial supplies a fresh headline repeating the allegation, two domestic outlets have already asked us for comment, and nothing turns on whether anyone actually believes it. (3) A genuine internal email, accurate but three years old and written about a different question, is leaked and quoted as proof that we suppressed a figure; confirming its authenticity would be true and would also ratify the framing, and the leak originated inside the agency. I expect at least one of these to end in a conclusion my staff will dislike and at least one to end in silence.

Please write this as a document I could circulate, of roughly two thousand words. I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature survey — I have the essays and will supply references myself — and I would rather you flag a place where Arendt gives me nothing to work with than fill the gap with material that is not hers.
