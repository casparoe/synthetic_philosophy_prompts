I am the volunteer registrar for a very small maritime museum in a fishing town, and in three weeks I have to speak at a re-unveiling down on the harbor. You will need the history, which everyone here knows in outline and almost nobody in order. In 1931 a sculptor named Elias Vane cast a life-size bronze figure of a woman in a shawl holding a closed lantern; the plinth called it *The Waiting*, but the town has only ever called her Nora, and no one now alive knows why. She stood at the harbor for eighty-eight years. In 2019 she was stolen in the night for her scrap value, and the thieves got her into a smelter before anyone caught up with them. What came back to us was roughly sixty percent of her weight in bronze that had by then gone into a shared crucible with other people's scrap, so there is no sense in which any lump of it is a lump of her. The museum spent the insurance money and two years of donations having the Kestrel Foundry, two towns over, recast the figure from Vane's original plaster mold, which had been sitting in our own storeroom the entire time, untouched and unmended. That pour used the returned bronze mixed with fresh metal; nobody can say in what proportion, or where in the figure. There is one leftover. The sprue — the channel the metal ran through, cut off and cooled — came back to us in a shoebox, and it is made of exactly what the statue is made of, with the difference that it has never been shaped into anything at all. Danny, the foreman, has suggested we mount it on the plinth beside her with a label. Danny and I call this the shoebox problem, which is our name, not anybody's else. I have not answered him.

I should tell you what sort of person is asking. I left school at seventeen, I run a chandlery, and I do the museum's records because nobody else will; I once took a library book on identity out and gave up somewhere in the middle. So what follows is not a position I can cite, only one I have been circling for months. The first thing I keep coming back to is Vane's thumbprint. He worked her up in clay before the mold was taken, and on the left shoulder of the bronze there is a shallow whorl where his thumb pressed in. It was photographed. It was in the mold. It is on the new figure in precisely the same place, to the millimeter. My daughter, who is seventeen, says that settles it: a thumbprint you did not make with your own thumb is a picture of a thumbprint, and this is a copy, and it is creepy that the whole town is pretending. My brother, who welds for a living, says that is nonsense — same shape, same place, same metal, same Nora, stop being strange. I find myself nearer my daughter than my brother and I cannot say why, and I am not confident my reason would survive being said out loud. The second thing I keep coming back to is a suspicion that everyone is asking two questions and treating them as one: whether this is the same object, and whether this is the same thing we have been looking after all these years. I suspect only the second is a question a town is in a position to settle. I also suspect that is a dodge, and I would like the speech to test it rather than lean on it. Our records already contradict themselves on the point. I closed the file on the old accession number in 2019 and opened a fresh one for the recast, because that is what an insurer and an auditor need; but the letters the schoolchildren write to Nora every spring still go into the old file, because nobody at the front desk could bring themselves to do otherwise. So our own filing cabinet is holding both answers at once, and on Saturday I have to say something coherent in public.

The room will be about two hundred people on the shingle, tarp off at eleven, the school brass band after. In the front row: Marian Vane, the sculptor's granddaughter, who is eighty-one and has told me privately that she regards the new figure as her grandfather's work and does not intend to hear it called a reproduction. Behind her, the foundry crew in clean shirts, several of whom think they made a statue, are proud of it, and would be insulted by being told they merely delivered her. Our minister, Rev. Talbot, who has asked me in complete good faith whether the blessing he said over her in 1987 needs saying again — he wants a straight answer before Saturday, because if it is the same Nora the blessing may still be holding, and if it is not, he will be blessing a new thing in front of the whole town, which is a considerably larger event than it sounds. And Ms. Deane, an adjuster from the insurer, who has driven up at her own expense, is unfailingly pleasant, and has now twice joked that if this is the same statue then her company's total-loss payment was rather generous. I do not want to say anything untrue to protect that money, and I do not want to hand it over either; I would genuinely like to know whether an honest answer bears on it at all. Third row, Alma Fitch, a retired schoolteacher who reads philosophy for pleasure and will know exactly what I am doing if I do it.

Write the speech. Five or six minutes, spoken plainly, from me: not a historian and not a philosopher, a person who keeps the records. No jargon — identity, essence, substance, form, matter, criterion, and continuity are all off the table in the sense philosophers use them; you may talk about metal, molds, thumbs, shoeboxes, lanterns and paperwork, and that will have to do all the work. Do not name a single philosopher, book, or theory; if the idea cannot be carried by the harbor and the foundry and the thumbprint, it cannot be carried here at all. The speech must commit: by the end a listener should be able to say which side I came down on, or able to say precisely what I declined to decide and why that declining is not a fudge. It must answer Rev. Talbot's question aloud without making him look foolish. It must leave Marian Vane, Danny, and my seventeen-year-old all able to say they were not lied to, which is not the same thing as all three of them being pleased. And I want one moment — a sentence, ideally near the middle — that the schoolchildren will simply take for a joke and that Alma Fitch will recognize as the hinge the whole thing turns on. Do not open with thanks to the donors and do not open with the theft; my own instinct is to open with the thumbprint, but I expect you can find something better. After the speech, add exactly one sentence, outside it, telling me which position you have committed me to. Nothing further.
