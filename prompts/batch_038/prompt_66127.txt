I cover the Supreme Court for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a piece about a piece of interpretive machinery that rarely gets examined as philosophy: the Court's practice of holding that what counts as cruel and unusual punishment is not fixed by the understanding of those who ratified the Eighth Amendment but tracks the moral standards of American society at the time of judgment. In operation, the Court reads those standards off how many state legislatures have abolished a penalty, how reluctant juries are to impose it, sometimes how other established democracies handle it — and then layers on its own independent judgment about whether the penalty serves any legitimate penological purpose.

What philosophy of history does that practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory. It seems to require that moral change has a direction rather than being drift; that the direction is legible from what institutions are already doing; and that the present is a better guide to the Constitution's meaning than the ratifying generation's own understanding of it. Tell me which of those the practice strictly needs and which it merely sits comfortably with, and which rival views it forecloses — including the possibility that today's consensus is less decent than yesterday's, which would seem to oblige the Court to endorse a regression.

I'm especially interested in a tension I can't resolve. The same doctrine is invoked to celebrate how far society has come and to insist that the Court must not move ahead of society. Is the arc of history here a prediction the Court is reading off, or an exhortation it is helping to fulfill? And could the practice survive if the Court dropped the assumption of direction and kept everything else — a purely conventionalist updating rule with no teleology in it at all?

Nine hundred words or so. No list of cases and no bibliography; I can look those up myself.
