I cover science and contested evidence for a general-interest magazine, and before drafting a piece on when extraordinary testimony deserves belief I want to know what my own verdicts commit me to. Here they are, from a mixture of cases I have reported on and cases I have constructed for myself:

1. One sober, intelligent friend tells me she watched a coffee cup rise off a table and hang motionless for ten seconds. I conclude she is mistaken or lying, and I do not believe the cup moved.
2. Twenty observers of unimpeachable character — several of them physicists — report the same hovering cup from different angles, with instrument readings and no opportunity to coordinate. Here I do believe something extraordinary happened, though I would still hunt for an unknown mundane mechanism rather than accept a suspension of natural law.
3. An anonymous account posts a video of the same hovering cup and it is viewed forty million times. The view count adds nothing; the video adds almost nothing.
4. My doctor reports a positive test for a condition that strikes one person in a million. I assume a false positive. The same doctor, with the same competence, reports a positive test for a condition that strikes one in ten, and I assume I have it. Identical testimony, opposite conclusions — and I think the asymmetry is correct.
5. A friend says her flight was delayed eight hours. I believe her at once, though she is no more reliable than the doctor.
6. A hundred townspeople separately report the same incapacitating symptoms and no pathogen is ever found. I believe they suffered something real; I do not believe their account of what caused it.
7. A disinterested lighthouse keeper says the sea withdrew a mile in an instant. I believe him. The same keeper adds that a figure then walked across the exposed seabed. I believe nothing of that second part, though his character, position, and opportunity are unchanged and the two events are contiguous.
8. A scientist reports an anomaly that, if genuine, destroys her life's work, and she has lost funding and standing by reporting it. I treat her sincerity as beyond doubt and her observation as less trustworthy than an ordinary one.

Is there a single principle that generates all of this? I would like it stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause, and with its verdicts on cases I have not listed — including whether it can explain why I discount the witness with the most to lose while crediting the witness with nothing to gain. Then tell me what it costs. Does Hume's rough rule in his essay on miracles, that testimony to a breach of nature is to be accepted only where the witnesses' being deceived or deceptive would itself be the greater marvel, deliver these verdicts, or does it break on one of them? And if the cheapest principle that fits my list is simply that prior probability always trumps witness quality, say which verdicts it overturns and whether that is a bullet worth biting.

Please commit to one principle rather than offering me a menu, and mark the clause you consider weakest. And if the request rests on a confused premise — if these verdicts are Hume's own argument with modern furniture on it, so that there is no independent principle to extract, or if the cases are too heterogeneous to be governed by a single rule at all — say that plainly instead of writing politely around it.
