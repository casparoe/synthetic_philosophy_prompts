For the last four years I have maintained a digital twin of a large commercial building — a simulation used to predict heating and cooling loads from weather forecasts, occupancy schedules, and several hundred sensor streams. The system has been through three substantial versions. v1 was a lumped-parameter thermal network built by a mechanical engineer who has since left the company: it modeled the building as a handful of coupled zones linked by resistances and capacitances, and it was validated against a full season of sensor data before it was allowed to drive anything. v2 added a fitted empirical coefficient we called the soak factor, which captured a slow lag that nothing in v1 accounted for, along with some crude occupancy proxies; it met our production accuracy budget for four years. v3, which I wrote, is a gray-box rewrite — a physically motivated envelope model with learned residuals. When we decomposed the v3 fit, it turned out that the soak factor had been absorbing two entirely distinct effects: the thermal mass of a concrete slab nobody had documented, and a slow calibration drift in one class of temperature sensors. The coefficient is gone; the slab is now an explicit state variable and the drift is handled in the ingestion layer. What did survive, essentially unchanged, is v1's zone topology — the coupling structure between regions and the fact that loads propagate between neighbors with a delay. Our validation suite still treats v2 as a passing baseline, we keep it in the repository, and it continues to hit its accuracy targets on the same data.
