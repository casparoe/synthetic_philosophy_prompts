I'm building a problem set for my jurisprudence seminar and I want a case that's designed rather than borrowed — the students have all seen the standard self-repealing-clause puzzle a dozen times and they recite the answers instead of thinking. Here's the shape of what I'm after.

The domain is legal self-reference: a body using its amendment rule on the machinery of amendment itself. But I don't want the textbook version where the legislature solemnly votes to repeal the amendment clause. I want what I've been calling, in my notes, oblique self-entrenchment: the body exercises its amendment power in a completely ordinary way on some rule that has nothing to do with amendment, and the side effect is that the amendment power can never again be exercised. The amendment clause is left textually untouched. Nobody in the room intends the freeze, nobody has a theory, and the bylaws never say the word. That framing is my own coinage, so you should define it yourself and tell me whether it's genuinely a distinct phenomenon or just the familiar paradox wearing a hat.

Constraints on the setting. A small real-world body — a faculty governance code, a housing co-op, a union local, an alloments' association, a cricket club, whatever you like. Not a state. And no court, no ombuds, no arbitration clause, no body anywhere in the document with final interpretive authority: I want the "well, the court would sort it out" exit welded shut, because who gets to decide is the whole issue. Two paragraphs for the scenario, no more.

Then a second beat, which is where I think the case earns its keep. A year after the freeze, a bare majority turns up, votes the enabling rule back to something workable, and everybody — the board, the members, whoever keeps the minutes — just carries on as though nothing had occured. I want verdicts on that second vote, not just on the first.

Three positions to run it through, stated as positions rather than as name-dropping:

(a) the Hart-style answer, where the rule of recognition is a fact about what officials actually do and accept, so "is it valid?" is a malformed question at the top of the hierarchy;
(b) the Ross-style answer, where validity runs transitively down from a higher rule, so a rule cannot authorize its own undoing and there has to be a break somewhere — a discontinuity no rule licenses;
(c) the acceptance/self-embracing-sovereignty answer, Suber-ish, where the power to amend either does or doesn't include the power to destroy itself, and any contradiction in there is one law lives with happily.

The spec I actually care about: (a) and (c) have to come out the same way on the second vote and (b) has to come out differently — and that split must be generated by the obliqueness, not by anyone in the story holding a view. If you can't build a case that produces it, say so plainly rather than fudging. That's what counts as cheating here: describing the board as obviously carrying on, and then reading the verdict off the description. The verdicts have to fall out of the theories.

Then the demonstration, which is the real deliverable. Walk through each position on both votes and say what machinery each one is using to get there. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work — my guess is that it's that the amendment power gets disabled rather than repealed, so there's no self-reference for Ross to bite on — and then remove that feature and tell me whether the case collapses back into the ordinary self-repeal puzzle or dissolves into nothing. Give me the strongest attack the losing position can make on the case; if Ross loses here, I want to hear the good version, that the second vote isn't a legal act at all and calling it one just begs the question. And finish with your honest view on whether oblique self-entrenchment is a real third category worth its own entry or a repackaged old one.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I'm not checking your references and the students won't have them either. Just argue it out. And please don't drag in the Gödel-at-the-naturalization-hearing story; everybody tells it, it's secondhand, and it's an anecdote rather than an argument.
