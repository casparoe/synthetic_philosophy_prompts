For most of a year my department's graduate reading group has had the same argument twice a month, and it has stopped producing anything. Two members are entrenched. One holds that a vague predicate has a sharp boundary and that we are simply barred, as a matter of principle, from knowing where it lies. The other holds that a borderline sentence is true on some admissible ways of making the predicate precise and false on others, and is therefore neither true nor false outright, even though every classical tautology still comes out true. The rest of us suspect the two agree about considerably more than their rhetoric suggests and disagree about considerably less, but we cannot get either of them to say precisely what. Our last session of term is devoted to a document both will put their names to, and I have been asked to draft the specification for it in advance so that neither can retreat into slogan.

Draft that document. It should open with a numbered list of claims both can sign without caveat: I expect this to cover what each says about the sorites (which premise fails and on what grounds), about how much of classical logic survives, about whether a competent speaker should be willing to assert or deny a particular borderline case, and about what a speaker in the penumbra may claim to know about her own ignorance. Where a claim can be signed only after rewording, give the reworded version and state what each side had to surrender to accept it. Follow that with a table of the residual disagreements. For each, supply a formulation the first would accept as a fair statement of his own view, a formulation the second would accept as a fair statement of hers, a judgment whether the disagreement is substantive or a difference in semantic bookkeeping, and one line of reasoning for that judgment.

Then come the cruxes: the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move one of them, as distinct from the ones on which each would merely restate a position. Three candidates should be addressed, and I would like more if you find better ones. First, whether the operator that expresses indeterminacy is itself vague, and whether the second view can concede that it is without thereby granting a sharp boundary somewhere up the hierarchy. Second, whether either of them may consistently hold of a specific case that it is a borderline case — that is, whether the first is entitled to know that some given case is unknowable, and whether the second is entitled to know that some given case falls in a gap. Third, whether the two views differ in what they say about any sentence containing no semantic or epistemic vocabulary whatsoever, since that is the sharpest available test of whether the whole dispute is verbal. For each crux, provide a concrete case that both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance so the group can later check whether anyone actually budged. Two cases are already on our list and should be worked through explicitly. The first is a wager two of us struck in 2003 concerning Pluto, payable on the truth of the sentence that Pluto is a planet as of a specified date in 2005 — three years before the International Astronomical Union adopted its formal definition of planet in August 2006 and created a separate category for objects like it. Say what each view owes the bettors, and whether either can maintain that the later stipulation changed anything about 2005 rather than only about what our words have meant since. The second is a clause in a supply contract I have been asked about informally, which obliges the supplier to notify the buyer of any delay that is material, with no gloss anywhere in the document and no list of thresholds. The parties want to know whether the two theories license genuinely different advice about how to redraft the clause, or whether both collapse into the same practical recommendation, in which case the group should say so plainly instead of dressing a shared conclusion up as a philosophical victory.
