I attend a journal club run jointly by our philosophy department and the evolutionary biology group, and last week we read a paper arguing that the human chin is a non-adaptive byproduct of the remodelling of the face and jaw under a softer diet and reduced masticatory stress. The discussion afterwards turned almost entirely into an argument between two of the regulars — Inês, who works on developmental biology, and Tomás, who is writing a thesis on the history of the adaptationist debates — and I found myself unable to judge it, partly because I suspect they were talking past each other and partly because I am not sure which of them has the better of the terminology.

The exchange ran roughly as follows. (1) Inês said the chin is a textbook spandrel: nobody has produced evidence that it was shaped by selection, and the whole point of Gould and Lewontin's paper was to break the habit of assuming every feature comes with an adaptive story, so if a feature was not selected for, calling it a spandrel is precisely what the paper tells us to do. (2) Tomás said that is a misuse of the term, and a costly one: on the paper's own picture the surfaces in the basilica are spaces that arise necessarily from the geometry of the primary structures — the roughly triangular areas between the arches and the dome resting on them — which the mosaicists then filled with decoration, so non-adaptivity is necessary but nowhere near sufficient for a spandrel, which must be a byproduct of the form of something else. He added that Gould himself later grew irritated by the loose usage and pointed instead to exaptation, the term he and Elisabeth Vrba introduced in 1982 to replace the teleologically loaded "preadaptation", for features co-opted to a use they were not built for. (3) Inês replied that the architecture is an illustrative image rather than a definition, and that the paper's own methodological advice — begin from the alternative hypothesis that a given feature may not be a direct adaptation at all but a consequence of some other feature — makes non-adaptation the default, in which case "spandrel" simply names the default and needs no further gloss. (4) Tomás said that if the word means nothing more than "not shaped by selection", it is redundant with a set of quite distinct causes the paper itself canvassed, among them random drift, phylogenetic inertia and developmental or allometric constraints, and that its value lies in singling out one detectable kind of cause: features that follow from the design of the whole. (5) Inês conceded the distinction but called it idle in this instance, since what really divides them is empirical — the adaptationists have their sexual-selection and speech stories, she has the absence of evidence for any of them — and terminological policing lets the other side leave the burden of proof where it does not belong. (6) Tomás answered that a strict notion cuts both ways, because it obliges the non-adaptationist to name the primary structures and show that the feature follows from them, rather than merely failing to find a function. (7) Inês rejoined that the strict reading therefore buys nothing, since the analogy is architecturally wrong in the first place: the relevant surfaces at San Marco are pendentives, which are three-dimensional, load-bearing and chosen by the builders from among alternatives, which is why Dennett argued in *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* that they are not spandrels even in the extended sense — a criticism the architectural historian Robert Mark pushed back on by insisting the builders had very little real freedom.

Could you referee this move by move? I am not after a summary of the spandrels debate, and I do not want a bibliography — I want your own reasoning about the arguments as these two actually made them, on the assumption that I know the general controversy but have not read the primary literature closely. Please organise your answer in these sections, in this order: **Bottom line** (up to 100 words: who has the better of it overall, and what the chin should properly be called if either label is apt); **The terrain** (about 150 words: the two competing readings of "spandrel", plus the adjacent distinctions the exchange depends on, such as exaptation and the difference between adaptationism as a research programme and adaptationism as a claim about how often selection actually shapes traits); **Move-by-move** (the bulk, roughly 800 words: take moves (1) to (7) in order, rate each on a scale from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, justify each rating, and flag any move that is right for the wrong reason); **What both of them missed** (about 250 words); and **Where I would take it next** (about 120 words: the single question I should put to the group at the next session that would genuinely help settle the chin case rather than the verbal one).
