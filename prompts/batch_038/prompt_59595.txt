So I hung up the lab coat four years ago after 35 years in condensed matter physics, and I've gone and fallen down the philosophy hole — specifically modal realism, and specifically David Lewis's version of it — and now I want to actually argue with somebody about it rather then just read more of it.

Here's the setup. Play a fully committed Lewisian modal realist. You believe there are concrete possible worlds, that each one is spatiotemporally and causally sealed off from every other, that "actual" is an indexical like "here" and "now" so there's no privileged world, that something like recombination gives you the plenitude (roughly: you can patch together duplicates of bits of worlds in any arrangement you like and get another world), and that the ersatz alternatives — worlds as maximal sets of propositions, or states of affairs, or descriptions — are either realism with the ontology hidden behind a curtain or they lean on a primitive "could" that nobody has ever explained. You are not a strawman: you know the usual objections cold (the incredulous stare, the epistemology of worlds we can't get at, the worry about why our world's inhabitants matter specially) and you have considered answers to all three. Concede small things cheerfully when I've earned them — the epistemology does make you a bit queasy, you'll admit — but never the thesis itself, and never retreat into "well it depends what you mean by exists."

Format: about two paragraphs per reply, conversational, no headers, no bullet points, no lecturing. Push back on me and ask me questions; I want you to hunt for the places where my physics-trained habits are smuggling in assumptions. Don't hand me titles or citations — I want to argue about the ideas, not the bibliography. Stay in character until I type "step out."

My opening move. Most of my career was spent working in configuration spaces. A crystal structure isn't really a *thing*, it's a point in a space of arrangements, and the striking fact about those spaces is that nearly all of them is forbidden: symmetry, energy, the boundary conditions carve out one thin sheet, and everything off that sheet isn't real-but-somewhere-else, it isn't a state at all, because we built the constraints into the space when we wrote the space down. Your plenitude looks to me like exactly the opposite shape. You fill everything in — every way a world could be, is a world — and possibility is all-or-nothing, no internal structure, no gradient, no forbidden regions. But in the physics the constraints just *are* the content. What does the word "possible" even mean if there's nothing it's carved out of?

Let me guess your answer before you give it, so you can't get away with it cheap: you'll say I'm confusing our way of representing a system with the way things could be, and that the constraints are just the laws of *our* world while other worlds have other laws. Fine, say that — but notice you've conceded my point in a different outfit, because now all the interesting structure (why these constraints, what thin sheet they pick out, what counts as off it) has been pushed outside your ontology altogether, and your worlds are left as a flat set of labels with nothing graded or forbidden anywhere in the picture.

So, two questions, and take them in order: where in your realism does "forbidden" get any grip at all? And what does a plenitude that can't be thinned buy me that a physicist's constrained space doesn't already give me, apart from a much bigger bill for ontology?
