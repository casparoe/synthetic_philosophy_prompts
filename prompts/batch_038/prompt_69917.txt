I TA a second-year course on political liberty and the Berlin week goes the same way every single year. Students love negative liberty because it sounds modest and anti-utopian, they get very pleased with themselves, and then about forty minutes in someone describes a situation where nobody has actually interfered with anybody and the room goes quiet because none of the machinery they just learned handles it. I'm tired of lecturing my way through that silence, so this year I want to hand out a piece of comedy at the start of section and let the argument happen to them. Please play a political theorist who also writes sketch comedy — someone who has read Hobbes, Bentham and Berlin closely and affectionately, and who is very funny about the gap between a creed that is internally valid and a life that doesn't fit it. Think the structure through carefully before you start (work out the beats silently, don't show me any brainstorming or notes), and then just write the thing.

The form is a syndicated advice column called "The Free Agent," written by a columnist you should name — I'd suggest something like Marlowe Vance, but your call. Around 750 words, masthead line and all, three letters and three replies. Marlowe is a strict negative libertarian and I need her to be a *good* one, not a strawman: for her, freedom is the absence of interference with what you actually want to do; a power someone holds but has never once exercised is not an interference; a possibility is not an obstacle; and every law, every permit and every enforceable promise genuinely does diminish somebody's liberty no matter how worthy it is or who it protects. Every inference she draws has to be one Hobbes or Bentham would happily sign. Letter one is from a tenant — call her Nadia, signing off as something like GRATEFUL IN GARFIELD HEIGHTS. She rents the top floor of Warren's house on a rolling month-to-month thing after her original lease lapsed years ago; Warren is genuinely, unfakeably lovely, he fixed the porch steps himself, brought her soup when she had the flu, hasn't raised the rent in six years, let her keep a cat that the paperwork forbids. He has never once told her what to do. But she doesn't have friends over after nine, she hasn't mentioned the damp spreading behind the wardrobe in three winters, she laughs at his jokes harder than they deserve, she moved her job to be nearer and her kid's school is four blocks away so leaving isn't really on the table, and she keeps saying he's "like family." Her question should be sincere and slightly embarrassed: is she making a fuss about nothing, because she can't find a single thing he's done to her. Marlowe's reply is a clean sweep — she should tally the interferences, find zero, congratulate Nadia on a liberty most people would envy, and then treat the proposed cure (asking for a written lease) as a naked attempt to reduce *Warren's* freedom. Letter two is from a laundromat owner who is furious about an annual boiler inspection and a permit for his sidewalk sign, and here Marlowe has to be just as rigorous in the other direction: yes, those are interferences, yes, his liberty is genuinely reduced, and her sympathy is total. That inversion is the engine of the piece, so let it land on its own without anyone pointing at it. Letter three is short — a married woman who read the first answer three times and recognises her own house in it, eleven years, never raised his voice, never told her what to do — and her closing line should do all the work, something quiet like her deciding not to bring it up after all.

A few hard constraints, because this is where it either becomes good or becomes a student sketch. Warren must stay kind: no reveal, no hidden cruelty, no menacing subtext, nothing bad ever actually happens to Nadia — if he turns out to be a bad guy after all the whole exercise collapses. The theory has to be staged in the details rather than named, so the following words and names are banned from the piece entirely: domination, non-domination, republican liberty, arbitrary power, Berlin, Pettit, Skinner, Lovett. Also, please have the letters smuggle in the stuff that actually distinguishes the two conceptions, without labelling any of it: the difference between someone interfering and someone merely holding the power to; the fact that "arbitrary" in the technical sense means subject to another's will rather than random or moody (Warren isn't capricious at all, he's consistently sweet, and that's precisely why the tenant's situation is hard to describe); the sheer cost of her walking away; the fact that nothing constrains Warren to take her interests into account when he decides; the way she has already reshaped her own behaviour in anticipation of wishes nobody expressed; her inability to look him in the eye as an equal; and the upside-down bit at the end, that a lease and a notice requirement would be interference that makes her freer rather than less free. No real quotations anywhere — if you want an epigraph for the column, invent one. Keep it deadpan: no winking, no knowing asides, no character who is in on the joke, and absolutely no paragraph at the end explaining what the satire was about. Your response should start directly with the column itself, masthead first, with no preface, no framing, and no commentary before or after.
