**Question 3 (25 marks)**

In a recent paper, the analytic epistemologist H. Kestrel proposes the following test for when a candidate source of knowledge deserves to be counted as a source in its own right: a source is genuinely distinct only if it can deliver a belief that no other source could have delivered. Kestrel then argues that testimony, inference, and reasoning from likeness all fail this test against perception, on the grounds that whatever they deliver could in principle have been delivered by going and looking for yourself; the other three, on this view, are routes to what perception could have reached by a longer path.

Set out how the Nyāya school defines each of its four pramāṇas, and then assess whether the Nyāya system has the resources to resist Kestrel's argument. Where some Nyāya notion does resist it, identify the mechanism by which it does so; where the system genuinely seems exposed, say so plainly.

**Candidate's script**

The Nyāya-sūtra opens by placing pramāṇa first among the sixteen topics it proposes to treat, and then names four instruments of veridical cognition: perception, pratyakṣa; inference, anumāna; comparison, upamāna; and testimony, śabda. Perception is defined as a cognition arising from the contact of a sense with its object, which is non-verbal, avyapadeśya, non-deviating, avyabhicāri, and determinate, vyavasāyātmaka. Inference is a cognition that arises on the apprehension of a mark, and it depends on vyāpti, the pervasion of the reason by what is to be proved; this is why the Naiyāyika refuses to accept a pervasion established from a single observation, and insists on repeated experience together with the non-appearance of counterexamples in the vipakṣa. When an inference is expressed it has five members: the pratijñā, or thesis, as in the claim that the hill has fire; the hetu, or reason, namely that it has smoke; the udāharaṇa, which states the pervasion along with a familiar instance, as in a kitchen; the upanaya, which applies the rule to the case at hand, noting that the hill has smoke of that sort; and the nigamana, which draws the conclusion. Reasons that look like reasons but are not, the hetvābhāsas, are catalogued for the same purpose, the irregular, savyabhicāra, and the contradictory, viruddha, being the best known. Testimony is the statement of an āpta, and the comprehension of a sentence is said to require mutual expectancy among the words, ākāṅkṣā, semantic fitness, yogyatā, contiguity, āsatti, and the speaker's sincerity, tātparya.

That exposition is standard, but the fourfold list is really a piece of pedagogy, and Nyāya's own machinery shows that only perception is a pramāṇa in the strict sense. This is what Kestrel's argument says, and I think the school is committed to conceding it.

Begin with inference. The mark must be perceived on the subject of the inference, and the pervasion that licenses the step must itself have been built up by repeatedly perceiving smoke and fire together in kitchens and the like. What we call inference is therefore perception plus bookkeeping: it establishes no contact with the world that looking had not already supplied. Nyāya all but admits this in its account of the operative cause of an inferential cognition, parāmarśa, which the commentators effectively add as a sixth member to the five above, being the recollection of the pervasion as belonging to the subject in question. If the argument has to carry that extra member simply to explain where its content came from, the content came from perception.

Comparison is a similar case. In the stock example, a traveller is told by a forester that a gavaya is an animal like a cow; later, in the forest, he sees an animal resembling a cow and thinks that this is what the word stands for. That is a loose inference whose reason is a perceived resemblance, and it is kept apart in the list only because the school never developed a general account of analogy.

Testimony looks harder to absorb, since its content is not the sort of thing one can go and look at: I cannot inspect what a sentence means. But the āpta condition gives the reduction away. A statement yields knowledge only if its speaker is trustworthy, and the hearer's sole route to that trustworthiness is an induction from experience, namely that those who speak as they know are usually right. Testimony is therefore known to be reliable by an inference about the speaker, and that inference rests on perceived regularities. Kestrel's test is met by perception alone.
