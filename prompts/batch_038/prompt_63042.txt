I spent thirty-seven years reading deep-sea sediment cores, mostly in service of the orbital theory of the ice ages, where one learns to be careful about which quantities are parameters and which are variables. Since retiring I have been auditing a continuing-education seminar in historiography, and we have just worked through the first part of Braudel's book on the Mediterranean in the age of Philip II — the near-immobile history of plains against mountains, coasts against interiors — alongside the popular bestseller that attributes the modern distribution of wealth to the east–west axis of Eurasia and its stock of domesticable species. In the third session I raised what seemed to me a modest objection: that a particular seventeenth-century run of harvest failures, and one admiral's decision to sail rather than wait, appeared to do genuine explanatory work that the slow structural facts did not. The seminar leader, a retired development economist, disposed of it with "geography is destiny," and the room accepted the phrase as though it settled the matter. People attach it now to Napoleon, now to Herodotus, now to nobody at all; I am not interested in its provenance, only in its content, and I have found that when I try to state what I think it must mean, the statement either becomes trivial or becomes something my colleague would not endorse.

What I should like is the full menu of distinct precise theses that this single sentence can be made to express, ordered from the least to the most committal, and treated as rival hypotheses rather than as shades of one opinion. For each reading, please give me: an explicit statement with its quantifiers visible — which features count as geography, over what interval of time, and what "destiny" is doing, whether necessity, high conditional probability, restriction of the feasible set of outcomes, a mere ranking of likelihoods, or irreversibility once a path is taken; a verdict on its epistemic status (analytic truism, substantive and defensible, empirically testable and probably false, or so vague as to be empty); the observation that would distinguish it from its nearest neighbour in the list; and, most importantly, whether it would in fact license dismissing my objection about the harvests and the admiral. My own tentative candidates, offered in the idiom of my old trade and quite possibly misfiled, are these: geography as an initial condition that is quickly washed out; geography as a standing boundary condition that never stops constraining; geography as an adiabatic parameter, changing so slowly that the fast variables equilibrate to it and can then be treated as functions of it; geography as defining the admissible region of trajectory space without selecting a point in it; and geography as shifting priors over outcomes in a way that leaves individual events free. Please do not simply restate these, and do not restrict yourself to them — I expect there are readings involving the direction of explanation, or the difference between a cause and a background condition, that I have not thought of.

Two further complications should be addressed within the menu rather than in a separate appendix. The first is that geography does not look to me like a constant: the same feature is an endowment or a burden depending on the state of technique and organisation, since a navigable river is nothing without vessels, a coalfield nothing without an engine that can use it, and a malarial plain ceases to be one when drainage and prophylaxis arrive. If a reading must be indexed to a technological level in order to be true, then the "slow" variable acquires fast components, and I want to know what that does to its claim to explain away the events. The second is the comparative test: for each reading, say what would count against it in the presence of regions with closely similar physical endowments and markedly divergent histories, or the reverse, and whether the reading is in principle falsifiable by such pairs or can always absorb them. I am not asking for references or suggested reading — I have the books and plenty of time — only for the analysis. Present the readings as a numbered list with the four fields above, and close with a final line beginning "Verdict:" naming by number the single reading that would actually have supported my colleague's dismissal, or stating that none of them does.
