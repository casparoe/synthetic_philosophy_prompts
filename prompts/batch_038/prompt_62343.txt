I retired four years ago from a museum entomology department, where I spent the better part of three decades studying an invasive, unicolonial ant whose colonies have fused along several hundred kilometres of coastline into a single enormous population with no aggression between distant nests. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind as an amateur, beginning, as I suspect many of us do, with Hofstadter's dialogue about an ant colony that turns out to be a mind when seen from a sufficient distance. Next month I am to give a fifty-minute talk with twenty-five minutes of discussion at an interdisciplinary workshop on comparative cognition, and my thesis is deliberately blunt: that the supercolony I have worked on is one mind rather than a great many small ones, that the individual workers are its functional parts and not its subjects, and that this is not a metaphor I am asking the audience to indulge but a hypothesis with a testable criterion. I want to be properly prepared for the question period, because I have learned that being right about the insects is no protection at all in a room full of philosophers.

The criterion I intend to defend is disconnection rather than report. Over nine seasons I repeatedly severed the chemical trail connections between two large nest clusters at a single choke point, using barriers that blocked recruitment without killing anyone, and then removed the barriers again. Three results are the backbone of the talk. First, before severing, the colony as a whole allocated foragers among a set of baited patches in a way that tracked relative profitability better than either cluster did afterwards; after severing, each fragment continued to allocate, but each persistently over-committed to a poor patch that had previously been exploited jointly and then abandoned jointly. Second, two clusters that had been mutually tolerant for a decade began, within a fortnight of severing, to fight along the barrier line and to raid each other's brood; the conflict was directed at the fragment that had been, until then, part of the same self. Third, when the barrier came down after a season, joint allocation returned within weeks, but the competence seemed to be carried by only one side: workers from the fragment that had retained the denser trail network appeared to remember, while workers from the other fragment behaved as though freshly recruited to a solution they had once helped produce. My claim is that this is the colony analogue of a commissurotomy, and that what makes a system one mind, in the only sense an outsider can ever establish, is what happens to it when you cut its connecting medium: whether the pieces each retain the whole's competences, whether they come into conflict over the same goal, and whether acquired competence transfers asymmetrically on reunion.

The audience will be about forty people, and I should like the objections calibrated to them rather than to a general reader: two analytic philosophers of mind, one sympathetic to the idea that the same mental states can be built out of very different stuff but deeply suspicious of any slide toward everything having a mind, the other a sceptic who holds that talk of a single point of view requires a single unified subject and will want to know how I avoid simply relocating the problem into the colony as a whole; a behavioural neuroscientist who works with split-brain patients and will press on whether anything in my barrier data licenses a claim about experience rather than about distributed control; a behavioural ecologist who considers colony-level decisions to be nothing but feedback loops with an adaptive history and will call my framing anthropomorphism, even while conceding that the colony does things no worker can do; a swarm-robotics engineer who will agree with me loudly for engineering reasons and may turn out to be a worse liability than the sceptics; and a scattering of graduate students who have recently been reading about cognition in organisms without nervous systems at all. Please prepare me a sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, in the order they are likely to arrive, phrased in the idiom each of these people would use rather than in the form they take in popular writing; a two-minute reply to each; an honest note on where each reply is soft; which objections would sink the talk if I fumbled them and which are merely irritating; and what I should concede in the first ten minutes so that two of them never have to be made. Then tell me the question I am dreading. I have a suspicion about what it is — either that my criterion of unity is doing the work I am crediting to the data, or that I myself published, years ago, evidence that my workers learn individually — but I should like to hear it put the way the neuroscientist will put it, and to be given something better to say than what I have been saying to myself in the garden.
