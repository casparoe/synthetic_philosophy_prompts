my grandmother died tuesday, nine days after her 88th birthday. I'm giving the eulogy on saturday at the funeral home, last item before they close the casket, and the director has given me five minutes. I need you to write it for me.

Here's the shape of the problem, and it isn't only a family problem. She had dementia for about seven years. By the end she didn't know my name, but she'd hum along if anyone sang and she'd squeeze your hand back if you held it. My aunt — her daughter — has said out loud at every gathering for three years that mum "left in 2019" and that we're burying an empty house, and she gets real comfort from that. My dad — her son — can't stand it, he says she was there the whole time right up to tuesday, and that anyone who says otherwise is throwing away her last seven years to make themselves feel better. They aren't speaking. I'm standing between them with a microphone.

And here's why this is a writing problem and not just a hard weekend. I did a philosophy degree and I have never been able to accept that my aunt is right about there being a fact of the matter — that some specific Tuesday she stopped being herself and the Monday before she hadn't. Not because I think there's a sharp cutoff we just can't identify either, which is the other camp in that argument. I'm on the side that thinks the question is more like asking which grain of sand was the last one before it stopped being a heap: no right answer, but not nothing either. And the thing I've actually been living on for the past year, the only thing that has helped, is a consequence of that view that I don't know how to get into a funeral — that a disjunction can be true even when neither of its halves is. So "either she had already gone by then or she hadn't" is sayable, and each half on its own is not.

That has to be the rule the speech runs on, not decoration or a wink. Which means I can't tell my aunt she's wrong, I can't tell my dad he's right, and I can't explain to either of them why. The words vague, borderline, precisification, heap, and any of the -isms don't appear anywhere in it. If the idea arrives at all it arrives as an image, and the room receives it as grief rather than as a position. Both of them will be in the front row listening for whether I've picked a side, and I need neither of them to be able to catch me out or to feel abandoned.

Material so you have something to work with: 41 years as a bank teller, then she kept the parish books in pencil for another twenty, and she could not stand the word "about." If you told her you'd spent about forty dollars she made you go get the reciept. She'd erase and redo a whole page rather than round a number off. She lost the ability to count before she lost anything else — she'd get three words into a sentence and the sentence would go somewhere else, and she'd look annoyed about it.

Room is maybe sixty people: family, the parish crowd, two friends of mine who did the same degree, and a lot of people who have never met anyone who thinks like this. Half of them are religious and half aren't and I'm not, so no afterlife promises and nothing that requires belief, but nothing that sneers either. Don't say she's at peace, don't say journey, don't say we've lost her. She hated sentimentality, so if it gets soft it should be because of what's true and not because of the adjectives.

Five minutes spoken, so somewhere around 700 words. Just write the speech, no notes about it afterwards.
