I am running a ninety-minute session on common knowledge for second-years on a joint philosophy-and-economics programme. Roughly half have had one term of game theory — Nash equilibrium, nothing epistemic — and half one term of logic — truth tables, no modal operators. At least three of them have encountered the blue-eyed islanders puzzle online and are convinced they already understand the material; I would like advice on whether to exploit that or to fight it. I am not asking you to explain the idea to me. I am asking for the plan.

First, the order of ideas. Common knowledge of a fact and common knowledge of rationality do different work in this session, and the second has to earn the backward-induction result in the closing twenty minutes. Lay out what must be in place before what, and state the dependencies explicitly: where the contrast between a public announcement and a set of private ones belongs; where the hierarchy of levels belongs relative to it; at what point Aumann's result that rational agents with a shared prior cannot agree to disagree can responsibly be mentioned without derailing the arc; and how one crosses from a fact's being common knowledge to the players' rationality being common knowledge, without glossing the second as a trivial extension of the first. I would also like your judgement on whether the centipede game is the right vehicle for that crossing, or whether it backfires by making rationality look absurd to a room that has only just been persuaded it is a coherent idealisation.

Second, the carrying example. I have drafted one and I am not certain it holds. A departmental seminar in which the speaker is inaudible and nobody follows anything, yet nobody asks a question, because each assumes the others are following; then one person says they have lost the thread, and half the room asks the same question at once. I want a variant in which that interruption is heard clearly by the front rows and reaches the back only as a murmur, to show that the shift is not all-or-nothing. Tell me whether this example can bear the weight, where it leaks, and whether the mumbled variant really delivers the point Rubinstein's electronic mail game makes or something considerably weaker. Say what a carrying example for this idea must have and must lack if the difference between everyone knowing and everyone knowing that everyone knows is to register as a difference in behaviour rather than in phrasing. If mine fails, replace it — but keep it inside a seminar room and off islands, and keep Lewis's account of convention in view, since the students will want to know why any coordination needs this machinery at all.

Third, the misconceptions, mapped onto that sequence rather than listed. The two I expect are the collapse of common knowledge into universal knowledge, and the objection that an infinite regress can never be completed so the notion is a useful fiction; I also anticipate pluralistic ignorance being offered as a counterexample, and the claim that any coordinated action requires common knowledge. For each, identify the precise point in the order at which it should be headed off, what I should say at that point, and what to do about the ones that surface only after the material that should have prevented them.

Fourth, one question I can put at the end whose answers would distinguish the students who have grasped the idea from those who have merely learned to repeat the definition.

Fifth, what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out — the fixed-point axiomatisation, the partition and Kripke representations, the surprise-examination paradox, the equilibrium analysis of the email game, and anything else you judge to be load-bearing but out of reach here.
