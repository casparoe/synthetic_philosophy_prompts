I've been arguing for three years with a friend who is an orthodox subjective Bayesian about the Ellsberg urn. His position: the 90-ball setup licenses a determinate credence, so preferring the red bet to the black bet while preferring the non-black bet to the non-red bet is a straightforward violation of the sure-thing principle and therefore irrational, unless the subject covertly suspects the experimenter of rigging the urn. Mine: the description fails to pin down a unique credence in "black," so there is nothing for the axiom to be violated by, and the apparent inconsistency is an artifact of forcing a single number into a representation that the evidence never supported. We've agreed to run a genuine adversarial collaboration and produce one document we can both sign. Draft it. First, a numbered section of claims we can both accept — about what the setup does and doesn't establish, about Savage's own small-world restriction on his axioms, about whether a prior-over-priors representation with a non-linear second-order attitude reproduces the same choices, and about what a dynamic Dutch book argument can and cannot show against a non-Bayesian rule. Then a disagreements table, where each row is phrased so that the side holding that view would endorse the wording used for it. Mark the cruxes — the sub-questions where a settled answer would actually move one of us — and for each, name the case or argument we'd both accept as bearing on it and record each of our predictions in advance, before anyone looks. I want one specific test case included among the cruxes: a market version in which the urn's composition has already been fixed and is known to your counterparty but deliberately withheld from you, so that you must quote a price for a bet on black against someone better informed. In that setting ambiguity-sensitivity widens the spread and protects you from adverse selection; have each side say whether that makes it rational, merely prudent, or neither. Finally, say which stretches of the dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and why — and don't hand the win to either of us.
