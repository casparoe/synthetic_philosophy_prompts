I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long read about why psychohistory keeps resurfacing in our public conversation — in superforecasting, in the longtermist scene, in the moral-arc rhetoric that runs from Theodore Parker to Martin Luther King Jr. and thence into every commencement address since. My editor has asked me to ground the piece in the original novels rather than in the TED-talk version of them, so I have gone back through Asimov's trilogy — *Foundation* (1951), *Foundation and Empire* (1952), *Second Foundation* (1953) — and I want help reading it as a work in the philosophy of history rather than as a prediction-market parable. Please leave the recent television adaptation out of it entirely; it changes enough that I do not want the two conflated in print. Here is the reading I have arrived at, which I would like you to test, sharpen, or demolish. There seem to me to be two quite different ways of holding that history has a direction and a destination. One is immanent: the arc is built into the structure of events themselves, so that the endpoint emerges from the ordinary play of interests and passions — the way Hegel's cunning of reason, Smith's invisible hand, Marx's laws of motion, or Kant's hope for a universal history with a cosmopolitan aim, assembled out of unsocial sociability, are each supposed to work. The other is custodial: the arc holds only because someone stands outside the process and steers it back whenever it deviates, as in a providential history of the Augustinian or Bossuet type, where events are guided by a will rather than guaranteed by a law. My claim is that Asimov begins with the first and ends with the second, and that the migration is the most interesting thing in the books. Seldon's plan is introduced as a statistical science of aggregates, indifferent to individuals, promising only that the interregnum will last a thousand years instead of thirty thousand. But the plan cannot see a single mutant, and what repairs it is not a law of history: it is a hidden college of mentalists who adjust minds, stage their own apparent destruction on Terminus, and manage the First Foundation's sense of its own freedom from the old imperial capital. If that is right, Asimov has not given us a secular providence at all. He has given us a providence with a staff.

Three places I want you to press, and I want the answers faithful to the books rather than to my thesis. First: does the trilogy anywhere claim the arc bends toward *justice*, or only toward order and continuity? The means by which the plan advances — Hardin's fabricated religion, Mallow's commercial coercion, the Second Foundation's quiet editing of emotions and beliefs — are precisely the means a theory of justice would indict, and my suspicion is that the books notice and shrug, treating persons as instruments of a millennium-scale outcome. Is that shrug an argument, a blind spot, or the actual thesis? Second: is the Mule a genuine problem of evil for psychohistory, the contingency that any providential scheme must fail to absorb, or is he a narrative convenience that lets Asimov keep his determinism and retain a villain? Note that the Mule is ultimately stopped not by the mathematics but by a chain of individual decisions — above all Bayta's killing of Ebling Mis before the psychologist can name a location, which is what leaves the First Speaker able to turn him at all. Does the trilogy thereby concede that persons, not populations, are where history's direction gets decided? Third, the reflexivity worry. As I read the opening story, Seldon lays down that psychohistory holds only over populations that do not know they are being predicted, yet the plan's entire institutional life revolves around a vault that periodically opens and broadcasts the long-dead founder explaining the present crisis to everyone standing in the room. Is that a contradiction Asimov never noticed, or is the vault doing something subtler — a controlled dose of foreknowledge, calibrated so that belief in the plan never becomes belief strong enough to change it? And is the framing device, those excerpts from the *Encyclopedia Galactica* set at the head of each section and written from a future in which the outcome is already settled, a way of smuggling a Whig reading into the typography of the book?

The deliverable is criticism, not summary: assume I know the plot and the cast, and tell me what the trilogy thinks. Where your account has to strain the novels to make the philosophy neat, say so, and say which way the strain runs; be willing to conclude that the immanent-versus-custodial distinction is mine and not Asimov's, and if you think the later turn to Gaia and Galaxia in *Foundation's Edge* amounts to Asimov conceding that a statistical teleology was never enough, fold that in. What I most want, in the end, is a judgement on what the novels contribute to the argument about historical teleology that a philosopher making the same points discursively could not — something about how it feels to live inside a story whose ending has already been guaranteed, or about the moral cost of being a variable in someone else's providence. Two colleagues have read an early draft and object that I am being uncharitable: the Second Foundation, they say, is Seldon's own instrument rather than a rival providence, a plan is still a plan however many hands maintain it, and the whole point of a custodian is that it was designed rather than discovered. Take that objection seriously; if it defeats my reading, I would rather learn so before the piece goes to press. Please give me no bibliography, no suggested further reading, and no quotation from the text — I have all three volumes on my desk, and I would rather have your interpretation than your page numbers.
