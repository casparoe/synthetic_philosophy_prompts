I've got a draft of an essay on the two-envelope paradox for a department blog and I want a real read on it, not encouragement. The thesis is that the puzzle was never about rational choice or expected value at all — it's a mis-specified measure, same species as Bertrand's chord paradox, and philosophers who keep filing it under "paradoxes of expectation" are chasing the wrong thing. I know that's an over-confident way to put it, which is part of why I want pushback.

Draft:

1. Set-up. Two envelopes, one has twice the other. You pick one, don't open it. Naive argument: whatever's in mine, call it x, the other has either 2x or x/2 with probability 1/2 each, so switching gives expected value (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 1.25x. Switching is good. But the argument is symmetric, so it should also say switching back is good. Contradiction with itself somewhere.

2. The three replies people give. (a) You can't take expectations of quantities that aren't bounded, so the calculation is just illegal. (b) The 1/2 isn't a probability you're allowed to have — once you condition on seeing a particular amount, a proper prior over the pair of amounts fixes the conditional, and it almost never comes out to 1/2. (c) The two branches of the calculation are two different games being averaged together, so the "expectation" isn't an expectation of anything.

3. My claim is that (b) is the only substantive one, and the reason to think so is that (b) can make the paradoxical conclusion true rather than merely dissolved. Take smaller amounts distributed over powers of two with p_k ∝ (2/3)^k. If you open your envelope and see 2^k, the odds that it's the smaller against its being the larger are p_k : p_{k-1}, and the expected gain from switching is proportional to 2^k p_k − 2^(k-1) p_{k-1}. That's positive exactly when p_k/p_{k-1} > 1/2, which holds at every k for this prior. So under a perfectly ordinary proper prior, switching is favorable no matter what you're holding. The naive argument reaches a conclusion that is sometimes correct.

4. Which is exactly why such priors feel like cheating: they can't be normalized with a finite mean. If switching is favorable at every amount then p_k > (1/2)^k p_0 for all k, so the mean of the smaller amount is bounded below by p_0 · Σ1 and blows up. Always-switch is available only at the price of an infinite expected stake.

5. Bertrand's chord paradox runs the same way. "Pick a random chord" gives you three defensible answers to the same question because "random" was doing unacknowledged work; specify the measure and every version evaporates. Neither case is about the world being strange. Both are about a word that looks like it picks out a distribution and doesn't.

6. Objection I expect. If the mean is infinite, expected value has stopped being a usable decision criterion, so section 3 doesn't vindicate always-switch — it just moves the puzzle into decision theory with infinite stakes, where you're back to needing a real diagnosis. I concede this. But the concession is my thesis: once you fix the measure the probability puzzle is over, and whatever remains is a question about how to decide under infinite mean, which is a different and much better-posed question.
