I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and the closing session takes up whether qualia — conceived as intrinsic properties of experience that do no causal work whatsoever — amount to a coherent posit at all. Most of the hour goes to Chase and Sanborn's "Epiphenomenal Qualia" and their coffee-drinker case. So that the poem does not depend on your having the paper to hand, here is the case as I present it:

- **Chase** is a longtime coffee drinker who now finds unpleasant the coffee he once loved. He is certain something has changed, but he cannot say how the taste differs from what he remembers, and nothing in his powers of comparing, ranking, or discriminating coffees has changed in the slightest.
- **Sanborn** is a neurophysiologist who has spent a career studying the coffee taste system. His discriminative powers have quietly eroded — he can no longer reliably tell a fresh roast from a stale one, or rank two cups the way he used to — yet he insists, sincerely and without hesitation, that the coffee tastes to him exactly as it always has.

The pressure point is that from the inside these two futures are indistinguishable: each man's testimony is equally available to either diagnosis, and neither can say what, if anything, remains once the capacities and the dispositions to report on them have been fully accounted for. I want a poem that enacts this rather than announcing it, to hand out at the end of class.

Please write two stanzas of equal length — eight lines each would suit — followed by a coda of no more than three lines. The form is where the content lives, so the following constraints are the substance of the request:

1. The two stanzas must be **interchangeable**. Each should read on its own as a plausible first-person testimony from either man, and each should be equally compatible with either diagnosis: that something changed in the taste, or that something changed in the tasting. A reader should be able to swap the stanzas with no loss or incongruity. If you label them at all, label them in a way that makes the labeling feel arbitrary; I would rather the men go unnamed until the coda, if they are named there.
2. At least four lines must appear **verbatim in both stanzas**, in different positions, so that the two futures turn out to be built from the same words. The remaining lines may differ, but only in ways that a second reading exposes as differences of arrangement rather than of content.
3. No abstract vocabulary. The poem may not use the words *qualia*, *quale*, *consciousness*, *experience*, *phenomenal*, *intrinsic*, or *epiphenomenal*. Everything must be carried by the coffee, the cup, the tongue, the kitchen, the ranking, the memory of the ranking, the machine that measures the nerve. If the idea cannot survive being said that way, leave it out.
4. The coda must not resolve the puzzle or take a side. It should register what the two men have left to say to one another — if anything — and leave the reader unable to determine which of them, if either, has lost something.
5. Rhyme is optional, but if you use it the scheme must be identical in both stanzas; the sameness of the pattern is part of the argument. Meter may be loose. Keep the whole piece under forty lines.

Accuracy matters as much as effect: do not let either man contradict the case as I have set it out. Chase must neither gain nor lose a discriminative power, and Sanborn must never concede a change in the taste itself. Someone who has read the paper should recognize the argument in the structure and not merely in the subject; someone who has not should still find it a good poem about coffee going wrong.
