I am preparing a handout for an advanced graduate seminar on distributive justice, and a classmate who came to philosophy by way of welfare economics has given me the following argument. I find it disturbing, because if it goes through, the distinction on which three weeks of my syllabus depends — the distinction between the priority view and telic egalitarianism — turns out to be merely notational. Here is the argument, in his numbering, together with the arithmetic he supplied.

Notation. Fix a population of three and let x_i be person i's well-being on a 0–100 scale. For a distribution D and a strictly increasing function w, write V_w(D) = Σ_i w(x_i).

1. The priority view holds that the moral value of a distribution is V_w(D) for some strictly increasing, strictly concave w applied to absolute well-being levels, and that the concavity is the entire content of the claim that a benefit matters more the worse off the person who receives it.
2. For a fixed population and a fixed total, V_w ranks distributions exactly by equality: the more equal always scores higher. Moreover this ranking does not depend on which concave w is chosen; concavity as such does all the work.
3. Telic egalitarianism, for a fixed population and a fixed total, ranks distributions the same way — the more equal is better — except that for the egalitarian this is a distinct, non-derivative source of value rather than a transform of well-being.
4. Therefore, on the class C of same-population, same-total comparisons, the priority view and telic egalitarianism induce one and the same ordering.
5. The leveling-down cases lie inside C. A leveling down rearranges a fixed stock of well-being among the same people without adding to it; nothing is created, so the stock in play is unchanged, and what differs before and after is only how that stock is spread.
6. Therefore the two views agree on every comparison capable of exhibiting the leveling-down objection.
7. Two views that agree on every comparison capable of distinguishing them are not two views.
8. Therefore the priority view does not escape the objection. It is committed to the egalitarian's verdict on the very move that generates it: from D1 = (64, 36, 25) to D2 = (49, 36, 25), where the best-off person loses 15 and nobody gains, the distribution becomes more equal, and the only difference between the two theories is that the priority theorist records that gain to equality as a small credit on a single scale that is then swamped by the larger debit for the loss. With w(x) = √x, V(D1) = 19 and V(D2) = 18.
9. So Parfit's third position, between egalitarianism and utilitarianism, collapses into the first.

He offered (2) as a matter of evidence rather than proof, and told me to try to break the w-independence claim from inside C. I spent an evening with w(x) = √x and w(x) = ln x and failed to produce a disagreement. The pairs I tried, both same-total:

- (45, 40, 40) against (64, 36, 25), total 125 each. Under √: 19.357 against 19.000. Under ln: 11.184 against 10.961. Same order both times.
- (80, 30, 30) against (70, 50, 20), total 140 each. My notebook records these as agreeing too, with the sums written in but never rechecked.

My questions are these. First: which step should I reject, and why did it look unobjectionable? I want the diagnosis to be specific rather than thematic — name the step, identify what it equivocates on or what it assumes without earning it, and demonstrate the failure instead of asserting it. Second: price the alternatives. For each of (2), (5), and (7), tell me what a prioritarian actually gives up if that is the premise she abandons, and in the case of (7) say whether the egalitarian's habit of comparing outcomes respect by respect, better in one way and worse in another, has any representation at all in a theory whose only output is a single number; if it has none, say whether that absence is itself a substantive difference between the views that (7) is too crude to register. Third: if the failure is in (5), do not stop at noting that a leveling down destroys well-being rather than merely redistributing it. Tell me what the priority view's verdicts look like on the comparisons that fall outside C, where totals differ, and whether those verdicts are any more defensible than the egalitarian's verdict on the leveling-down case itself. Fourth: recheck the second pair against both weightings and report what you get. If the two weightings do disagree there, explain what the disagreement is a disagreement about — what question the choice of w is answering that the vocabulary of equality and inequality never manages to ask, and whether that question is one the egalitarian should want asked.

If I am forced to choose, I would rather surrender (7), since I have no independent attachment to any criterion of theory identity, than surrender (2) or (5), which look to me like a plain fact about concave functions and a plain description of what a leveling down does. I suspect that this preference is exactly what the argument is exploiting, and I should like to be told so if it is.
