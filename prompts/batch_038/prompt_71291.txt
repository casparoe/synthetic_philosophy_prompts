I have just begun a placement on an NHS early intervention service as part of a clinical psychology doctorate, and one of the first things I was asked to do was to write a formulation for a client carrying a psychosis diagnosis. What I produced was, in effect, a causal story told entirely in psychological terms: the client's conviction that other people in the room are quietly appraising him was said to cause him to scan faces; the scanning was said to cause him to notice a great many neutral expressions; those neutral expressions were said to be taken as confirmation, because a face that gives nothing away is exactly what a hostile observer would present; and the confirmation was said to feed back into the conviction, tightening it. In the same meeting the consultant psychiatrist described the same man as having a disorder of salience attribution, in which ordinary stimuli come to feel portentous and self-referential, and located the matter ultimately in dopaminergic transmission. Nobody in the room experienced these as rival accounts. If anything, my formulation was treated as the more useful of the two, and the neurochemical description as dignified background.

Having since read up on the causal exclusion argument — the one Kim presses against non-reductive physicalism — I cannot shake the sense that the practice I was initiated into that afternoon is quietly committed to something it never states. Two friends on my course think there is nothing to worry about, but they defuse it in incompatible ways: one says causation is level-relative, so a psychological cause and a physiological cause cannot compete, and the other says the formulation makes no causal claim at all, only a claim about which variables in a model make a difference to which. Could you reconstruct what the practice of psychological formulation actually presupposes about mental causation, and adjudicate between those two defusing moves? I would like to know which positions the practice rules out, as distinct from which ones it merely sits comfortably alongside. Specifically: could a hard-line type-identity theorist, who holds that the conviction simply is a neural state, continue writing precisely the same formulations without altering a word? Could an epiphenomenalist do the same, and if so, does that not show the practice is weaker in its commitments than its practitioners suppose? And where exactly is the practice philosophically underdetermined — that is, at which points would several incompatible metaphysics license the very same document in the notes?

One detail puzzles me more than the rest. My formulation does not say merely that some neural event caused the client to scan the room. It says that what caused the scanning was the content of his belief: that it was the thought *they are appraising me*, rather than any other content carried in the same neurological vehicle, that made the difference. The psychiatrist's account seems to render the content idle, since aberrant salience is indiscriminate about what it attaches to, which is precisely why the same mechanism can produce persecutory ideas in one person and grandiose or religious ones in another. Does formulation therefore presuppose that semantic content is causally efficacious, and is that presupposition something the exclusion argument can leave standing, or must the loop be redescribed as a pattern rather than a chain of causes? Please finish by telling me what would concretely have to change on the ward if exclusion went through and we accepted it: what we would be permitted to write, what we would be permitted to say to clients about why talking to us might help, and how we would continue to justify a psychological therapy to commissioners who increasingly ask for mechanistic evidence.
