I run corporate development at a company that grows mostly by buying things — call it six or seven contested processes a year, anywhere from $20M to $400M, usually run by a bank against a hard timetable. We also make a handful of big internal platform bets that come back every year for a continue/kill review. My problem is the same in both places and I finally found the name for it: escalation of committment.

I went down a rabbit hole on Shubik's dollar auction and two things stuck. One, the second-highest bidder loses their bid too, so the person currently trailing is the one with the strongest reason to raise, and it's the trailing bidder who drives the price past anything the prize is worth. Two, once you're in there isn't a point where raising is obviously the wrong move — the disaster gets locked in by the decision to enter, not by the decision to stay. Our processes aren't literally that game, but I think a lot of them are secretly that game, because we pay our bankers, our diligence vendors, and three months of senior management attention whether we win or not, and nobody ever totals it up. Last year our two losses cost more in process spend than the incremental value of the deal we actually won, and I only know that because I dug it out of four different cost centers myself.

What I want from you: a decision procedure my deal teams can run — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where it flatly says walk, and the points where it hands the call to a person because the question is about values and not about facts. Constraints: it has to live on one page as a worksheet, it gets run inside a 90-minute deal committee by people who never took game theory, and — the hard part — it gets run by the same people who want the deal. So any step that depends on the team being candid about their own attachment is a step that will fail. Build in whatever structural workarounds you think are needed: who sets the walk-away number, when it has to be set, who physically holds it, whether it can be revised and by whom, and what has to be true for a revision to count as legitimate rather than as a rationalization. Also assume our bank only gets paid if we close, so treat anything they report about the other bidder as a claim that needs an independent source.

Things I specifically want in there:

- a test for whether a given process is really all-pay — we bleed whether we win or lose — versus a normal auction where only the winner pays, and what to do differently in each, because I don't think my team can tell them apart today;
- a test on the recoverability of what we've already spent, meaning whether continuing is the only route to getting value out of it, since I think thats the actual line between a dollar-auction situation and an ordinary investment where sunk costs really are irrelevant;
- a question that keys off whether we are currently the high bidder or the trailing bidder, because those two positions look to me like opposite risk profiles and we treat them identically;
- the two sentences you'd want a smart associate to walk away remembering about why the lesson is about entry and not exit, and then what to do anyway once we're already in, which we always will be.

Then run the procedure on three cases. Case one: live process, we're second at $310M, our written kickoff ceiling was $285M, the team re-ran the synergy model last week and now says $315M still clears our hurdle, and our bankers assure us the other bidder is emotional. Case two: internal platform, three years in, $40M spent, needs $15M more over eighteen months; the team says the remaining value is positive and doesn't depend on the $40M either way, and one board member keeps saying sunk cost, sunk cost — I suspect he's wrong but I can't explain why without sounding like I'm splitting hairs. Case three: pick a case where your procedure hands me an answer I'll hate — either it tells us to keep bidding past our own ceiling, or it tells me to kill something I personally championed. Don't soften it and don't give me a fake case where the answer is easy.

Last thing: be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume, and tell me what the whole apparatus can't handle. I'm already worried about a counterparty who credibly commits to never dropping out, since that seems to change the game rather than just being aggressive play. And please don't hand me a list of bias names — if you mention a bias it has to earn its place by changing a step.
