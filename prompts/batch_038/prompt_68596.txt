I have been asked to prepare a short written intervention for a seminar in practical philosophy, and rather than write about a theory I would like to put a set of practices on the table and ask what they commit us to. I should say at the outset that I come at this as someone who has spent several years designing employee benefit programmes, not as a philosopher, so I may be describing things clumsily.

The practices I have in mind share a single shape. A person, while in a reflective or ambitious mood, arranges matters so that a choice they anticipate making later will be unavailable to them, or expensive. The examples I keep meeting are these. A student installs an application that blocks social media and streaming sites on her own devices for a fixed window each day; the block cannot be lifted early, and some versions of the application will, if the user pre-authorises it, publish an embarrassing message or make a donation to a cause the user detests should she succeed in overriding the lock. A bank in a low-income market offers a savings account that pays below-market interest and forbids any withdrawal until either a target date or a target balance is reached; the account holder signs up knowing this, and when a relative falls ill and she asks for her own money early, the bank refuses her and everyone involved says the refusal is in her interest. An employer runs a wellness programme in which staff pledge to exercise three times a week and authorise payroll deductions that are forfeited if the pledge is broken; the forfeited money goes to a charity the pledge-maker selected in advance, and the human resources department enforces the forfeiture even when the employee later protests that the pledge no longer reflects her situation.

My question is what view of the person, and of the person's own authority over her future, a scheme of this kind must presuppose in order to be more than a trick. Specifically: whose act is the enforcement, when the third party carries it out over the objection of the person it was arranged for? What must be true about the relation between the one who signs and the one who later wants out for the arrangement to count as her binding herself rather than as one person imposing on another? And is there a substantive claim here about which of a person's states speaks for her — about reflective judgment outranking present desire — or is the practice merely a piece of machinery that works whether or not any such claim is true? I would also like the following asymmetry examined. When a family petitions a court to place an elderly relative's affairs under a guardian, we treat the arrangement as a restriction on that person's autonomy, justified if at all by her incapacity; when the same relative, or anyone else, signs a device of the kind above, we describe it as an exercise of autonomy, indeed as autonomy enhanced. The two cases look structurally identical — a judgment made at one time displacing the choices of the person at a later time — so either the practice is quietly paternalistic while advertising itself as the opposite, or the guardianship case is being described too uncharitably. Please tell me which, and say what the practice strictly requires as opposed to what it merely sits comfortably with; I suspect a good deal of it is underdetermined and that several incompatible theories of the person would license the same product. Finally, tell me what would have to change in how these schemes are designed, sold, and enforced if the presupposed view turned out to be false — for instance if there were no principled way to rank one temporal slice of a person above another.

Two requests about how you present this. First, please distinguish clearly between what you take to be the correct reconstruction of the practice's commitments and what are merely available readings; do not let the two blur. Second, at the end, state how confident you are in the central claims you have made and single out the one point in your answer where you think you are most likely to be wrong, and say why.
