I sit on the board of a small housing co-op, and last month we got into an unusually ugly argument about how our elections should be run. Our treasurer proposed that members who have lived here more than ten years, or who have served on the budget committee, should cast two ballots instead of one, on the grounds that they are the only people who actually understand how the reserve fund works and that the last two elections had been decided by members who had never read a financial statement in their lives. A longer-serving member shut him down in roughly four seconds with 'one person, one vote,' announced that there was nothing further to discuss, and several others nodded along as though an argument had been conclusively answered rather than merely interrupted. I voted against the proposal myself and would probably vote against it again, but I was left with the uncomfortable feeling that the sentence which ended the meeting had not touched the proposal at all.

What makes it worse is that I cannot claim the slogan is obviously true in every setting. My sister holds a few shares in a company and gets an annual proxy ballot on which her votes count in proportion to how many shares she owns, and nobody calls that an outrage; the condominium association down the street weights votes by the size of your unit, and their meetings are perfectly civil. So 'one person, one vote' cannot simply amount to 'voting power should always be distributed equally,' or those arrangements would be scandals too. When I try to say what it does amount to, I end up with several sentences that all sound like the slogan but pull apart the moment you look at them: something about how many ballots each person receives, something about how much each ballot is worth once districts or share classes are drawn, something about how much influence a person actually ends up having after turnout, campaigning and who bothers to show up are taken into account, and something more moral about each person's interests counting for as much as anyone else's. I cannot tell whether those are four readings of a single claim or four different claims wearing the same coat.

Could you separate out the distinct propositions that 'one person, one vote' can be used to assert, and arrange them from the ones nobody would deny through the ones that are substantive but defensible to the ones that are contentious or straightforwardly false? For each reading I would like to know three things: whether it would actually rule out our treasurer's proposal, meaning whether someone could accept that reading and still think the long-standing members deserve an extra ballot; whether it survives the counterexamples I mentioned, since the shareholder proxy and the weighted condominium votes are real and apparently unobjectionable; and whether it is aimed at the reason the treasurer gave, so that unequal votes are barred specifically because they were justified by superior knowledge, or whether it bars the arrangement no matter what justification is offered for it. Then tell me which reading the member who closed the debate most plausibly had in mind, given how little work she seemed to think was required, and whether any single reading is strong enough to settle the question by itself or whether all of them need a further premise about why unequal voting power is wrong before they can do that job. I have no training in political philosophy and have thought about all of this on my own, so please keep technical vocabulary to a minimum and explain each term when you introduce it.
