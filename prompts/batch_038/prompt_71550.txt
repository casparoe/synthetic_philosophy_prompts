I am in the second year of an MFA in literary translation. Last term our workshop undertook a collective exercise on Clément Marot's short poem "A une Damoyselle malade" — the piece around which Hofstadter built *Le Ton beau de Marot* — under a standing brief: the English version must match the original point for point in line count, line length, stanzaic shape, and rhyme scheme, and may alter the imagery as far as necessary to make that form come out. A visiting poet whose own work I admire read my version aloud and then said, in effect, that I had written a good poem and a bad translation, and that in any case *traduttore, traditore* — the enterprise was doomed before it started, so the honest course would have been to drop the constraints and paraphrase freely. Two others in the room agreed. I could not answer them then; I would like to be able to answer them at our next meeting.

So please take that proverb apart for me, rather than defending or attacking it wholesale.

First, a menu of the distinct claims it could be making, ordered from the weakest to the strongest. For each, state it precisely; classify it as descriptive, normative, ontological, or conversational (a rule about how arguments are to be conducted); grade it as a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, false, or indeterminate for want of a stated criterion of adequacy; and say what would count as evidence for or against it. Please do not manufacture readings merely in order to knock them down, and do not collapse two readings that differ in what they would license.

Second, tell me which of those readings, if any, would actually support his conclusion — that my constrained version was a worse choice than a free paraphrase. My suspicion is that the proverb cannot discriminate between the two options, since whatever it asserts about inevitable loss applies equally to the paraphrase he prefers; if that is right, show me exactly where the inference fails, and if it is wrong, expose the hidden premise that does the work.

Third, map the chain he ran through in a single breath: from a claim about what translation necessarily loses, to a claim about what my text *is* (not a translation at all, but a new poem), to a recommendation about what I ought to have done. Identify which link is unsupported and what would have to be added to repair it.

Fourth, describe the retreat: the sequence of ever-weaker propositions a user of the proverb typically falls back on once each stronger one is pressed, and the point at which the retreat lands on something no one would dispute and nothing follows from it.

Finally, two narrower questions. Does the pun itself carry any argumentative content, or is the proverb's persuasiveness entirely a matter of sound — and does that difference matter to how seriously it should be taken in a workshop? And what is smuggled in by the choice of *traitor*, given that a traitor is an agent who owes loyalty and voluntarily breaches it, whereas the losses in my version may have been forced on me by the two languages rather than chosen by me?

I would like something structured that I can bring to the table and read from directly: a numbered list of readings with their classifications and verdicts, then short sections on the inference, the chain, the retreat, and the two narrow questions. Plain argumentative prose, no survey of translation theory and no bibliography.
