Here's the Week 7 question, which I set myself (800 words asked for, they wrote about 750):

"Norman Malcolm argued that it is a mistake to treat a dream as an experience that happens while the sleeper sleeps. Daniel Dennett took up the same question in his paper Are Dreams Experiences? in the Philosophical Review in the mid-seventies. Set out Malcolm's argument. Then explain how Dennett's way of getting at the problem differs from Malcolm's — in particular, what work the rival scientific stories about the sleeping brain do for him. Does the difference in method make any difference to where the two of them end up?"

And here's the learner's answer:

Malcolm's starting point is that we talk about dreams as if they were a show put on inside the head with the sleeper in the audience. He thinks that picture is a mistake, and not an empirical mistake — a mistake about what our words can be used to say. When I say I dreamt I was in my grandmother's kitchen, there's a pull to think there must have been a kitchen-shaped event going on in my head during the night which I'm now reporting on. Malcolm says there's no such event, or rather that there's nothing that could count as my having observed one. What makes it true that I dreamt isn't any happening in the night but the impression I have on waking. The report is the criterion, not a description of something behind it.

So the experience gets moved to the moment of waking up. A dream is a postponed experience. It's still an experience, it just happens a bit later than we assumed, at the point where I sit up and say I've had a dream. That's a small correction but it saves the phenomenon, and it's why Malcolm doesn't come out saying nobody ever dreams, which is what people assume when they first hear about him.

The trouble with moving it to waking is that it leaves the sleeping mind empty. Malcolm is committed, on this account, to there being nothing it is like to be asleep. And dreaming isn't special here — if there's no experience in the night for dreams there's no experience in the night for anything. That's a claim about the brain as much as it is about grammar, and it's the part of Malcolm that hasn't aged well, because sleep research has since found plenty of organised activity in the night, including exactly the story-shaped kind that people report in the morning.

Dennett comes at it from the other side. He takes the question as a question about what actually happens, and he notices that there are several quite different stories you could tell about the sleeping brain which would produce precisely the same report in the morning. The dream might be assembled while it's being slept. It might be assembled out of fragments at the moment of waking. It might be something more like a recording that gets played back when you come round. Nothing in the report can tell you which of these happened, because the report is the only evidence on offer and every one of them predicts the same report. Dennett thinks that's the real scandal: we have a concept, experience, which is supposed to be the most private and best-known thing there is, and here's a case where we can get no purchase on it whatsoever.

Where do they end up? I think the same place. Neither of them believes in the movie in the head. Malcolm gets there by looking at the grammar of 'dream' and Dennett gets there by looking at how little the evidence could ever tell us, but the upshot is that dreaming isn't the sort of thing that is watched. Which suggests the difference in method doesn't really matter — you can argue it from the armchair or from the laboratory and land in the same spot, and that's a comforting thought for armchair people like me.

One thing I'm not sure either of them deals with. If the only thing that makes it true that I dreamt is my impression on waking, then somebody who's bad at reporting their dreams is, for Malcolm, somebody who doesn't dream much. My brother says he never dreams and I say I dream every night, and on this view that difference is entirely a difference in how we talk over breakfast. That makes how many dreams happen in a house depend on how articulate the people in it are, which seems wrong, and I can't see either author escaping it.
