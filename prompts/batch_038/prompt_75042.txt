I am applying for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship at a center whose faculty is split roughly evenly between economists and philosophers, and none of the three readers on my selection panel works in epistemic game theory. The application asks for a project description of about 1,500 words. My half-formed idea concerns the assumption usually labelled common knowledge of rationality, and I want it turned into a proposal with a sharp, answerable question rather than into a survey of a standing debate.

The seed is this. Two of the most discussed results in the field behave as though that assumption were the load-bearing wall, but the ways in which it gets weakened in the two cases are not the same. In Rubinstein's electronic mail game, what is perturbed is the players' knowledge of which game is being played: with small probability the payoff matrix differs from the one everyone knows, communication about it is unreliable, and the unique equilibrium for every positive probability of communication breakdown fails to converge to any equilibrium of the complete-information game as that probability goes to zero. In the centipede debate, by contrast, what is perturbed is the players' knowledge of each other's rationality while the game itself stays common knowledge; the familiar complaint is that after an unexpected early move a player cannot coherently maintain both that the opponent is rational and that play has reached the node it has reached. Work on approximating common knowledge with graded notions — common p-belief, and the result that common p-belief in each other's posteriors forces those posteriors to be close — is normally deployed against one of these two perturbations and not the other, and I suspect the literature treats them as interchangeable when they are structurally quite different: one concerns a state of the world the players might in principle come to learn about, the other concerns a property of the players that no amount of signalling can verify from the inside. The observation I want to build the project around is that the two perturbations appear to point in opposite empirical directions. A tiny amount of uncertainty about the game suffices to destroy cooperation in the mail game, whereas a tiny amount of uncertainty about rationality is precisely what gets invoked to make cooperation in the centipede intelligible. I want to know whether that asymmetry is a real theorem-shaped fact about the two kinds of perturbation, an artifact of the particular information structures people happen to have chosen, or a conflation of private perturbations with common ones.

Turn this into the proposal. I need: a statement of the research question precise enough that a referee could tell whether it has been answered, and one that does not collapse into a restatement of the mail game; an account of why the existing positions leave the question open, naming the views and arguments you can without assembling a reference list, since I will supply citations myself; a staged plan for the twelve months saying what I would actually do in each phase, including which toy models I would build first and why those rather than larger ones; an explicit defence of this approach against the obvious alternatives — treating the centipede as a problem about bounded rationality, about quantal response, about level-k reasoning, or simply declaring common knowledge of rationality incoherent at off-path nodes; a description of what a positive result would look like, and of what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that the two perturbations turn out to be interchangeable once properly formalized, in which case I want the proposal to say what the salvageable contribution would be; and an honest scope assessment naming the part most likely to overrun, together with a minimal publishable fallback if the general result does not materialize. Keep it legible to a mixed panel: the question should be statable in two sentences of ordinary prose before any apparatus appears, and the technical content should be signposted rather than displayed.
