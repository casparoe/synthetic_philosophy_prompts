Still buzzing from a three hour argument with my flatmate last night — he did an econ PhD and works at a central bank now, and he thinks I'm wasting my life. Topic was Friedman's F-twist. His position: once you accept it, the whole "are the assumptions realistic" debate is dead, full stop. When I said no, the live question is *which* false assumptions get a licence and how you'd ever tell, he said "by prediction, obviously — that's your criterion too, you just don't want to admit it," and I genuinely didn't have an answer at 1am.

So. I'm teaching this next week in a 75 minute seminar, second-year social science students, intermediate micro behind them and no philosophy at all. Half the room has absorbed a watered-down version of my flatmate's view from their econ classes; the other half has absorbed the opposite cynicism, that models are just stories told by people with funding. Both groups are convinced they already know what the F-twist says.

Don't explain it to me. Plan how I should explain it.

- The order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what. My hunch is you can't get to "which idealisations are licensed" until they've got something like the difference between an idealisation you could in principle undo and one you can't, but tell me if that's the wrong dependency.
- One example that carries the whole session, and what it has to have and what it has to lack. Not the billiards player — they'll have met it in a Wikipedia summary. Not the frictionless plane either, or they'll just say "but that's physics, economics is different." I want an idealisation about how people behave that is doing real work (you can't just remove it and add more maths) *and* load-bearing for something a government would actually want to do, because otherwise the licensing question never gets any teeth.
- The misconceptions, and the exact point in the sequence where each gets headed off. Ones I'd bet on: "Friedman says assumptions don't matter so nothing can be criticised"; treating the F-twist as a claim about truth rather than a methodological rule; thinking "all models are wrong but some are useful" is the same insight when its actually a way of dodging the question; and using "idealisation" and "false assumption" interchangeably when one of those carries a promise the other doesn't. Tell me which of those are real and what I'm missing.
- A check for the end that would show they've got the licensing question rather than the slogan, and that would fail loudly for *both* camps.
- What I should openly refuse to get into in 75 minutes — my guess is the whole realism/instrumentalism metaphysics, but you tell me what else.
