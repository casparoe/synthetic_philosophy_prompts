So my friend N and I burned basically all of Sunday on this and I still cant tell which of us lost, which usually means we both did something wrong. We're both grad students — I lean Lewisian on modality, he's a transworld-identity guy who thinks Kripke's Humphrey point is decisive — and what started as a completely ordinary fight about whether the Humphrey objection actually does any work against counterpart theory ended with him writing this arguement on the whiteboard and claiming it settles the debate. The problem is that when I pushed on it, it settled a lot more than either of us wanted. Here it is, roughly in his order:

1. If it's true that Humphrey might have won the election, something must make that true, and what makes it true has to be a situation in which Humphrey himself wins.
2. In any candidate situation, either Humphrey himself is there winning, or something numerically distinct from Humphrey is there winning.
3. Counterpart theory says it's the second option: a distinct individual, related to Humphrey only by resemblance.
4. A situation in which someone else wins is not a situation in which Humphrey wins, so it can't make step 1's claim true. Hence on counterpart theory, every de re possibility claim about Humphrey is false.
5. But step 4 is just an instance of a general principle: a truth about x can be made true only by a situation in which x itself features and itself bears the property ascribed.
6. That principle applies to the past exactly as much as to the merely possible. On stage theory or perdurantism, what makes it true that I was a child is that some earlier stage — a thing distinct from me, the one typing this — was a child. So by (5), it isn't true that I was a child.
7. Same for fiction and counterfactuals: what makes the Holmes-in-Baker-Street claim true is a situation containing a merely fictional detective, and what makes my dropped-glass counterfactual true is a situation containing a merely possible dropping. By (5), both are false.
8. But (6) and (7) are absurd — nobody thinks we've discovered that people can't truly say they were children, or that novels don't say anything.
9. So (5) is false.
10. And once (5) is false, step 4 loses its only support; but the parallel in (10) cuts both ways, because what's over there in another world isn't literally here on any account, transworld identity included — so no analysis of de re modal truth survives, and the whole counterpart-theory-vs-transworld-identity fight was verbal.

His landing point was that last clause, and I don't buy it, but I also can't point at the exact step where it goes wrong, which is annoying because I want to before I see him Thursday.
