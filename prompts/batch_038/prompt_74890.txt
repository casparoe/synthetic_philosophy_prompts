I work in the strategy unit of a mid-sized government department — the kind of place that sends maybe forty policy papers a year up to ministers, most of them forgettable, a couple of them consequential. Last month a colleague of mine, who is genuinely sharp and got us through the pandemic in decent shape, circulated a proposal that from next quarter every paper going up should carry a one-page annex on historical precedent: at least two past episodes judged comparable, an explicit statement of what the analogy is supposed to show, three disanalogies, and a line on what the precedent implies about our likely failure modes. His argument is that we currently invoke 1938 or 2008 or whatever loose parallel is floating around the building, and nobody is ever made to say what inferential work the comparison is doing. I agree with that diagnosis and still feel uneasy about the remedy. My worry is that the annex gets filled in by whoever has ten spare minutes before the deadline, the three disanalogies will be the trivially true ones — different technology, different century, different media environment — and a paper that arrives with a precedent annex will thereafter look historically grounded whether or not the analogy supports anything in it. So instead of disciplining our analogies we'd be laundering them, with a departmental seal on top.

There's a recent episode behind all this, and I think it cuts both ways. In a briefing on our exposure to a single foreign supplier for critical minerals, someone reached for the Thucydides trap framing — the idea, drawn from Thucydides's remark about the growth of Athenian power and the fear it provoked in Sparta, that war becomes likelier when a rising power threatens an established one. That framing usually travels with Graham Allison's much-cited tally from his Harvard project: twelve of the sixteen historical rivalries he and his team identified ended in war. A junior analyst asked, perfectly reasonably, where the sixteen come from and on what principle they were selected, and the room sort of froze. Nobody knew, and it emerged that nobody had noticed the framing was smuggling in a base-rate claim at all. My colleague says that's exactly what the annex would have caught. My hunch is that it might equally have given the claim a bureaucratic afterlife, because the annex as drafted asks for similarity and difference, and I'm not convinced similarity judgments are where analogies go wrong. Two other things I keep turning over: the stock analogies are bidirectional, so the same episode gets recruited for opposite conclusions depending on which features you foreground — 1938 to argue against negotiating with anyone, Vietnam to argue against committing to anything, and neither use ever has to answer to the other; and a list of disanalogies is basically inexhaustible, so demanding three of them isn't a constraint, it's an invitation to list the irrelevant ones while leaving the load-bearing similarity untouched. Where I'd push instead, half-formed as it is: make the annex name the causal mechanism the analogy is meant to transfer, and then require the drafter to say whether the conditions that made that mechanism operate are still in place. Whether that's better than my colleague's version, or just a prettier box to tick, I honestly can't tell.

So: draft the decision memo I'll table at the panel next Thursday. It needs an actionable bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with named amendments — and the reasoning behind it. Keep it usable: criteria a drafting officer under deadline pressure can actually apply when deciding what goes in the annex and when a paper should say that no historical comparison is worth making at all, rather than a lecture on why analogy is philosophically fraught. Treat my colleague's case at its strongest, because he'd say the box-ticking risk is a reason to write the requirement better and not a reason to abandon it, and I don't have a good answer to that yet. If you land on amendments, be concrete about what they'd say and what the panel would be giving up by adopting them — including any loss of the deterrent effect his version has on lazy drafting. Please write it in British English, since half the panel notices and I'd rather not spend Thursday on spelling. And be straight with me about where your own reasoning is weakest: which consideration, if I've got it wrong, would flip your recommendation, and whether you'd rather we found that out now then in eighteen months when the first batch of annexes comes back. No bibliography needed — if we go ahead I'll assemble the reading myself.
