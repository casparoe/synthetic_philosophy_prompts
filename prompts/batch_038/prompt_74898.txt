In discussions of Nozick, two claims routinely get collapsed into one: that his entitlement theory is historical rather than end-state, and that the Wilt Chamberlain case shows voluntary exchange continually disturbing any patterned distribution. I would like these separated, because I suspect they rest on different features of a principle. Patterning and looking backward are not the same property: a principle that allocates to each person in proportion to what that person produced looks backward to facts about production, yet it still ties holdings to a structural feature of the distribution as a whole.

Please explain, in order:

(a) what makes a principle of distributive justice patterned, as distinct from what makes it an end-state or time-slice principle, and how a principle can possess one of these properties without the other;

(b) which of the two features the Chamberlain example actually depends on — specifically, whether the argument goes through against a principle that is historical in the relevant sense but still patterned;

(c) what becomes of the argument under a reset scheme in which the favored pattern is imposed only once per generation, holdings are then handed over to their occupants, and every voluntary transfer thereafter is left entirely untouched for the remainder of that period.

For (c) I want a definite verdict rather than a survey of options: is such a scheme refuted by the example itself, refuted only on the addition of a further premise about what it takes to maintain a pattern over time, or not refuted at all?

Please say how confident you are in each of the three answers, and single out the point in your account you are least sure of.
