so i've been reading about nuclear deterrence for a while now, and the part that gets me isn't the weapons, its the credibility industry — the fact that a whole profession exists to solve the problem that nobody actually believes the threats. i want a piece about that.

write me a satirical quick-start guide, the kind folded up in the box with a router or a smart thermostat: cheerful, helpful, deadpan consumer-tech register, numbered steps, a troubleshooting table, safety warnings in caps, a bit of fine print about the warranty. Around 700-800 words for the guide itself. The product is a deterrence package sold to governments, marketed exactly like a home security system, and you pick the brand name.

the funny has to live in the accuracy. if a line would work just as well in a parody of anything military, cut it. specifically:

- the headline feature is genuine loss of control. the customer is told, approvingly, that they won't be able to switch it off in time, and that this is precisely why it's worth buying. ordinary reliability engineering gets flipped: redundancy becomes a liability, a human in the loop is a defect, and 'you can always abort' goes in the list of things competitors say.
- the guide forbids testing the product, and explains patiently why — a test consumes the thing tested, and one successful test doesn't establish anything about the next one.
- three model tiers that a strategic studies person will instantly map onto three real historical postures. don't name the postures, and don't name any real country, doctrine, system, or person. if nobody in the field can tell which is which, i've failed; if anyone can point at a real name in your text, you've failed.
- one troubleshooting row for: the customer has realized they would not in fact carry out the retaliation. the recommended fix is the standard one, presented as routine maintanance rather than a moral event.
- something about allies and third parties who enjoy the coverage without paying for it, and who can helpfully trigger it.
- the escalation ladder, sold as a feature, missing its top rung, with the missing rung listed under specifications.

no winking, ever. the guide is sincerely trying to help this customer succeed, and the discomfort should come from how individually reasonable each instruction is. at least four of the warnings need to be things a real strategist would sign off on as correct advice, not as jokes.

then drop out of character for about 100 words and tell me where you took liberties: which bits exaggerate a real feature instead of being one, and whether you leaned on a contested reading of deterrence theory — there's a live argument about how much of this stuff is rational-choice folk wisdom that got retrofitted onto what actually happened. no citations, i'm on a plane with no internet and don't want a reading list. what i do want is for you to say which bit you're least sure about, what argument would make you pull it, and what would change your mind about whether the piece is punching at the doctrine or just at the aesthetics of it.
