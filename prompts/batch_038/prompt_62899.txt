I retired four years ago from a national measurement laboratory, where the greater part of my career was spent comparing pressure and force standards against one another and, in my last decade there, arguing with colleagues about how far a stubborn disagreement between two well-calibrated instruments ought to shake one's confidence in anything. Since retiring I have been auditing a logic course at the local university and have fallen, rather against my inclination, into the literature on the liar paradox and on paraconsistent logic — the systems that refuse to let an arbitrary conclusion follow from a contradiction — and thence into the stronger claim, associated with Graham Priest among others, that some contradictions are not merely tolerable but true. I confess that what attracts me to the stronger claim is a professional habit: in the laboratory one is constantly in the position of asserting something and its denial simultaneously, and nobody thinks one has thereby lost one's licence to reason. I should like to do something rather unfashionable with this material. Rather than ask you to expound the dialectic between the dialetheist and the classical logician, I want to set out my own verdicts on a batch of cases, most of them drawn from my working life, and ask what principle, if any, they add up to. Please reason from the cases themselves rather than rehearsing the state of the art, since I cannot easily look anything up, and please write throughout in British English.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. First: two independently calibrated transducers on the same rig disagreed by eleven per cent for three days, and I wrote in the notebook that the vessel pressure both was and was not 4.12 bar, pending a check that eventually found a fouled diaphragm. I think that entry was not merely permissible but the honest one. Second: a friend who retired from the bench describes a statute requiring a certain disclosure and a subordinate regulation forbidding it, both in force, with no rule of priority; he says the law both requires and forbids the act, yet insists that no official may both perform it and refrain from it. I agree with both halves of that. Third: a hospital records system in which one field states that a drug was administered and another that it was not; I hold that the query engine must not, on that account, return every record in the database as a match. The contradiction must be quarantined, not allowed to propagate. Fourth: after a month of resistance I came to think that the sentence This sentence is false really is both true and false, and I am content to say so. Fifth: but its close cousin, If this sentence is true, then my laboratory falsified its own results in 1994, I flatly refuse, even though the self-reference looks to me of exactly the same kind, and even though I cannot say what structural feature the fourth case has and the fifth lacks. Sixth: the strengthened version, This sentence is false and not true, troubles me more than the plain liar; I suspect something must give there, since accepting it would dissolve the very distinction I relied upon in the fourth case. Seventh: a heap of 4,712 grains both is and is not a heap — I am inclined to accept this, though I notice that if I accept it at every number I can no longer say anything determinate at all. Eighth: a probe leaving an airlock; at the instant it crosses the boundary I am inclined to say it is both inside and outside, though here my training protests, since the mathematics handles the crossing with a limit and generates no contradiction whatever. Ninth: a novelist who gives a recurring character blue eyes in one book and brown eyes in another; within the fiction the character both has and lacks blue eyes. I think this is fine, but fine in a cheaper way than the others, and I cannot articulate the difference. Tenth, and the one that most exercises me: I would not write that the defendant both did and did not enter the room, although two credible eyewitnesses say opposite things, whereas I did write the contradictory entry about the transducers. My own explanation is that in the instrument case there was a finite, specifiable procedure — recalibration — that would eliminate one conjunct within days, while testimony offers no such procedure. I am not confident that this is the real difference rather than a rationalisation.

My tentative principle has three clauses, and I know it is in poor shape. A contradiction may be asserted only where its two conjuncts rest on sources independent of one another and independent of the sentence itself; only where there is a finite, specifiable procedure that will in time eliminate one of the conjuncts; and never where asserting it would license arbitrary further conclusions. The third clause does the work I want in the fifth case, but the first condemns the liar, which I accept, and the second condemns the legal conflict, the crossing of the threshold, the fictional character and the borderline heap — all of which I also accept. So the principle is worse than my verdicts, not better. What I should like from you is this. Offer me one or, if necessary, two candidate principles stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a new case without further guidance, indicating for each clause which of the cases above forces it in. Then tell me whether the principle you recommend is a semantic one, about which sentences receive both values, or an epistemic and normative one, about which contradictions an agent may assert and record — I strongly suspect that I have been sliding between the two, particularly in the second and third cases, and I should like that sliding diagnosed. Apply your principle to four cases I have not settled: an unmeasured electron in an interferometer, where I am tempted to say it both has and lacks a determinate path, and want to know whether that is a genuine contradiction or merely a limit on what may be said; the state of believing the reactor is safe while not believing it, which strikes me as impermissible though it looks like a harmless pairing; a sentence printed by a machine that prints only what it has verified, reading This sentence printed by the machine is not true, where I cannot tell whether your independence clause decides it; and a theory patched so many times that it both predicts and fails to predict the same anomaly. Finally, state honestly which of my ten verdicts your recommended principle cannot save, and whether the price of saving all of them is a two-tier semantics of some kind or the surrender of a verdict I care about — and if you conclude that my verdicts are simply inconsistent with one another, tell me which one is the culprit and why.
