I write a monthly column on the foundations of physics for a general-interest magazine, and this month I have been assigned the tired but perennial topic of what it means for science to make progress. As background I picked up a well-reviewed trade book about how the sciences change their minds — physicist-author, lots of good anecdotes, a chapter on realism — and one passage in it struck me as unusually crisp, so I copied it into my notes with the intention of paraphrasing it. A friend of mine who works in philosophy of science glanced at the passage over dinner and said only that it was "a forgivable mess, but a mess," which is not actionable feedback, and she has since gone offline for a few weeks. Before I build an article on top of it, I would like a proper audit. The passage runs as follows:

"Popper's difficulty was this. Science plainly improves, but on his own account every interesting theory is a conjecture that will sooner or later be overthrown, so improvement cannot consist in becoming true. What he therefore needed was a notion of getting closer to the truth that could apply to theories that are, strictly speaking, false. His solution was to divide a theory's content in two: the true consequences it entails, which he called its truth content, and the false consequences it entails, its falsity content. One theory is closer to the truth than another just in case it has at least as much truth content and no more falsity content; the difference between the two is what he meant by verisimilitude. The definition has a pleasing consequence. The bolder and more informative a theory is, the closer to the truth it must be, since boldness simply means having more content, and truthlikeness is a matter of having content that is true rather than false. This is how Popper could insist that Einstein's theory is nearer the truth than Newton's even though both are false: Einstein's theory entails all of Newton's and a great deal besides. The whole construction came down in 1974, when two logicians independently proved that the definition cannot be satisfied by any pair of theories unless the weaker of them is already true. Popper's account could therefore rank true theories, which never needed ranking, and could not rank false ones, which had been the entire point. He made no attempt to repair it, falling back on the remark that truth is a regulative ideal, and the technical notion largely disappeared from serious work. The honest conclusion for the rest of us is deflationary but bracing: theories are not true, or nearly true, they are more or less useful, and 'closer to the truth' is a picture we tell ourselves rather than a claim we can cash out."

Could you go through this claim by claim and sort it into three piles for me: what a philosopher working on truthlikeness would accept as stated, what is a fair simplification that loses something but does no damage, and what is actually wrong? I would particularly like to know whether the boldness point survives — does Popper's account really deliver the result that the more informative a theory is, the closer to the truth it must be, or has the passage dropped some further condition on which theories we are entitled to compare? I would also like a straight answer on the Einstein and Newton sentence, since I can see two different things possibly going wrong there and cannot tell which. And I want you to be precise about the 1974 result: what exactly did it establish, what did it not establish, and is it fair to say that it discredited the idea of closeness to the truth as opposed to one particular definition of it? If there are live accounts that order false theories, say so plainly and tell me whether any of them are continuous with Popper's own content-based strategy rather than a replacement for it, and whether the passage's remark about true theories never needing to be ranked is itself accurate. Finally: does the deflationary conclusion in the last two sentences follow from anything in the passage that turns out to be right, or is it an independent thesis that has been smuggled in under cover of a technical result? I should say that I am temperamentally a realist and would like the conclusion to be unwarranted, so please tell me if I am about to be told what I want to hear. I would like a verdict at the end about which sentences, if any, I could safely reuse in my own words in print.
