I work in software and have no formal philosophy training, so I hope the framing below is legible. I have just rewatched Inglourious Basterds and cannot stop thinking about the fact that it knowingly corrupts the historical record — Hitler and Goebbels die in a burning Paris cinema in 1944 — while containing, inside itself, an instance of exactly what it is doing: the propaganda feature Nation's Pride, which recasts Fredrick Zoller's sniper stand as a national myth, and which the film's own Nazi audience watches with approval.

I would like a reading of the film through the conflict between collective memory and history: what a community remembers about its past versus what the record establishes, and which should give way when the two come apart. Please use two reference points substantively rather than decoratively: Nietzsche's second untimely meditation, with its monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes of history — which mode is Inglourious Basterds operating in, and does it slide between them? — and Margalit's Ethics of Memory, with its claim that we owe remembrance to the victims of radical evil alongside its worry that memory can become an instrument of revenge rather than reconciliation. Does the film's revenge structure discharge or violate that duty? And does its fairy-tale framing license the falsification, or merely conceal the fact that most viewers will absorb it as a picture of what happened?

Here is an analogy I keep returning to, and I would like you to push on it rather than flatter it. Two codebases can pass an identical test suite while one carries its commit history — reverted experiments, comments explaining why a decision was made — and the other carries none. We treat the second as worse to inherit even though it does the same work, because the history is where the reasons live. Is collective memory the commit log of a people, with the archive merely the build we run, or is it the reverse: history proper as the log and collective memory as the lossy artifact that actually executes? Say which side of that split the film takes.

Finally, tell me where this reading breaks down. I would rather learn that the film resists being an illustration of Nietzsche and Margalit than see it flattened into one, and I want to know what Inglourious Basterds shows about memory and record that a straightforward argument about the two does not.
