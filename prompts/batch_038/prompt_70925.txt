I am a high school junior, and for three years I have competed in mock trial, which means I have had to learn the law of evidence with some care: relevance and prejudice, hearsay and its exceptions, lay versus expert opinion, authentication, impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, judicial notice, burden of production versus burden of persuasion. This year I have been reading, on my own, about the Nyāya school of classical Indian philosophy and its account of the pramāṇas — perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda) — and I keep involuntarily converting one framework into the other. I would like you to do that conversion properly, and then tell me where it breaks.

First, the translation. Treat the four pramāṇas as four distinct rules about what may count as a legitimate source of a finding, and tell me what in evidence law corresponds to each: what is the hearsay analogue of śabda, and what makes it admissible for the Naiyāyika but not for me; what corresponds to the qualification of an expert witness; what corresponds to authentication; what corresponds to impeachment. Then take the five-step public form of a Nyāya inference — thesis, reason, illustrative example, application of that example to the case at hand, conclusion — and say which of my courtroom habits it genuinely matches and where the match is only superficial. Take up vyāpti, the invariable pervasion that must hold between the reason and what is inferred before the inference is legitimate, together with the way it is supposed to be secured by observing agreement and absence, and tell me whether anything in my framework plays that role, or whether my framework simply assumes it.

Second, the residue report, which matters more to me. For each claim, say whether it translates cleanly, whether it translates only if I quietly change what it means, or whether it has no expression at all in a system whose purpose is to settle a dispute between two adverse parties in front of a trier of fact under time pressure. Where something fails to translate, I want your judgment about which side is at fault: is evidence law missing a category, or is the Nyāya claim doing more work than it appears to, or perhaps less?

Be especially candid about two cases. Upamāna seems to me to have no evidentiary counterpart whatsoever, and I want to know whether that is a gap in my framework or a sign that comparison was never an independent source to begin with. And the requirement that testimony come from an āpta: in my framework the trustworthiness of a witness is something you test procedurally, through cross-examination and exclusionary rules, whereas in theirs it looks like a standing fact about the speaker's knowledge and honesty. Tell me whether that difference is substantive or merely procedural.

Two constraints. Please do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations — I want the argument itself. And if some part of my translation instinct is actively misleading me, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over to be encouraging.
