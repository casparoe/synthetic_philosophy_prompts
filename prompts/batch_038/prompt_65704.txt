The question was: "A region is considering a large-scale insecticide program against an invasive crop pest. It's sometimes said that the moral case against such a program rests entirely on whether insects are sentient. Assess that claim."

The student wrote, more or less verbatim:

"The claim is basically right. Bentham already settled the criterion -- suffering is what matters, so if insects can't suffer, spraying them is no more wrong than mowing a lawn. The numbers are what make it serious: one application covers a staggering number of individuals per hectare, so even if the probability that an insect is sentient is small, expected-value reasoning says we should treat the program as a massive harm until proven otherwise. Singer's equal consideration of interests means each insect's interest counts the same as each human's, which is why the case is so strong. Two things weaken it. First, pest populations rebound, so the insects killed would have been replaced anyway and the total amount of suffering barely changes. Second, biological control releases natural enemies instead of chemicals, and because its targeted it's automatically better for insect welfare. So the case does rest on sentience, but it's a strong case, and the precautionary principle plus expected value put the burden of proof on anyone who says insects don't suffer."
