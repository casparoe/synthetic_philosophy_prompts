I'm writing a paper for my seminar on interwar philosophy of value, and I want to test an extrapolation rather than repeat the usual summary. As we read him, Schlick's essay on the meaning of life holds that meaning is not available from an afterlife or from any purpose imposed on life from outside, but only from within life itself; that work belongs to the domain of necessity and is valuable instrumentally, whereas play is pursued for its own sake and is therefore where value proper resides; that youth is the natural home of play and that maturity typically sells it off to labour; and that what makes a life worth living is the intensity of joy in the moment rather than its duration, so that endless life would be no gain and might well become tedious.

Two cases I want you to run him through. First: a professional esports player — twenty-two, contracted to an organisation, practising nine hours a day under coaches and performance analytics, playing a game he chose as a child, with a career that will end before he is thirty. Schlick's own criteria seem to make this the most meaningful life available to a modern person, and yet every feature that makes it a profession looks like precisely the conversion of play into work that he thinks destroys meaning. Where does he actually land, and which of his commitments must he revise or reinterpret to land there? Second: suppose he were shown a fully immersive virtual world, commercially operated and engineered to keep its residents in continuously enjoyable play for decades, together with a treatment that holds off ageing. Given his hedonism and his hostility to immortality, would he endorse this arrangement, reject it, or endorse it with a distinction I have not thought of?

Please commit to an answer in both cases — a definite verdict with reasons, not a weighing of the possibilities against each other — and say explicitly where your prediction rests on a contested reading of Schlick rather than on a commitment he plainly held.
