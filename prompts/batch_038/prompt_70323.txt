I have no academic training in any of this — everything below is something I worked out for myself from reading around — so please tell me plainly where I have got the shape of the problem wrong.

My starting point is an ordinary situation. Every morning I watch the same stretch of river, and something moves along the far bank in poor light. I come away with a feeling I could honestly put numbers on, call it sixty percent heron and forty percent otter, yet I never receive anything I would call a piece of news I am sure of. What I have read says the familiar rule for updating, conditionalizing on what you have learned, is built for cases where some proposition becomes certain for you, and that a case like mine needs a different move: shift the probabilities across the whole set of possibilities while holding the conditional probabilities within each of them fixed, which then redistributes everything else in proportion.

My own first reaction was that no new rule is needed. There must be some proposition describing what I got — something like "the movement looked heron-ish" — and I can either assign it a probability or treat my having had that experience as the certain thing I conditionalize on. When I try to write this down, two snags stop me. First, I seem to need a prior probability for the experience-proposition itself, and the only way I can think of to get one is by asking how likely each animal was to produce an experience of that kind, which already assumes the very sixty–forty split I was trying to derive from it. Second, if I describe my evidence coarsely I get one result and if I describe it more finely — heron-ish at that distance, in that light, seen from the side — I get another, and nothing I can see makes one of these the correct description of my evidence rather than the other.

So what I want explained is what is actually wrong with that "just conditionalize on your experience" reply. Is the trouble that the experience-proposition has to be given its probability out of nowhere, that no such proposition is really available to me at all, or something about the difference between being caused to have a belief and being given a reason for one? And is my second snag a separate flaw, or the same flaw appearing twice?

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary, covering in order: what the reply gets right; where it fails; which of my two snags is the serious one; what the alternative rule is doing differently, given that failure; and what you would advise me to do in practice about how to describe the experience.
