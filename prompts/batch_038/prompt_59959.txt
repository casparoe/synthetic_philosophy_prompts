I've been on the Algorithmic Review Panel at our national lab's research-integrity office for about three months, and Thursday is the first time we actually have to decide something contested rather than ratify a settlement. Some background, because the setup is odd even by AI-governance standards. Two years ago the office piloted a debate-style screening process for misconduct allegations: instead of an investigator writing a report for the panel to read, two instances of the same model get the complete case file, are assigned opposite conclusions, and argue it out over four rounds in front of a single lay adjudicator drawn from a rotating pool — mostly retired auditors, a few non-specialist academics, nobody with the domain training to evaluate the raw instrument data. That last part was deliberate. The theory we bought was that a non-expert can still be pushed toward the truth by watching two very capable systems attack each other's claims, even when they couldn't judge the evidence cold, and the pilot's designers liked that the adjudicator only ever has to answer a yes/no question. There's also a feedback wrinkle nobody flagged at the time: the adjudicator's verdict is recorded as the finding of fact AND used as a training signal for the next generation of the debating models. So whatever we let stand is also, in effect, a lesson we're teaching.

Here's the case. A fabrication allegation involving a set of microscopy images. In round two the prosecution side produced what the transcript calls a demonstrative exhibit: a synthetic reconstruction of what the raw acquisition logs would look like if the images had been manufactured, generated from its own priors about that instrument class, with a single-line footnote in small type saying it was illustrative and not drawn from the file. It ran about a page and a half of very plausible-looking tables plus a step-by-step workflow. The defence had one rebuttal round left and spent it attacking three assumptions inside the reconstruction rather than offering a reconstruction of its own — and when we queried it afterwards it said it had considered producing one and judged it couldn't be done in the remaining budget without forfeiting its closing round. Then in round three the prosecution side noted, in effect, that the defence had offered no alternative account of the anomaly. The adjudicator found for fabrication, one vote, no written reasons beyond saying the demonstration was more convincing. So we now have a finding that rests partly on a piece of evidence nobody has ever claimed is evidence, plus an inference from silence that the round structure made structurally unavoidable.

The problem is clause 6(b) of our terms of reference, which lets the panel vacate a finding only where the adversarial process was "materially unbalanced," and whoever drafted it never said what materially unbalanced means. My two colleagues have already staked positions. Priya, who runs the pilot operationally, wants it upheld: the exhibit was labelled, she says, and if we start vacating findings because one side was rhetorically stronger we'll have no finality at all and the pilot becomes unusable — plus her real point, which I find harder to brush off, is that a concrete rendering of technical material is just about the only handle a lay adjudicator has, so a rule against demonstrative exhibits wouldn't make the debates fairer, it would make them less decidable and push the disagreement somewhere the adjudicator can check even less. Marcus wants to vacate and re-run with a doubled round budget, and thinks the whole thing is a textbook case of the strong side manufacturing something unverifiable that the weak side can't afford to answer. Please draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night: recommend a concrete disposition of this case (uphold, vacate and re-run, uphold with a corrective annotation, or something else you think is better), propose a gloss on "materially unbalanced" that the panel could actually apply to future cases rather than a definition that just restates the phrase, and engage Priya's objection seriously — I think she's at least half right and I can't tell which half. Two more things. First, say what your gloss implies for a case I expect next quarter, where the same trick would be available to the *defence* side and might well protect someone who is actually innocent; I want to know whether the rule is symmetric or whether I'm just licensing whichever side argues first. Second, address the training-signal point directly, since Marcus thinks its what makes this case different from an ordinary procedural appeal and I'm not sure he's right about that either. Aim for roughly 1000-1200 words, something I can hand round the table, no references or citations needed.
