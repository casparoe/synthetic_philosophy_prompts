I'm in year four of a philosophy of mind PhD and the third chapter of my dissertation is about the "one mind" line — the idea that the plurality of conscious subjects is somehow an artifact, and that there's really only ever one of them looking out. My anchor case is Schrödinger, who pushed that view harder than almost anyone writing in the twentieth century, and who leaned on Vedantic sources plus his own argument that you never actually run into a plurality of consciousness, only consciousness, and that what makes a biography a single biography is just memory stitching. The problem for me: he died in early 1961, basically on the eve of every piece of evidence that now dominates the debate. So I want your best reconstruction of how he'd have responded, in your own voice — not a summary of what the secondary literature says about him, and definitely no reading list or bibliography at the end, since I've got the books.

Three things I want you to run him through:

1. Split-brain patients. The commisurotomy work got going in the sixties — the disconnected-hemisphere experiments, the conflicting-hand cases, the naming failures — and then a more recent round arguing that perception comes apart while the conscious field stays unified. For someone whose whole case rested on never encountering consciousness in the plural, is this a counterexample, or is it a gift? My hunch is he'd have grabbed it as confirmation that the count of subjects was never tied to bodies in the first place, but I want you to say whether any specific premise of his actually breaks, and if so which one.

2. Many-minds interpretations of quantum mechanics. He lived to see Everett's relative-state formulation but not the mind-branching versions that showed up in the late eighties, where each observer carries a continuum of definite minds over a superposed body. Would he have co-opted that as the marriage of his wave mechanics with monism, or is it an inversion — it makes the plural fundamental and the singular derivative, which is backwards from what he wanted?

3. Large language models and the current fights about machine consciousness. Given that he thought the mechanistic picture was real but incomplete, would he have conceded machine consciousness, or insisted that any consciousness in the machine is the same single consciousness, so the question of whether there's "another" one is malformed?

Also tell me which of the three he'd have found most damaging and which he'd have been most tempted to turn to his advantage, with rough credences rather than hedges. And flag one place where you think my reading of him — monism about subjects, not about experience, bodies as apertures — is just wrong.

Start with your bottom line in the very first sentence, a one-sentence prediction, then give me the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back at me. Be opinionated and committed: I'd rather argue with a confident reconstruction than be handed a both-sides survey. And if you think the whole exercise is built on a bad premise — that his Vedantism was decorative rather than something he argued from, or that counterfactual intellectual history of this kind can't be done honestly — say that plainly up front instead of humoring me.
