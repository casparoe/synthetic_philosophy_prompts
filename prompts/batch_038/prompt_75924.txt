I am writing a chapter of a dissertation on how an institution with more than one decision-maker can be said to hold judgments of its own, and I have found that the standard treatment of the discursive dilemma has gone stale for me: every account I read runs through the same three-judge contract-liability table and then stops, as though the interesting question ended once the arithmetic was displayed. I would like a treatment that keeps working after the messiness of a real deliberative body is added back in. Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words between two equally sophisticated proponents of the two opposed responses to the doctrinal paradox, set in the deliberation room of the board described below immediately after the votes have been counted and before anything has been recorded. One speaker is Ines, the chair and general counsel, who holds the group-agent line: the board is an entity that reasons, its judgment-set has to be capable of being consistent if it is to act and to be held to what it did, and therefore the premises carry the conclusion even when that overrides what a majority of members would have decided about the outcome. The other is Marcus, a senior member, who holds the outcome-first line associated with skeptical legal-process thinking about courts: the board is not a mind, only its members are; its job is to produce a decision, and the vote on the decision is the only vote that records what each member actually thinks should happen, so votes on premises are at most structured advice to the people casting them. Neither may be a strawman, neither may win by catching the other in an elementary slip, and each turn must take up what the other has just said rather than return to a prepared speech. Keep individual turns to a couple of paragraphs so that it reads as argument and not as alternating position papers.

The case I have stipulated is this. A five-member licensing board decides whether to grant an application. The agenda has three premises — A, the efficacy evidence is adequate; B, the risk profile is acceptable; C, the monitoring plan is adequate — and a conclusion R, the license is granted. Standing orders assert that every member accepts that R holds if and only if A, B, and C all hold. The votes come out as follows: Adaeze accepts A, B, C and votes for R. Bruno accepts A and B, rejects C, and votes against R. Chen accepts A and C, rejects B, and votes against R. Dario accepts B and C, rejects A, and votes against R. Elif accepts A, B, and C and votes against R. So each premise passes four to one, which on a premise-based procedure yields a grant, while the conclusion is rejected four to one, which on an outcome-based procedure yields a refusal. Three complications make this more than the textbook table. First, the board has for the past year operated under what its standing orders call a rapporteur protocol: the premises are voted first, and then a rotating rapporteur is obliged to record whichever conclusion follows from the premise majority and to write the opinion stating the board's reasons. Members have begun to notice that this changes how they vote on the premises once they can see that their premise vote may be decisive. Second, Elif's rejection of R does not come from any premise: she holds that a condition absent from the agenda altogether — the applicant's parent company is under an active fraud investigation — independently bars the license, so the standing orders' claim that everyone accepts the stated biconditional is false of her, and her own judgment-set is internally consistent on her view of what the agenda ought to have been. Third, when the board tried last term to settle its own procedure by voting separately on three amendments to the protocol, it ran into the same reversal at the level of the rules: a majority favored each amendment and a majority rejected the package.

Here is what I want the dialogue to do, in addition to being a genuine exchange. The two speakers must say what the board should actually record in this case and defend that against the other's objection; they must confront Elif's off-agenda reason, since it decides whether a premise-based procedure is tracking a conclusion that any member accepts; they must confront the strategic voting problem, that is, whether making the rule premise-based corrupts the premise votes on which it then relies; at least once one of them must propose a concrete institutional fix — a revised protocol, an enriched agenda, a two-stage vote, a standing rule for choosing among rules — and the other must show that the fix re-imports the paradox at a higher level rather than dissolving it; and the dialogue must end in an honest standoff, with concessions on particular points but no conversion and no agreed summary. Please include no citations, no bibliography, and no reading list: named authors may surface only as they naturally would in speech between two people who work in the field, and where a published result matters, have the character describe the result rather than attribute it precisely. After the dialogue, add a short referee's note in your own voice assessing how evenly matched the two came out, where you may have quietly handed one side the better resources, whether the disagreement is substantive or partly a dispute over whether saying that the board believes anything is doing any work at all, and whether anything in my stipulations is internally incoherent — in particular, whether a four-to-one premise tally alongside a one-to-four conclusion tally can really stand together with Elif's position as I have described it, or whether the case is not a single doctrinal paradox but two different problems wearing one profile. If you think the exercise as I have posed it cannot be done well — if the two views turn out to collapse into one another once the stipulations are fixed, or if one of my three complications is illusory — say so plainly at the top of your reply and explain why, rather than writing a courteous dialogue that papers over the difficulty.
