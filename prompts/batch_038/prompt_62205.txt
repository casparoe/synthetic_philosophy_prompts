I am a software engineer with roughly twelve years in numerical and simulation infrastructure — no philosophy coursework at any point, though I have been auditing an informal reading group that meets on Thursday evenings and works through philosophy of science. For the past month the group has been on the pessimistic meta-induction against scientific realism, and I found it compelling enough that I sat down over the weekend and tried to write out my own version of the reasoning as a numbered chain, the way I would pin down a bug report before filing it. The result is below, and my difficulty is that it appears to prove far too much: it ends in a skepticism so total that it seems to consume the argument that produced it, and then, in a last step I am not confident about, it turns around and hands realism back to me. I read it aloud to the group and four people objected to four different lines, which is precisely why I want a single careful diagnosis rather than a tally of opinions.

Two pieces of context for the steps below, since I drew on my own work to motivate them. For step (2), I was thinking of a failure mode we call circular validation: building a test oracle out of the same model you intend to validate, so the suite passes no matter what the world does. For step (4), I was thinking of a library that changes the meaning of a type while leaving its signature intact — callers still compile, but they silently receive different quantities than before, and there is no syntactic way to detect the shift from the calling side.

(1) The only thing that can justify believing a scientific theory is approximately true is its track record: its success at predicting and explaining phenomena, especially novel ones, better than its rivals manage to.
(2) A theory's track record cannot be assessed from outside it. Assessing it requires instruments, calibration procedures, error models, and auxiliary assumptions that are themselves justified by theory — often by the theory under test, and otherwise by its close relatives.
(3) So the evidential support a theory receives from its successes is generated inside a framework that the theory helps constitute; there is no framework-neutral tribunal at which theories may be judged.
(4) The history of science supplies numerous theories with excellent track records by the standards of their own day — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, Ptolemaic astronomy — which were subsequently displaced by theories that assign different truth values to their central claims and arguably different referents to their central terms.
(5) Therefore the property that (1) identifies as our sole ground for believing theories approximately true is not a reliable indicator of approximate truth: applied by competent inquirers in the past, it repeatedly licensed belief in theories now taken to be false.
(6) The reliability of an inference rule does not depend on when or by whom it is applied. If a rule led careful, well-equipped investigators astray again and again, it leads us astray now.
(7) Hence we are not justified in believing of any current theory that it is approximately true.
(8) But the historical claims deployed in (4) and (5) — that those theories were false, that a displacement occurred, that the older terms failed to refer — are themselves deliverances of current science and of a historiography that leans on it, so by (7) we are not justified in believing them either.
(9) Consequently, if the argument is sound, it removes the evidential basis of its own premises, and its conclusion is not merely anti-realism but a skepticism that includes the premises of the argument itself.
(10) That skepticism is intolerable. It would leave me with no reason to trust a convergence proof, a load test, or the rigid-body solver I maintain for a living, and it would make the observed reliability of engineering inexplicable.
(11) Therefore (1) is false: predictive success does justify belief in approximate truth, but by some route other than a track record assessed from within a framework — and realism survives.

What I am asking for is a diagnosis, not a survey of the debate. Tell me which step or steps actually fail, and if your view is that the chain is valid, tell me instead which premise is least costly to surrender. For each candidate you weigh, I want to know why the step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it and what giving it up costs — in particular, whether the rejection forfeits the inference from predictive success to truth altogether or only a specific version of that inference, and whether the rest of the argument can be repaired around the gap. Two structural questions I could not settle on my own deserve direct answers. First, does a conclusion that undermines the support for its own premises thereby come out false, or does it merely become unavailable as a piece of dialectic while remaining possibly true; I cannot reconstruct which of those I meant at (9) and (10), and I am not sure the argument is entitled to either. Second, is (11) licensed at all, since the falsity of (1) would seem to leave several replacements on the table, of which the one I gesture at is only one. My own inclination, for what it is worth, is to hold on to (6) and (10) and let (1) go: (6) reads to me like a bare requirement of consistency about rules, and (10) reads like a description of how I actually behave at work. I am aware that this is the kind of attachment that makes people defend the wrong premise, so please push on it rather than accommodate it. Finally, tell me whether the two engineering analogies are doing any work I have not noticed, in either direction — including the possibility that "approximate truth" has no counterpart in type-compatibility vocabulary at all, so that importing the analogy quietly replaces a graded notion with an all-or-nothing one that no realist was committed to. Please write in American English. I have no library access and no interest in citations, attributions, or a reading list; I want the reasoning itself.
