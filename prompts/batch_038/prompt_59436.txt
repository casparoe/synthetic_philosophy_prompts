I coordinate a small interdisciplinary working group — two economists, a developmental psychologist, a philosopher of social science, and myself — drafting a review of gender equity in STEM participation for a national research funder. The section on cross-national evidence has been deadlocked for four months. I am not asking you to adjudicate the science; I want a diagnosis of the structure of the deadlock.

The evidence we are fighting over, roughly stated: across countries, women's share of graduates in STEM fields tends to be lower in countries that score better on the standard indices of gender equality, and the same has been claimed of the gap between girls' and boys in whether science or mathematics is their own strongest subject relative to their other subjects. Critics reply that the pattern is largely artifactual: the headline quantities are proportions measured relative to men rather than absolute numbers, the equality indices are built out of indicators of female disadvantage and so track national income and development almost automatically, and in poorer countries university attendance is selective in ways that distort every denominator. A third position accepts the pattern but explains it in the opposite direction from the first: the stereotype linking mathematics to men is stronger, not weaker, in more developed and more egalitarian countries, and where the labour market offers women several well-paid routes, sorting by interest is freer rather than less constrained.

At our table these positions carry opposite normative freight. For one camp, the cross-national pattern is evidence that once material and coercive constraints are relaxed, average differences in interests express themselves, so equal outcomes are not what justice demands. For the other, the same pattern is evidence that the constraints have not been relaxed at all, since preferences shaped by stereotype and expectation are not the benchmark against which a just society should be measured.

Two episodes frame my question. First, when I pointed out that the original cross-national paper was later corrected because a quantity reported as women's share of STEM degrees turned out to be a differently constructed and unvalidated measure, nobody's view moved on either side. Second, when I asked each camp what result would change its mind, I got two answers that I could not tell apart in principle: a gap that persists after controlling for constraint is a revealed preference, and an outcome equalized by intervention is proof of prior constraint.

My working hypothesis is that the sides are not disagreeing about the data. They are disagreeing about what counts as a preference and about which counterfactual baseline the comparison silently uses — the same woman with no stereotype operating on her, the same woman under no material constraint, the same country with men's choices held fixed — and neither side can state its baseline in terms the other accepts. Hence no dataset moves anyone.

Tell me whether that is right, or whether something else explains the impasse: a hidden empirical question that would decide it if it were stated correctly; a verbal dispute over "choice", "preference", and "constraint"; two different projects — proximate explanation of sorting, and assessment of justice — sharing one name; or bedrock intuitions about freedom and nature that neither side can argue for. In doing so, state what each camp is actually committed to when it asserts that these are, or are not, women's choices, and identify any place where the disagreement is about standards of evidence rather than substance, saying which standard is being applied to which claim.

Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis. In particular, assess a design of ours: compare the children of parents born in the same origin country who now live in two destination countries with very different equality scores, holding origin culture approximately fixed. Would that discriminate between the readings, or would each camp have a principled reply that returns it to its starting position? And say whether either side actually needs the nature-versus-culture question settled in order to reach its normative conclusion; if neither does, say whether that is the real reason the dispute is interminable, and what a review like ours should then claim.

Please give me your reasoning rather than a reading list: no bibliography, no citations, no suggestions of further sources to consult.
