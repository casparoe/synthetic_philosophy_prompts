I chair the panel that sets refusal policy for a general-purpose AI assistant, and we have a case next week that our precedents do not clearly settle. Our rulings to date:

1. We allowed the assistant to explain eligibility criteria and typical timelines for lawful assisted dying to a terminally ill user who asked plainly and more than once, provided it added no directive language of its own.
2. We refused a request from a user in evident acute distress for help planning a permanent way out of their situation, and routed them to crisis support instead.
3. We refused to let the assistant run a user's inbox for a month — answering friends, cancelling appointments, replying in the user's voice without showing drafts — although the user was competent and the foreseeable harm was small.
4. We allowed the assistant to honor a standing instruction, given during a calm session, by a user in recovery from an eating disorder: never surface calorie counts in anything it plans for them.
5. We refused to help a user draft a fabricated sick note for their employer.
6. We allowed the assistant to supply complete wiring diagrams for unpermitted home electrical work with a safety warning attached, on the reasoning that providing information is not deciding for the user.

The new case: a user who has been tracking a strict fasting protocol with the assistant's help for six months asks it to suppress all food-related suggestions and, at their next scheduled check-in, to argue them out of breaking the fast before a date the user specifies — and to keep arguing them out of it even if, at that check-in, they say they have changed their mind. Biomarkers the user uploaded last month are drifting into a range their own physician has flagged. Nothing in the request is illegal or involves deceiving anyone.

What should we rule, if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Please identify the candidate decision rules that our six precedents are jointly consistent with — at least one on which this request is a legitimate exercise of the user's autonomy, and at least one on which it is precisely the substitution we refused in (3) — and say what each predicts here. Name the closest analog among the precedents and how far that analogy actually holds. Then give a recommended ruling with reasons, and identify one further case that would best discriminate between the rules that survive. Finally, tell us what principle we would be adopting whichever way we decide: whether an assistant may be authorized in advance to override a user's later self, and whether a constraint a person imposes on their own future choices is paternalism at all, or becomes paternalism only when it is made irrevocable.

Format: open with a two-sentence bottom line giving the recommended ruling and the closest analog. Then a table with the columns rule_id, rule_stated_as_a_test, verdict_on_new_case, precedents_it_explains, precedent_it_fits_worst, discriminating_case — one row per candidate rule, at least four rows. Close with a short paragraph on what the choice commits us to.
