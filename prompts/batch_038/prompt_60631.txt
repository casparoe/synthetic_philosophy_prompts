For thirty-four years I worked as an experimental physicist at a national laboratory, almost all of it on the instrumentation side of low-temperature measurement: designing detectors, chasing noise floors, and acquiring a professional suspicion of any signal I could not trace back to a calibrated reference. I retired five years ago and have since been reading philosophy with an earnestness my former colleagues find faintly comic. My present subject is the history of the idea that the brain might be understood as a computing machine, and in particular John von Neumann's Yale lectures — the ones he prepared while already seriously ill, never delivered, and published after his death in 1958 as *The Computer and the Brain* — read alongside his earlier work on constructing reliable systems from unreliable components. Before anything else, let me say what I do not want: no bibliography, no reading list, and no attempt to quote him. I have the book, and I would much rather have your own careful paraphrase of a position than a sentence in quotation marks that you are not entirely certain he wrote.

The occasion is a disagreement. A former doctoral student of mine now directs machine-learning research at a company here in Cambridge, and over lunch last month he advanced a thesis he calls the depth inversion. His account of von Neumann runs roughly as follows. The digital computer of the mid-1950s bought its reliability in one particular way: high precision at each elementary operation, short chains of complicated logical operations, and a memory retrieved by address. The nervous system appeared to him to buy reliability in very nearly the opposite way: minimal precision at each elementary step — a handful of significant digits at most, and noisy ones — combined with enormously long chains of simple operations, in which redundancy and statistical averaging do the work that exactness does in a machine, and in which what the organism knows is embodied in the structure of the organ rather than filed in a register one can point to. My former student's claim is that the past decade of machine learning has quietly moved our machines out of the first regime and into the second. A large trained network computes with weights held at four bits or fewer, is deliberately exposed to injected noise during training, survives the deletion of a substantial fraction of its units, and owes its dependability not to the exactness of any single operation but to the redundancy of very many imprecise ones. On von Neumann's own axes, he argued, a modern language model resembles the nervous system von Neumann described far more closely than it resembles the machine against which von Neumann was describing it: statistical and arithmetical depth has displaced logical depth as the source of reliability.

What I want from you is the reply von Neumann would have given, had he lived past February 1957 to see the thing built. Please be specific rather than oracular, and organise the answer around the following pressure points. First, what would he have conceded as vindication, and what would he have rejected as a misreading of what he meant by reliability? My own suspicion, which I should like you to test rather than flatter, is that his notion was a much stricter one — the correct output of a system assembled from error-prone components, secured by redundancy re-applied periodically and subject to a threshold — and that the depth-inversion thesis slides from that notion into something he would not have recognised, namely the approximate adequacy of a system's outputs across a distribution of tasks. Would he have called that robustness rather than reliability, and thought the distinction fatal to my former student's argument? Second, on memory: he distinguished memory retrieved by address from memory embodied in physical structure, and supposed the nervous system to use predominantly the second. A single contemporary system uses both at once — competence carried in weights that are frozen during deployment, plus a context window that behaves as a transient, directly addressed buffer, wiped at the end of each episode and never consolidated into structure. Would he have taken this as a refutation of the dichotomy, or as evidence that the dichotomy was never about brains against machines, but about two kinds of store between which any competent system must divide its labour? And what would he have made of the fact that we now consolidate that transient buffer into structure only occasionally and at great cost, by retraining — a practice whose resemblance to what the brain appears to do in sleep I suspect he would have found more suggestive than I do? Third, on his conjecture about the code of the central nervous system: he held that it is not the language of mathematics or of formal logic, that it is of a more statistical and less logically articulated character, and, crucially, that it is not the language the organism speaks. A large language model is trained on nothing but the language human beings speak, yet its internal states are demonstrably not in that language. Would he have read this as confirmation of the conjecture, or would he have objected that a system trained on utterances and evaluated on utterances tells us nothing whatever about the code of a nervous system that was never trained on utterances at all — and that the current fashion for probing internal representations is a way of reading structure off behaviour, which he regarded as an inference, not an observation?

Fourth, and more broadly: in the mid-1950s the computer was the instrument with which one interrogated the brain, whereas now the traffic runs largely the other way, with engineers borrowing sparsity, event-driven timing, and a budget of some twenty watts from biology. Would he have judged the analogy exhausted, or would he have welcomed its becoming a two-way street? And finally, I should like you to identify the place where his own commitments pull against one another: he was in effect a computationalist before the word existed, and yet he repeatedly insisted that the nervous system's code differs in kind, not merely in implementation detail, from anything the machines he designed could do. Which of those two commitments would the intervening seventy years have pressured more severely, and would an honest reconstruction have to concede that he could not have held both? Throughout, please distinguish clearly between what is anchored in positions he actually took and what is extrapolation on your part, and mark the latter as such; where you think the evidence cuts against my former student, say so plainly, and where you think it cuts against me, say that too. Somewhere between 1,200 and 1,600 words would be right, and I should be grateful if you would write in British English throughout.
