I am drafting a chapter on the extended mind and want my own case verdicts stress-tested before I commit them to print. The framework I take myself to be working inside is Clark and Chalmers's parity principle from their 1998 paper — roughly, that if "a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process," then it is part of the cognitive process — together with the conditions they attach to Otto's notebook: constantly carried and consulted whenever relevant, contents available at a glance, automatically endorsed on retrieval, and so endorsed because they have proven reliable in the past. Here is what I actually say about cases, in the order the cases occur to me.

1. Rotating a falling shape on a screen by pressing a button rather than rotating it in my head: the button is part of the cognitive process.
2. Working a long multiplication out in pen and paper, or rearranging letter tiles to prompt recall: part of it.
3. Otto's notebook: part of his mind, in the sense that notebook-plus-Otto holds a belief that Inga holds in her head.
4. My hearing aid: part of my perceptual apparatus, not merely an instrument I use to perceive.
5. The layout of my kitchen, which I have tuned over a decade and in which I can cook a complicated meal without deliberation — and in which, when a housemate rearranges two drawers, I measurably cannot: part of my cooking competence.
6. A notes file I have kept for nine years, full of thoughts I almost never reread and could not reproduce on demand: not part of my mind. It is an archive, not a memory.
7. Turn-by-turn navigation on my phone, used daily, never checked against my own sense of direction, and relied on to the point that I could not retrace a commute I have driven two hundred times if the phone died: not part of my mind. It has not extended my sense of direction; it has retired it.
8. The calculator I use for the household budget, consulted weekly, trusted, never re-derived: part of it — even though I must concede that my unaided arithmetic is much worse than it was before I started using it.
9. My co-author of fifteen years, who handles all the statistical modelling for our joint work, whose methods I never learned and whose output I cannot audit, but whom I trust absolutely: part of a cognitive system comprising both of us, though not part of my mind.
10. The language-model assistant that summarises papers for me, which I have shaped over two years to my own annotation habits, consult before reading anything, trust, and cannot audit: not part of my mind. Whatever that arrangement is, it is rented cognition belonging to something else.
11. The ring that scores my sleep and, on mornings when it reports a bad night, leads me to feel and perform as though I had had one whether or not I did: not part of my mind. It does not so much track my state as install one.
12. A mnemonic tattooed on my forearm, which I read several times a day without noticing that I read it: part of it.
13. A chess engine I study with every night, which has reshaped my positional instincts, but which is not in the room when I play: the reshaped instincts are mine; the engine is not part of my mind during the game.

My tentative principle is this: a resource is part of my mind just in case (i) I consult it without deciding to consult it, (ii) I accept its deliverances the way I accept my own recall, without checking them, and (iii) I would experience its sudden loss as the loss of a capacity rather than of a convenience. I suspect this is not the same as the Clark–Chalmers conditions, and I suspect it does not survive contact with the list above.

What I want from you is reflective equilibrium done properly, not a survey:

- State the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. Give each clause precisely, and identify which of my cases that clause exists to handle.
- Two pairs are what keep me awake. Cases 7 and 8 look alike on every condition I have written down — constant, unreflective, trusted consultation, and acknowledged atrophy of the underlying skill — yet I say the GPS is no part of my mind and the calculator is. Cases 9 and 10 also look alike — permanent, unaudited, wholly trusted delegation of a cognitive task — yet I grant one a place in a cognitive system and deny the other any. Tell me whether a single principle can save both pairs, what it would have to say to do so, and whether the price in complexity is worth it compared with a simpler principle that overturns one verdict in each pair. If you recommend overturning a verdict, argue for it rather than announcing it, and say which of my cases then becomes the odd one out.
- Test my three-clause principle against all thirteen cases and report exactly where it delivers the wrong result.
- Say whether the winning principle is exposed to the coupling–constitution objection pressed by Adams and Aizawa, or whether the conditions I have added in order to get my negative verdicts are precisely what protects it.
- Deliver the principle's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a neural implant that performs the rotation at the same speed as the screen button; a dream journal kept faithfully for thirty years but consulted only in the hour before writing; and my spouse's memory of every social obligation this household has, which I rely on as unreflectively as I rely on my own recall of where I parked. Then propose one further case of your own that would discriminate between the candidate principles you have offered, and say how each of them rules on it.
- Finally, be blunt about this: can the parity principle as I have quoted it be made to deliver my negative verdicts in 6, 7, 10, and 11 at all, or have I already left the framework I said I was working inside?
