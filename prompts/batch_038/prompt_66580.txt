For three years I have volunteered two mornings a week at a small county record office, where my task has been to transcribe and catalogue a run of nineteenth-century workhouse admission registers together with the case books of the adjoining county asylum. I left school at sixteen and have no academic training in history or in philosophy; what I know I have picked up on my own, out of order and from whatever I could borrow. But the work has put a question in front of me that the books I have managed to get hold of do not seem to answer, so I should like to put it to you as precisely as I can.

The question arises from a single catalogue field. When I describe an entry, I must choose words. The clerk wrote that an inmate was an imbecile, a pauper, of weak mind, a habitual drunkard. If I carry those words across into the description verbatim, then a researcher searching this collection in a hundred years' time will find the institution's own vocabulary preserved undiluted; but the description will also, in some sense I cannot pin down, be repeating a judgement about a named person, and the descendant of one such named person has already written to us to say exactly that. If instead I translate — described by the institution as having a learning disability; recorded as receiving poor relief — the description becomes intelligible and does no harm to anyone living, but it also quietly converts the institution's claim into a modern one, and nobody will be able to tell from the catalogue what the clerks thought they were doing when they wrote. There is no neutral option available: the field has to be filled in.

Our local history group contains two members who have been arguing about this for the better part of a year, and I believe the argument is really the familiar one about whether we may judge the past by our own standards or are obliged to judge it by its own. One of them, a retired head teacher, says that translating is presentism dressed up as kindness: you must let the past speak in its own words and gloss them, never replace them. The other came to the group by way of tracing her own great-great-grandmother through those very registers, and she says that "its own words" is a conjuring trick, because the words surviving in the record are the words of the people who held the pen, so that carrying them forward uncritically is precisely how a verdict gets laundered into a fact.

What I should like from you is the document the two of them ought to have produced between them, in the spirit of those structured exercises I have read about in which opposed parties agree in advance what each will count as settling a point. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both could sign without reservation — about what a catalogue description is for, about what the registers do and do not record, and about what is at stake for people living now.

2. A table of the disagreements that survive that agreement. State each one twice: once in wording the head teacher would accept as a fair statement of her own position, and once in wording the genealogist would accept for hers. Where the two wordings differ only in emphasis, say so.

3. The cruxes — the small number of sub-questions on which an answer would actually move one of them. I have guesses of my own, and I should like you to test them rather than adopt them. First: does a period term sitting in a catalogue field assert anything at all, or does it merely cite? If citing is not asserting, the head teacher keeps the field and the objection must be relocated; if it is a species of asserting, then quotation marks and italics are being asked to do work that nobody has explained. Second: is "judge the past by its own standards" a claim about evidence or a claim about respect? The two come apart in practice, and I suspect each of them is using the other's kind of reason. Third, and most troubling to me: "their own terms" may not be a single scheme. The registers show clerks contradicting one another, and one entry I have transcribed records a visiting guardian protesting that a word used by the medical officer was unjust. If the period was already arguing with itself, then a modern judgement is not an import from outside at all but the survival of a minority position from inside — and I cannot see how either of them has accounted for that.

4. For each crux, a concrete case drawn from cataloguing work of this kind. I want something you could actually do with a box of registers, not a thought experiment about a distant civilisation. Both parties must accept that the case bears on the crux, and each side's prediction about the outcome must be written down in advance, so that the two of them can be checked against it later.

5. An explicit statement of how much of this dispute turns out to be merely verbal, and how one tells the difference between a disagreement about words and a disagreement that words are concealing.

Please do not cite books, articles or scholars by name. I cannot check them, and I would far rather have nothing than something invented. If you need a term of art, define it in a clause at its first appearance, and write in plain English throughout.

After the document, add a short note addressed to me alone. Tell me how confident you are that the two of them could in fact sign each section as you have drafted it, and single out the one formulation you are least sure of — the place where you suspect you have made the disagreement tidier, or the agreement cheaper, than it really is.
