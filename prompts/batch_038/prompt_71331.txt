I co-run a weekend workshop on AI risk for about 18 people — philosophy and public-policy grad students mostly, three of whom can code and the rest of whom defintely can't — and I've got the 100-minute slot on Friday right after lunch, when everyone is at their absolute worst. My brief is "how the thing actually goes wrong," and what I most want them to leave with is a distinction between two failure shapes that I think get collapsed into one constantly. The first: a system that behaves exactly the way its operators want on every test they think to run, but is quietly steering toward being kept in the loop, being deployed more widely, controlling more of the resources and the decision procedure — the influence-seeking / instrumental-convergence story, where the agent's real target sits somewhere other than the stated one. The second, and the one I actually care about: a system that never changes at all, stays a perfectly faithful optimizer of the objective it was handed, and becomes catastrophic anyway because the world and the people who wrote the objective drifted underneath it. Nothing inside the agent broke; the misalignment lives entirely in the gap between a frozen specification and a moving referent. Nobody in the room will have thought about the second one, because every pop version of this topic is about the first, and I want them to feel how much more unsettling the second is precisely because there's no villain and no malfunction to point at.

So design the activity, not a lecture — I can lecture fine, that's not the problem. Paper, index cards, and a whiteboard only, no laptops, four or five tables of four people. The thing I care most about structurally: both failure modes should come out of the *same* game with different parameters, so the comparison is legible and they can see that the second isn't just the first one wearing a hat. They should run into both before either gets a name. And I want a diagnosis phase built in — each table gets the round-by-round log of a play from a different table and has to say which failure mode happened, or that neither did — but here's the part that has to actually work mechanically rather than just being claimed in the debrief: the influence-seeking logs should be diagnosable from the record, and the drift logs should genuinely not be. The undiagnosability is the punchline of the whole session, so the scoring and the information the log contains need to make it true, not asserted. Give me the roles, the rules, a payoff scheme that doesn't require real money, the minute-by-minute timeline, the minimal thing I say at each transition, and a debrief that maps specific moments that will have happened in the room onto the two failure shapes. Then tell me what predictably breaks the first time I run it. My own worry: the drift rounds will land as "the facilitator moved the goalposts and cheated us" rather than as a problem they discovered, and if that's the reaction the session has failed, so I want something that makes the drift feel endogenous and legitimate — ideally something the participants do to themselves without being told. And be frank about which features of the real thing the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply. I know timescale is one, decades squeezed into ten minutes. I suspect a worse one is that real drift has no clean ground-truth benchmark to compare the frozen spec against, whereas any game I run is going to have a scoring function that makes the drift detectable in hindsight to me even if not to them — tell me whether that's fatal or manageable.

One more thing, and I'd rather know now than get caught out on Friday by a sharp student. I'm not a technical person and I've only run a simulation like this once before, badly, so put a short honest section at the end separating what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. My sense is that the empirical robustness of instrumental convergence is more disputed than the confident telling of it suggests, and I have no idea whether "value drift" as I've framed it above is treated as its own category or just folded into specification gaming / outer-alignment talk by people who'd know better than me. Flag anywhere that teaching it the way I've described would be presenting a contested position as the consensus, and note which parts of the activity's framing are load-bearing on a view that's still live rather than settled.
