I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar on prediction, falsification, and the long run, cross-listed with the economics department, and I am writing the three-hour final. It is a two-of-four essay paper, each question worth 25 marks, so candidates have roughly 45 minutes per answer and write closed-book. Last year I set a question on whether Malthusian population theory had been refuted by the nineteenth-century English experience, and the scripts were dismal in a very specific way: almost every one said some version of "Malthus was falsified in outcome, but Lakatos would classify the theory as a progressive research programme because it kept generating auxiliaries," and then stopped. The labels did all the work, and nobody had to say what made an auxiliary progressive rather than degenerating. I want this year's question to make that move impossible to pull off.

Please build the question around a genuine inversion, and put the empirical material in the stem itself, so that no candidate needs to recall studies or dates. The material I have in mind runs as follows. Malthus's 1798 Essay argued that population, checked only by misery and vice, presses against a food supply capable of expanding only arithmetically; in the greatly expanded 1803 edition he added a third check, moral restraint, meaning the postponement of marriage until one could support a family. For pre-industrial England the demographic record broadly supports the mechanism he described: marriage rates and fertility tracked real wages with a lag, so that improved living standards produced earlier marriage and more children, and a large share of the population growth of the later eighteenth century came from rising fertility rather than falling mortality. Then the mechanism stops working. English working-class real wages were roughly flat for about half a century while output per head rose, and from the middle of the nineteenth century onward wages rose persistently while population kept growing, with the wage-marriage link weakening and eventually reversing; rising yields, imported grain, and later industrial fertilizer and improved crop varieties did the rest of the damage to the arithmetic-ratio premise. That leaves two candidate repairs to the theory, and I want the question to force candidates to compare them under an explicit criterion of ad hoc-ness. Repair one is the addition of moral restraint, which on its face looks like a pure immunizing stratagem, since it converts a predicted catastrophe into a conditional one whose failure to materialize can always be attributed to insufficient virtue. Repair two is the appeal to unforeseen exogenous improvement in food production, which on its face looks like ordinary scientific practice, since it points at independently measured quantities. My own suspicion — and this is what the question should test rather than assume — is that the ranking is backwards: moral restraint is the auxiliary with real independent content, because it implies that otherwise similar populations with a late-marriage pattern should be better off than populations that marry early, a claim checkable across regions and against later fertility declines, whereas the productivity appeal is produced only after the failure and constrains nothing unless it is measurable in advance. Set alongside these, in the stem, the contrast case of the Phillips curve: when the stable unemployment-inflation trade-off broke down, the expectations-augmented version rescued the relationship and in doing so predicted a combination the original curve had ruled out. Finally, the question should make candidates say what the verdict "right but early" could coherently mean at all — which component of a theory is being judged, the mechanism or the long-run equilibrium claim — instead of letting them use the phrase as an escape hatch.

Deliverables, please: (1) the question stem, 350–450 words, self-contained and closed-book, ending in a task completable in 45 minutes that requires candidates to state a criterion separating a legitimate auxiliary adjustment from an immunizing stratagem without condemning ordinary ceteris paribus reasoning, then apply it to both repairs and to the Phillips-curve case, then render and defend a verdict; (2) a model answer of about 750 words; (3) a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, organised around six must-hit moves, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it; and (4) an explicit statement of the boundary between a first-class answer and a competent but generic one, drawn in terms of the specific moves present or absent rather than in adjectives. Alongside these, give me a short self-critical note. Which of the empirical claims summarised above would you want hedged or cut before the stem goes in front of students? How confident are you in your own ranking of the two repairs, and what consideration — a particular argument, or particular evidence about the wage-marriage relationship after 1850 — would flip your verdict about which repair is the ad hoc one? And if you think a genuinely strong candidate could resist the framing sketched here and still deserve full marks, describe what that dissenting answer would look like and where the marking scheme should make room for it.
