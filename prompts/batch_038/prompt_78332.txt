Right, so my mate Priya chairs the grants panel for a small disability charity — it runs an "independence fund", about £60k a year of discretionary money, £5,000 cap per person, mostly kit and adaptations the NHS won't touch. We spent two hours on Sunday rowing about whether she's actually got a principle or just vibes, and I've come away unable to tell. Settle it for me. She insists there's a proper answer to the equality-of-what question and that she's got it; I think she's got a soft spot for expensive hardware and a reflex against anything that smells like a lifestyle choice.

Her verdicts over the last few years, as best I can remember them:

- Man with MS, £4,200 for a stairlift. He hadn't left the house in eight months and was by any measure wretched. Funded.
- Deaf woman, £1,500 for a light-alert doorbell and a video relay setup. She told the panel she was perfectly happy as she was and texts everyone anyway; she mainly wanted it for ringing her mum, who's 84. Funded.
- Man with no impairment at all, recently redundant, said he was the unhappiest person in the county and wanted £800 for something. Refused, on the grounds the fund is for disabled people.
- Young woman with a rare degenerative condition, £5,000 — the maximum — for a powerchair with tilt and recline. Famously sunny about the whole thing, kept insisting she was fine and the money should go to someone worse off. Funded anyway.
- Man with a spinal injury, £3,500 for a bespoke adapted guitar plus lessons. He'd picked the instrument back up recently and said his mood had gone to pieces without it. Refused — "we're not a hobbies fund."

Thursday's application is this one. Woman, 52, condition stable-ish for a decade. She wants £4,800 for a custom-moulded seating system and pressure-relieving setup for her chair. Nobody disputes that it's a cost her impairment creates — a non-disabled person would never buy the thing. But her GP's letter says there's no clinical risk being managed, it's about posture and comfort; by her own account and everyone else's it would buy her maybe twenty extra upright minutes a day; she says she's "not fussed" and has said some version of that for years; she doesn't drive, isn't in work, and the panel can't see much of a social life. There are two other applications in the pile that month and the fund won't stretch to all three.

So, what I want:

1. Spell out the rule or rules that actually fit all five of those verdicts. There's more than one, and I want them stated precisely enough that they'd give different answers somewhere — not "she cares about need", which fits everything and therefore nothing. Include at least one obvious candidate that her record rules *out*, and say which verdicts kill it.
2. Bet on which rule she's running, and therefore what she does with the seating system on Thursday. Give me your reasoning, and be honest about how confident you are.
3. If you think she should fund it to be consistent with herself, say which of the five is the closest analogue and what principle she'd be signing up to either way — including what she gives up if she refuses.
4. Design a sixth application that would finally tell us apart which rule she's on. Make it realistic and specific (impairment, item, cost, what it would actually let her do, what she says about her own life), and crucially set it in a month when the pot has plenty in it, so a refusal can't be explained away by scarcity. Tell me which way each candidate rule points on it.
5. If you think one of the five was simply a mistake — an outlier she can't live with — say which, and what she'd have to abandon to admit it.

One thing: don't hedge into "it depends on the fund's remit". I've read the remit. It says the panel should "enable disabled adults to live as independently as possible", which as you can imagine settles absolutely nothing.
