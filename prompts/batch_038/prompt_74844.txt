Context, sorry: I'm in my last year of a clinical fellowship, and in six months I move into a role where I'm the one signing off on a particular treatment decision. The evidence on that decision is completely frozen — one middling trial, a meta-analysis, and the trialists have said publicly there's no new readout for like 18 months. Right now I'd put about 0.8 on "A beats B for this patient group." I'm also about 95% sure that once the decision is actually mine and there's a person in the room, my number drops to something like 0.55. I've watched four attendings I genuinely admire make that exact move, and not one of them learned anything new — same frozen data the whole time. What changed is what was riding on it. And my mom is in this patient group, so it isn't academic for me.

Van Fraassen's Reflection principle, as I understand it, says that if I'm that confident about what my future credence in a claim will be, my present credence should already match it — otherwise I'm incoherent across time and exploitable. So on my own numbers I should apparently be at 0.55 now. Something in me resists that hard: the shift isn't me getting more informed or more rational, it's the stakes moving, and I don't see why my low-stakes present self has to import my high-stakes future self's caution. But I also don't have a principled reason to say that, which is the problem.

I'm putting together a short talk for a philosophy-of-medicine workshop and every version of my situation I try gets eaten by an obvious objection ("you'll get new evidence", "your future self will just be tired and scared", "you're not really that sure what you'll think"). So: build me the cleanest possible version as a designed thought experiment.

- The protagonist (can be me or an invented character, whichever makes the design cleaner) knows to very high confidence what their future credence in some ordinary empirical proposition P will be, and it's sharply different from their present one.
