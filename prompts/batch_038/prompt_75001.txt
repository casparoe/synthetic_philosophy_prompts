Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, in close third person, set in a municipal emergency service that maintains what it calls the Surplus Register.

The Register works as follows. When a member of the service performs an act that no statute, employment contract, roster assignment, or professional standard required of them, and that act nevertheless produced a benefit the service was in any case obliged to secure by other means, a supervisor may certify the act as surplus. Certified surplus is entered to the member's account as credit. Credit may later be spent: a member holding sufficient credit may decline a lawful order, miss a training cycle, or take a posting for which they are otherwise ineligible, and none of this counts as dereliction. The service is proud of the arrangement and describes it, in its recruitment literature, as finally giving the extra mile a fair price.

Two further rules govern the Register, and the story should turn on both of them.

First, reclassification. Once an act-type has been certified a specified number of times across the service as a whole, the standing orders are amended so that acts of that type fall within the ordinary duty of every member holding the relevant rank. Thereafter the act can no longer be certified, and failure to perform it becomes a disciplinary matter.

Second, retrospection. When an act-type is reclassified, the amendment is treated as having been in force throughout the preceding assessment period. Members who performed no acts of that type during the period, and who had been recorded as merely satisfactory, are re-recorded as deficient.

Your protagonist holds the largest surplus balance in the service's history. The story should open on the day she learns that the fiftieth certification of her signature act — the one she has performed more often than anyone else in the service — will trigger reclassification under the first rule and, under the second, will retroactively demote some dozens of her colleagues, among them the man who trained her and who is six weeks from a pension calculated on his final grade.

The story should make the reader feel the pull of two claims that cannot both be honored: that what she has been doing is genuinely more than anyone was entitled to ask of her, and that her doing it has quietly altered what may now be asked of everyone else. It should also press a narrower question through scene rather than argument — whether an act performed partly in order to build a balance, by someone who knows the act will be priced, numbered, and counted, can still belong on the far side of the line separating the required from the beyond-the-required.

Constraints on execution: no character may name or define a philosophical term of art, and the narration may not supply one either; the conceptual issues are to be legible only in what people do, what the paperwork says, and what they decline to say to each other. Include at least one scene in which the paperwork is itself a dramatic presence — a certification form, a credit number, a draft amendment circulated for comment — and at least one extended conversation in which the trainer and the protagonist disagree without either of them being made a fool or a villain. Do not resolve the dilemma by having the narration endorse one choice as correct; end instead at a point where the reader can see plainly what each available course would cost, and whom it would cost it to.
