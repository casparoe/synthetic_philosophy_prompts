so my friend Dane runs an s-risk reading group and last week he unveiled what he calls the safe-option argument, and i've been unable to find the leak in it for five days now. He's sharper than me and he says its airtight. Roughly in his order:

Setup. Three trajectories for the next million years. F = civilisation survives and scales, on the order of 10^16 flourishing lives, call its value +V. E = we're gone soon and nothing further ever happens, value 0. S = lock-in, a permanent unexitable state in which something like 10^16 beings are made to suffer intensely, value −200V. Dane's probabilities: P(F) = .60, P(E) = .395, P(S) = .005.

1. The only thing that gives the far future moral weight is that it would contain vast numbers of sentient lives, and what matters across them is the balance of suffering against wellbeing.
2. A unit of intense suffering is much worse than a unit of flourishing is good — Dane says a factor of at least 200, and when pushed he says it isn't really a finite factor at all, that some suffering just isn't tradeable against happiness at any rate. He treats that as a stronger version of the same claim rather than a different claim.
3. On any trajectory where we survive and scale there's a nonzero chance of S. He uses .005.
4. Our present choices don't move that .005 by much. Somebody builds the relevant systems eventually whatever we do, so the chance of S is about the same whether we push, pause, or quit.
5. So surviving-and-scaling has expected value .6(V) + .005(−200V) = −0.4V, which is worse than E, which is 0.
6. Therefore it would be better if the last of us died sooner rather than later. We can't choose that, so the next best thing is a permanent, global stop on everything that raises the chance of scaling.
7. And that's a duty, not a taste: anybody who keeps working on the future is gambling with −0.4V using chips that aren't theirs.

The bit that makes me think it proves too much: by (4), quitting carries the same .005, so (5) and (7) seem to condemn the quitting that (6) recommends, and I can't see how he gets out of that. Also if I nudge P(S) from .005 down to .001 the expected value flips positive, which makes the whole thing feel like it's hanging off two decimal places — but Dane says the arithmetic is besides the point, and then he only ever makes the argument with the arithmetic.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (7). Tell me where it goes wrong and what holding onto (2) costs me.
