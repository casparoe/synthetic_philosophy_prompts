I'm in my second year and doing a module on death and the meaning of life, and this week we've been on Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife -- the doomsday one where you're told the earth will be destroyed shortly after you die, and the infertility one, and his claim that way more of what we care about leans on other people still being around after us than any of us would admit. My seminar slot is next Thursday and I don't want to just re-run the doomsday scenario, everyone in the group has already seen it. I want to build a case that actually seperates two rival explanations of why it lands the way it does.

Here's the two readings, in my own words, and I'd like you to use these labels rather than inventing others:

- Load-bearing: humanity carrying on is genuinely part of the structure of a lot of our reasons. If everyone else were wiped out right after me, the things I value would really lose their point, not just feel like they had.
- Amplifier: nothing about value in general depends on the future. The doomsday scenario just takes two entirely self-regarding things -- fear of my own death and resentment that my own efforts will be erased -- and makes them vivid and cosmic-sounding. The horror is personal, dressed up in universal language.

Design me a thought experiment in which those two come apart: a person who reacts exactly as the load-bearing reading predicts, but whom the amplifier reading has nothing left to work with. Constraints:

1. The scenario itself is one paragraph, contemporary and ordinary, and the only stipulated element is the announcement that the world is going to end a little while after she dies. Don't explain how, don't add anything sci-fi, no visions, no dreams, no mysterious knowledge of the future.
2. She has no children, grandchildren or students, nothing she's left unfinished, no reputation or legacy she's protecting, no religious hope of any kind, and she's old enough and ill enough that her own death isn't a shock to her and she's long since made her peace with it. Basically: strip out every hook the amplifier reading could grab onto.
3. The thing she cares about has to be a present-directed, self-contained good -- something she does for its own sake on an ordinary morning, with no payoff that needs a future to be realised in. Not a novel she's writing, not a garden she's planting for somebody else.
4. Her reaction must not be fear, or grief for other people, or sadness that she'll die. It has to be a judgement that this particular thing would stop making sense.
5. She must not be a philosopher or have read anything like this, otherwise she might just be parroting a thesis back at us. And please don't have her say anything that sounds like a slogan -- she should sound like a person at a kitchen table.
