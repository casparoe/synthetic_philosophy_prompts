For the past two years I have been reading, entirely on my own and without any academic training, about whether the shift from foraging to farming was a catastrophe. It started with a magazine essay from the late 1980s by a physiologist who went on to write about guns, germs, and steel, arguing that agriculture was the worst mistake in the history of the human race; then I got pulled into the older anthropological claim that hunter-gatherers were the original affluent society and worked something like fifteen hours a week; and then, because I could not leave well enough alone, I got pulled into the counterarguments — that the famous work-hour figures came from one well-watered group during a drought year when their farming neighbors' crops failed, that they counted the time spent acquiring food but not the time spent processing it or carrying children, that the peoples usually cited as untouched foragers were in fact long entangled with herders and traders and had been pushed to the margins, that cultivation was taken up slowly and independently in a dozen regions rather than chosen at a moment, and that forager affluence depended in part on keeping population low by means nobody would call comfortable. My brother-in-law, who runs a small farm, has told me twice that this whole line of thinking is romantic nonsense, and he is not entirely wrong. I find the subject genuinely funny in a bleak way, and I want to write something that captures it for a call for submissions from a small magazine here in town that publishes humorous nonfiction about big ideas; their limit is fifteen hundred words and the deadline is the end of the month, so I have room to work. My day job is in IT operations, and the only prose form I know from the inside is the incident post-mortem we file at work after something goes wrong, so that is the form I want to use.

Please act as a senior site reliability engineer who is also, improbably, a competent anthropologist and archaeologist, and write me a complete blameless post-mortem, roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, filed by a fictional internal platform team on behalf of the species: an incident review of the Neolithic transition, in which foraging is the deprecated legacy architecture and cultivation is a migration that has been running for ten thousand years without a tested rollback plan. Use the section headings such a document ordinarily has — summary, severity, impact, detection, timeline, root cause, contributing factors, what went well, what went wrong, where we got lucky, action items with owners and priorities and statuses, a closing status line — and finish with a short comment thread in which two or three stakeholders identified only by initials argue about whether the ticket can be closed. Invent freely at the organizational level: ticket numbers, an internal dashboard, an on-call rotation, a per-user wellbeing metric with a name like Average Session Quality, a past planning cycle in which that metric was formally deprecated as "not actionable" in favor of total concurrent users, a metric-integrity auditor sitting on the review board. What I do not want invented is the substance. The jokes should be load-bearing on real findings and real arguments, so that an archaeologist laughs at the accuracy rather than at the jokes: the impact section should be able to draw on what skeletal and dental evidence says about stature, caries, and the narrowing of diet to a few storable carbohydrates; the contributing factors should include the demographic mechanism by which sedentism and weaning foods shorten birth spacing; the timeline should reflect that sedentism and storage preceded full domestication in at least one well-studied region and that the whole thing unfolded over millennia in multiple centers; and the blocked rollback action item should list its dependencies honestly — carrying capacity, stored surplus, heritable land, record-keeping, and a user base that no longer fits on the land that can support it.

Before you write the document, give me a short planning note of five or six sentences naming the two or three targets of the satire and, for each, the factual claim the joke depends on; think carefully about which of those claims you can actually defend, and if one of them is shakier than I have made it sound, write the joke so that it survives the shakiness. Then give me the finished post-mortem. The deepest target, as I see it, is not farming and not farmers: it is the habit of scoring a change of that magnitude by a single aggregate number, and the further irony that the genre itself is rigged, because a post-mortem presupposes an owner, a decision, a control group, and a rollback path, and the record suggests there were none of those. Please let the piece notice that — that the baseline was flattered by the very metric used to praise it, and that the treatment group was never isolated from the control — but keep it deadpan and let the reader notice it too: no winking, no authorial nudges, no closing paragraph that explains the point. The satire has to be fair to both sides of the argument I sketched, which means the what-went-well section has to be genuinely persuasive rather than a token gesture, and it should be aimed at a practice and a style of discourse rather than at any named living person, so please do not build jokes out of real researchers' names or put words in anyone's mouth. Write it in American English throughout, and do not include a references section, a bibliography, or citations to real books and articles — if the document gestures at evidence, have it do so the way internal documents do, by describing findings rather than by fabricating sources.
