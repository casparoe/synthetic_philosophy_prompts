I am drafting a chapter of my dissertation on the dispute between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature, and before I commit it to paper I want an audit of an analogy that has quietly become load-bearing in my own argument.

The analogy is the legal one, which everyone in my reading group reaches for: laws of nature are like the laws of a legal system. In the more refined form I have been using, the comparison splits down the middle of the debate. On the anti-Humean side, laws of nature are like statutes: binding on the parties whether or not anyone has tabulated the resulting behaviour, and the source of what happens rather than a record of it. On the Humean side, laws of nature are like a Restatement: a systematic digest compiled after the fact by experts who have worked through the entire body of cases and written down the shortest and strongest generalisations that make sense of them, with no binding force of their own. The Restatement is answerable to the cases; the cases are not answerable to the Restatement.

My supervisor's response was to turn the framing against me. Since both sides are described inside a single legal register, and since a Restatement and a well-drafted code can state exactly the same propositions about what is and is not permitted, he argues that the difference between them is one of editorial policy — whether you want your corpus organised for compression or for authority — and not a difference in what the world contains. On his view my chapter should stop pretending to adjudicate a metaphysical dispute and should instead argue about which register serves which theoretical purpose. He adds that the register is doing quiet damage to the anti-Humeans in any case: statutes presuppose a legislator, an enforcement mechanism, and subjects capable of disobeying, none of which the target possesses, and breaking a law of nature is not even possible in the way breaking a statute is, since a law that is broken turns out to have been misstated, whereas a statute that is broken remains perfectly good law.

Then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the merely-editorial conclusion from those that are decorative. I am especially interested in which features of the legal source the analogy imports without anyone noticing — external authority, an agent standing behind the rule, the possibility of non-compliance, a standpoint outside the system from which the code binds it — and whether the anti-Humean is genuinely saddled with them or whether they are artefacts of the vehicle rather than commitments of the view.

Then give me a verdict on my supervisor: does the merely-editorial conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If it fails, say what the real content of the dispute is that the legal register failed to capture — direction of explanation, modal force, or something else — in terms that do not simply restate the metaphor. And because I may want to retain some version of the comparison in the chapter, tell me whether a better one exists.
