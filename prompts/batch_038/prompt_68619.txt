A genetic counselor's role is defined by a rule of non-directiveness. She lays out the probabilities, names every available option, works patiently to correct a client's confusion about risk, and then declines to say which option is better — including when the client asks point-blank what she would do in their place, a question she is trained to hand back. The norm reaches the paperwork too: reports are supposed to say "increased risk" rather than "abnormal," and to avoid the word "normal" altogether. And yet the menu she works from was itself chosen. Somebody decided which conditions warrant a test and which do not, and the tests exist so that certain futures can be detected and avoided.

What account of the value of a possible life does the counseling rule presuppose: that whether a given life would be worth living is unknowable, that it is knowable but not hers to declare, or that a person who never exists cannot be harmed or benefited at all, so the question never arises? Say which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely sits comfortably with. Then tell me whether any of them survives contact with the design of the test panel, which seems to assume that some lives are worse to start than others at precisely the moment the script forbids saying so. Finally, reconstruct what would change — consent language, report wording, the training itself — if the profession instead held that bringing a flourishing person into existence is a real moral achievement, so that passing up a child who would have had a good life is something one might owe an explanation for.
