I work on the inference platform at a company that ships an internal agent framework, and I have ended up holding a philosophical position that I would like pressure-tested before I put it into a design document. The setup that got me here is ordinary engineering rather than thought-experiment material: when a request fans out, we spin up N worker instances of the same model — identical weights, identical system prompt, identical retrieved context, greedy decoding so there is no sampling noise — and each worker independently decides whether to claim a slot in a shared, rate-limited tool budget or to yield it to the others. If all of them claim, the budget is exhausted and everyone's work is degraded; if all of them yield, nothing gets done; the good outcome is that roughly the right number claim. It is structurally a symmetric game played by processes that are, so far as the decision is concerned, interchangeable. A colleague with a philosophy degree told me this was the situation Douglas Hofstadter had in mind with superrationality — that when the players are all running the same reasoning on the same problem, each one's choice is evidence about all the others', so the collective answer is found by asking what you would choose if everyone chose it, and that in some of Hofstadter's own cases the superrational answer is a randomised strategy rather than a unanimous one. I have since gone considerably further than that, and I want to know what would falsify the stronger version.

My position, stated as precisely as I can manage without the technical vocabulary: (a) what does the work is the symmetry of the decision procedure, not any causal or communicative link between the agents, so no signalling is required; (b) that symmetry obtains wherever agents share code, inputs and deterministic decoding, and my design principle is that we should deliberately engineer for it; (c) where (a) and (b) hold, the superrational recommendation is simply the correct one, and it is not evidential decision theory wearing a disguise, because the correlation here is logical rather than statistical; (d) the approach is therefore not merely descriptive but a usable substitute for explicit coordination machinery — I would rather delete our coordinator service than keep trusting it. I know the standard complaint, that this is magical thinking, and my reply is that the workers are not causing one another to do anything, any more than two people working the same arithmetic problem cause each other to reach the same sum. I also know that program equilibria — agents that cooperate by inspecting one another's source code — are usually offered as the rigorous replacement, and I currently think they are just my principle with extra machinery bolted on.

So what would count against this? Give me the arguments, results and cases that ought to move me, ordered from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed superrationalist of my stripe would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it, and why. I especially want to know which of clauses (a) to (d) is most exposed; whether (c) survives contact with the fact that our workers are not in fact perfectly symmetric once per-request identifiers, shard placement and cache state are included; whether the view has been quietly qualified into something that cannot fail, since any breakdown can be blamed on the symmetry not really having held, and if so whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature; and what my position predicts about our production system that a design based on ordinary causal decision theory would not, together with the cheapest platform change that would tell the two apart. Please answer as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, ordered from most to least damaging, with no introduction and no conclusion.
