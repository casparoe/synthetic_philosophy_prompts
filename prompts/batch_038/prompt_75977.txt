A certain account of political morality holds that some actions a leader is right to perform are nonetheless wrong — wrong all things considered, not merely unfortunate — and that this wrongness leaves a residue the justifying considerations do not absorb. On this view the residue is not simply the overridden duty itself; it generates fresh obligations (to acknowledge, to apologize, sometimes to submit to punishment) that compensating the victim cannot discharge, and it is claimed to be distinctive to the political role rather than one more species of private moral tragedy.

Map this view's exposure. Which arguments, cases, or findings elsewhere in ethics would count against it, and how decisive is each — what should be treated as fatal, what as merely awkward? Three lines interest me most. First, is there any principled criterion separating this residue from an ordinary defeated pro-tanto obligation, or is the view moral conflict renamed? Second, can the residue always be reconstructed as consequentialist regret about the road not taken, and what would show that it cannot? Third, what would establish that the phenomenon is not politics-specific after all? For each, note what the view predicts that its main rivals do not, and say whether a committed defender would concede the point as a real cost or dismiss it. Close with a verdict on whether the thesis, once suitably qualified, risks anything at all — and whether that insulation is itself a defect for a claim of this kind.
