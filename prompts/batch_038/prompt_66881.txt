DRAFT — "What Revealed Preference Doesn't Reveal"

Economics used to be embarassed about utility. "Satisfaction" sounds like a soul, and souls are not measurable, so how is this a science? Samuelson's move in the 30s was: stop asking what people feel and watch what they pick. If you take the apples when both the apples and the oranges are within your budget, you've revealed you prefer the apples. Utility stops being a thing inside someone's head and becomes a way of talking about choices.

The obvious objection is that this is circular. "She prefers X" now just means "she picks X when X is available," so the theory boils down to: people choose what they choose. Totally empty.

I think that objection fails, and it fails for an interesting reason. It's only empty if you get one data point. Once you have the same shopper facing lots of different budgets and prices, consistency becomes a real constraint that data can actually violate — there's a weak axiom, a strong axiom, and I think a generalized one which the textbook I got from the library says is equivalent to "there exists some utility function that fits this data." So the theory is falsifiable in a way that "people do what they want" isn't. And the circularity charge proves too much: by the same logic you could say temperature is just whatever the thermometer reads, so thermodynamics says nothing.

But here's the problem I actually care about, and it isn't circularity. Revealed preference doesn't delete the unobservable, it relocates it. Instead of an invisible feeling called satisfaction, we now have an invisible rule called the choice function. Example: my older brother always orders the second-cheapest item on a menu. Same restaurant, same prices, forever — he never violates an axiom, because he never picks inconsistently. But he isn't ranking food, he's ranking positions on a price list. I'm pretty sure you could catch him if the restaurant changed its prices, since the second-cheapest dish would change and you'd get him choosing A over B one week and B over A the next with both affordable both times. So maybe revealed preference does rule him out. Fine. But notice how much data it took to rule him out. With one menu, it can't distinguish a preference from a habit, a rule, politeness, or whatever the waiter recommended. It can only say "consistent."

So I want to coin a term, tell me if it's stupid: call it menu-drift. Menu-drift is how much slack a theory has to redescribe the very same behavior differently every time the situation changes. A utility theorist who's allowed to refit her utility function to each new budget set has basically infinite menu-drift and is saying nothing at all. Revealed preference with enough varied data has low menu-drift and is saying a lot. The whole fight about whether utility is "real" is really a fight about how much drift you're willing to tolerate.

Conclusion: revealed preference beats the circularity charge but loses somewhere else. It buys empirical content by giving up welfare content. If all you know is choice, you can't tell whether the choice was good for the person — the guy who chooses to fast, the girl who picks what's expected of her, anyone who'd prefer something they can't get. Sen's point about the difference between doing what you want and doing what you can't stand not doing seems to be exactly what revealed preference is structurally unable to represent. So "utility is meaningless" is wrong and "revealed preference is all you need" is also wrong, and the true claim is narrower: it's a good theory of consistency and a bad theory of well-being.
