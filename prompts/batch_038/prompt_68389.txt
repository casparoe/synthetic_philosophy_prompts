I'm a fourth-year undergraduate reading philosophy and neuroscience jointly, and this term's seminar is on the nature of suffering; my assessed essay is due in three weeks and I want to build it around pain asymbolia, so I've been developing a line of argument I'd like you to help me tighten and, where necessary, dismantle. The clinical material is, I think, now reasonably well described: patients with lesions of the insula and adjacent operculum typically report that they do feel the pain and can say where it is and how intense it is, but that it simply doesn't bother them — the aversive quality is gone while the discriminative aspects remain — and the case reports we were given describe some of them as relaxed, even joking, during noxious stimulation. Comparable reports come from patients who have had a cingulotomy for intractable pain. My flatmate, who is on the clinical neuroscience programme, adds that the congenital cases — children born unable to feel pain, who chew through their lips and fracture limbs without flinching — are simply the developmental version of the same dissociation, so whatever the syndrome shows should hold for them too.

Here is the argument I want to make. In asymbolia the pain is present and yet, by the patient's own report, is not bad for her: she is in pain and does not mind. Since the pain exists without its badness, the badness cannot be part of what pain feels like; it must be contributed by something on the subject's side, namely her aversion to the sensation. Three consequences seem to follow, and I want each of them checked. First, hedonism about welfare is false: asymbolia gives us a conscious experience that is not bad for its subject, so welfare cannot consist in the balance of one's felt experience. Second, unpleasantness turns out to be a detachable module fastened onto an otherwise intact sensory state, which suggests both that the standard clinical definition — the one that builds unpleasantness into what pain itself is — has been empirically refuted, and that the current disagreement between imperative accounts of unpleasantness, on which the feeling is a command to stop, and evaluativist ones, on which it represents a bodily condition as bad, is beside the point, since both of them locate the disvalue inside the sensation. Third, there is a clinical upshot: because an asymbolic patient's pains are not bad for her, declining to give her analgesia does not harm her, which ought to matter for how such patients are managed in palliative settings.

My supervisor's only comment on the draft was that she thought there was a slide somewhere in the middle of this, but she wouldn't say where, and I haven't been able to find it myself. So: is there a slide, and at which step? Once it is exposed, what has to be given up — the inference from not-minding to not-bad-for, the assumption that the sensory component retains its full phenomenal character with the affect subtracted, the assimilation of the congenital cases, or the whole move from a dissociation observed in one patient group to a conclusion about where pain's disvalue lives? I would also like to know what the asymbolia material actually supports as between the two theories of unpleasantness, whether the clinical definition survives the syndrome at all, and whether anything here tells us when indifference to a pain is itself a misfortune rather than a relief. Please don't send me off to further literature: I have the seminar bibliography and the case reports in front of me. What I need is the reasoning worked through step by step, with any equivocation identified, named, and shown at the point where it does the damage.
