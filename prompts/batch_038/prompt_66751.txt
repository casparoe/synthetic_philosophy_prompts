I'm a second-year political science major taking a course on distributive justice, and for the final paper I've gotten stuck on something that came up because our city ran a guaranteed-income pilot last year (I did some outreach volunteering for it, so I know the mechanics from the inside). Here's what the program actually looked like: 150 residents picked by lottery from a waitlist of about 3,000, $600 a month for 18 months, loaded onto a prepaid debit card. No work requirement, no drug testing, no restrictions on what you could buy, no reporting back on your spending. But — and this is the bit I keep circling — the card wouldn't do ATM withdrawals, so you could only spend it at a register, never get actual cash in hand. Everyone had to sit through one two-hour "financial capability" workshop in the first couple of months. And the payment was reduced dollar for dollar once your earnings from work passed a threshold, so the $600 was never unconditional in the sense UBI people mean.

In class we've been comparing this to the federal programs and its weird how different the treatment is. Social Security mails checks to retired millionaires with no means test and no questions about what they do with the money. SNAP is earmarked to food, closely watched, and comes with an asset test that punishes saving. The EITC is straight cash, but you only get it if you work — so we pay people for having earnings and pay nothing to people who don't. Net effect: we trust the elderly with cash, we don't trust poor working-age adults with cash, and we reward labor rather than need, even when need is the stated rationale. My roommate's take is that this is all just path dependence and budget politics and there's no philosophy in it at all, but I don't buy that, because every time someone proposes to level it out the objection that comes back is moral, not administrative.

So: what theory of justice, freedom, or responsibility does this patchwork only make sense under? I want you to actually reconstruct the implicit theory rather than just saying "paternalism" and stopping. Is there one coherent view that can license all three of the asymmetries — elderly vs. working-age poor, labor vs. need, cash vs. earmarked benefits — or is it a pile of historical compromises that people then dress up in principle? Which rival views does the pattern quietly rule out (I'm vaguely thinking of sufficientarian stuff, responsibility-sensitive egalitarianism, and the view that people are the best judges of their own good, but tell me if I've got the wrong candidates)? And then flip it around: what does UBI-as-normally-defended presuppose that even my pilot's more radical defenders might reject — like the assumption that cash is the right thing to equalize in the first place, when a person who uses a wheelchair and a person who doesn't turn the same $600 into wildly different amounts of actual freedom? Please be willing to tell me that the "no strings attached" branding is mostly marketing if that's your honest read.
