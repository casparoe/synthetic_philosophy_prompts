I'm hand-labeling training cases for an assistant that's supposed to infer what a user wants from what they actually do, and my "counts / doesn't count" judgments don't seem to hang together:

- Clicks every clickbait headline, then says she hates that she does it. Doesn't count as a preference for more clickbait.
- Orders takeout at 11pm most nights, says she wishes she'd meal-prepped. Counts — she does prefer the takeout, at that moment.
- Buys cigarettes weekly, has said for years she wants to quit. Counts as a preference for cigarettes, and I separately marked "shouldn't be satisfied."
- Turns down a party invite because she's convinced the host dislikes her. Doesn't count — the belief is false and the preference is hostage to it.
- Drives 20 minutes out of her way every morning to dodge the intersection where she got rear-ended. Counts as a genuine preference to avoid it, even though the intersection is perfectly fine.
- Skips the dessert when it's $2 dearer than usual, takes it at the normal price. Counts as a preference for the dessert and for saving $2, and I have no idea how to weigh those against each other.
- Says she wants to save for a house, moves zero dollars a month into savings. Doesn't count as a preference against saving; I wrote "intends to save, fails to."
- Used a feature exactly once during the free trial and never after. Counts as evidence she doesn't want it — except that trial week she was also on vacation, and I still counted it.

The pair I can't reconcile is the first two: clickbait and late-night takeout look like the same shape of thing, a repeated choice she'd disown on reflection, but I only let one of them count as evidence. Same with the invite and the detour — both are actions driven by a false belief about the world, and I threw out only one.

Is there a single principle underneath my labels, precise enough that I could hand it to someone else and get the same verdicts? I keep reaching for something like "count what they'd choose if they knew the facts and weren't rushed or depleted," but that seems to demolish the cigarette and dessert cases, which I want to keep. Give me the best candidate principle or two, what each clause is doing and which case forced it in, what it says about three cases I didn't list, and which of my existing labels has to be given up. And: am I just contradicting myself by treating irrational behavior as evidence about the reward sometimes and as noise other times, or is there a line I can actually draw there?
