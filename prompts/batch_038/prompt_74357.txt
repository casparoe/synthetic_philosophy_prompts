So, background: I've been a strength and conditioning coach for about twelve years, mostly with distance runners and one rugby club. My whole professional vocabulary is training load — acute:chronic workload ratios, monotony and strain indices, deload weeks, tapers, the difference between functional overreaching and actual overtraining, HRV and readiness monitoring. I can put a spreadsheet together that tells you whether an athlete is about to break, and I trust it more than I trust anyone's feelings about being tired.

Two months ago my younger brother, who works at an ad agency in Osaka, had a heart attack at 34. Ninety-hour weeks, no days off during pitches. Since then I've been trying to explain to my family what happened to him using the language I already use for athletes — chronic load with no off-season, monotony through the roof, no taper, catastrophic failure — and at the same time I've been reading around the actual debate about work and leisure: Weber on the Protestant ethic and the calling, the French 35-hour week and the RTT days it created, the karoshi literature and the overtime thresholds Japan uses to decide whether a stroke legally counts as caused by work, plus the older leisure-is-good-in-itself stuff (Russell's four-hour day, Keynes's fifteen-hour week).

Here's my problem, and it's the thing I want you to dig into. I have a growing suspicion that my vocabulary is not neutral. In load management, rest is never an end in itself — it's just the part of the training block that produces adaptation. There's no such thing as a session that's good for nothing; my own sport has a term for that, junk miles, and it's said with contempt. That sounds an awful lot like ascetic Protestantism's contempt for idleness wearing a lab coat. And the coach-athlete relation, where someone else owns and optimizes your recovery, is just the managerial relation with better data.

So: take those positions — Weber's account of why work became an end in itself, the case for legally shortening the week, the karoshi claim that overwork kills, and the claim that leisure is worth having for its own sake — and restate them as faithfully as you can in load-management terms, the ones I listed above. Don't cheat by quietly importing my framework's assumptions and calling it a translation, and don't reassure me that balance matters — I want to know where the translation actually fails.
