So I work at a smallish animal advocacy nonprofit, and in about six weeks we're running a two-day residential workshop that half our board thinks is nuts: one side of the room is us (campaigners, two academics, a couple of people who do that wild-animal-suffering research, an ex-processing-line worker who now negotiates cage-free commitments with food companies), and the other side is livestock people — a dairy, a cow-calf operation, a contract broiler grower who finishes something like 200,000 birds a year, and a feedlot nutritionist. My older brother is coming as one of the growers. He's a large part of why I ended up in this line of work and we have not had one honest conversation about it in fifteen years, so I volunteered to give the opening remarks on the first night, mostly so that whatever gets said first in that room gets said by somebody who loves one of the people in it. Five or six minutes, standing, everyone holding a plate and a glass of wine, which is the worst possible conditions for saying anything real.

The reason I'm asking for help is that I don't want to give the obvious speech and I don't trust myself not to. The obvious one is "look how much we already agree, animals matter, let's find common ground," and it's a lie, because what the weekend is actually about is a disagreement that hasn't been resolved and maybe can't be, and if I paper over it Friday night we'll all spend Saturday being exquisitely polite at each other. The disagreement is mostly an accounting one. A beef steer is a single animal with a fairly long life, but it eats something like six to ten kilos of grain for every kilo of meat it puts on, and every hectare of that grain gets planted, sprayed and harvested by machinery that kills an enormous number of mice, voles, rabbits and ground-nesting birds. A broiler is a shorter and I think worse life, but you get something on the order of a couple hundred of them for the same tonnage of meat, and their feed counts too. So beef looks worse per calorie on the field-animal side and better on the animals-deliberately-killed side, and chicken looks like the mirror image, and nobody in either half of that room agrees about which of those numbers is the one that morally matters. Then there's the wild side of the ledger: some of my colleagues think the animals outside all the fences are most of the problem and that farmed animals are nearly a rounding error, and others think that framing is a very convenient way of letting ourselves off the hook for the ones we breed and kill ourselves. And my brother has an argument I can't dismiss, which is also an old one in our own literature — I can't even remember who ran it first — that a steer on permanent pasture is turning grass on marginal ground nobody could grow corn on into food while killing almost no mice doing it, so a person eating that beef may be responsible for fewer dead animals than a person eating lentils and tofu out of plowed fields. He is not wrong about the ground. I genuinely don't know whether he's wrong about the conclusion.

What I need the speech to do: it can't resolve any of that or pretend the arithmetic comes out clean, because everyone in the room would smell it in ten seconds. It can't use the crop-deaths point as a gotcha aimed at my own side — "so your kale kills mice too, we're all even" is the fastest way to make half the room set down their wine and stop talking for two days — but it also can't leave that point unmade, because if I never say it my brother will hear me pretending his best argument doesn't exist, and he'll be right. It has to admit that we don't know how to weigh a mouse against a chicken, or whether an insect has anything it's like to be, without letting that admission turn into a shrug, since every single person there has organized their actual life around a bet about the answer and "it's all so uncertain" would insult all of them at once. It should say something true about why these two halves are in the same building that isn't the phrase "we're all trying our best." One genuinely funny moment, at nobody's expense, ideally about food or about meetings, that both sides laugh at. And it has to land on something the room can actually do on Saturday morning. Write the speech itself, six or seven hundred words, in the voice of a thirty-four-year-old who is nervous and knows it. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose only, exactly as I would read it out loud, like a letter. If you want to tell me something about delivery or where to pause, put it in a plain sentence or two at the very end.
