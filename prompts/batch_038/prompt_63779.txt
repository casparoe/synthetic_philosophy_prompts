I am a second-year undergraduate reading philosophy, and the final three weeks of my personal identity module are devoted to Buddhist accounts of the self. Our department's student philosophy society runs peer-led evening workshops, and I have put my name down to lead one on anattā. I would be grateful for your help in designing it properly, since I want it to be something the participants go through rather than something I explain to them.

The parameters are as follows. Fourteen participants: mostly first- and second-year humanities students, of whom perhaps four will have encountered Parfit and two will have some background in Buddhist thought from a religions module, but nobody will have read the primary texts. Seventy-five minutes, one room, tables for small groups of three or four. Materials must be cheap and low-prep: index cards, pens, a whiteboard and a timer. No handout longer than one side of A4, and no devices.

What I want them to arrive at, in this order, is threefold. First, a felt inability to locate, among everything they can enumerate about a person, the thing to which the enumeration belongs. Second, the realisation that the argument they have just been having about whether the person survives some change is not settleable by any further evidence available in the room — that they have been negotiating the use of a term rather than reporting a fact about the world. Third, and hardest, the sense that the vivid, insistent feeling of being a subject is itself one further item in the inventory, not the proprietor of it.

Please observe these constraints on the design. None of the stock apparatus may appear: no teletransporters, no brain transplants or hemispheric division, no body swaps, no Prince-and-cobbler, no ship of Theseus. The activity should draw on the participants' own autobiographical material, because that is what gives the third point its bite, but it must include a clean opt-out that lets anyone use invented material without announcing themselves, and the mechanics must work equally well either way. Participants must commit privately and in writing at some early stage, so that a later stage can reveal those commitments and set them against one another. And the discovery must be produced by the rules: I should be able to run the entire session saying nothing beyond what the instructions require.

Deliverables: complete rules with a phase-by-phase timeline and minute allocations; the exact wording of every card prompt or board instruction I will read out; an identification of the moment at which you expect the third point to land and what precisely triggers it; a debrief that maps named moments in the activity onto the reductionist claim, onto the intuition that something must be over and above the bundle, and onto the Buddhist claim that a person is designated upon a causally continuous stream of moments rather than discovered within it — and that connects what has just happened in the room to the exchange between Nāgasena and King Milinda in which the chariot turns out to be a mere designation in dependence on its assembled parts; a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, including the risk that handling their own memories makes people more convinced of a substantial self rather than less, together with how to recover; and an honest statement of which feature of the doctrine the activity cannot reproduce and what I would have to supply verbally in its place. My own suspicion is that a card exercise cannot touch the point that the doctrine is meant to be liberating rather than merely true; tell me whether you agree, and what to say.

Please write your answer in British English.
