stuck on the last sentence of the instinct chapter in Origin of Species, the one right at the end:

"Finally, it may not be a logical deduction, but to my imagination it is far more satisfactory to look at such instincts as the young cuckoo ejecting its foster-brothers, ants making slaves, the larvae of ichneumonidae feeding within the live bodies of caterpillars, not as specially endowed or created instincts, but as small consequences of one general law leading to the advancement of all organic beings--namely, multiply, vary, let the strongest live and the weakest die."

a few things I cant pin down.

(1) What exactly is being conceded in "it may not be a logical deduction"? For my notes I've been splitting concessive hedges in Victorian science prose into two kinds — "real hedges," where the author genuinely thinks the inference is shaky, and "courtly hedges," which are politeness toward the reader while the author thinks the inference is fine. Which is this? There's a third option I keep circling: that he isn't hedging the inference at all but the *standard* — i.e. the created-instinct account never gets far enough to offer a deduction either, so grading both of them on deductive grounds is already a category error. Does the grammar of the sentence permit that reading, or am I over-reading "may not"?

(2) What work is "satisfactory to my imagination" doing as a reason? I've started labelling this kind of move an aesthetic warrant, where the writer leans on how a picture feels rather than on new data. If that's what this is, is it a bad move *here*? My instinct is no, because the satisfaction looks checkable — one law generating a pile of unrelated oddities versus one bespoke endowment per oddity — but I'd like the strongest version of the worry: at what point does "it feels more unified" shade into just being fond of your own theory?

(3) The choice of examples. He had the honeybee comb available, the pretty case he spends real space on earlier. Instead the finale is the cuckoo heaving its foster-brothers out of the nest, slave-raiding ants, and wasp grubs eating a live caterpillar from the inside. All three are the revolting ones. What is the repulsiveness buying him? Is it aimed at a benevolent-designer intuition specifically, or at something else — like the suspicion that these behaviours look far too well-contrived to have been stumbled into?

(4) "multiply, vary, let the strongest live and the weakest die" — is that the mechanism, or a compressed restatement of the outcome? If it's the latter he's just said the survivors survive, which would cancel whatever he seemed to gain in (1) and (2).

and one loose thread: does the sentence commit him anywhere to the idea that these insects are getting extremely tricky things right with no idea why — competence with zero comprehension in the loop? If that thought is in there, which words is it hiding in? I suspect "small consequences of one general law" is the load-bearing bit, but I can't tell whether that's the argument or just me hearing what I want to hear.

sorry for the sprawl — (1) and (3) are the ones I actually care about.
