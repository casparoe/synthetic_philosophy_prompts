So I sit on the jury for a regional fiber-arts prize — five of us, $8,000, one winner, and the bylaws say our decision is final with no appeal, which turns out to matter a lot for what I'm about to ask. The prospectus says entries must be "wholly the handwork of the maker," with a clause permitting "limited machine assistance in the preparation of materials." This year's shortlist includes a piece where the artist designed the pattern and dyed all the yarn herself, had the ground fabric produced on an industrial knitting machine she programmed and operated, and then spent roughly 200 hours hand-embroidering and hand-finishing over it. Two jurors say disqualify: the ground of the piece is machine-made, full stop, and embroidery on top doesn't launder it. One says obviously eligible: nothing in the piece was designed, selected, or finished by anybody but her. I'm the swing vote and I've been asked to circulate something before we meet Thursday night.

What I want is a memo, call it 700–900 words, that I could actually hand the other four without embarrassment. It should come down on this piece — with reasons a dissenting juror could engage with rather than just re-assert — say what we tell the artist and what we announce publicly either way, and propose a working procedure for future years, because we get two or three of these a season and right now we settle them by whoever argues hardest. For context on how bad it is: last year we disqualified a quilt whose top fabric was commercially printed but whose piecing and quilting were entirely by hand, and nobody on the jury could tell me whether that's consistent with anything.

Here's the part I'm most curious about, so please don't just humor me. Years ago I took a philosophy of language course and the bit that stuck was the argument over vague predicates — roughly, the view that "handwork" has a sharp boundary we simply can't locate, versus the view that there are many acceptable ways of drawing the line, a borderline case is neither in nor out, and so you should only assert what comes out the same on every acceptable way of drawing it. My hunch, which I can't defend, is that the second view tells a deliberative body something the first one doesn't: if the case is genuinely indeterminate, the only ruling you can stand behind is the one that survives every reasonable precisification. My friend thinks that's a cop-out dressed up as rigor, and that a jury which has to hand over a check should make its best guess, own it, and move on — the prize is a decision, not a truth value. Tell me wether either of us is onto something, and specifically whether the metaphysics changes what Thursday's memo says or is just decoration on it. I'd also like a straight answer on how confident you actually are in the recommendation, which step in it you think is weakest, and don't be polite about the framing — if you think this isn't really a vagueness problem at all, or that a jury memo is the wrong vehicle for the question I'm asking, say that plainly instead of writing the document I requested.
