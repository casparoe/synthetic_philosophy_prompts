The answer came in at about 700 words and reads as follows.

"Douglass's mature view is that the Constitution, rightly read, is an antislavery instrument, and that the fight against slavery should therefore be carried on inside the political system rather than outside it. He did not always think this. In the 1840s he accepted the Garrisonian position that the document was a compact with the slaveholding interest, a position that carried the further conclusion that conscientious northerners should refuse to vote or hold office and should support dissolving the Union. What changed his mind was reading Taney's opinion in the Dred Scott case, which convinced him that the proslavery reading of the Constitution was not a neutral legal finding but a political act, and that if the Court could read slavery into the text so could its opponents read freedom into it. From the late 1850s he argued the antislavery reading publicly, most fully in a speech he gave in Glasgow in 1860. His method is textual and, he insists, strict: where a law is capable of being used to oppress, nothing should be conceded to it, and it must be shown that the offending thing is actually written into the bond. On that basis he makes several points. First, the words slave and slavery appear nowhere in the document; the framers resorted to evasions and circumlocutions, and Douglass takes this as evidence that they dared not enact a property right in human beings in the founding instrument, so that whatever the practice of the government may have been, the text does not create such a right. Second, he refuses to read the Constitution by the private intentions of its drafters or by what the government subsequently did under it, holding instead that the declared objects in the preamble govern: an instrument cannot be construed so as to defeat the ends it announces, so any clause that might look like a shelter for slavery has to be read against it if that is possible. Third, he treats the three-fifths rule as a concession extracted by the slaveholding states and paired with the clause on the importation of persons, so that the South bought extra representation in exchange for letting the trade be closed after a fixed date; on his account this is the clearest proslavery feature in the document, and he argues that even here the Constitution stops short of recognizing property in man. Fourth, and most importantly for his practical politics, he holds that the Constitution puts powerful weapons in the hands of abolitionist statesmen: due process of law, trial by jury, the writ of habeas corpus, and the guarantee of a republican form of government to every state, any one of which, wielded by people with the right moral sentiment behind them, could be turned against slavery. Here he agrees with Lincoln that Congress cannot simply legislate slavery out of existence inside a state that has established it by its own municipal law, but he parts company with Lincoln over what follows from that: where Lincoln treats the federal government as largely a bystander to the institution except in the territories, Douglass treats it as obliged to withdraw every form of support and to use the war power and the guarantee clause to intervene whenever slavery puts the republic in danger. His position is therefore neither disunionism nor containment: it is the claim that the Constitution is already on the side of freedom and only needs to be enforced."
