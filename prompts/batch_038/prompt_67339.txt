I have worked for six years as a program evaluator at a county health agency, and for the last two I have also been assembling our unit's evaluation handbook, partly because we are up for state reauthorization next spring and the reporting template insists on a single headline figure per program. Over that time I have kept running into the same configuration, in enough different settings that I now think it is a pattern rather than a run of bad luck, and I cannot find a name for it. Here it is. An intervention genuinely improves outcomes for every subgroup of the people it reaches, and yet the pooled statistic for the system as a whole moves the wrong way — not because anything was measured badly and not because the subgroup numbers conceal something, but because the intervention's own success changes who makes up the population being measured. Our wound-care clinic is the clearest case: before it opened, a group of patients with severe lower-limb complications was cycling through the emergency department and out again, and they were effectively invisible in the county's chronic-care reporting. After it opened, patients in every severity band and every age group did better than comparable patients had done before, but the county's reported complication rate rose sharply over the same two years, because people who had previously never appeared in the denominator now appeared in it. A friend of mine who evaluates school programs describes something structurally identical: a tutoring initiative raised reading scores in every grade and every prior-achievement quartile, while the district average fell, because students who would formerly have been absent or had dropped out before test day stayed enrolled and took the exam.

I have worked through the neighbouring concepts and none of them is quite it. This is not Simpson's paradox, as far as I can tell: there, an association holding within the groups runs the other way once the groups are pooled, whereas in my cases the direction never flips at all — every subgroup agrees with every other, and the pooled figure is answering a different question about a different population. Nor is it a bias that conditioning could repair. There is no lurking variable to hold fixed, because the subgroup rates are all accurate and the pooled rate is also an accurate description of the pooled population; the trouble is that the two are not descriptions of the same thing. It is not survivorship bias, and it is not the Will Rogers phenomenon in cancer statistics, where more sensitive detection shifts borderline patients between categories and lifts the reported average of both groups with no real improvement; mine runs in the opposite direction, with real improvement producing an apparent deterioration. Goodhart's law is close in mood but wrong in mechanism, since nobody is gaming the metric — the metric is being entirely honest about a shifting denominator. "Fallacy of composition" is too general to carry any weight in a report. And I am not even certain my two examples are the same phenomenon: in the first, people who were never counted enter the count, while in the second, everyone was always in the frame and what changes is how much weight each part carries.

So I have three requests, and the label is the least of them. First: does this configuration have an established name, in statistics, epidemiology, program evaluation, or anywhere else? If it does, tell me which name, and tell me how the literature distinguishes it from Simpson's paradox and from reclassification effects of the Will Rogers kind — and if the honest answer is that it has several partial names with no one concept underneath them, say so and lay out the pieces. Second: if there is no established term, coin one, and define it precisely enough that I could defend it in a document going to a state auditor — including whether your definition covers both of my examples or only one of them, and what you would call the other. Third, and most important, give me the edges of the thing. I want to know the boundary case at which it stops being a bookkeeping illusion and starts being evidence that the program failed, since a worsening pooled rate caused by enrolling worse-off people can shade, without any clear line, into a worsening pooled rate caused by the program's crowding out better outcomes. I also want the conditions under which the correct verdict is that the aggregate is the right number after all: if the county's population really has become sicker and more visible, perhaps the county's rate really should be higher, and an evaluator who keeps reaching for the disaggregated figure is the one evading the question. What I need from you is the carving — what this phenomenon is, what it is not, where it stops — and then practical guidance on how to decide which of the two numbers deserves to be called the result.
