Since the spring I have served as the repatriation coordinator at a state university museum, and the work has forced on me a question I do not know how to answer. My job is to prepare determinations of cultural affiliation: for each group of ancestral human remains and associated objects in our holdings, the museum must decide whether the people who made and deposited them share a group identity with a present-day tribe, publish that determination, and then transfer the material to the claimant. A community that does not come forward before the transfer loses the right to claim those items permanently.

The evidence I am permitted to weigh is unusually mixed. Provenience and acquisition history count, and so do typological sequences and mortuary patterns. Physical analysis counts too, but only where the consulting tribes consent, because the current regulations require free, prior, and informed consent before anyone may study, examine, or even acquire new information about the material. At the same time the regulations require deference to tribal traditional knowledge, and some of the categories I must apply are defined by that knowledge: an object counts as funerary because a tribe's traditional knowledge connects it to a death rite or ceremony, not because of anything observable on the object itself.

The difficulty is this. In our own site reports we refuse to read belief off form; an archaeologist who described a deposit as ceremonial on the grounds that no mundane explanation suggested itself would be criticized, and where function is genuinely unknown we are expected to say so. Yet in the affiliation files I sign determinations asserting that these objects were made, used, and understood by a group whose identity persists in a living community — a claim about the continuity of meaning across centuries, sometimes supported largely by the account of the claimants themselves, an account we have already relied on in deciding what the objects are.

I would like an analysis of what this practice presupposes, not a defense or an indictment of repatriation. What account of a group's persistence through time must the affiliation standard assume — numerical identity of the group, continuity of practice, continuity of narrative, genealogical descent, self-ascription, or some weighted combination — and which of these would the practice exclude if it were taken seriously? What epistemology allows testimony about the deep past to do work that stratigraphy cannot, and is the circularity I described vicious, or benign in the way that all interpretation of a culture by its inheritors is? Please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely happens to fit, and mark the places where it is underdetermined, where incompatible theories would license the very same determination. Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change — in the form the determination takes, in the consent gate on research, in the finality of a transfer — if the assumed account of group persistence were replaced by one on which identity comes in degrees and can be held by several communities at once.
