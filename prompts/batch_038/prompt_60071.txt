Write a short story of 1,500 to 2,200 words.

**The anchor.** Build the story around the color phi phenomenon, and let the setup emerge from action rather than exposition: two small lights of different colors, separated by a few degrees of visual angle, flashed in quick succession at an interval short enough that they are seen as one moving thing. Observers report a single dot traveling from the first position to the second and changing color somewhere along the way — and in the strongest version of the effect, the reported change falls before the second light ever came on. Somebody saw a color change at a moment when, on the screen, there was not yet any second color to change into.

**The philosophical core.** Two accounts of what happens are available. On the first, the revision is made before the material ever reaches experience: no one, not even for a moment, sees the unedited version; the record is corrected in advance and then presented as though it had always been that way. On the second, the observer really does see the first color continue along its path, and only afterward is the memory rewritten so that the recollection contains a change mid-trajectory. The crucial point, which your story must treat as a fact rather than an oversight: these are not rival physical theories. They agree about every neural event, about where and when the erroneous content enters the causal chain, and about how the whole thing must feel from the inside — a subject cannot tell an experience that was misbegotten from one that was misremembered a heartbeat later. They disagree only about whether the place where the error enters is upstream or downstream of consciousness, which is to say about whether there is any such place at all.

**The invention.** Someone builds an apparatus that appears to tell the two apart. State plainly, in the world of the story, what it reads and when it reads it. It should sound like a piece of lab equipment, with a limitation, a calibration procedure, and a way to fail — not like magic. If it works, the story must show, through what happens rather than through argument, that it works only because something was added that the two accounts did not already share, and what adding it costs. If it fails, the failure must be informative rather than merely technical.

**Characters and scenes.** Tell it from the point of view of someone who runs or maintains the apparatus, not the person who theorizes about it. Include one scene in which a subject's answer to an ordinary question — which color did you see, and when did it change — carries a consequence that makes sense under one account and not the other. Keep that consequence small and human: a form filled out, a statement signed, a promise, a diagnosis, an argument at a kitchen table. Also include a third position, held by a character who insists the question is malformed: that there is no finish line in the brain past which processing becomes experience, and that the other two are quarreling over where an imaginary screening took place. Give this character an argument the other two cannot answer. Do not defeat them by making them wrong about a fact, and do not make them insufferable.

**Prohibitions.** No character may explain the distinction to another character who does not already understand it; the reader should be able to reconstruct the difference from what happens. Do not use the words *Orwellian* or *Stalinesque* before the final page, and use neither more than once. Do not name any real philosopher, any real researcher, or any real paper — the story should stand without them. No author's note, afterword, or closing paragraph that steps outside the fiction to summarize the philosophy. Avoid the stock beats: no journalist being given a tour, no one in a white coat pronouncing on what it means to be human, no artificial intelligence, no dream-within-a-dream reveal.

**The ending.** You decide whether the apparatus works. Either way, the cost of the result must be specific to the answer: the story should come out differently for one particular person depending on which way it goes, and the reader should be able to say why. No shrugs, and no ambiguity that is merely decorative.
