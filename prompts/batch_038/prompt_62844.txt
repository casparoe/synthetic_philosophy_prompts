I built an argument I thought was bulletproof and my partner ran it last tournament, and then some guy in a philosophy discord walked me through what it commits me to and I can't find the exit. Here's the thing, numbered:

(1) If your preferences over gambles are rational they can be represented by expected utility. If they can't, you'll pay to switch back and forth between options, which isn't a different value system, it's just incoherence.

(2) A society's moral ranking of uncertain prospects is supposed to be a rational ranking, so it obeys the same axioms as in (1).

(3) Pareto: if a prospect is better for somebody and worse for nobody, society ranks it higher; and if everyone is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent between them too.

(4) Harsanyi's theorem is a proof and not an opinion: (1)–(3) force social value to be a weighted sum of the individual utilities. There's no wiggling out of it, it's linear algebra.

(5) Taking people equally seriously means equal weights, and equal weights plus a sum is exactly classical utilitarianism.

(6) So egalitarians and prioritarians aren't offering a rival moral theory, they're failing arithmetic. Caring about the shape of the distribution on top of the total is the same species of error as preferring a sure $5 to a coin flip between $0 and $20. Inconsistency, not disagreement about values.

(7) Nothing in the theorem says the "individuals" have to be all of humanity, so it works for any group at all. Which means a couple choosing a city, a school board, a jury — any decision procedure that isn't sum-maximizing, including consensus rules and voting and rights that block the option with the biggest total — is formally irrational.

(8) Also nothing in it says the individuals have to be human or have to be the same set of individuals from one prospect to the next. So animals with positive welfare get counted, and since the social value is a sum, adding anyone with net-positive welfare is an improvement. So (1)–(3) by themselves force the repugnant conclusion: a gigantic population of lives barely worth living outranks a small flourishing one whenever the total is bigger.

(9) And because (1)–(3) are supposed to be necessary truths about rationality, someone who denies (8) isn't just wrong, they're irrational, in the same sense as someone who prefers a dominated bet.
