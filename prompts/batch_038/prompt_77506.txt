I've spent a fair number of years in the calibration-and-forecasting corner of the rationalist blogosphere, and I've run into a pattern I can't name. My estimate of attendance at a conference was 200, built as 1,000 invitees times a 20 percent acceptance rate. The truth turned out to be 250, built as 500 invitees times a 50 percent rate. When I learned the real invitee count and updated to 100, one component of my model became exactly right and the output moved three times further from the truth, because two errors had been pointing in opposite directions and cancelling. The same shape appears without any arithmetic: I leave five minutes early because my watch runs five minutes fast, and if I fix the watch but keep the habit I'm late.

I'm aware of the familiar truthlikeness puzzle — that a precisely false claim can outrank a true but empty one. This seems different, and more troubling: the ordering by closeness to the truth isn't monotone under the very operation we treat as epistemic progress. Is there an established concept for it, in philosophy of science or in the modelling literature? A bare label won't help me. I want the nearest candidates and how each differs from what I've described, whether the effect is an artefact of scoring outputs instead of components, and, if nothing fits, a proposed term with a definition plus boundary conditions — under what circumstances does correcting one error still guarantee an improvement?
