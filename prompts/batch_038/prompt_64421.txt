I am finishing a master's seminar on self-knowledge, and as the assessed component each of us gives a twenty-minute paper followed by a half hour of open questions. My paper argues that the empirical evidence usually cited for the unreliability of introspection does not bear on the philosophical thesis of first-person authority, because it measures a different capacity. Roughly, I distinguish (i) registration — the self-ascription of one's own occurrent, conceptually available attitudes and feelings, which I take to be constitutive rather than detective, in that the avowal is not a report on an independently settled fact but partly settles what the state is, so that authority here is a matter of standing rather than of accuracy — from (ii) self-interpretation, the fallible, theory-driven business of explaining why one acted as one did, reconstructing what one felt earlier, and predicting how one will perform. I claim that choice-blindness paradigms, confabulation-of-reasons studies, and confidence-rating work all measure (ii) and none of them touches (i). More aggressively, I claim that a metacognitive confidence rating is not an introspective report at all but a wager on one's own performance, so that the sensitivity indices built from confidence — the ratio-style measures the metacognition literature treats as bias-free estimates of metacognitive efficiency — quantify how well calibrated a predictive system is, not how faithfully a receptive one reports. I concede that we are poor judges of the fine-grained structure of experience, and I maintain that authority was never a thesis about fine grain.

The trouble is the composition of the room. Alongside the usual philosophers of mind, there are three visiting graduate students from a metacognition lab who work on confidence ratings and will hear my claims as an attack on the intelligibility of their own measurements; two committed higher-order theorists of consciousness, who will want to know how a constitutive account can accommodate a higher-order state that is supposed to render its target conscious without itself being introspected, and whether I have merely traded an epistemic regress for a voluntarist one; one senior figure in the seminar who has argued for decades, on both phenomenological and experimental grounds, that we are systematically bad judges of our own current experience, and who treats the move from a question about what I believe to a question about whether it is true as a description of how beliefs are formed rather than of how they are known; and two quieter students from the phenomenology tradition who will be irritated by my use of a word like "registration," as though there were some inner taking-note. Our seminar also has a convention that replies must be answerable from the paper itself, so I cannot defer to technical literature I have not read.

Prepare me for that question period. Give me roughly the eight objections I should be braced for from these particular people, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument as I have stated it, and phrased in the register the objector would actually use — the lab people will frame theirs in terms of what is measurable and what pattern of results would count as a dissociation, the higher-order theorists in terms of representation and regress, the skeptic in terms of cases where I am simply wrong about what I am now experiencing. For each, supply a reply I could deliver in under two minutes without technical machinery, an indication of how much of the objection I should concede at the outset rather than fight, and a candid note on where the reply is softest. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour, and identify the question I most hope nobody raises: the one that presses the suspicion that I have drawn the registration/self-interpretation boundary in exactly the place that makes it impossible for any conceivable result to count against registration, thereby leaving it a capacity with no observable consequences.
