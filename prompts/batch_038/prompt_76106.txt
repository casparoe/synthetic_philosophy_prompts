I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for next month's issue my editor has cleared space for a satirical sidebar. I would like you to write it.

The conceit: a mid-sized American city has stood up an Office of Doxastic Accountability, founded on the premise that the accuracy-first program in formal epistemology states outright — that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good, that degrees of belief are the right objects of evaluation, and that accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule. Write a Swiftian civic-reform op-ed in the voice of the office's founding director, announcing the program to residents and answering the first round of complaints. About 750 words, with a headline and an invented byline, followed by a boxed FAQ sheet of roughly 200 words that the office mails to households, where the technical detail gets precise. Around 950 words total.

The director is sincere, competent, and proud of his work. He is not a straw man and not an idiot, and his proposal must follow the program's logic honestly; the reader should recoil from where the logic lands, not from any caricature of it.

The jokes have to come from getting the framework right. Please build the piece out of the following beats, each of which is a real feature of the accuracy-first program rather than an invention:

- The choice of scoring rule is treated as a routine administrative decision, on the order of picking a tax form, and residents litigate it. Someone works out that the logarithmic rule imposes an unbounded penalty on a confident falsehood while the quadratic rule caps the damage, and that the difference decides who gets fined.
- The office's benchmark is the vindicated credence function — one at every truth and zero at every falsehood — and the consequences are played straight. The city's top scorer turns out to be a man with a handful of beliefs held at 0.99, and the office newsletter urges residents toward confident extremes, since calibrated humility costs points.
- Incoherent credences are fined, but never on the grounds that they violate the probability axioms; the office's explanation is that they are accuracy-dominated, so there is nothing to argue about. A complainant points out that he cannot identify the coherent credence function that dominates his own, and the office replies, with total good faith, that this is not its problem.
- The office must fix a partition before it can score anybody. A resident discovers that the same beliefs earn a better score under one carving of the possibilities than another, and petitions to have his doxastic zoning changed.
- Residents are required to file their updating plans in advance, and are penalized for revising a belief for any reason other than incoming evidence, because the plan to conditionalize is what looks best from where you stand before the evidence arrives — even when it looks foolish afterward. One household discovers that only propositions with a determinate truth value can be scored at all, and is told to refile.
- Someone asks what score the office would assign its own founding claim that accuracy is the only thing that matters epistemically, and the framework cannot grade its own foundation without already assuming it.
- The reductio, which closes the op-ed: since only an already-true credence function is undominated, the director proposes the natural endpoint. The city should stop paying people to reason, identify whoever scored best last year, and require everyone to adopt that person's credences, on the grounds that reasoning is merely an expensive intermediate step toward a target you could have copied. He presents this as the program's triumph.

House rules. Deadpan throughout and no winking: not one sentence in which the director or the FAQ concedes that any of this is misguided, and no objection ever stated as an objection. Everything quoted inside the piece must be invented — no real texts, no real philosophers, no titles, dates, or bibliographies, and nothing that reads like a citation. Invent the city, the office, the director, the complainants, and the newsletter. The prose must be readable by someone who has never heard of a Brier score, so terms get explained through the in-world material — a form, a fine notice, a helpline script — rather than through parenthetical glosses, while staying exact enough that a formal epistemologist finds nothing sloppy to complain about.
