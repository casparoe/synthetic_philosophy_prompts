I run a little reading group in my living room — six of us, mixed bag (two programmers, a lawyer, a nurse, me, ex-physics). Last week we did the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery chapter from Metamagical Themas and it went completely off the rails for about two hours. One guy insists the whole thing is magical thinking dressed up as logic: nothing you do can reach across to the other nineteen people, so the symmetry assumption is doing no work at all. The lawyer said it's just "what would happen if everybody did that" in a trenchcoat, i.e. a moral rule pretending to be a decision rule. One of the programmers said it's obviously Newcomb-flavored and belongs with the people who take the box and not the boxes, and that anyone who disagrees is refuted by the fact that they're not rich. Nobody could agree on whether those three positions are actually different positions or the same position with different accents. So for the next session I want a self-sorting instrument rather than another argument.

Here's the deliverable: a decision tree, eight to ten questions, each one a concrete situation the reader can answer without knowing any of the labels, plus a key that maps answer patterns onto named views. I want at least these as leaves: superrationality as Hofstadter actually means it (not the pop version), evidential decision theory, causal decision theory, the Kantian "maxim"-style reading where the question is whether your rule could be a universal law, a Parfit-flavored leaf for someone who thinks you decide on behalf of everyone relevantly like you, one leaf marked unnamed for a view you think is coherent but that basically nobody defends, and one leaf for the person who thinks the whole dilemma rests on a confusion about counterfactuals involving logical facts. Where two leaves can't actually be separated by any question you can ask, say so explicitly instead of faking a branch. And where a combination of answers is logically available but nobody occupies it, mark it and say why — incoherent, just deeply unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Three things I want the tree to handle, because these are exactly where our group got stuck. First, at least three of the questions should be about finding things out rather than about the original setup: does it matter if you learn that eleven of the other nineteen have already sent their telegram? does it matter if you learn the other nineteen were picked at random and have never read anything by Hofstadter? does it matter if you learn the trillionaire is running the same thought experiment on twenty perfect copies of you? Each leaf should get a verdict on each of those, since that's where I suspect half the views actually come apart. Second, the tree has to say for every leaf whether it gives the same recommendation in the two games — the Platonia version where one and only one telegram wins the billion, versus the actual Luring Lottery where the million was divided by the total number of entries and one entry drawn at random — because we discovered those two come apart and nobody could say why or which view predicts the difference. Third, where do the people who really did mail in a googolplex land? Apparently so many absurd entries came in that the prize collapsed to a fraction of a cent and the magazine couldn't even work out who had won, so I'd like the key to tell a reader which leaf predicts that behaviour, and whether that leaf is thereby vindicated or refuted. Please don't write me an essay on decision theory — I want the questions, the branching, and the key. And if any of your questions turns out to be a label wearing a disguise ("do you think your choice causes theirs?"), rewrite it as something a person could answer honestly without guessing which camp it sorts them into.
