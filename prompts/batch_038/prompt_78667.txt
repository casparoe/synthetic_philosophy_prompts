The pitch is that it still works because the argument gets broken down into little pieces the judge *can* check. My friend Marco, who's way better at this than me, turned that pitch into an argument, and I'm pretty sure it proves way too much, but when I told him that he said that's a vibe and not a refutation. Which is fair?? Here's his argument as best I can write it down:

(1) A debate is a zero-sum game: one debater wins exactly when the judge sides with it.
(2) The core result is that under optimal play from both sides, the judge ends up siding with the true answer, because the honest debater can stay consistent indefinitely while the liar eventually has to defend something checkable that's false.
(3) What makes this work is that the judge never has to verify the big claim directly. Each side's claims get decomposed until they bottom out in atomic statements the judge can check on their own.
(4) So the judge needs zero expertise, zero ability to verify the real answer, and zero insight into the system's reasoning. The only ability the judge needs is the ability to check the atomic statements at the bottom.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(4) says the judge has to be human. So swap the human for a much weaker AI judge, one that can only check even simpler atomic statements.
(6) As long as you push the decomposition one level deeper so the new atoms fit inside the weaker judge's checking ability, (2) still applies, and the weak judge gets the true answer too.
(7) And you can keep doing this. For any level of judge weakness there's some decomposition fine enough to suit it.
(8) So a very dumb overseer can reliably extract true answers to very hard questions from a very smart system. Taken to the limit, a superintelligence could be overseen by something roughly as smart as a rock, provided the debate is decomposed enough.
(9) Therefore scalable oversight was never really a problem. Alignment reduces to building capable debaters, and we don't have to worry about the judge at all.
