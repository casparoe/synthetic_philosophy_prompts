I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting concerns an institution that brings people into existence in order to harvest something from them. Before I attempt it myself I want to watch a finished work negotiate the difficulty, and Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* is the precedent that matters. Please read it through the nonidentity problem. The clones would not have existed at all but for the donation programme, so it is obscure what person-affecting complaint any of them can make about having been created for organs; their only alternative was never to have been. And yet the novel is engineered to make the reader feel that something has been done to Kathy and to Tommy.

I should like the reading to do three things. First, separate the choices the novel actually stages: the bringing-into-existence of the clones, which is a different-number choice, and then the decision to harvest them rather than raise them as ordinary children, together with Hailsham's attempt to soften conditions for people who would have existed regardless, which are same-number choices of the kind Parfit's argument does not reach. Say for each whether the problem bites. Second, tell me whether the novel's force depends on a person-affecting intuition that the nonidentity problem renders unavailable, or whether it locates the wrong elsewhere — in status, in recognition, in being treated as a mere means — and, if the latter, whether that escape is earned by the text or simply announced. Third, take up the scene of Madame weeping in the doorway at Kathy dancing with her pillow to the borrowed tape whose title the novel takes, along with Tommy's later speculation about what she thought she was witnessing, and Miss Emily's closing account of what the gallery was for; say what each contributes to, or subtracts from, that question. I should also like your view on whether Kathy's equanimity in the final pages amounts to a philosophical claim of its own: that a life can be wronged without being made worse than the alternative of never having been lived.

Where the novel resists this reading, say so plainly; I would rather learn what Ishiguro declines to settle than be handed a tidy moral. Since I am drafting rather than teaching, end by saying which of his moves remain available to me and which depend on features of his setting that I cannot borrow.
