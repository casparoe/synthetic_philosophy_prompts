I help run a small reading group attached to our university's centre for the history of economic thought, and this term we have been working through a methodological question that sounds easier than it is: whether a long-run prediction can ever be vindicated after the fact, and what would have to be true for the phrase "right but early" to mean something more than a diplomatic excuse for a theory that failed. Last week the question stopped being abstract. After a session on Malthus, two of the regulars — an economic historian who works on pre-industrial wages, and a philosopher of science whose day job concerns the reliability of long-horizon climate modelling — carried the argument over to the wine reception, and I wrote down what I could. Her first, his second, and so on:

(1) She opened by saying Malthus is the standard-bearer for the right-but-early category rather than a casualty of it. For essentially the whole of recorded history up to about 1800 his mechanism operated as described: a technological improvement raised living standards, incomes above subsistence raised population, and population growth pulled incomes back down. On several long-run reconstructions, English real wages at the end of the eighteenth century were not materially above their late-medieval level. A theory confirmed by several thousand years of history and then merely interrupted is not a refuted theory.

(2) He replied that "interrupted" is carrying all the weight. The central empirical claim — that population growth would press against the food supply and immiserate the labouring classes — failed for two centuries, beginning in the country where he wrote and among the population he was writing about. He also committed to magnitudes and horizons: a doubling period of twenty-five years for unchecked population, and the assertion that the pressure of population on subsistence was already producing misery rather than something to be observed in the remote future. A mechanism that can be permanently switched off by an event the theory neither anticipated nor can rule out has no predictive content left to be vindicated.

(3) She said that misreads the architecture of the model. Technology is not a variable the theory predicts; it enters as an exogenous input. What Malthus offers is a conditional: given a rate of increase in the means of subsistence that is at best arithmetic, population left unchecked will outstrip it. Industrial technology and the subsequent rise in agricultural yields changed the input, not the law, and one does not say a comparative-statics result has been falsified because the curve shifted.

(4) He said this is exactly where the drainage of content becomes visible, and that Malthus himself demonstrated it. In the first edition the preventive check was largely vice; by the greatly expanded edition of 1803 he had added moral restraint — delayed marriage coupled with celibacy — as a separate and voluntary check. From then on the principle could not fail: if population pressed on subsistence, misery and vice were checking it; if it did not, moral restraint was. He asked whether she would accept the same treatment for a climate model that, whenever its projections missed, was revised by adding an unmodelled offsetting process.

(5) She answered that the relevant test is not whether a programme was patched but whether the patches and extensions yielded novel predictions, and that here the neo-Malthusians score. The unified growth models of the past couple of decades — the ones in which a Malthusian regime with a fixed factor and a modern regime built on reproducible capital coexist, and the transition between them is generated endogenously through a quantity–quality trade-off in children — retain his core feedback mechanism while producing something he never claimed: that the escape from the trap would arrive together with collapsing fertility rather than with mass misery. On his own philosophy-of-science criteria, that is a progressive problem shift, not a degenerating one.

(6) He said she was crediting Malthus with the achievements of a successor theory that happened to keep one heuristic device, which is like crediting Democritus with the internal structure of the atom. Then he pressed what he called the asymmetry: had the trap persisted to the present, she would count that as confirmation of the mechanism; because it ended, she counts it as a change in an exogenous variable. A hypothesis that comes out safe on both branches of the outcome is not confirmed by either.

Could you go through this turn by turn and tell me, for each of the six, which of them has the better of it? I would like a rating per turn — clearly mistaken, partly right but overstated, or essentially right — with a short justification that engages the substance rather than the rhetoric, followed by a separate judgement on which single turn is the strongest for each side and why. Please also identify where the two are simply talking past one another: I suspect a good deal of the argument turns on which artefact counts as the theory — the ratios and dated prophecies of the 1798 edition, the wage–population mechanism, the revised check taxonomy of 1803, or the modern growth models that descend from it — and I would like you to say explicitly whether any of the six verdicts would change once that is pinned down. Flag any factual claim in the exchange that is wrong or misleading as stated, whether about English real wages before industrialisation, about what Malthus asserted and in which edition, or about what the unified growth models actually assume, and correct it; you need not identify the papers she had in mind, and please work from my description rather than reconstructing a reference list. Finally, draw the general lesson I was after: what conditions, if any, must a long-run prediction satisfy for a later event to vindicate it non-trivially, and is right-but-early a coherent category or a way of saying that a theory was never riskily testable to begin with?

One discipline I would ask you to keep throughout: separate what is genuinely settled from what is contested among people who work on this material, and do not let a contested position masquerade as consensus. Some elements are not seriously in dispute — the existence of prolonged near-subsistence wage stagnation before industrialisation, the fact that Malthus altered his check taxonomy between editions, the broad structure of the unified growth models. Others are live: how much evidential weight the Malthusian episode carries for debates about ad hoc rescue, whether the end of the trap is best described as a change in an exogenous parameter or as the obsolescence of the mechanism itself, and whether the demographic transition should be counted as a Malthusian prediction at all. Where you come down on one of those, say that it is contested, indicate roughly how the disagreement lines up among economic historians and philosophers of science, and mark your own confidence accordingly. If you are unsure about a specific historical or textual detail — what a given edition says, for instance — say so plainly rather than filling the gap with something confident-sounding.
