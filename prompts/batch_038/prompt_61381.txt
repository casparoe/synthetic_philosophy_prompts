I'm running a small grad seminar on democratic boundaries this term — nine students, mostly political theory plus two from the law school — and week six is where it reliably falls apart. They show up having seen the usual menu: enfranchise everyone whose interests a decision significantly affects; enfranchise everyone who will be subject to the deciding body's binding, coercively enforceable rules; and then the third thing I've been pushing in my own work, which for want of a better name I call the joint-agency or co-authorship view — the demos is made up of those who are already participants in one ongoing scheme of collectively binding themselves, so standing to decide comes from being inside that shared project of self-rule rather than from being affected or coerced by any particular decision. It's half-baked, I know; that's partly why I want the case, since I'm hoping a concrete scenario will show me where it breaks.

So: build me one constructed scenario, contemporary and institutional — a special-purpose authority, a hospital or water district, a port or transit body, a housing co-op with real decision-making power, whatever you like, but no states of nature and no sci-fi — in which those three principles yield three pairwise non-nested extensions. Each principle has to enfranchise at least one party the other two exclude. And I need the case sharp, because last year I tried a river-basin version and it died in about four minutes: one student said affectedness comes in degrees so there's no fact of the matter about who's in, and another said the all-subjected set just collapses into the all-affected set since anyone you can fine is ipso facto affected. Stipulate the facts so neither move is available — the affectedness relations should be definite rather than degreed in the respects that matter, and there should be at least one party who is unambiguously subject to the body's coercive rules but whose interests the decision in question genuinely does not touch. No villains: every exclusion has to be one a reasonable person could regret, not one where you'd just say good riddance. Keep the scenario itself to roughly 250 words, with named parties and roles I can drop onto a slide.

After the scenario, don't just assert that it meets spec — show me. First a table, one row per principle, with these columns: `principle` | `enfranchised parties (by name)` | `excluded parties (by name)` | `the specific fact in the case that generates this extension` | `strongest move a defender of this principle has for refusing the result`. Then a short JSON object auditing the design, using exactly these fields: `load_bearing_feature` (the single feature doing the most work, one sentence), `if_removed` (what happens to the three extensions if I delete that feature), `hardest_principle_to_defend_here` (which of the three comes out worst in this case), `decision_swap_survives` (true/false: does the non-nesting survive if the body is deciding something else of the same kind — say, a fee change instead of a service change), `decision_swap_explanation` (one or two sentences), `fourth_principle_worth_adding` (name and describe one candidate principle my menu is missing that would cut the case differently, or null if you think there isn't one), and `confidence` (low/medium/high that the case genuinely does what I asked, with a one-line reason). Casual prose is fine everywhere else, but please keep the JSON valid and the field names exactly as written — I'm going to paste it into a script that checks whether my students' own attempts satisfy the same constraints.
