I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on the science of consciousness this term, and our final assignment is unusual: rather than defending a position, each group must draft the document that an adversarial collaboration between two entrenched camps would produce, as though the two sides had spent a year trying to isolate their real disagreement. My group drew the dispute between the sparse and the rich camps over the relation between phenomenal consciousness and access. I would like help drafting ours to a high standard. Please write in American English.

The two parties, as I have characterized them for the assignment:

**Party A — a global-workspace theorist.** A holds that what makes a mental state conscious is that its content becomes globally available: broadcast to the systems responsible for reporting, reasoning, deliberation, and the guidance of action. A does not treat reportability as a criterion of consciousness but as its normal downstream consequence, and A is a deflationist about how much is conscious at any given moment. On A's view, what is conscious is roughly what is, or was very recently, available for such use, and the apparent richness of the visual field is largely an impression generated by how easily its contents can be sampled, not a census of simultaneously conscious content.

**Party B — a defender of the access/phenomenal distinction and of phenomenal overflow.** B holds that phenomenal consciousness is not exhausted by global availability; that at any moment perceptual phenomenology carries more content than can be accessed, reported, or recruited into reasoning; and that a theory of the workspace is at best a theory of access, silent on the character of experience. B insists that the overflow is not a mere disposition to become conscious but a way of being conscious already.

Both accept the standard background: the partial-report superiority effect with briefly presented arrays; the fragility of whatever survives once the array is gone; change blindness and inattentional blindness; and the existence of no-report paradigms in which a stimulus can be discriminated without the subject producing a report. Neither takes report to be constitutive of consciousness.

What I need is the joint statement itself, not an essay about it, drafted so that neither party would feel strawmanned. Please include the following.

1. **A numbered section of claims both can sign.** Include at least: what the partial-report paradigm does and does not establish on any reading of it; the status of report as evidence rather than criterion; what being available and being global commit A to, stated in terms B accepts as a fair description of A's view; and the phenomenological datum B takes as the starting point, stated in terms A accepts as a fair description of the datum even though A denies its import. Mark explicitly which of these numbered items are substantive agreements and which are only agreements about how to describe each other's position — my instructor will be looking for that distinction.

2. **A disagreements table**, in Markdown, with columns for: the topic; A's preferred formulation of the disagreement; B's preferred formulation; a neutral formulation meant to be acceptable to both; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is genuine or at least partly verbal. Each side's formulation should be one that side would actually sign.

3. **The cruxes**: the sub-questions such that a definitive answer would move at least one party. I want at least four, and for each I want it stated which party would move and in which direction. At least one crux should concern whether the overflowing content, if there is any, is determinate — a particular orientation, a particular letter — or merely determinable, a generic sense that there is more to see which supports rapid uptake without carrying specific phenomenology. At least one should concern whether the phrase "the contents of consciousness at time t" picks out the same kind of thing for A and for B, or whether the two are quantifying over different domains.

4. **For each crux, one concrete experimental design or analysis** that both parties agree in advance would bear on it, together with each party's predicted outcome recorded before the fact. One of these should be the design my supervisor proposed, which both parties are stipulated to have accepted as admissible: a dual-task version of the partial-report paradigm in which a briefly presented visual array is followed immediately by a demanding central load that occupies the workspace — say, a continuous auditory n-back — and in which no report of the array is solicited at any point during the trial; at the end of each block, subjects give graded confidence and a forced choice about a cued location. The question is whether above-chance cued-item information can be demonstrated while the workspace is demonstrably occupied elsewhere, and what that would license. State A's prediction and B's prediction, and then state what each says the result would mean — including the possibility that both predict the same pattern of data and diverge only in how to read it. Where that happens, say so plainly and explain why the crux is then not decidable by that experiment.

5. **A closing section** in which each party states, in its own voice, what result or argument it would accept as counting against its own view; followed by a short list of things both agree would not move the debate at all, with reasons — for instance, any evidence that trades on report as though report were constitutive, or any appeal to how rich experience seems that does not distinguish seeming-rich from being-rich.

6. **A jointly authored paragraph on which parts of the dispute you take to be merely verbal.** I want this to be discriminating rather than diplomatic. If your conclusion is that the richness dispute is substantive while the dispute over what counts as a content of consciousness at a time is largely a matter of stipulation, say exactly that and explain where the dividing line runs.

Two constraints on the drafting. Please do not adjudicate: the deliverable is a text both parties could sign, and I lose marks if it reads as a verdict for one side. And please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to particular papers — our instructor has asked for documents that stand on their own, and named studies are things I cannot check. Where you need to refer to an experimental result, describe it rather than cite it.
