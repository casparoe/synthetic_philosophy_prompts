QUESTION: Moore writes that "By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects." Explain how Moore argues for this claim and what work the principle of organic unities does in the argument. Then say whether his view can accommodate friendship being valuable for the friend's own sake, or whether it turns friendship into a species of aesthetic appreciation.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Moore's starting point is that good is a simple, indefinable property, so you can't settle what's valuable by defining good as pleasure or as what people desire — that's the naturalistic fallacy, and he thinks philosophers have committed it just as often with beauty, e.g. by defining the beautiful as whatever produces certain feelings in us. Since good can't be defined, the only way to find out what's good in itself is to take candidates and test them. The test is the method of absolute isolation: imagine the thing existing completely alone, with nothing else in the universe, and ask whether its existence would still be worth having. Whatever survives that test is intrinsically valuable, whatever else it may be useful for.

Applying the test, pleasure fails, or at least isn't the only good, and material objects fail pretty comprehensively. A beautiful landscape or painting that nobody ever sees has no value at all; beauty only becomes valuable when it gets taken up into someone's consciousness. What does survive are complex states of consciousness, above all the enjoyment of beautiful things and personal affection.

The reason these are so valuable is the principle of organic unities: the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts. Aesthetic appreciation is a whole with two elements, a cognition of the beautiful object and an appropriate emotion toward it. Separately each is worth almost nothing — knowledge of a beautiful thing with no feeling for it is nearly valueless, and so is a warm emotion directed at nothing in particular. Put together they make something worth vastly more than the two ingredients add up to. The same structure applies to affection, except that the object cognised is another person's mental qualities. Moore adds that a true belief in the reality of the object raises the value of the whole considerably, which is why appreciation of a real landscape beats an equally vivid appreciation of an imagined one, and why reciprocal affection between two worthy people is better than unrequited affection for someone who doesn't exist.

So the argument runs: (1) intrinsic value can only be identified by isolation; (2) what survives isolation are consciousnesses of appropriate objects; (3) organic unities explain why those wholes are worth so much more than their components. And since the whole is always worth more than the sum of its parts, piling up more of these goods is straightforwardly better, which is how Moore gets from an account of value to a recommendation about producing as much appreciation and as many friendships as possible.

On the last part: I think Moore's view does reduce friendship to aesthetic appreciation. To love someone, on his account, is to contemplate their mental qualities with the appropriate emotion, which is just to find them beautiful. So you love them for their qualities rather than for themselves, and if the qualities changed or turned out to be ugly the love would lose its value. Friendship is valuable "for the friend's own sake" only in a thin sense: the friend is the object of a valuable state of consciousness, but the value itself sits on the consciousness side. Moore therefore can't really capture what people ordinarily mean by caring about a friend for their own sake.
