Please write a satirical internal policy document: the complete written standard on permissible assertion issued by the compliance function of a large life assurance and annuity provider (invent the firm's name), adopted after the board resolved that no employee may assert anything they do not know. The piece should run to roughly 850 words and should be formatted as a real corporate manual — a short preamble stating the policy's authority and scope, numbered sections and subsections, defined terms rendered in small capitals or bold at first use, one boxed sidebar, and a closing appendix of sanctions. The voice must be deadpan, bureaucratic, and wholly sincere: nothing in the prose should wink, and the comedy should come entirely from a firm having worked out, in operational detail, what it means to treat a norm of assertion as a rule of employee conduct.

The document should meet the following requirements.

1. Establish that the choice of norm was contested. Include a brief section recording that rival standards were considered and rejected — one keyed to truth alone, one to sincere belief, one to justification or reasonable belief, and one on which the standard shifts with what is at stake for the audience — each dismissed in a sentence or two of corporate boilerplate that nevertheless betrays why its partisans found it attractive. Do not name any philosopher, paper, or school; a specialist should be able to identify each view from the description alone.

2. Confront the fact that the firm's commercial product is the confident statement of probabilities. Devote a section to the classification question — whether a pricing estimate, a mortality projection, or a statement about the likelihood of a borrower's default counts as an assertion at all, and if not, what species of speech act it has been reclassified as — and let the manual be visibly proud of the solution it has found.

3. Build the operational machinery out of the norm's familiar pathologies, treated not as objections but as compliance problems to be administered. At minimum, I want: a clause governing statements about low-probability outcomes, under which no officer may tell a policyholder or a colleague what is overwhelmingly likely to happen, together with the workaround the firm has had to invent; a rule covering documents containing many claims at least one of which the author expects to be false, and the attestation that must accompany them; a procedure for the case in which an employee's claim was true, well supported, and arrived at by an accident the audit trail subsequently reveals; and a provision on testimony, specifying what an employee may assert on the strength of a colleague's report, an external expert's opinion, or the output of a model the firm cannot itself inspect.

4. Make the manual self-applying. Whatever standard it imposes on employees, the document itself must satisfy it, and you should show the strain this produces — in the preamble's claims about the policy's effects, in the sanctions appendix, in the signature block — rather than resolving it by quietly exempting the compliance function.

5. Include the boxed sidebar as a formal grievance from an employee disciplined under the standard, with the compliance function's reply beneath it. The grievance should be one the employee can win on the facts and still lose under the policy.

6. Land, without stating, the objection that regulating assertion merely displaces communication. Once the firm polices what may be said, everything migrates into what may be suggested; the manual should therefore document as a feature — indeed as evidence of successful adoption — the elaborate system of rhetorical questions, leading framings, and strategic omissions that has grown up in place of outright assertion, so that a reader feels the policy has changed nothing about what the firm's audiences end up believing.

Constraints: no real philosophers, journals, or quotations from the published literature, and no explanatory coda, footnote, or glossary decoding the philosophy — the piece ends with the appendix. Any quoted language should come from invented internal documents. It should read as a workplace farce to someone who has never encountered this debate and be considerably funnier to someone who has.
