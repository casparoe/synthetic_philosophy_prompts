I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and much of my reporting over the past few years has concerned the infrastructure of how states remember losing. The examples keep rhyming. Australia and New Zealand mark their most widely observed day of national commemoration — a public holiday, with dawn services — on the anniversary of the day their troops went ashore at Gallipoli in 1915, rather than on the withdrawal, which military historians generally regard as the one operation of that campaign the Allies executed well. One of Britain's most durable pieces of wartime self-description concerns the evacuation of an army from a French beach: an event that preserved forces and conceded the continent. Serbia's national and religious holiday for a 1389 battle commemorates what the historiography describes as an inconclusive engagement — both commanders died, and most of both armies were destroyed — and the legend attached to it consolidated in stages, acquiring its modern political weight in the nineteenth century rather than the fourteenth. Israel made a desert fortress where Jewish rebels died during a Roman siege into a national symbol, complete for a period with military swearing-in ceremonies on the summit, even as archaeologists and sociologists have argued over who the defenders were and how much of the ancient account the ground supports. And the Confederate memory industry took shape not during Reconstruction but decades later, when the losing side's descendants built the monuments and wrote the catechisms for schoolchildren.

I want a reconstruction of what this practice of canonising defeat presupposes — not a history of it, and not a survey of the cases. In particular:

1. What theory of political value makes it intelligible to place a loss above a win? Is the practice committed to the view that the worth of collective action is independent of its consequences, or to something narrower — that honour attaches to willingness rather than achievement, or that a polity is constituted by what it has suffered rather than by what it has accomplished?

2. Distinguish strictly what the observances presuppose from what merely sits comfortably with them. Several readings look available at once: an anti-consequentialist honour ethic, a Durkheimian cohesion mechanism, a legitimation device for the state that sponsors the ceremony, a purely expressivist practice that asserts nothing about value at all. Tell me which of these the practice could not survive losing, and what feature of the ceremonies would discriminate between the rest, given that I can only observe what is said, built, and omitted rather than what anyone believes.

3. Reconcile, if it can be reconciled, the following: these same states evaluate their own programmes by outcomes, publish cost-effectiveness analyses, and hold their officers to a professional duty not to squander lives — a commander who sent troops into a plainly hopeless engagement for the sake of the gesture would end his career. Yet the ritual honours precisely the decision to keep fighting after the strategic question was settled, and in at least one case honours conduct no modern military code would permit. Does the practice secretly hold that judgement applies to commanders and honour to the commanded, or is there a single view that licenses both?

4. The observances are also selective in a way that strikes me as doing real theoretical work. Defeats are canonised only where the loss cannot be laid at the commemorating polity's own door; there are no national days for defeats that state inflicted, or for disasters traceable to its incompetence. Does this mean the practice does not actually presuppose the independence of worth from outcomes, but something about innocence and victimhood instead?

5. Here is a hypothesis of my own, which you should attack rather than adopt. Because the cause was lost, nothing it would have produced can ever be inspected. A triumph can be soured by the world it made; a defeat cannot, since it made no world. Canonised losses therefore enjoy an immunity that victory memorials structurally lack, and the impossibility of the cause is what supplies the immunity. If that is right, is the practice doing something epistemic — protecting a claim from refutation — rather than something ethical? And does the same apply to the counterfactual that the ceremonies uniformly omit, namely what the polity would have looked like had the cause succeeded?

Finally: state what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a frankly consequentialist one. What would a memorial calendar organised around outcomes actually commemorate — successful withdrawals, negotiated settlements, wars that did not happen — and which of the existing observances would have to be struck from it? Flag, as you go, which of my examples are load-bearing for your reconstruction and which are merely illustrative, since I would rather not defend a claim that rests on one case. I am not asking for sources or a bibliography, and I would prefer no invented ones; argument and analysis are what I need.
