Galileo's reasoning about two bodies joined by a cord is the case on which John Norton's reconstructionism and James Robert Brown's appeal to a priori insight into universals most squarely collide: Norton maintains that the episode is merely a picturesque argument whose premises are drawn from experience, whereas Brown maintains that no such reconstruction is available, since a committed Aristotelian need not accept that tying bodies together produces the retardation the reductio requires. Argue that the disagreement, so framed, mislocates the issue. In particular, identify the assumption both sides quietly share — that joining two falling bodies alters nothing about the composition of their motion beyond the addition of their weights — and argue that this assumption is neither an empirical premise an Aristotelian would grant nor an object of a priori insight, but a claim about how dynamical effects compose, one that could be probed directly by varying the character of the connection (taut cord, slack cord, rigid rod, fused mass). Then conclude, and defend, the thesis that the epistemic yield of thought experiments of this kind lies not in delivering new information about nature but in isolating a specific composition principle and showing that a given theory cannot coherently endorse both that principle and its own account of the phenomenon.
