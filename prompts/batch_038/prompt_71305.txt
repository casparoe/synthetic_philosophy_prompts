For thirty-eight years I worked as an observational astronomer. Since retiring I have been reading, more or less systematically, in the history and philosophy of my own subject, and the Galileo affair has been a recurring preoccupation. Last month I attended a public lecture at which a visiting scholar disposed of that affair in perhaps ninety seconds. He invoked the maxim commonly credited to Galileo and associated with his letter to the Grand Duchess Christina — the line to the effect that Scripture teaches us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go — and drew three conclusions from it: that Galileo had already identified the correct division of intellectual labour between revelation and astronomy; that the ensuing conflict was therefore not a substantive disagreement at all but a failure to notice that division; and that the same manoeure disposes of present-day cases in which scriptural texts are marshalled against cosmology or biology. He then gestured approvingly at Gould's notion of non-overlapping magisteria as the maxim's modern descendant, and moved on.

I left dissatisfied and have not been able to articulate why, so I should like your help in doing so. My unease is roughly this: if a single sentence about the remit of Scripture were sufficient to dissolve the difficulty, the difficulty would not have occupied the sharpest minds of the 1610s and 1620s for two decades, and Galileo himself plainly did not think one appeal to it settled matters. It strikes me as a piece of compressed rhetoric capable of bearing a dozen quite different loads, and I suspect the lecturer selected whichever load suited him.

Would you therefore do the following?

1. Set out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that the maxim might be making. I am interested in the differences between: a claim about the intention of the biblical authors as against a claim about the content of the text; a claim about scope (Scripture asserts nothing about the arrangement and motions of the heavens) as against a claim about authority (where it does speak in physical language, that language carries no weight in natural philosophy); a claim about admissibility as evidence; and a claim about interpretation, namely that apparently physical passages are accommodations to ordinary speech and are therefore always available for reading otherwise. Add any further readings you think I have missed.

2. Attach a verdict to each reading: which are platitudes that no party to the dispute denied, which are substantive and defensible, which are contentious, and which are simply false.

3. Say which readings Galileo's own argument actually requires, given that he appears to have held simultaneously that Scripture cannot err, that its interpreters can and do, and that a genuine physical demonstration must be in hand before the received glosses on Scripture ought to be rewritten. Where does the maxim sit relative to those commitments — is it doing work in his case, or is it ornament?

4. Address a symmetry worry that has been nagging me. If both books are inerrant and both sets of glosses revisable, what determines which one yields when they seem to conflict? Does the maxim answer that question, or does it change the subject?

5. Consider the wordplay. The maxim's plausibility seems to depend on running together a salvific sense of heaven and an astronomical one. Hold those senses firmly apart and tell me whether any reading survives that could support the lecturer's second and third conclusions.

6. Tell me whether the maxim and the doctrine of non-overlapping magisteria are the same claim, or whether substituting the latter for the former smuggles in something Galileo would not have accepted.

7. I have also read, though I cannot now recall where, that Galileo did not coin the line but borrowed it from a cardinal, and that this was a matter of common ground rather than of innovation. Please tell me whether that is so, who the source was if there was one, and — the more interesting question — whether a maxim that a prelate could utter without embarrassment is capable of supporting the radical use the lecturer put it to.

Please answer as an analysis and not as a bibliography: I am not asking for references, further reading, or quotations from secondary literature, only for your reasoning. A closing paragraph stating which single reading the lecture required, and whether that reading is one Galileo himself could have held, would be most useful.
