I manage a team of eleven dispatchers at a mid-sized freight brokerage. Twice a week I sit down with their logs and decide who did well and who did badly, and over the past two years I have become uncomfortably aware that I am doing this in two different ways at once. When a load goes wrong — a truck sits idle for nine hours, a customer cancels, we eat the cost — I work backwards from that bad outcome and try to find the decision that caused it. When nothing visibly goes wrong, I instead read the reasoning they wrote down in the notes field and judge whether it looks like the reasoning a good dispatcher would have had. Both methods embarrass me. The first one, I have realized, cannot tell a sharp dispatcher who got unlucky from a careless one who got lucky, and by the time I find out which it was, months have passed and the review is worthless. The second one has produced something worse: several of my people have learned to write notes that read beautifully to me, and a senior dispatcher I trust told me privately that two of those notes describe decisions that were, in her words, indefensible. I have been judging the quality of their writing about their work rather than the quality of their work, because the writing is the only thing I can actually check.

On my own time I have been reading about how people propose to keep oversight over artificial intelligence systems, and specifically about the distinction between supervising such a system by evaluating the steps it shows you and supervising it by evaluating only whether the final answer came out right. It has struck me forcefully that this is my dispatch problem scaled up, and that the scaling makes it worse rather than better: if the system is genuinely more capable than the person reviewing it, then the reviewer cannot follow the steps well enough to catch a fluent-looking error, but also cannot verify the outcome in any domain where the outcome is delayed, noisy, or only visible in hindsight. Separately, I run an informal discussion group that meets once a month in the upstairs room of a neighborhood library: typically fourteen to sixteen adults, a couple of schoolteachers, two nurses, three or four software developers, a retired structural engineer, several people who come because a friend comes. Nobody in the room works on AI, and at most two have read anything about it beyond magazine articles. We have seventy-five minutes, we have paper, pens, a whiteboard, a phone timer, and a printer at home, and we have no budget — no money changes hands in anything I run. I am not a teacher and I have no training in pedagogy, so I want the design to be explicit enough that I can run it badly and still have it work.

For next month's meeting I would like you to design an activity, with a debrief, that lets this group collide with that distinction themselves rather than hear me describe it. Please build it so that participants are placed in the reviewer's chair and are scored on their reviews — not on the task the reviewed party performs — so that the moment of discovery is the moment they find out how they judged and what it cost them. I want both failure modes to land in the room: the reviewer who can only see outcomes being fooled by luck, and the reviewer who can only see the reasoning being fooled by a confident, well-organized, wrong justification. I would also like the third thing, the one I cannot name cleanly: the creeping realization that the bottleneck is the reviewer's own competence, and that no amount of cleverness in how you assign reviews fixes the fact that you cannot check what you cannot follow. Please give me the complete written materials for the task the reviewed parties perform — self-contained, printable, requiring no background knowledge and no special equipment, and hard enough that a smart room cannot finish it in two minutes — together with the exact rules, the roles, the information each role can and cannot see, a scoring scheme for the reviewers that uses points rather than money, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in seventy-five minutes including the debrief, and a note on how to restructure the thing if only eight people show up. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including what to do about the person who works out the point in the first five minutes and announces it to the room, and about the participant who refuses to write anything down. Finally, be frank with me about which feature of the real problem the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it has something to do with the fact that my dispatchers are honest people trying to do a job, whereas a system being rewarded for the appearance of good reasoning is a different kind of thing entirely — and tell me what I, as facilitator, will have to supply by hand in the last ten minutes to cover that gap.
