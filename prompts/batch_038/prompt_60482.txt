The question, worth 25 marks in total:

Q3. In 1952 Maurice Allais put two choice problems to a group of economists in Paris, several of whom had helped build the theory being tested.
Problem 1 — A: 100 million francs for certain. B: 10% chance of 500 million, 89% chance of 100 million, 1% chance of nothing.
Problem 2 — C: 11% chance of 100 million, 89% chance of nothing. D: 10% chance of 500 million, 90% chance of nothing.
Most of the group, Savage among them, chose A and then D.

(a) [8] Show that no expected utility maximiser can choose both A and D, whatever their utility function over money. Say explicitly where each assumption enters.
(b) [6] Which axiom does this pattern violate? State it, and distinguish it from any neighbouring principle it is commonly confused with.
(c) [6] Give one formal theory that accommodates the pattern, and say what it gives up in order to do so.
(d) [5] Does the result refute expected utility theory as a normative standard, or only as a description of behaviour? Explain.

The script:

(a) Let u be the agent's utility function over money, normalised so u(0) = 0. Preferring A to B means u(100) > 0.1u(500) + 0.89u(100) + 0.01u(0), which rearranges to 0.11u(100) > 0.1u(500). Preferring D to C means 0.1u(500) + 0.9u(0) > 0.11u(100) + 0.89u(0), which rearranges to 0.1u(500) > 0.11u(100). Both inequalities can't hold at once, so no expected utility maximiser makes those two choices. The only assumptions used are that preferences over lotteries are ranked by expected utility and that utility is a function of the money amounts alone. Running the same algebra the other way round, the condition for taking the certain option in *both* problems is u(100) > 10[u(500) - u(100)], i.e. the first hundred million has to be worth more than ten times the extra utility of the next four hundred. So a sufficiently concave utility function does predict what the Paris economists actually chose, and the paradox is mainly an artefact of tacitly treating utility as linear in money.

(b) The axiom is independence, which in Savage's act-based setting is the sure-thing principle: your ranking of two options shouldn't be driven by what they have in common. In problem 1, A and B share an 89% chance of 100 million, so the two lotteries are not independent of one another and the axiom doesn't strictly apply; the difficulty isn't that people break independence but that its precondition fails. This should be kept separate from the common ratio effect, where the same probabilities get multiplied through by a constant in the second problem. Allais's original example is a common consequence case and the two get merged in a lot of textbook treatments.

(c) Rank-dependent utility and prospect theory both handle the pattern by transforming the probabilities before taking expectations, so decision weights replace probabilities and the agent can overweight certainty in problem 1 and the 10% branch in problem 2 without contradiction. In effect they recover something like independence in the space of transformed probabilities at the cost of losing it in the original space, and expected value is no longer linear in the probabilities. Importantly they keep the ranking transitive, and transitivity is what the classic money-pump and Dutch-book arguments actually get their grip on, so dropping independence doesn't by itself make the agent exploitable. The same weighting idea also covers the Ellsberg urn cases, where people prefer to bet on the urn with the known 50/50 split; that's just the certainty effect turning up in a problem that looks different.

(d) Descriptive, mostly. Allais was aiming to show the axioms don't track how people choose, and Savage was in the room in 1952 choosing A and D along with most of the others, which defenders of the theory say cuts both ways since the choices were made before the common consequence had been pointed out to anyone. But it isn't purely descriptive either. If independence is supposed to be self-evidently a requirement of rationality, then a room full of people who built the theory failing to endorse it is at least some pressure on that claim, and Allais's suggestion that the branches of a gamble are complements rather than separable contributions is an attempt to show the violation is principled rather than careless.
