I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, which in practice means a steady diet of books and long features about how non-human forces — pathogens, droughts, cold snaps, ash clouds — have shaped human affairs. After several years of reading and line-editing this material, I have become preoccupied with a configuration for which I cannot find a name. I would like to know whether one exists in historiography, the philosophy of language, or the philosophy of science, and if it does not, how the thing ought to be defined.

Here is the pattern. In the framing sentences of such work, a natural event occupies the subject position of a transitive causal verb whose object is a human society, and people appear, if they appear at all, in a subordinate clause. One reads sentences of the following shape — these are my own reconstructions of a genre, not quotations from any book — "the drought that settled over the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE emptied its cities"; "a volcano in Indonesia rewrote the nineteenth century"; "a bacterium carried off the steppe remade the economy of Europe." The argument surrounding these sentences is almost always far more modest. The same work will later say, carefully and correctly, that reduced rainfall appears to have depressed harvests in certain regions for a few decades, that the affected cities were already strained for other reasons, and that the responses of rulers, merchants, and farmers varied enormously. The strong claim exists only in the sentence that hands out the grammatical subject.

The second feature is an asymmetry, and it is what persuaded me this is not merely overwriting. When the outcome is dramatic, the natural event takes the subject slot. When the outcome is undramatic — a society that absorbed a comparable shock — the same sort of event is demoted: it appears in a subordinate clause, in the passive, or as scene-setting ("there was also a stretch of unusual cold"), while humans take the subject slot ("farmers diversified," "the administration stockpiled," "the guilds renegotiated"). The distribution of grammatical agency tracks the outcome rather than the magnitude of the event. Sometimes the switch happens inside a single paragraph: famine and plague are the subject of one sentence, and two sentences later peasants, facing better wages, are the subject of the next, with nothing marking that the account of who is doing what to whom has changed.

The third feature is that the pattern is immune to the obvious criticism. When a reviewer charges the author with determinism, the reply is that no determinist thesis was asserted: this is a narrative, the phrasing is vivid, and of course human beings chose things. The disavowal is sincere — I have made it myself at a desk, in the margin of someone else's manuscript. Yet the framing returns in the next title, in the jacket copy, in the subtitle, and in the sentence the author picks out for an interview. There is a standing gap between what the author would defend if asked point-blank and what the prose systematically does.

What I want is not the label but the carving.

- Is there an established concept, in any of these fields, for the case where a text's causal and explanatory commitments are carried by grammatical structure and narrative placement rather than by any assertion the author would avow — and can therefore be withdrawn as a matter of style?
- I know several near neighbours and why each seems to miss. Attributing feelings to weather has an old name, but nothing here feels anything; the weather is made a doer, not a person. Hypostatization and reification concern treating abstractions as concrete things, whereas this treats a concrete thing as a chooser. Metaphor theorists will say the usage is loose and non-committal, but that is exactly what is in dispute: does a systematic, repeated pattern of loose usage commit an author to anything? There is also, I gather, a strand of theorising about historiography holding that the choice of narrative form itself carries commitments the historian has never argued for; I want to know whether what I describe is an instance of that, a special case of it, or something else again. And there is a strand that wants to grant agency to non-human things deliberately, as a corrective to human-centred history — which my phenomenon resembles from the outside but not from the inside, since here the agency is granted covertly and then refused.
- Where is the line between innocuous vividness and the thing I mean? Is there a principled test — a way to tell whether a sentence of the form "the eruption ended the dynasty" is doing causal work in the argument or only decorating work done elsewhere? Does the asymmetry matter, and if so, why does it matter more than the strong verb on its own?
- Is the disavowal ever legitimate? What would have to be true of a text for "I was only writing vividly" to be an adequate answer to a charge of determinism?
- If no term exists, propose one, define it, and give me the boundary cases: the nearest things that are not it, and the weakest thing that still counts. I would also want it not to collide with an existing term, and I believe the most obvious coinage is already taken in linguistics for the strong claim that the language a person speaks fixes what that person can think.

I should say why I think this deserves pinning down rather than a shrug. The argument over environmental determinism is generally conducted at the level of theses — whether climate constrains or compels — and I suspect a good deal of it is settled in advance, in each direction, by sentence structure that no participant is inspecting. If I am wrong about that, I would like to be told so plainly; if I am right, I would like the vocabulary in which to say it.
