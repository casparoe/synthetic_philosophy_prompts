I got talked into writing the theory section of a memo for a municipal reform commission here, and I've hit a wall. They've got three live proposals on the table at once: drop the voting age to 16 for city elections, extend the franchise in city elections to permanent residents who've lived here five years or more, and make turnout compulsory for everyone who does have the vote, enforced with a small fine (australia-style, nothing dramatic). The chair was explicit that she doesnt want three separate arguments stapled together — she wants one account of who belongs in the demos from which all three verdicts follow, or a demonstration that no single account can do that. My problem is I can't evaluate any candidate principle until I know what I'm supposed to be demanding of one, and right now every principle I try seems to come out right on one proposal and wrong on another and I can't tell whether that's a fatal flaw or just noise.

So: write me the spec. What does a theory of enfranchisement have to deliver to count as adequate for this purpose — not a survey of the existing views, I'll read those later, just the requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it. A few families I'm already circling, in case it helps you calibrate: the boundary cases it has to get a verdict on without special pleading (toddlers, the 16-year-olds in question, adults under guardianship, long-term resident non-citizens, citizens who emigrated at age six and have never been back, seasonal workers, prisoners, people who are heavily affected by our decisions but live elsewhere, and people who'll live here in thirty years); the distinctions it must not collapse (having the right to vote versus being obliged to exercise it, being affected by a decision versus being subject to the coercion that enforces it, competence versus stake, individual claims versus group claims, who's in the demos versus how much each vote weighs — and actually tell me whether that last one belongs inside the spec at all or is a separate question); whatever it has to stay consistent with empirically, including what the compulsory-turnout cases show about who shows up and whether results move, and what we actually know about the political knowledge of 16-year-olds relative to the low-information adults who already vote, and if the honest answer is that they're comparable, say what that does to any competence-based criterion; and self-application, i.e. who gets to decide the boundaries and whether a demos can change its own composition without going circular. For each requirement mark whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and mark which ones make the theory hostage to an empirical premise it currently can't discharge — call those its empirical debts, because I think that's where a lot of these theories are quietly loading the work.

Two things I especially want. First, tell me if the chair's demand — one principle, three verdicts — is actually a desideratum or just an aesthetic preference for theoretical economy; I suspect the latter but I don't trust my suspicion. Second, flag any subset of the list you think can't be jointly satisfied, since if there is one, the list itself is the finding and I'll write the memo around it. My own hunch is that affectedness and subjection-to-coercion come apart precisely in the cases the commission cares about: a 16-year-old is squarely subject to compulsory-schooling and juvenile-justice law but pays essentially no tax, a five-year permanent resident is taxed and coerced in full but gets no vote, an emigrant citizen keeps a passport and a ballot while being barely touched by the zoning code. So any single criterion either over-includes or under-includes in exactly the wrong places, and I want to know whether that's fatal or fixable. Then say which one requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates, and finish with a few sentences — really just a few, no citations, no reading list, I'll chase those down myself — on how the obvious contenders do against the list: something Dahl-ish built on affectedness, a coercion/all-subjected account, and a membership or social-justice account. A table is fine for the requirements as long as the motivating case is spelled out per row.
