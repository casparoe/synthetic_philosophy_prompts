Awkward favor. I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student and the engineering school here has a required one-credit professional ethics seminar for their autonomous systems master's students — 22 of them, mostly controls and ML people — and I'm runing one 50-minute session of it in three weeks. My assigned topic is the responsibility gap: the thought that when a genuinely adaptive system causes serious harm, it can turn out that no person satisfies the ordinary conditions for moral responsibility, and yet somebody obviously ought to answer for it. The instructor who did it last year ran the whole thing through self-driving trolley problems, and the program director told me the students came out thinking it was philosophy cosplay and that anyone who raises the topic just wants to ban their technology. So I want to rebuild the session from scratch, and what I need from you is the teaching plan, not the lecture. Start with the dependency structure: which pieces have to be in place before which, and why. My current intuition is that they need the control and knowledge conditions on blame before the word "gap" even means anything, and that I need some version of the problem of many hands before I can get to the specifically machine-learning wrinkle, which is that the behavior isn't traceable to anyone's design decisions because the system's dispositions came out of a training run and a data stream rather than out of a specification. Tell me where that ordering is wrong, and tell me what depends on what so I don't end up asserting the conclusion in week-minute five and then trying to backfill it for forty minutes.

Then the carrying case. I've been sketching one of my own: a hospital infusion pump running a vendor-supplied dosing model, a routine over-the-air update goes out, one ward has been informally muting the pump's alarm escalations for months because of false positives, and a patient ends up seriously overdosed. Afterwards it turns out the dose came from an interaction between the update, the ward's muting habit, and a calibration drift nobody was monitoring; every individual person involved did something locally defensible, the vendor's documentation was honest, and the nurses' workaround was making patients safer on average. I want to know whether that can carry the whole thing at this level or whether it has too many moving parts and I should build up from something smaller first, and if so what that smaller thing should be and what it has to lack. The two properties I need are: the students can't make the gap disappear by finding a villain, and they also can't shrug and say "fine, blame the hospital, institutions can bear responsibility, topic closed" — which is absolutely the escape hatch they'll grab within ninety seconds.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I care about most. I can already predict four: "the programmer wrote the code so obviously he's responsible"; "the gap is just a legal liability question, assign strict liability, next topic"; "if nobody's responsible then the machine must be, so let's start talking about moral status for systems"; and "this is just an argument against deploying anything adaptive, which means you'd rather people get hurt by the dumber safer alternative." Give me the ones I'm missing, the order they'll actually surface in given the sequence you propose, and the exact point in the sequence where each has to be headed off before it hardens — including which ones I should kill by declining to engage rather than by arguing, since I only have 50 minutes. Also tell me what to deliberately leave out and announce that I'm leaving out, so the omission doesn't read as dodging: free will, the legal-doctrine side, robot rights, the various repairs on offer (forward-looking responsibility, collective responsibility, virtue-flavored moves) — I can't do all of them and I need to know which ones are safe to cut at this level. And give me one question I can put on the board for the last five minutes that would show me whether they actually got the concept or just agreed with me politely, because these students are too polite to argue. Last thing: please don't hand me a menu. Pick an ordering, pick the case, commit to which misconceptions get argued with and which get refused, and give me your reasons — and if you think my pump scenario is a bad carrier, say so plainly and replace it rather than offering me three options to choose between. Flag anywhere your plan quietly leans on a contested claim about what responsibility requires, because those are exactly the spots where a smart controls student will push, and I'd much rather know in advance which of my premises are load-bearing than find out while I'm standing at the whiteboard.
