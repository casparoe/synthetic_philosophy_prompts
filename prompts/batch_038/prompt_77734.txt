I'm working on a paper arguing that the standard Bayesian line about archival silence — a missing record counts against the practice it would have documented, once you condition on the archive's recording rate — is sound in some cases and structurally useless in others, and I want a test case that shows the split cleanly. So build me one fictional but perfectly ordinary institutional archive (port notary, plantation ledger, parish register, company personnel file — your pick) and two scenarios that use it. In the first, a historian infers from the gaps that some practice basically didn't happen, and she's right. In the second, the surface evidence looks the same — same kinds of gaps, same apparent recording rate — but the silence was produced by who the institution bothered to write down, and the practice was common. Someone reading only the surviving documents shouldn't be able to tell which scenario they're in, so dont cheat by planting a diary or a rival archive.

Constraints: no fires, floods, or lost collections — the gap has to come out of the recording habit itself, not from destruction. Each scenario one paragraph.

Then run the check. Name the likelihood claim the historian is leaning on in each case, say why it holds up in one and breaks in the other, and isolate the single structural feature of the record-generating process doing the work. Tell me what happens if you weaken that feature, and whether a Bayesian could rescue scenario two just by conditioning on a more finely grained model of who got recorded. If she can, my paper's in trouble and I'd rather hear it from you than from a referee.
