Thirty-five years in an analytical lab, retired four years ago, only now reading philosophy for fun, so bear with me if this comes out sideways. Last chunk of my career I also ran sensory panels — trained tasters, reference standards, blind randomized presentation, replicate sessions, the works — which is why a certain wine story has been bugging me ever since I hit the paper I'll get to below.

The episode is the Paris tasting of 24 May 1976, the one everyone calls the Judgment of Paris. A British wine merchant, Steven Spurrier, set up two blind flights: Chardonnays (Burgundy against California) and reds (Bordeaux against California Cabernet). Eleven judges, most of them the French establishment — the inspector general of the appellation board, the co-director of the Domaine de la Romanée-Conti, the sommelier at the Tour d'Argent, restaurant owners, a wine editor or two. Each graded out of 20 points, and here's the bit I keep coming back to: no rubric was handed out, every judge was explicitly free to grade by their own criteria, and the official ranking was just the arithmetic mean of the French judges' scores. A Napa Chardonnay and a Napa Cabernet came out first in their flights, the red by a hair on the 20-point scale, the one American journalist present wrote it up, the French were not amused, the judges scorecards became a sore point, and when comparable bottles were tasted again decades later the order shifted around. Related data point I'll just assert rather than cite: there's also that work where a white wine was colored red with an odorless dye and the tasters' write-ups came out full of red-fruit descriptors.

The theory is Dennett's Quining Qualia, specifically the coffee-taster case: a professional taster who reports after years on the job that the brew no longer tastes the way it did, and the argument that what actually changed is his discrimination and judgment machinery rather than any property he was ever directly acquainted with. The punchline as I've got it is that the word quale is being hired for two jobs that pull against each other — being the thing directly given in experience, and being the postulated item that explains how the subject sorts, rates and reports — and since nothing can do both, the concept should be scrapped rather than tidied up.

So: map Paris onto that argument, and be disciplined about it. Which item in that room is supposed to be the quale, who is the judgment machinery, and how do you separate stimulus, transducer, decision rule and report? Does Paris actually instantiate the coffee-taster structure at all, or is it a different animal — simultaneous error across many normal subjects rather than slow drift in one — in which case say so instead of forcing the fit. Then take my own rescue attempt apart: in my old life I'd call this pure criterion shift, sensitivity intact, the wine registered fine and then scored against an internal standard contaminated by expectation. Does that rescue a stable quale, or is it precisely the move quining predicts, since my rescue seems to explain everything without the quale ever having to show up? Does the trained-panel fix help — anchors, reference standards, forced choice, replicates — meaning that if you can make reports reproducible and interchangeable you've made them about something, or have you only built a better instrument?

Run it the other way too. Is Paris a confirmation for Dennett, an anomaly, or a case the thought experiment never anticipated? Would he have predicted that result, and what would he have to bolt on to handle experts who insist they were experiencing the wine perfectly clearly while their reports invert? And where is the theory simply silent — I'd rather know the gaps than have them papered over.

Finally, in my language: what measurement would discriminate the two hypotheses, reports tracking a stable qualitative character versus reports being the whole story? Sketch the experiment you'd actually run, and be honest about which part of it can't be done — and whether that impossibility is itself the argument. No reading list, please, I'll chase things down myself.
