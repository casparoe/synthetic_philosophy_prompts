I am a senior at a public high school, and in three weeks I will give a twelve-minute talk at our state humanities forum in the philosophy division, followed by roughly eight minutes of questions from a faculty panel. I would like a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a defense of my thesis and not a survey of the background.

The panel has three members. The first is a computer science professor who works on machine learning and has written that he finds analogies drawn from visual art to computation intellectually worthless. The second is a mathematical logician who teaches first-course metamathematics and is visibly irritated whenever Gödel, Escher, Bach is recruited into debates about consciousness. The third is a printmaker and art historian who has spent a career on Northern European print techniques, Escher's included. The rest of the room is high school students and their teachers: some have watched popular videos about strange loops, and some have never met the term.

My thesis, stated flatly: the inviolate level is never eliminated in a strange loop, only relocated. Hofstadter's line in the chapter of Gödel, Escher, Bach devoted to strange loops and tangled hierarchies is that below every tangled hierarchy lies an inviolate level. I want to argue that this is not a limitation on his picture but the whole truth of it, and that it is general. I call my claim the conservation of levels: for any description in which a hierarchy appears genuinely tangled, there is an equally accurate description in which some level stands outside the loop, and the difference between the two is a choice of frame rather than a discovery about the world.

My argument runs in three moves. First, a descriptive survey. In Escher's Drawing Hands, the 1948 lithograph, the cuffs stay flat against the sheet while the fingers rise out of it and draw one another; behind the drawing is the stone, behind the stone Escher's own hand, behind that the viewer's visual system, and at every stage there is a level that is not inside the loop it makes possible. The same holds for the computational cases I plan to use: a compiler that recompiles its own source, a program that prints its own manual, and a training loop in which a system rewrites the code that trains it. In each case we can name the inviolate level — the operating system, the power supply, the objective function, whoever pressed enter. Second, the conservation principle itself: tangledness is therefore frame-relative, and nothing is a strange loop simpliciter, only a strange loop relative to a particular cut through the description. Third, the consequence I most want to defend: the Gödelian argument against mechanism, in the form Lucas gave it and Penrose later revived, and the standard replies to it, are both making frame-relative claims and treating them as absolute. Lucas needs the mathematician to stand outside the machine's formal system; the reply that the mathematician is also such a system does not refute him, it merely moves the cut. On my account the dispute is not about whether minds are machines but about where the cut goes, and Gödel's theorem cannot settle that, because the theorem is itself established from a standpoint outside the system it concerns.

What I need from you:

- The eight to ten questions this particular panel will actually ask, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk, with likelihood noted separately where the two come apart.
- Each question phrased in that panelist's own idiom. The logician will worry about provability and about whether I am equivocating on the word outside; the machine learning person will deny that a trained network has the kind of levels my argument needs; the printmaker will say I have got the print wrong and that Escher had no philosophical program to be extracted from it.
- For each, a reply I can deliver in under a minute, since the moderator cuts speakers off; what I should concede immediately; and where the reply is at its softest, stated honestly rather than diplomatically.
- The two or three questions I am hoping nobody asks. One of them I can already see, and I want it handled directly: if all level-talk is frame-relative, is my conservation principle anything more than a frame I have chosen, and does my argument undercut itself in exactly the way it accuses others of doing?
- What I should concede in the first two minutes of the talk in order to take the sharpest weapons away from the panel before they are drawn.
- Any place where I have probably run two real distinctions together — levels of description versus levels of causation, for instance, or the difference between what a picture shows and what an argument establishes.

Please do not recommend readings, sources, or citations; I have no way to look anything up and would rather you work from what you already know. Do not spend words explaining the incompleteness theorems or Escher's biography to me; assume a strong undergraduate introduction to both. And if you judge that the conservation principle is empty — that it amounts to nothing more than the observation that anything can be redescribed — say so plainly, and then tell me whether the talk can be salvaged by weakening the claim or whether I should build it around a different thesis entirely.
