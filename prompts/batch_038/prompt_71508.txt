I am a senior and chair the peer mediation board at my high school: six students who hear roughly a dozen referred conflicts a year, mostly between students but occasionally between a student and a teacher, and who recommend either a written agreement or a sanction to the dean, who nearly always accepts it. Hearings run forty-five minutes, we have no training beyond a single orientation afternoon, and our written reasoning has to be legible to the dean and to the families involved. Three of us have been reading Adam Smith on sympathy and the impartial spectator, and I would like to build next year's orientation around a procedure derived from him: an ordered set of questions the board asks during and after a hearing, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops short of a recommendation, and where it must hand the decision back to judgment because the issue is what these particular people can feel rather than a fact we could look up. Two features I want preserved in the translation, since the obvious version — ask what a reasonable outsider would think — seems to flatten them: Smith's spectator is not an actual bystander we could poll but a standpoint we take up ourselves after correcting for our own partiality, and his first question concerns the propriety of a feeling, whether a spectator can enter into it, rather than how much harm resulted; where resentment is involved, he appears to require sympathy with both the person injured and the motive of the person who acted. Correct me if I have that wrong. Then run the procedure on three cases: a student whose notebook of original poems was thrown out by a classmate who took it for scrap paper, and who now wants that classmate suspended; a student quietly dropped from a group chat and a study group, whose complaint is hurt rather than anger; and a student who plagiarized, was caught, has apologized, and has been forgiven by the teacher, where most of the board would like to close the file. Mark each step that is really a judgment call in procedural dress, say what the procedure cannot do, and tell me how confident you are in your reading of Smith, singling out the point you are least sure of.
