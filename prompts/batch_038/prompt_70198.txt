I was at a philosophy-of-mathematics panel a couple weeks back — two speakers, one a pretty hard-nosed set-theoretic Platonist who works on foundations of analysis, the other someone sympathetic to the picture where sets only ever get formed in stages and the universe of sets is never a finished thing, more of a "you can always go further" view. Somebody did the diagonal argument on the board, and then an undergrad in the second row asked a question I've not been able to stop thinkng about. The two speakers answered it completely differently and then kept at it through the wine reception. The student's question, as best I can reconstruct it from memory: "You assumed there was a list of all the reals, and you showed that any such list is missing at least one. So you've shown there's no list. But that's a negative result about lists. Where in any of that did you show me the collection of all the reals exists as a completed thing? All you've shown is that we could never finish writing one down." The Platonist said the question was confused, that the diagonal argument is a theorem of ZFC and theorems are about sets and not about anybody writing anything down, and that the student was smuggling in a picture where mathematics is something humans do with pencils. The other speaker said the student had, whether they knew it or not, put their finger on exactly the right spot: that the proof's real content is negative, and that going from "no function from the naturals hits everything" to "there's a completed totality here that is bigger" is a further step that needs its own defence.

So: write that conversation. The two of them alone in the seminar room afterwards, the diagonal still up on the board in green chalk, and let them actually go at the board — one of them should erase or annotate something at least once, and that edit should matter to the argument. I want them equally good. No strawmen: the Platonist shouldn't be a smug "obviously the sets are there" person, and the potentialist shouldn't be a crank who thinks mathematics is a human activity and nothing else. Real back-and-forth, meaning each one responds to what the other just said rather than delivering a prepared speech. Specifically I want the Platonist to press hard on this: when the other one says "for any stage, another stage is possible," what is that claim about? Saying it seems to require a determinate range of possible extensions to quantify over, which looks like precisely the completed totality they refused — so either the potentialist's own view is inexpressible, or they owe an account of that modality that doesn't smuggle in what it denies. And I want the potentialist to press back on the fact that even Platonists describe the cumulative hierarchy in exactly stage-by-stage, never-finished language, and that the concept of set is supposed to be one you can always extend — so who here is the one with a mysterious completed infinity, and what does the Platonist's universe-of-all-sets actually consist in if it's never a set itself? Have them end by trying to draft the two or three sentences they'd email back to the undergrad, and either land on wording both of them can genuinely sign, or fail and say out loud that they failed. Don't turn it into a roll call of names and books — I'm not after a reading list, I'm after the argument, so if a move depends on a technical point, have them say the point informally in dialogue instead of citing anything. Roughly 900–1400 words, enough room for at least five or six substantive exchanges.

Then step out of the fiction for a short note, maybe five sentences. Tell me which of the two you think came out ahead on argument rather than on rhetoric, and what single consideration would flip that verdict for you — which of the moves above you think is actually the weakest link and could be repaired or refuted by something I haven't thought of. Also flag where you're least confident you got a technical point right: if you overstated what the potentialist owes on the modality problem, or if you flattened the difference between "there's no surjection from the naturals onto the reals" and something stronger, say so plainly. And note any place where one of the characters is holding a position that's a real position but that you've simplified to the point of being a bit unfair to it.
