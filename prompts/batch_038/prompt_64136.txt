The question (700 word limit):

A painting long attributed to a major seventeenth-century Dutch master has been exposed as a forgery made in the 1930s. Before the exposure, experienced viewers could not tell it from the attributed master's work by looking at it. Two claims are commonly made about cases like this:
(i) the forgery is aesthetically inferior to the works it was passed off as;
(ii) since the exposure changed nothing about how the canvas looks, any difference in value between it and a genuine work cannot be an aesthetic difference.
Can (i) and (ii) both be true? Say what account of aesthetic properties makes them compatible or incompatible, and say what your account implies for an art form in which the identity of the work is fixed by a notation rather than by the history of the object.

The student's answer:

The first thing to notice is that this object is not a copy. A copy announces itself as a copy; nobody is deceived about who made it. A forgery is made to be taken for somebody else's work, and that difference in intention is what generates the puzzle, because (i) and (ii) look flatly contradictory only if we assume that everything aesthetically relevant about a picture is available in a single act of looking. (ii) is also ambiguous. It can mean that exposure changed nothing about how the canvas appears, which is trivially true, or that nothing aesthetically relevant distinguishes the forgery from a genuine work, which is a much bigger claim. Only the second reading is worth arguing about.

Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts explains why painting generates the problem in the first place. Painting is autographic: there is no notation that fixes the identity of the work, so an object that was not produced in the right historical way is not an instance of the work no matter how it looks. Music and literature are allographic: the work is picked out by compliance with a score or a text, so any compliant performance is a genuine instance of it. It follows that in the allographic arts the question of the value of the original cannot even arise. Since every compliant instance is an instance of the work, there is nothing left over for authenticity to add, and the last part of the essay question has an empty answer.

On the aesthetic difference, Goodman concedes (ii) in its interesting reading. He agrees that at the moment of viewing there is no aesthetic difference between the forgery and the original, and argues that we should care about the difference anyway for an epistemic reason: even if I cannot tell them apart today, I might learn to, and the fact that I could learn to tells me there is something there to be learned. On this picture the reason to prefer the original concerns what looking at it teaches me, not what it is aesthetically. So Goodman belongs on the side of (ii), and the pull of (i) is a confusion between aesthetic value and art-historical value.

Walton explains why that pull is so strong anyway. A work's aesthetic properties depend on the category it is perceived in, because the same nonaesthetic properties are standard, variable, or contraindicated relative to different categories, and the aesthetic properties track that. Categories are conventions, and which one we bring is up to us. Before the exposure, viewers saw the canvas as a Dutch master's painting, so it had the aesthetic properties of one; after it they see a 1930s pastiche, and it has those properties instead. But the canvas did not change. So aesthetic properties are not in the object at all, they are a function of how we take it, which supports (ii): what the exposure changes is a change in us.

The forgery is still a lesser achievement, though. It can be a work of art in its own right and it can show enormous technical command, but it cannot be credited with the innovations it repeats. If the picture introduced a new way of handling light in an interior, the forgery did not introduce anything; it echoed. Originality is an aesthetic property, so this settles (i): the forgery really is aesthetically inferior, not just historically discounted.

Danto's Brillo Boxes make the same point from the other direction. Two things can be perceptually identical and differ in aesthetic value, because what makes the difference is the theory and the history the object sits in. That is Walton's point again, and it shows that (i) and (ii) cannot both be true once 'aesthetic' means anything interesting. The two claims are reconcilable only because 'aesthetic' is doing double duty in them, and separating the two jobs is the whole answer.

So both can be true. The forgery is inferior as an artistic achievement and lacks the historical properties that make the original the work it is, but nothing about how it looks is any the worse for it, and the exposure altered only our relation to the canvas. That is also why good connoisseurs were taken in: they were tracking appearance, and appearance was never where the difference lay.
