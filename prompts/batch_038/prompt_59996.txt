I run an essay prize for sixth-formers — 1,500 words, modest cash award, we usually get four hundred-odd entries a year. This year's set question went out in the brochure last week and it reads:

"Why did the Industrial Revolution happen in Britain and not in China?"

Two of the four judges are now insisting the question is malformed and want it pulled and re-advertised, which would cost us most of the entry window. I think they're being precious. As I see it the question just asks what Britain had that China didn't, and if historians disagree about the list, students pick a side and argue it, which is precisely the muscle we want them to exercise. One of the objectors keeps saying "you've assumed the question picks out a single thing" and muttering about "the foil", and I honestly couldn't follow him. He also says the units are wrong — that you can't set a country the size of Britain against a continental empire, that the real comparison is England against the Yangzi delta, and that the two were roughly level around 1750 on things like life expectancy and consumption, which would mean the question as printed invites an answer that's already been refuted. And he says the standard coal story (Britain's coal sat near water, China's was up in Shanxi a long way from where the manufacturing was) tells you where the coal was but not why that made any difference, so it can't be the answer to a "why here and not there" question at all.

Separately, my grandfather always called this the Needham question after the man who wrote the big history of Chinese science, and I've also heard of some "high-level equilibrium trap" idea where China was too efficient to need labour-saving machines, which I assume is part of the same argument.

So: is my judge onto something real, or is this a squabble about phrasing I can wave through on Thursday? And if it's real, I need to walk into that meeting with a replacement question I can defend to a room that includes him.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **What's wrong with the question** — about 400 words. Don't soften it. If I've made a category error rather than a wording error, name it in plain English and explain why the "just let the students pick a side" defence doesn't rescue it.
2. **How many questions are hiding inside it** — about 400 words. Lay out the distinct readings a bright 17-year-old could reasonably take the printed sentence to have (different foils, different units, different time windows, "why here" versus "why not there"), and for each say what the honest answer would look like and whether there's an answer at all.
3. **What still survives** — around 200 words on how much of the original question can be kept without pretending the problems aren't there.
4. **Three replacements** — about 300 words total. Three candidate set questions, each with one line on what it commits the student to and one line on why it's still writable by a teenager working from school library books and no specialist sources.
5. **Thursday** — five sentences maximum that I can read aloud, which concede whatever has to be conceded without making the objecting judge look like he was grandstanding.
