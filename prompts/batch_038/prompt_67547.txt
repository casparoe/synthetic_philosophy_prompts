I facilitate an annual two-day operations retreat for a regional federation of worker cooperatives and mutual-aid organizations — roughly sixty participants, mostly dispatchers for a shared delivery fleet, coordinators of a communal cold-storage warehouse, two people who administer shared server capacity, and the rotating committee that allocates all of it. We have no internal market: member organizations submit requests on a form and the committee adjudicates, which everyone finds tedious but tolerable. Last year we piloted something more ambitious. The committee assigned point values to a van-hour, a pallet-slot-day, and a block of compute time, revised those point values each quarter on the basis of self-reported usage, and let members spend a fixed quarterly allotment of points. The results were genuinely confusing and I would like the retreat to work through them rather than step over them. Queues at the warehouse disappeared almost immediately. Total reported demand rose by something like thirty percent across the board, apparently because members had worked out that understating last quarter's usage earned them a cheaper rate this quarter. And quite separately, one member cooperative cut its van-hours roughly in half using a consolidation routine that a single driver had quietly known about for two years and had never written down — it surfaced only because he grumbled about the point system at a potluck and somebody asked him to explain himself.

For the retreat's two-hour plenary I want a decision tree that sorts participants by what they actually believe about where the numbers governing these allocations should come from, not by what they like to call themselves. Build it from the questions first and locate the positions afterwards. I would like eight to ten branch nodes and six to eight leaves. Every node must be a question that a person with no economics training can answer while thinking about vans, cold storage, and compute time, and no node may name a school of thought, an economist, a country, or a slogan — nothing of the form 'do you trust markets?' or 'is planning socialist?'. The questions should turn on matters such as whether the information the committee needs already exists somewhere and could in principle be written down; whether our disagreements are about arithmetic or about what people do once they are being measured; whether a point value that clears a queue has thereby settled the question; whether the relevant numbers could be worked out in advance given enough processing power; whether we only discover what an option was worth after people have acted on it; and whether a federation of a few dozen organizations is a small-scale instance of an entire economy's problem or something structurally different.

The leaves must include, at minimum: the view that without prices generated by genuine exchange there is no rational way at all to compare alternative uses of the same van or the same shelf; the distinct view that the arithmetic might well be possible but the decisive knowledge is dispersed, partly tacit, and constantly changing, so that only an ongoing process of rivalry, experimentation, and correction can bring it to light — participants invariably collapse these two into a single position, and the tree should be able to tell them apart; the view that a competent central body can administer the point values as parameters and revise them by trial and error until excess demand and excess supply vanish; the view that this is now merely a computational problem, solvable by working the required quantities out directly; the view that the fix is to let members buy and sell their points among one another and let the trading reveal the values; and the view that our whole situation is a false analogy, because a firm or federation can plan internally precisely to the extent that it is embedded in real external prices that discipline and inform it. Each leaf should carry the customary name of the position if one exists (otherwise mark it 'unnamed'), one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what it would have predicted about last year's pilot. Separately from the tree, list the combinations of answers that your nodes make available but that no recognized position occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has thought to defend it — and where it is merely unexplored, sketch who might plausibly end up there. Flag any pair of leaves your nodes cannot genuinely separate, and say what further question would separate them. Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; the names in the leaf labels are all the sourcing I need. The whole instrument has to run on paper, in forty minutes, in front of sixty people.
