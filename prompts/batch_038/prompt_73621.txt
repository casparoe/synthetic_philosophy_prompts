I teach an upper-division applied ethics seminar of twenty-two students, most of them pre-health, meeting once a week for eighty minutes. In November my mother was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's disease and named me her healthcare agent, and the unit on advance directives that I have taught for six years has stopped being an abstraction for me. It has also stopped working.

The activity I used to run is the familiar one: students draft a living will, exchange documents, and receive a vignette in which they must apply someone else's. Every year they apply it faithfully, report that it felt straightforward, and conclude that advance directives are a tidy solution. I have come to think that result is an artifact of the design. The vignettes I supplied were ones the documents actually covered, and the patients in them were suffering. Neither condition holds in the situation I am now living through, and I no longer believe my students are being shown the problem.

What I want them to come away seeing is harder. First, that a directive is not self-executing: it delegates its own application to an interpreter, and the interpreter arrives already holding a tacit theory about which of the author's interests survive the author's capacity to have them. Second, that when the person in front of you is not suffering — is in fact content, sociable, and pleased to see you — literal compliance with the document and kindness to the patient come apart, and there is no neutral standpoint from which to rank them. I do not want them to leave settled about which view is correct. I want them to leave unable to explain why they did what they did.

Design the activity. Constraints and requirements:

- Eighty minutes, twenty-two students, a single session, no assigned reading beforehand, no devices. Each handout must fit on one printed page.
- Every student writes a real directive of their own under a hard word limit. I want the limit to be the villain of the piece: the compression should be what makes their own document unrecognizable when someone else reads it. Recommend a limit and defend it.
- Authorship and enforcement must be separated. No student interprets their own card, and no student knows whose card they hold.
- The later self must be played by a live participant working from a role card, not described aloud by me. The role must be playable without caricature and without cruelty: warm, forgetful, mildly insistent, unafraid. I will not run an activity that turns dementia into a comic turn, and some students in the room may have affected relatives.
- There must be at least two decision points where the directive is silent or ambiguous, and one where it is clear and compliance would make the person in the room worse off by any ordinary measure.
- A mechanic of my own, on which I want your judgment: after each decision the interpreter marks up the author's card in pen — circling the clause relied on, striking through what they set aside, and writing in the margin what they took the author to have meant. The annotated card is returned to its author before any discussion. My expectation is that seeing one's own words edited is more confrontational than hearing a report about them. Tell me whether that is right, whether it will degenerate into apology and politeness, and what to change if it will.
- Neither the theoretical positions nor the names associated with them may surface until the debrief.

Please deliver: a minute-by-minute timeline; the verbatim text of every handout, role card, and scenario prompt; the debrief question sequence, stating for each question what it is meant to surface and what answer you expect; an explicit map from particular moments in the activity onto the two insights above; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — including the student who refuses to comply and converts the round into a stand-off, and the student who becomes upset; and a frank account of which feature of the real problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply myself.

One further worry, which I would like you to engage rather than reassure me about. I suspect the design is rigged. Staging a cheerful, untroubled later self makes the earlier self's claims look cruel by construction, and will deliver the outcome I expect whatever anyone argues. Tell me whether that is so. If it is, tell me whether to correct it, whether to correct it by running a second round that rigs the case the other way, or whether to leave the rig in place and make the rig itself the final question of the debrief.
