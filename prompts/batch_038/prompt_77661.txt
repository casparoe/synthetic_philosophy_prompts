I am the lettings manager for a municipal housing authority, and I train the allocation officers who administer our points-based scheme for social housing. Roughly twice a month we encounter a case the scheme was never drafted to handle: the last available unit of a given type and size, and two or more applicant households landing on exactly the same priority score — same band, same waiting-time count to the day, same medical and hardship points. The scheme's own tie-break ladder then simply runs out. Officers currently improvise. Some weigh which household they feel more sympathy for, some quietly favour the applicants with children, some take whichever file was opened first, and none of these practices is written down, taught, or defensible if the unsuccessful household appeals. A random draw has been confirmed as legally permissible but is politically awkward, and every one of these decisions must survive an internal review in which the losing household asks, in terms, why them and not us.

What has struck me is that this is the practical mirror image of Buridan's ass. In the standard telling the animal sits between two identical piles of food and starves, because there is no asymmetry to pull it one way rather than the other, and the puzzle is usually read as a reductio: rational choice supposedly requires a difference in reasons. Our situation inverts it. The reasons have genuinely run out, and yet a decision is mandatory, dated, recorded, and appealable. I would like a written procedure my officers can follow on these cases — questions in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the point at which the procedure terminates in a draw and precisely what the officer is to say to the unsuccessful household, and the point at which the procedure stops and refers the matter upward to a panel because the remaining question is one of value rather than fact.

Two branches need particular care. First, the possibility that the tie is not a tie in the world but an artifact of the scheme's coarse grain — equal scores concealing unequal claims the scoring language cannot express. The procedure must say what an officer does with that discovery in the case in front of her, and what she does with it as feedback about the scheme, and it must say how those two tasks are kept from collapsing into one another. Second, the asymmetries that are real but are not reasons: arrival order, file numbering, which household an officer finds herself hoping for. Are these legitimate resources, impermissible ones, or is the question only about which of them one exploits and whether one admits to it?

Ground the procedure in whatever the analysis of choice under exact indifference actually supports, but write it for officers who have read none of it. If the philosophical upshot is that every option is permissible once the reasons are exhausted, the procedure still has to show how a permissive conclusion becomes a determinate, auditable, explainable one, and it must take a position on whether a draw is the only resolution that respects the tie or an abdication of judgment by the people paid to exercise it. Please engage with the thought that lotteries are sometimes adopted not because they are fair in themselves but because they spare a community from having to state publicly a ranking it is unwilling or unable to state, and say whether that applies to us.

Then run the procedure on three cases. One: two households tied on everything the scheme measures, where one file indicates a person fleeing domestic abuse who has not yet been formally assessed under that heading. Two: a three-way tie in which one household is already resident in the borough and two are in temporary accommodation outside it. Three: a tie where the officer is confident from the documents that the scheme has mis-scored one applicant but cannot prove it within the deadline. In at least one of these the procedure should deliver an answer I will not like; say so plainly, and explain why the procedure should not be quietly overridden at exactly that point. Finish by stating what the procedure cannot do — which class of tied cases it will get wrong, why, and how we would come to know it.
