A colleague has been pressing the following argument against expressivism. What disquiets me is not its conclusion but the fact that it seems to prove far more than that conclusion.

(1) If a sentence has a meaning, that meaning fixes what the sentence contributes to every larger sentence in which it can occur.
(2) "Lying is wrong" can occur unasserted, as the antecedent of "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong."
(3) That conditional is valid, and it is valid in virtue of the antecedent contributing exactly what "Lying is wrong" contributes when it is asserted.
(4) Nothing that is itself an act, or the expression of an attitude, can make that same contribution, since acts and attitudes are things agents perform and have, not things that can sit unasserted inside a conditional.
(5) So the meaning of "Lying is wrong" is not the expression of an attitude but a content that is either true or false.
(6) Nothing in the reasoning is special to morality: any embeddable sentence of English is thereby truth-apt, and truth-aptness just is what it takes for a sentence to represent how things stand.

My worry concerns (6). It appears to foreclose non-truth-conditional treatments of embeddable sentences across the board — epistemic "might," predicates of taste, probability claims — and it appears to make whether a stretch of discourse is descriptive a matter of its syntax, which on its face would hand the expressivist truth-aptness for free. So either the argument breaks somewhere earlier than I can locate, or it goes through and establishes something nobody should want established.

Which step do you reject, and what made it look innocent? Then price the alternatives: what does each of (1), (3), and (4) cost an expressivist who gives it up, given the strategies actually on offer — plan-laden normative contents, relational accounts of the state expressed, hybrid semantics? For what it is worth, I would most like to keep (2) and (3). Finally, suppose the expressivist simply accepts (5) at the thin, syntactic level: state what substantive disagreement with the cognitivist is left over, and whether the argument should then be counted as a success or a miss.
