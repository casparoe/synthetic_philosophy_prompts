ok so my roommate and I just spent three hours arguing about Hilbert's hotel and I have to turn the argument into a 20-page paper for my phil-of-math seminar, and before I write a word of it I want the spec, not the arguments. I want the list of requirements any account of actual infinity has to meet before it's allowed to call itself adequate.

Where we got stuck: he's basically a finitist. He says the hotel shows actual infinities can't exist, because you get absurdities — it's full and yet takes more guests, everyone shifts rooms, and now the hotel plus countably many new arrivals is the same size as before. I said that's confused, since nothing in the scenario is impossible; every step is a finite well-defined move, and the "absurdity" is just our finite-collection intuitions failing to transfer. He said fine, then I owe an account of why the intuition fires at all. Neither of us could say what would settle it. Hence the spec.

Write the desiderata. Each requirement with the case or consideration behind it. Include the stock ones — the hotel and its variants, Cantor's diagonal and the hierarchy of sizes, supertasks (Thomson's lamp, Zeno), whether an infinite past is traversable, uniform distributions over countably infinite outcome spaces, mereological sums of infinitely many parts, Banach–Tarski-style results — but tell me which of those are actually load-bearing and which are just some camp's favorite example. Also state the distinctions a theory isn't allowed to collapse: Dedekind-infinite versus not-finite, potential versus actual, the size of a collection versus the possibility of completing a process over it, cardinal versus ordinal. And add a conservativeness requirement if you think it belongs: whatever you say about infinite collections shouldn't change the truth of any purely finite claim. If you think that one begs the question, say so.

The part I care about most is where the list is overconstrained. I already suspect one spot: if you demand countable additivity and you also demand that a countably infinite lottery can have a genuinely fair, uniform distribution, something has to give, and I want to know whether that's a real no-go or just a sloppily stated requirement. Find every other place where two or three of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, because if the list is inconsistent then the list itself is the result of my paper and I'd rather know now than in week nine.

Also add a requirement I don't know how to state properly — call it the selectivity datum. Why does the alarm go off for the hotel and not for the infinite decimal expansion of pi, or the set of all possible chess games, or the infinite sequence of natural numbers I use to index my bookshelves? An account that explains the absurdity-intuition in one case and stays silent in the others is incomplete, and I want that as an explicit criterion with its own motivating cases. Plus a companion criterion about framing: the exact same scenario described as a warehouse with infinitely many crates doesn't feel absurd to me at all, so does the theory have to explain that divergence, or is it allowed to write it off as noise? Argue which.

Then sort the whole list into metaphysical requirements (what infinite collections would be like) and epistemic ones (how anyone could ever be justified in believing claims about them), because I think half of what he's objecting to is epistemic and he keeps stating it as metaphysical. Flag every requirement that's a smuggled conclusion rather than a neutral criterion — on both sides, his and mine.

Finish with one or two lines per camp — strict finitist, Aristotelian potentialist, full-blooded Platonist, and the people who replace real-valued measures with infinitesimal ones — saying which requirements they drop and whether dropping it looks principled or ad hoc. No bibliography, I'll add citations myself: positions and arguments only, and please don't invent references.
