I work on fault-tolerant distributed consensus — state-machine replication, Byzantine agreement, the FLP impossibility result, Lamport and vector clocks, and the knowledge-based account of simultaneous agreement that treats a protocol's correctness as a matter of what each process knows at a point in a run. That vocabulary is native to me: I read arguments in terms of runs, admissible schedules, message deliveries, faults, and the distinction between local knowledge, mutual knowledge, and common knowledge. I have essentially no training in economics or epistemology beyond what the two-generals problem taught me.

What I want to understand is Aumann's agreement theorem — the 1976 result in "Agreeing to Disagree" that two Bayesian agents with a common prior cannot disagree once their posteriors are common knowledge — together with the dynamic material around it: the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result that two agents who successively announce their posteriors converge to agreement in a finite state space, whatever becomes of that result when there are three or more agents, and Aaronson's work on how much communication is needed to agree only approximately.

Restate all of this in my vocabulary, as literally as you can manage. Specifically:

1. Give a term-by-term mapping. For each of the following — state space, prior, common prior, knowledge-as-partition, posterior, announcement, common knowledge of posteriors, agreement — name the nearest thing in the distributed-systems repertoire (initial configuration? identical code and identical inputs? synchronous rounds? a knowledge operator indexed by runs and points? a decided value?) and state explicitly whether the match is clean, whether it holds only after distortion, or whether there is no counterpart at all. Wherever you distort, say what you distorted.

2. Restate the theorem itself as a claim about a protocol: what are the processes, what do they exchange, what is the termination condition, and what is guaranteed when it terminates. Then recast the proof in that frame. I want to see whether the standard argument — form the event on which both agents' posteriors take some fixed value, observe that this event is a union of cells of the finest common coarsening, and conclude the value is agent-independent — has any recognizable shape as an argument about knowledge over runs, or whether it is doing something my vocabulary has no idiom for.

3. Do the same for the dynamic results: cast successive posterior-announcement as message-passing rounds, say what the two-agent convergence result implies about the number of rounds required, and say whether anything breaks when the number of agents rises to three.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

- Is the common prior anything more than the stipulation that all processes begin in the same initial state with the same code? If it is more, what is the excess; if it is not, why is Aumann's theorem not a triviality in my terms, given that my protocols assume identical initial states as a matter of course and nobody supposes that gets them agreement?
- Common knowledge is the hinge of the theorem, and in my field it is precisely the thing that is hard or impossible to attain: coordinated attack, the two-generals problem, and the Halpern–Moses line of argument that common knowledge cannot be attained in an asynchronous system without synchrony assumptions. Is Aumann's common knowledge the same object as mine, or is it a stipulation about a static epistemic situation rather than something a protocol must achieve? Which of the two results is doing more work, and are they even about the same thing?
- Agreement in his sense looks unlike agreement in mine: his is concordance in the probabilities assigned to a proposition, mine is concordance on a decided value. Does that distinction survive translation, or does it dissolve once a decision value can be encoded as a proposition?
- His agents never lie, never crash, and never lose messages; my entire literature is about what happens when they do. Is the absence of faults a benign idealization I could factor back in, or does the structure of the argument depend on it in a way no additional assumption can repair?
- Finally, for each piece of residue you identify, give me a verdict: does it show that my vocabulary is missing something — a notion of prior, of accuracy, of belief-state as distinct from decision-state — or does it show that Aumann's result is less substantive than its reputation, closer to an unpacking of what common prior means than to a theorem with bite? I want a definite answer with reasons rather than a diplomatic hedge.

Do not smooth over mismatches to make the translation look tidier, and do not smuggle my field's assumptions into Aumann's position and present them as translation. No bibliography and no reading list; name a paper only where the name is doing argumentative work.
