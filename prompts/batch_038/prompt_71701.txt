TA-ing a second-year political philosophy course and I have to hand these back tomorow. The midterm short-answer question, worth 25 marks, was this:

"Popper argues that unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance. Rawls takes up the same problem in his discussion of toleration of the intolerant in A Theory of Justice. Set out each argument, and then say exactly where the two agree and where they diverge."

Here's the answer I got:

Popper's paradox starts from what tolerance costs. If a society extends unlimited tolerance even to the intolerant and declines to defend itself, the intolerant will eventually destroy the tolerant society, and tolerance along with it. A tolerance that never draws a line is self-defeating: it hands its own abolition to the very people who would use the freedom it grants in order to take that freedom away. So we are entitled, in the name of tolerance itself, to refuse tolerance to the intolerant — though Popper stresses this is a last resort. We should first try to answer intolerant movements with rational argument and public opinion, and withhold tolerance only from those who refuse to argue at all and answer criticism with force.

Rawls comes at the same problem from the other direction and arrives at the same place by a longer route. In the original position nobody knows their own comprehensive doctrine, so nobody could choose a principle letting a majority suppress doctrines it merely dislikes; equal liberty of conscience follows. But Rawls recognizes that a just society may face sects whose aims are themselves intolerant, and here he agrees with Popper: the tolerant are not obliged to let such groups destroy the institutions of justice, and where there is a genuine danger to those institutions the sect's liberty may be restricted. The difference is in the foundation, not the conclusion. Popper argues from consequences — tolerance is a practice, and a practice can fail, and a failed practice protects nothing — so his limit is prudential and open-ended, judged case by case. Rawls argues from right, so his limit is fixed by the principles of justice and can never be wider than the danger requires.

That is also why Rawls's version resists the obvious abuse. Popper's leaves the door open to anyone claiming to defend tolerance while really defending their own position. Rawls's does not, because the original position fixes a single standard that applies equally to the intolerant and to those who would suppress them.

So the paradox of tolerance is not a logical paradox of the kind the liar paradox is. Nothing in the concept of tolerance is contradictory; it's a practical problem about how a tolerant order keeps itself in existence. And both authors solve it the same way: tolerance is limited at exactly the point where its own continuation is threatened.
