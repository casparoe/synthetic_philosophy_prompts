You are a senior scholar of comparative memory studies who has reviewed many fellowship applications and chaired dissertation committees. Think carefully before drafting: first sketch two or three ways my research question could be sharpened or reframed, say which you recommend and why, and only then write the proposal.

The background, briefly. I grew up in a mid-sized city in the American South and went home two years ago to help clear out my grandmother's house, arriving in the middle of the fight over the monument on the courthouse lawn. What stayed with me was not the removal but the aftermath: the bare plinth became a place where people left flowers, notes, and eventually paint, and within a year the council had quietly repaved the whole square. On the same trip I learned that the brass cobblestones set into German sidewalks outside victims' former homes are still scrubbed clean by neighbours and school classes decades after being laid.

My hunch, which may be wrong: the field largely measures memory regimes by official acknowledgment — did the state apologize, what do the textbooks say — but durability may instead turn on how widely the *labour of carrying* a memory is distributed. Dispersed, cheap-to-maintain, redundantly reproduced carriers survive hostile successor governments; monumental, state-funded, single-site memory is easy for citizens to ignore and easy for a new administration to defund or reinterpret.

I need a two-page proposal for a nine-month residential fellowship containing: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer rather than a mood; why the standard framing (Germany did Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan and Turkey did not, the American South is only beginning) treats memory as political will and so cannot explain why acknowledgment persists or collapses; the method, and why conceptual and case-based work rather than survey data or straight historiography; what a useful deliverable looks like for people deciding what to build after a monument comes down; what would count as failure — for instance, if carrying-labour turns out to be a redescription of state willpower with no independent explanatory purchase; and an honest scope assessment.

Take one complication seriously rather than dropping it: Turkey is hard for my own view, since there the state's labour goes into preventing carriers from forming at all. Address how the project handles enforced non-commemoration. No bibliography or citation list, please; I will add those myself.
