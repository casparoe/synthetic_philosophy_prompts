American growers who plant Bt maize or cotton must devote a share of their acreage to a refuge of ordinary crop that the target insects are simply allowed to eat. The stated rationale is genetic rather than moral: resistance is assumed to be recessive, and the engineered plant assumed to express a dose high enough to kill heterozygotes, so a standing population of susceptible insects will mate with the rare resistant survivors and keep the trait from spreading. Please answer in British English.

I would like to know what theory of value this practice presupposes. Structurally it is the mandated preservation of a population, secured by signed grower agreements rather than by exhortation, and yet no one describes it as conservation, and the insects enter the justification nowhere except as a reservoir of alleles for future harvests — while the same regulatory regime elsewhere counts non-target insect death with great care, restricting chemistry that threatens pollinators and aquatic invertebrates. Does the refuge requirement strictly presuppose that pest insects have no moral standing at all, or could a view on which their deaths count but are outweighed license it just as comfortably? Does the mechanism, which depends on random mating across a whole region, also presuppose that the individual farm is the wrong unit of agency and that growers cannot be trusted to do voluntarily what is collectively required — and if so, what does that make of the same regime's reliance on voluntary uptake for nearly everything else, and of the later shift to refuge blended into the seed bag, which was adopted chiefly because compliance with block refuges had collapsed? Which rival theories of value would license the practice unchanged, and what would have to be redesigned — the refuge, the crop technology, or the justification offered — if insect deaths were admitted to the ledger as a cost rather than a nullity, given that a refuge deliberately increases the number of larvae alive to be killed?
