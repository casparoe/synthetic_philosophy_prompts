I'm a junior, and I help run the philosophy club at my high school. At our last meeting two members, Nadia and Omar, got into an argument that they asked me to referee. I couldn't settle it, and I'm not looking for a survey of who has written about this — no reading lists or citations, please, since I can't check them anyway. I want your own careful adjudication of the arguments exactly as they made them.

The case they were arguing over concerns a student at our school, whom I'll call Theo. Theo played cello seriously all through middle school, well enough that he could have auditioned for the regional youth orchestra, and then stopped completely two years ago. He doesn't dislike the instrument or resent it; he simply no longer wants anything to do with it, and he now spends nearly all his free time rebuilding a motorcycle in his family's garage. His older sister keeps telling him that he has a reason to start again and that he'll be glad in ten years that he did. Theo says he has no such reason, and that all she's really saying is that she wants him to play.

Nadia, arguing from a broadly Williams-style internalist line, and Omar, arguing against it, went back and forth like this:

(1) Nadia: saying that someone has a reason to do something has to be able to explain and justify that person's action. Theo has a reason to go back to the cello only if he could be brought to do it by reasoning that starts from what he already cares about. No such route exists, so his sister's claim is either false or a disguised report of her own preferences.

(2) Omar: that makes reasons far too cheap. Suppose Theo had a standing desire to humiliate a younger student in his chemistry class. On Nadia's account, that desire generates a reason for him, and she's left unable to say he has more reason not to. Internalism can serve a motivation but never criticize one.

(3) Nadia: that misdescribes the view. The starting point isn't a list of raw desires. It includes Theo's loyalties, his dispositions to admire particular people, his capacity to be moved by imagining a situation vividly and by correcting his own false beliefs. Reasoning from that whole set, the humiliation desire wouldn't survive — he'd picture the younger kid's face, and see himself the way his own older sister sees him. The cello case is different in kind: nothing in the set gets any grip on it.

(4) Omar: that concession is the collapse. If the deliberative route may revise, reorder, and extinguish motivations as freely as you just did, then almost any conclusion is reachable from almost any starting point, and internalism stops constraining anything. Either the route is tightly limited, in which case you keep the ugly result that vicious motivations do yield reasons, or it's loose, in which case you've quietly imported an external standard saying which revisions count as improvements — and you're an externalist.

(5) Nadia: externalism has a problem of its own. If Theo has a reason that could not in principle move him by any reasoning he would recognize as reasoning, then calling it a reason does no explanatory work whatsoever. A reason that is permanently inert with respect to the agent isn't a reason at all; it's just a fact that Omar approves of.

(6) Omar: the inertness is the whole point. When we tell Theo he's making a mistake we don't first check whether he can be moved. The reason is there whether or not he can see it, and his inability to see it is a defect in him, not evidence that there's nothing to see.

Near the end Nadia allowed one thing: if Theo picked the cello back up at twenty-eight and thanked his sister, that would count for something — but she said it would only show the reason was latent all along, meaning a route existed from the set he had then. Omar said that was redescription, since the gratitude arrives after the relevant desire is long gone, so the reason can't have depended on the desire.

Please take the six moves in order, and then the exchange about the later gratitude, and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating rather than a general verdict. Then tell me who is more right overall. Two specific questions I'd like answered directly: does move (4) actually work as a dilemma, or is there a principled middle position available to Nadia that Omar hasn't ruled out? And is there something both of them are treating as settled that shouldn't be — for example, whether they are even disagreeing about the same thing when they say someone has a reason? If you think one of them wins partly on a technicality, say so and explain what the technicality costs.
