I sit on my university's research integrity panel — seven of us, mostly faculty plus one student rep — and the chair has asked me to circulate a written recommendation before we meet on the 14th. For background, I'm a cell biologist, not a philosopher; the one research-ethics course I took in grad school left me more confused than I started, so I'm kind of winging this. Here's the mess. A neuroscience grad student (I'll call him D) is charged with fabricating values in two figures of a paper he submitted. The complainant is a senior professor, K, and honestly her evidence is strong — she has the raw instrument files and D's numbers just dont come out of them. The problem: eleven years ago K was herself the subject of a misconduct finding over exactly this kind of thing, doctored image intensities, and the university settled it quietly. No retraction, no dismissal; she stayed and later got an endowed chair. The settlement had a confidentiality clause, but D dug it up anyway through a records request to a state office, and his response to the panel is essentially that K has no standing to accuse him of anything, and that it would be grotesque for this university to expel him for the very thing it let her build the rest of her career on. Our general counsel then sent a terse email saying the panel shouldn't touch the settlement file at all.

Two of my colleagues want to dismiss the case against D outright on the standing ground. Two more want to admit K's history and cut D's sanction down to reflect the inconsistency. The rest of us think the whole line is a tu quoque dodge but nobody can articulate why, and I keep finding I'm not so sure — there's something in D's complaint that feels real, and I can't tell whether it's about whether the charge is true, or about who gets to bring it, or about the university being the hypocrite rather than K personally. Also unresolved procedurally: our charter says a finding has to rest on material properly before the panel, and it's genuinely unclear whether the leaked settlement counts.

Could you draft the memo I'll send around? I need it to land on a concrete disposition — whether we proceed against D, what happens to K's record and to K herself, whose name the finding issues in, and on what timetable, since D's funding runs out at the end of term and the journal is asking us for a status update. Take D's objection seriously rather than swatting it as a fallacy, and give the strongest honest case for whichever side you come out against — a real argument, not a strawman paragraph I can see through. Something the other six can read in ten minutes over coffee. Lead with the bottom line in your very first sentence, the actual recommendation so nobody has to hunt for it, then the reasoning; no preamble and no restating my situation back to me.
