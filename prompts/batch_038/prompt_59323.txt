Our history-and-philosophy-of-computing reading group spent last month working through Turing's 1950 *Mind* paper line by line, and I have been unable to let go of the final item in his catalogue of objections — the argument from extrasensory perception. Everyone in the group treated it the way it is usually treated: as an embarrassing biographical artefact, the moment where the father of computer science lets his interest in psychical research leak into an otherwise careful paper. But I want to build a dissertation project out of the opposite reading, and I need it turned into a proper proposal for a twelve-month fellowship from a foundation that funds work at the boundary of intellectual history and philosophy of science. The half-formed version of my idea: this is the only place in the paper where Turing does not reformulate a concept but specifies a *control on the experimental situation*. He writes that the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," that "this argument is to my mind quite a strong one," and then, rather than abandoning the imitation game, proposes a repair — "If telepathy is admitted it will be necessary to tighten our test up. The situation could be regarded as analogous to that which would occur if the interrogator were talking to himself and one of the competitors was listening with his ear to the wall. To put the competitors into a 'telepathy-proof room' would satisfy all requirements." My suggestion is that this reveals what the imitation game was always supposed to be: not a measurement of an intrinsic property of a system but a claim about what an interrogator is entitled to infer from an interaction conducted under a specified isolation protocol. If that is right, the ESP section is not a detour from the paper's argument; it is the place where its evidential structure is made explicit, and it does more work than the more celebrated passages.

The second half of the idea, which is where I most need your help thinking about scope, is that the same structure has reappeared in a form nobody expected. Contemporary evaluation of language models turns almost entirely on isolation protocols — sealed held-out sets, contamination audits, worries about whether a system that answers correctly has inferred or merely retrieved — and these are recognisably the telepathy-proof room wearing different clothes. There is also a detail in Turing's passage that I think is doing unappreciated work and that a good proposal has to confront rather than wave away: having sketched a card-guessing version of the game in which a telepathic human beats a machine, Turing notes that if the machine answers by means of a random number generator, then "the random number generator will be subject to the psychokinetic powers of the interrogator," so the evaluator might bias the very sampling process that produces the answers. That is a worry about the direction of influence running from interrogator to system, whereas contamination worries run the other way, from the world's data into the system; and I genuinely do not yet know whether that asymmetry strengthens my reading or quietly destroys it, since one could argue that Turing's point collapses as soon as you notice that the two kinds of leakage call for different controls. The proposal should not pretend that question is settled.

Please write the proposal itself, roughly 1,300 words and in two pages' worth of prose. I need: a statement of the research question precise enough that someone could disagree with my answer to it; an account of why the three available treatments — the historians who psychologise the passage, the philosophers of mind who quietly omit it from their reconstructions of the test, and the AI practitioners who quote the telepathy-proof room as trivia — each leave the question open rather than answering it; a defence of the method (why a close reading plus a structured comparison with current evaluation practice is the right instrument here, rather than archival work on Turing's relationship to psychical research, which I have neither the time nor the languages for, and rather than a purely conceptual analysis that ignores the 1950 context); a month-by-month plan for twelve months with explicit decision points; a description of what a successful deliverable looks like — I am imagining two articles and a jointly written position piece aimed at people who actually design benchmarks — and what would count as the project failing, including the specific failure mode in which the historical case and the modern one turn out to be analogous only superficially. Finally, the foundation always asks about wider significance, so include a short paragraph that answers why a funding body should care about the epistemology of a seventy-five-year-old party game, without overselling it. Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation — I will supply the references myself — but you may and should name positions, arguments, and standard objections.
