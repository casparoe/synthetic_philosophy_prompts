For thirty-one years I was the cataloguer at a county historical society, and for roughly twenty of those I was the only one. Our photograph collection is mostly industrial: canneries, a shipyard, rail repair shops, the plants that closed one after another through the eighties. When the standard subject headings proved a poor fit for material this local and this narrow, we invented our own list of about sixty terms, and I applied it. No definitions were ever written down. The collection itself — some eleven thousand finished records — was the definition.

I retired in the spring and came back in the autumn on a small stipend to help clear a backlog. Over the summer two part-time volunteers worked through a large accession: several boxes of photographs taken in the mid-nineties by a local newspaper photographer. A great many show the old plants after conversion — the cannery is now a maritime museum, the repair shops house a brewery and a Saturday market. The buildings, machinery, and signage are all still there, but arranged for visitors.

One volunteer tagged most of these with our term for a commemorative or heritage subject rather than our term for an operating industrial site. That is not how I would have done it, and I feel certain it is not how I would have done it: I would have kept them as industrial sites, because that is what is pictured. But here is the difficulty I cannot get past. I have gone back through my own records, including the ones I remember as hard calls, and I cannot find a single photograph I tagged that she tagged otherwise. Wherever the two collections overlap, our practice agrees completely, odd cases included. The divergence appears only on material of a kind I never handled, because nothing like it arrived while I was doing the work. The other volunteer agrees with her, and neither of them can articulate a principle either; they simply find the tagging obvious.

I have tried several times to write the distinction down. Every version fails in one direction or the other: it excludes records I am sure I made correctly, or it permits what she did. And something else unsettles me further. If you had asked me in 1997 how to tag a photograph of a cannery turned into a museum, I honestly do not know what I would have said. It is not that I had an answer I have since misplaced; it does not seem as though there was ever anything in my head on the point at all.

I would like your assessment of three things. First, is there any fact of the matter here, or is my conviction that she has made a mistake simply a preference for my own habits dressed up as correctness? Second, if there is such a fact, what is it made of? It is evidently not in the records, since they side with her everywhere they speak at all, and on my own admission it was not in my head either — yet when I look at one of her entries I experience it as an error, not as an alternative. Third, what should I do about it over the coming months, given that six more boxes of the same accession are waiting and I would rather not spend them litigating this with a volunteer? In particular I want to know whether leaving it to her is a defensible option rather than a dereliction, whether there is any way to settle the question better than by my say-so, and whether turning to the practice of larger institutions handling comparable material would genuinely resolve things or only cover over the difficulty with someone else's vocabulary.
