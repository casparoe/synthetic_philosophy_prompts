For the past year I have been sitting in on a reading group on moral responsibility at my university, run by a senior colleague who is writing a book about blame. She has a habit of settling disputes by pronouncing on invented cases, and because I hope to write something on the standing to blame myself, I have been keeping a record of her verdicts on the hypocrisy cases in particular. Here is that record, in the order she gave them, together with the reasons she offered when she offered any:

1. A man who has been unfaithful to his wife for years discovers that his brother is having an affair and delivers a sustained condemnation of it; when his brother points at the parallel, he insists the two situations are nothing alike. Verdict: no standing. The condemnation may be perfectly accurate, but he is not in a position to make it.
2. The same man, except that before saying anything he tells his brother that he has done the same thing, has not stopped, and is not going to offer excuses. Verdict: standing substantially restored — "he isn't claiming anything he doesn't have."
3. A club treasurer who is quietly and currently embezzling, and who at a committee meeting argues forcefully that another member should resign over a small unexplained shortfall. He never excuses himself in his own mind and is privately miserable about what he is doing. Verdict: standing intact but impaired; her phrase was something like "he can say it, but he can't say it from where he is."
4. A stranger on a train, with no comparable fault of his own, who correctly rebukes a mother for striking her child. Verdict: he may intervene and he may think it, but he is not in a position to blame, because nothing connects him to either of them. I confess I expected her to allow this one.
5. A mother who lies constantly and punishes her son for lying. Verdict: standing intact, on the ground that a parent's authority to enforce a rule does not depend on her own conformity to it — though the son's resentment is legitimate and she owes him an account of herself.
6. A woman who engineered the situation in which her friend betrayed a third party, and who then joins loudly in the blaming of that friend. Verdict: no standing, and she treats this as worse than ordinary hypocrisy rather than as a species of it.
7. A man who bitterly resents his wife for a fault he shares but never says anything to her about it. Verdict: standing is not even the right question here, since on her view standing attaches to blame addressed to a person, not to the attitude sitting in the head.

Her new case, which she has told us will close the book and on which she has not yet ruled in front of anyone: a department chair who for several years billed personal travel to a grant, was never caught, quietly repaid the money out of her own pocket four years ago, told no one, and has genuinely stopped. She must now write the official letter responding to a graduate student's falsified notebook entries. Her draft includes an acknowledgment that she is not writing from a position of innocence, that she misused grant money herself and has made it right, and that she raises this not to set her situation beside the student's but because he needs to hear it from someone who still remembers how the rationalizing goes. She believes the disclosure is true and useful. She also knows — and this is the feature she flagged for us — that the disclosure will lead the sanctions committee to see any serious penalty as disproportionate, and that she chose the wording partly for that reason.

What I want is a prediction together with the reasoning behind it. Which principles of standing remain live candidates once (1) through (7) are on the table? I can see at least a comparative-fairness principle (you lose standing by claiming a record you do not have), a dispositional-consistency principle (you lose it by being disposed to condemn in others what you are disposed to excuse in yourself and your own), a bare-conduct principle (you lose it by violating the norm you invoke, whatever you say about yourself), a norm-commitment principle (your blame must express a commitment rather than deploy one), and something about relationship or role that cuts across all of these; I may be missing others, and I suspect that a couple of the ones I have listed are not genuinely distinct from each other. For each surviving candidate, say what it predicts about the chair, and tell me where (3) and (5) sit awkwardly with it — those two are the ones I cannot fit. Then give me your best guess at her verdict and your reasons for preferring it: I would have said the chair keeps her standing, since she looks like case (2) with restitution added and a role that obliges her to write the letter at all, but I cannot tell whether my colleague will treat the strategic element in the wording as fatal, given how sharply she handled (6). Finally, does a partly instrumental motive bear on standing at all, or only on the character of an act she performs while standing fully intact, and what single further case, added to her list, would most efficiently separate the principles that disagree about her? If you can tie any of the candidate principles to a position actually defended in the literature on standing to blame, please do, but only where you are confident of the attribution; the logic of the rules matters considerably more to me than the names attached to them.
