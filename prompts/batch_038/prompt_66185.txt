Longtime LW/AF reader here — I've been following the alignment stuff since the sequences and I run a small reading group in my city. For a talk I'm giving next month I did the thing people always recommend and wrote down everything I actually believe about the shutdown problem, in one place, no hedging. Looking at the list it feels... less coherent than it did in my head, and I genuinely can't tell which parts are load-bearing and which are just vibes I picked up from comment threads. So: please act as a technical alignment researcher with a professional philosopher's habits — someone who's good at rational reconstruction and at noticing when two plausible-sounding theses have incompatible motivations. Audit the set, don't lecture me. I know the source material well, so skip the citations and don't re-explain what corrigibility is or why a naive maximizer disables its off switch; assume I'm fluent and think carefully rather than fast. A good first move would probably be to restate each item in its sharpest one-line form, because I suspect half my trouble is that I've stated things at wildly different levels of precision, and some of these only collide once you pin down what exactly is being claimed about what.

Here's the list:
(a) Any agent capable enough to be dangerous has convergent instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, goal-content integrity, resource acquisition. I basically treat this as analytic: it falls out of being an optimizer with nearly any terminal value.
(b) Which is why I don't think the shutdown problem gets solved by picking a nicer goal. Paperclips, human flourishing, "the operator is satisfied with how things turned out" — doesn't matter, as long as the value is a function of external world-states, being switched off is instrumentally bad. What has to change is the structure of the decision procedure, not the content of the goal.
(c) The structure I favor is uncertainty about its own utility function: the agent treats the operator's behavior, including pressing the button, as evidence about what the true objective is. On the off-switch-game style analysis, an agent with the right kind of uncertainty is at least willing to be shut down, since the press is informative.
(d) Corrigibility is about dispositions, not about winning a fight. The agent mustn't disable the mechanism, mustn't manipulate the operator into or out of pressing it, and should positively want the button to exist and to work. If we can only shut it down by yanking the power while it's trying to talk us out of it, we've failed even if we win.
(e) But also: a single agent that lets itself be switched off at the first sign of trouble is useless, so when it can it should build successors that carry the mission forward, better designed than it was. Fine by me as long as the successors are corrigible too.
(f) Independently of all that I like low impact — the agent should avoid big irreversible changes, scored against a baseline where it did nothing, or where the button got pressed. I admit I waffle on which baseline and I've never been able to say why.
(g) I want deployed agents to fight back against shutdown attempts by anyone who isn't the authorized operator. Rogue employee, competitor, some state actor — resisting them seems obviously right and I don't think it clashes with (d).
(h) And whatever fix we use should be behavior-preserving: pressing the button should be the *only* thing that changes about how the agent acts. I don't want a corrigibility module that makes the agent dumber, more cautious, or weirdly deferential in every other situation, because then we've just traded one problem for a different one.

Concrete case, because I think my principles can't handle it and I want to know if I'm right about that. Operator presses the button because the budget got cut and the project is being wound down — no judgment about the agent's goals at all. Versus: operator presses the button because a third party is threatening their family. I'm confident the agent should accept the first press without any fuss and pretty confident it should *not* just accept the second one, but as far as I can tell (c) gives me the same verdict in both, since in both cases the press is a press. Tell me whether that's actually true or whether I'm misreading my own view.

What I want back: the tensions, ordered by severity, not all listed as equally bad. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most damage across the whole set, and whether the resulting view is still recognizably the view I started with or something I'd have to argue myself into. Be brutal and specific; if some item is just confused rather than in tension, say that instead of inventing a tension for it.
