A friend and I spent most of an evening on money pump arguments, and I want a case that isolates the point we could not settle. My position was that the pump argument for transitivity has no force against a reflective agent: anyone who can see the sequence coming simply declines the first trade, and that ends the matter. Hers was that this defence is less general than I supposed, because it quietly assumes the agent is one continuous evaluator from the first offer to the last. Neither of us could build a case that decided it.

Construct a sequential choice problem to the following specification. The agent finishes exactly where she began in terms of goods but with strictly less money — a pump in the ledger, with all sums counted at face value and no discounting in the final tally. Yet: (i) her preferences are complete and transitive at every moment of the sequence, and they never shift in a way she fails to anticipate; (ii) she is fully informed about every offer and correctly predicts, at each node, what she will choose at each later node; (iii) at each node she chooses what she then prefers, and is never weak-willed, startled, or muddled; and (iv) the pump runs regardless. The mechanism may not be intransitivity, and it may not be any failure of foresight. It has to arise from the structure of her agency across time — the cost or unavailability of commitment, a change in what she cares about, an asymmetry in how she weighs earlier and later standpoints, an evaluation that does not aggregate over her own future choices — and the scenario should make explicit which of these it is.

Constraints: an ordinary commercial setting with modest sums (a dealer, a collector, an equipment hire firm), no science-fictional machinery, no one with prophetic knowledge of the market, no bets turning on improbable states of the world, at most four decision nodes and three goods besides money, and the scenario itself in one or two paragraphs.

Then show that the design works. State her ordering at each node, walk the sequence from first offer to last, and verify (i) through (iv) one by one, saying where in the case each is satisfied. Since the point is to settle my argument with my friend, the case fails if the verdict is stipulated rather than generated: an agent who is merely myopic, or merely intransitive, or who is merely offered a package she turns down, does not count. Finish by naming the single feature doing the work, saying whether the pump still closes once that feature is removed, giving the best reply available to someone on my side of the argument — that foresight is a complete answer to any pump — and telling me candidly whether what you have built refutes that claim or only relocates the problem from her preferences to her unity as an agent over time.
