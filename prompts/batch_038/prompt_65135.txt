I direct the privacy and information-release office of a nine-site regional hospital network. Between bedside nurses, registration clerks, and the switchboard, my staff field roughly forty external requests a week for information about identifiable patients — from relatives, employers, journalists, police without process, and strangers claiming to be family of a victim. Each request is decided in under two minutes by someone with no legal or philosophical training, and the decision must afterwards survive three audiences: the patient, our general counsel, and the ethics committee that reviews our disclosure log quarterly. Our current guidance is one consequentialist line — "disclose only what is necessary and never mislead" — and it collapses precisely where it matters, because "never mislead" condemns both the false denial and the true refusal to answer. My staff have quietly resolved that ambiguity in whatever direction feels kindest at the time.

I want to replace it with a decision procedure built on Augustine's analysis of lying in *De mendacio* and in the later treatise written in response to inquiries about whether Catholics might feign agreement with the Priscillianists in order to catch them. Not because my staff are religious — most are not — but because his apparatus has three features our guidance lacks, and I want each operationalized. I take his core claim to be that one may conceal the truth but may never assert a falsehood; correct me if that misstates him, since the whole procedure turns on it.

1. He defines a lie by the relation between what the speaker holds in mind and what the utterance signifies, not by the relation between the utterance and the facts. State plainly what that buys us and what it costs: a clerk who repeats a data-entry error she has no reason to doubt has not lied, while a nurse who says what she believes false and happens to be right has. If you keep that criterion, tell me how a supervisor is supposed to establish, after the fact and under audit, what a staff member held in mind — and if that is impossible, say whether the criterion must give way to a belief-plus-record test.

2. He excludes jokes and other non-assertive speech because they carry their own signal that no deceit is intended. I need the institutional analogue: which of our scripted utterances count as assertions and which as signals — a refusal, an "I can't discuss any patient," a transferred call, a deliberately flat tone, a form letter — and whether a scripted line read aloud by someone who does not believe it signifies that person's mind at all.

3. He ranks lies by gravity, with lying in religious teaching at the top and lies that harm no one and save someone from death or from violation at the bottom, while refusing to make any grade lawful. Transpose the ranking onto our setting, and say which grades survive the removal of his theological premises (speech as having a God-given function it violates; the double heart as a defect of the soul) and which quietly stop doing work once those premises are gone.

Deliver a numbered procedure my supervisors can run in the two minutes available: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which it outputs "give the true answer," "withhold without asserting," or "stop and escalate to counsel or the ethics line," and the specific steps where the appearance of a test conceals a judgment call. Then run it on three of our recurring patterns: (a) a man who supplies correct identifying details and asks whether his wife is in the emergency department, where her file carries a domestic-violence safety flag; (b) a plainclothes officer, no warrant, asking whether we are treating a stabbing victim matching a description; (c) a records clerk who answers a family member from an index containing an error she has no reason to suspect. For at least one of these, the procedure should require my staff to do something I believe is worse for the patient than the lie they were about to tell — do not soften that result, and then say what the institution may do about it short of authorizing false statements. Close with what the procedure cannot do: the cases where his analysis gives no purchase, and the places where our legal duty to disclose forces an assertion the procedure forbids withholding.

No citations, bibliography, or reading list — I want the reasoning, not references. Where you are unsure what Augustine actually holds, flag it as an interpretive risk inside the procedure rather than settling it silently.

End with a single line applying your procedure to case (c), in exactly this form:

Verdict: <LIE | NOT-A-LIE | CONCEAL-PERMITTED | ESCALATE> — <one clause, twelve words or fewer>
