Last night a friend and I spent four hours arguing about democratic backsliding and got nowhere, and I have come to suspect that the nowhere is the interesting part. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not an adjudication of who is right about the cases.

Here is roughly the terrain. The examples we kept circling were Hungary after Fidesz won a two-thirds majority in 2010 and the new constitution that replaced the 1949 text, Poland after 2015 and the fight over its Constitutional Tribunal, Turkey after the 2017 referendum converted it to a presidential system, and the United States after the 2020 election and the January 6 attack, along with the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022. One camp says these are instances of a single pattern: incumbents using lawful means to entrench themselves until the electoral playing field is irreversibly tilted. The other camp says the label is doing partisan work — that Hungary still holds competitive elections, that Poland's 2023 change of government shows the system self-correcting, that Turkey's opposition still wins major cities, and that applying the term to the United States stretches it until it means little more than losing.

Now my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. I think the impasse arises because a backsliding claim bundles three separable assertions that are almost never pulled apart: a directional one, that a country is moving down some scale; a threshold one, that it has changed status and crossed out of democracy; and an agential one, that some identifiable actor brought this about deliberately rather than it being structural drift. My reading of last night is that one of us was asserting a directional claim while the other was denying a threshold claim, and each heard the other as making the claim they were not making. If so, the stalemate is partly an artifact of a single overloaded word rather than a genuine conflict about the world.

My friend has a different diagnosis. She thinks the dispute is operational: that it is really about which measure governs — V-Dem's liberal democracy index, Freedom House's aggregate score, or a bare electoral index — and that once a measure is fixed, what remains is an ordinary empirical disagreement about trend lines that better data will settle.

Between those two, and any third diagnosis you find more convincing, which is it? Is the disagreement verbal; is it about vagueness and sorites-style boundaries in the concept of democracy; is it about baselines, meaning whether a country is scored against its own past, against its peers, or against an ideal; is it about the concept's function, descriptive versus evaluative; is it a hidden empirical question; or is it a hard but substantive disagreement that is simply proceeding slowly and normally? Ground the answer in what each side actually says about cases like these rather than in a survey of the literature, and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, an operational test, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected.

Two requests about how you answer. Be committed: choose a diagnosis and defend it, rather than setting out the options with their respective merits and leaving the choice to me, since I have already had that version of the evening. And identify the weakest step in your own diagnosis, together with the case or evidence that would most likely undermine it.

Finish with a final line in exactly this form: Verdict: <one short label naming the diagnosis, drawn from the list above or, if you prefer your own, explicitly coined>.
