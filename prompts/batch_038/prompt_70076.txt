In November I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on religion and economic development, hosted by an economic history group at a European university. The room will be small — perhaps twenty-five people — but the format is forty minutes of talk followed by forty of open discussion, which is unusually generous and therefore unusually dangerous, and the paper grew out of a reading group that went badly enough that I want to be properly armed this time. My paper takes a philosopher's line on the century-old Protestant ethic controversy, and its thesis is deliberately unfriendly to both camps. I argue that the thesis as it has actually been disputed since 1905 is not the sort of claim that any of the evidence marshalled for or against it could have confirmed or refuted — neither the mid-century archival and statistical objections, nor the modern identification strategies — because making it measurable always requires translating notions such as spirit, calling, and this-worldly asceticism into variables, and those translation decisions are themselves contested interpretations rather than neutral operationalizations. The same body of evidence is read as vindicating Weber by one side and burying him by the other, and that is not a disagreement about the evidence. I then argue that this is a defect rather than a mark of interpretive depth: the usual defenses — that the spirit of capitalism was offered as an ideal type and never as a variable, that Weber insisted he was not proposing a one-sided spiritual account to replace a one-sided materialist one, that his procedure was one of objective possibility and counterfactual comparison rather than subsumption under laws — do not answer the charge, they merely relocate it from the hypothesis to the interpretation of the hypothesis. I close with what I take to be the real payoff: the general question of whether culture can be a cause should be detached from this case altogether, because culture-as-cause claims do become testable once they are stated about a particular local belief with a specifiable mechanism, and the fact that the field has produced exactly such claims is what makes the contrast visible.

My argument proceeds in three moves, and I expect the objections to track them. The first is the translation problem sketched above. The second is what I call the retreat problem: every empirical failure has historically been met by narrowing the thesis — Calvinists rather than Protestants generally, the first generation of converts rather than their descendants, saving behaviour and occupational choice rather than aggregate growth, ascetic sect discipline rather than doctrine as such — and I want to claim that this narrowing is a sliding explanandum rather than ordinary refinement, on the grounds that the narrowing is selected in response to the failure and no version of the thesis has ever been exposed to risk in advance of the data. The third is an asymmetry: when the thesis is narrowed until it acquires a mechanism one can actually measure — literacy and schooling, access to print, the organizational form of the congregation — it becomes testable, but what remains testable is a claim about schooling and institutions rather than about an ethic, and the results in the literature that most people now find convincing, including designs that instrument the spread of the Reformation by distance from Wittenberg, belong to that category.

I need you to describe the room for me, because the same thesis draws completely different fire from different quarters, and I would rather be blindsided now than in person. There will be two or three quantitative economic historians of early modern Europe who have never met the word untestable that they did not regard as an admission of never having looked at the data; they will point out that the thesis has in fact been tested many times, that Protestant adoption shows no measurable effect on city growth in some settings and that income gaps show up through a schooling channel in others, and they will ask why my philosophical verdict should override a body of results whose proxies — school enrolment, literacy at marriage registration, book inventories, occupational structure, age at apprenticeship — seem to them perfectly adequate translations of the relevant attitudes. There will be at least one historical sociologist who works on Weber's methodology proper, who will insist that I am judging Weber by criteria he explicitly repudiated, that treating an ideal type as a failed variable is a category error, and that my reading flattens a deliberately counterfactual comparative procedure into a causal hypothesis. There will be one or two philosophers of social science who are sympathetic to interpretivist accounts of action explanation and to the view that reasons can be causes, and who will press me on whether untestable is doing any work in my argument beyond expressing disapproval, whether my own criterion of testability presupposes a variable-based or covering-law model of explanation that I have not argued for, and whether my third move is self-defeating: if the only testable version of the thesis is no longer Weberian, then what I have shown may be that culture cannot be a cause after all, with schooling and institutions doing all the work. And there will be graduate students who will want to know what any of this implies for the papers currently being written that use religion as an explanatory variable.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the paper. Give me roughly nine or ten objections in the order they will actually arrive, weighting how likely each is against how much damage it would do, and phrase each the way that particular subgroup would phrase it — in the cliometrician's idiom, in the Weber scholar's idiom, in the interpretivist philosopher's idiom — rather than in a neutral register, so that I can recognize them when they come. For each objection, give me a reply that could be delivered in about two minutes, mark whether it is a reply or a concession I should have made in the first five minutes of the talk, and say plainly where the reply is softest. Then tell me which two or three of them I genuinely lose if pressed hard and should design the talk to defuse in advance, and which ones I can afford to let slide. Finally, identify the question I will be hoping nobody asks — I suspect it involves turning my own diagnosis back on my critique, since a claim about what the literature has and has not tested is itself an interpretive claim resting on contested readings, but tell me if there is a worse one. Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I know the literature and I would rather you flag uncertainty than invent references. Relatedly, I need you to be explicit about the epistemic status of what you tell me: separate what is actually settled among people who work on this material — for instance, on what the empirical results do and do not show, and on what Weber himself claimed — from what remains genuinely contested, including contested within Weber scholarship, and do not let a disputed reading of the ideal type, or of the scope of the original thesis, or of the verdict on the identification work, come through as though it were the consensus view. Where your characterization of a position depends on a contested interpretation, say so in a line so that I know not to assert it from the podium as fact.
