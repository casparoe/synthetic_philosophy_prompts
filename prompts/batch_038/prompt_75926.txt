I'm putting together a problem set for a small grad seminar on truthlikeness and I need a thought experiment I can't just lift out of the usual places. The students already have the standard story down cold: Popper's original definition, the Tichý–Miller refutation, and then the split into the two families that grew up afterward -- the content/consequence-side accounts (roughly Oddie's likeness approach) and the similarity-or-distance accounts (roughly Niiniluoto's metrics). What they dont get is that the two families can actually disagree about a concrete case, rather than just describing the same ordering in different notation. So build me a case where they disagree for real.

The specs:

- Two theories, both false, about the same ordinary domain -- something a smart non-specialist can follow with zero setup. No physics, no technical vocabulary. Think along the lines of why a crop failed, what's getting into a town's water supply, or why one particular stretch of road keeps icing up when the rest doesn't.
- Each theory has to be stateable as a short list of plain claims. Give me the lists.
- One account scores them with a true/false content ledger over a fixed finite set of relevant claims. Pick the ledger rule and state it explicitly before you use it.
- The other scores them by distance from the actual world, cashed out as a weighted sum over respects of similarity. State the respects and state the weights out loud -- dont bury them.
- The two accounts must rank the pair in opposite orders, and the flip has to fall out of the machinery you just stated. No stipulating verdicts and hoping I don't check.
- The flip must not come from any of the usual escape hatches: not gerrymandered or artificially conjunctive predicates, not one theory merely being logically stronger than the other, not a language with a lopsided number of atomic sentences, and not the ledger being sensitive to which seperate claim-set you happened to write down. On that last one -- if your ledger is sensitive to it, say so, and then show me that some other equally reasonable claim-set doesn't quietly restore agreement.
- Something has to hang on the ranking. Someone needs to act, and acting on the wrong-ranked theory costs them something concrete. Keep it realistic; no life-or-death theatrics.

Then the write-up:

1. Run both calculations in full, showing your work.
2. Name the single feature of the case doing the work. One feature, not a cluster of three with one doing most of it.
3. Delete that feature and show what happens. Do the accounts snap back into agreement? If they do, does that mean the original disagreement was substantive or just an artifact of how you rigged the setup?
4. Give the strongest response available to a defender of the distance account -- I assume it's reweighting the respects -- and say plainly whether it's ad hoc. Then do the mirror image for the content side.
5. Two sentences on what the case is supposed to teach, and your honest read on whether a determined student could dissolve it in seminar.

And the part I care about most: if you can't make it work, say so. If the only way to get the flip is to break one of my constraints, tell me which one and why you think that constraint is doing too much work. A clean failure is worth more to me here than a case that cheats in a way I have to find myself.
