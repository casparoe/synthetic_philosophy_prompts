I sit in on the weekly deployment reviews at a company that ships an agent built on a general-purpose language model: the model is handed a business objective, decomposes it into subtasks of its own choosing, writes and executes code, calls internal tools, and runs for hours at a time while a human reviews a sampled subset of its actions after the fact. Much of the base model's task competence comes from outcome-based reinforcement learning rather than from step-by-step supervision. Two of our leads argued about how to think about this arrangement, and I was taking notes without being able to tell who was winning.

Lead A said the right mental model is a new hire in their first month: you hand over the objective rather than the procedure, because specifying every step is precisely what forfeits the value of delegating at all; you manage by outcomes and spot checks; and you accept that some of what they try will fail, because the alternative is a very expensive tool that only ever does what you already knew how to do. On this picture, autonomy is not a risk to be minimized but the thing being purchased, and excessive step-level review is micromanagement with extra latency.

Lead B said the analogy is exactly backwards. An intern's judgment is their own: formed outside the organization, containing its own errors, and capable of resisting what you want from them. The model's judgment is fitted to a distribution and a reward that we specified, so what looks like delegation is really amplification — one correlated failure mode, reproduced identically across every instance, at machine speed, with no independent check inside the loop. B added that you can fire an intern and hire someone else, whereas you cannot fire a set of weights: the cost of a bad disposition is sunk and then multiplied by every deployment, and the model lacks the intern's self-interested reason to notice and correct its own mistakes before someone else does.

A rejoined that "their own judgment" is a fiction: a new hire's dispositions were installed by parents, schools, and previous employers, and "independent" simply means "not installed by me" — which is also true of the model, since we did not do the pretraining. A further argued that the amplification objection proves too much, because a franchise's standard operating procedure is likewise one written policy replicated across hundreds of sites, and the remedy is the same in both cases: monitoring, sampling, and the ability to revert.

B's last move was that this concedes the point while dressing it up. A standard operating procedure is written down, inspectable line by line, and revisable by the people who own it; a trained policy is none of those things. So borrowing the trust we extend to a human hire — trust that is licensed by the intern's separate formation, their legible reasoning, and their exposure to being replaced — and transferring it to a system that has none of those properties is how you end up with a review process calibrated for an employee and a system that is not one.

Please audit the analogy rather than the deployment. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. State the mapping compactly, naming which feature of the new-hire picture is being made to stand for which feature of the system — the objective handoff, the formed judgment, the probationary period, the spot checks, the possibility of dismissal, the hire's own interest in doing well.
2. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on whether A's hands-off review policy is warranted from the ones that are rhetorically vivid but decision-irrelevant, and say which of B's three objections fall on which side of that line.
3. Identify what the source case quietly imports that the target lacks — in particular whether correlated error across instances, the absence of a labor market for replacements, and the difference between inspectable procedures and trained policies are being smuggled past by the word "delegation."
4. Give a verdict on the two contested turns: does A's point that a hire's judgment is equally secondhand neutralize B's independence objection, and does A's franchise-SOP comparison succeed in showing that amplification is nothing new?
5. Propose a better analogy for the tool-to-agent transition — the principal-agent framework from contract theory is one candidate, but choose or construct whichever fits best — and say what it preserves of A's legitimate insight that the new-hire picture was tracking.
