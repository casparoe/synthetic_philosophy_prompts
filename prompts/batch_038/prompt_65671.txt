I am trying to work out what I actually believe about whether genuine moral dilemmas exist, by recording my verdicts on concrete cases as honestly as I can. The cases and verdicts are below; what I want back is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures the pattern.

1. A field medic has one dose of an antivenom and two patients who will each die without a full dose. Nothing distinguishes them: same prognosis, same dependents, no prior claim, and a coin flip would be a tiebreaker of convenience rather than a reason. Verdict: a genuine dilemma. Whichever patient she treats, she has done nothing wrong. But something is left over. She owes the other patient's family a truthful account rather than a formula; the distress she feels is fitting rather than excessive; and a colleague who faced the identical choice and reported feeling nothing at all would strike me as morally deficient, though I cannot identify any wrong he committed.

2. A contractor accepted two jobs for the same Saturday and failed to notice the overlap when any reasonable person would have. Only one can now be done. Verdict: not a genuine dilemma. The conflict is an artifact of something he already did wrong, and the only live question is what he ought to do now.

3. Two unlabeled vials; one holds the cure, one holds a fast-acting poison. No test is available and there is no time to obtain one. Verdict: not a genuine dilemma. Exactly one action is right and the agent simply cannot tell which. The conflict is in her evidence, not in the world.

4. Officials tell a schoolteacher that unless she names the student who wrote a banned pamphlet, the entire class will be expelled and their families investigated. She names one. Verdict: a genuine dilemma, and she did nothing wrong, since the alternative was worse and was not of her making. Yet when she says afterward that she has something to atone for, that strikes me as correct self-understanding rather than confusion. I am uneasy here: atonement seems to presuppose a wrong, and I have just denied that she committed one.

5. I promised a friend I would help him move on Saturday. Driving over, I find a car in a ditch with an injured occupant who must be taken to a hospital immediately, which will consume the day. Verdict: not a dilemma. The rescue is required, the promise is overridden, and there is no interesting question about which to do. But my friend is owed a phone call, is entitled to be annoyed, and I would be making a mistake if I said the episode left nothing between us.

6. Two cases of confidentiality set against harm, where my verdicts come apart despite the shared structure. A public defender has learned from a privileged admission that her client committed the charged crime, and must either mount a defense she expects will acquit him or breach the confidence; I feel she does wrong whichever she chooses, and that this is a genuine dilemma. A therapist has good evidence that her patient will seriously harm an identifiable person, and must either warn or keep the secret; I feel this is no dilemma at all — warn, and nothing is left over afterward except the cost of having been in that position.

7. I can accept a post at which I would do modest good for a great many people I will never meet, or stay where I am and care for my aging mother. Verdict: I want to call this a genuine dilemma, but I suspect the label is doing no work beyond marking that the choice is hard and consequential, since I think neither option is wrong and I think nothing is left over except the ordinary grief of a life not led.

Against that background, I want the following.

First: is there a principle that delivers all seven verdicts, or close to all of them? State it precisely. It should say what separates a genuine conflict from an epistemic one and from one that is derivative of the agent's own prior conduct; under what conditions an overridden consideration leaves a remainder at all; what kinds of remainder there are — regret, guilt, atonement, compensation, explanation, an apology owed — and what determines which is appropriate; to whom, if anyone, the remainder is directed; and whether wrong can attach to both horns of a single choice.

Second: say which of my cases motivates each clause, so that I can see what work each clause is doing and what would be lost if I deleted it.

Third: give the verdicts your principle delivers on cases I have not listed. Choose at least two yourself, and make them ones that discriminate between the principle you favor and its closest rival; include at least one on which you expect my verdict would change once the principle is applied to it.

Fourth: state plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep. If keeping all of them is possible only by adding machinery, tell me what that machinery is and whether I should refuse to pay for it. Where a simpler principle forces me to abandon one of the seven, say which one and make the case for abandoning it rather than merely noting the cost.

Fifth: standard deontic logic makes obligation imply permission and closes obligation under conjunction, which appears to render conflicting obligations inexpressible rather than false. If the principle you favor requires genuine conflicts of obligation, tell me the minimal repair — dropping closure under conjunction, dropping the implication from obligation to permission, distinguishing an all-things-considered ought from a pro tanto one, or something else — and what each repair costs. If your principle avoids conflicts of obligation altogether, tell me what it makes of cases 1, 4, and 6, where I appear to be reporting precisely such conflicts.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — either the principle you endorse, compressed into a single claim, or your verdict that no principle fits all seven — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of the question.
